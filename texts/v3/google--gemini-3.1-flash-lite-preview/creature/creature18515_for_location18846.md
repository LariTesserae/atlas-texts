To inhabit such a place is to exist in a state of suspended biology. If anything occupies this vaulted lung, it is not a creature of breath or blood, but a creature of *turgor*.

The inhabitants, if they can be identified as singular beings at all, are the **Lumen-Gliders**. They are translucent, flat-bodied entities, roughly the circumference of a human embrace, possessing a texture indistinguishable from the waxy, opalized leaves upon which they drift. They do not walk; they adhere. They are the slow-motion agents of this stasis, existing in a physiological rhythm so protracted that a single "step" might take a decade to complete.

They are formed of a substance akin to hardened sap, clear as polished amber, containing within their central cavities a suspended network of bioluminescent filaments. When they move, they do not move *across* the floor; they shift their molecular anchor, a subtle liquefaction of their underside that allows them to slide a few inches, only to re-harden, becoming a permanent part of the landscape again.

There is no hunger here, but there is a slow, rhythmic requirement for the silver condensation that gathers in the junctions of the fronds. The Lumen-Gliders are the curators of this moisture. With a delicate, almost invisible protrusion—a proboscis that resembles a fine thread of spun glass—they bridge the gap between two pools, tethering the silver beads together. They do not drink; they participate in the pooling, their bodies acting as conduits to ensure the mineral-rich fluid remains evenly distributed across the vast, closed circuit of the valley.

They have no eyes. The blinding, noon-white ceiling provides all the information they could require. They sense the world through the radiant heat of the fungi; they are sensitive to the *pop-hiss* of the spore-sacs, which act as their only markers of time. When a sac expands, a ripple of kinetic energy travels through the floor-vines. The Gliders detect this vibration and respond by shifting their internal phosphorescence, darkening from a brilliant alabaster to a soft, bruised violet, absorbing the hum of the environment like a battery absorbing a charge.

They are not solitary, yet they do not socialize. They exist as a constellation of points in the vast, green geometry. Occasionally, two Gliders will draw close enough for their boundaries to touch. In that union, there is no language or mating; there is only a shared osmosis, an exchange of internal crystalline structures that reinforces their collective stability. They become a temporary, larger shape—a bridge of flesh—before slowly pulling apart, leaving behind a faint, hardened residue, one of the many "calcified rings" found scattered across the floor.

They have no fear, for there is nothing here that can harm them. They are not predators, nor are they prey; they are merely the living ornaments of an eternal arrangement. They are the gardeners who do not prune, the observers who have forgotten the concept of looking. 

In the center of their existence, they are indistinguishable from the architecture itself. If you were to press your ear to a tower of waxy veins, you would not hear a heartbeat; you would hear the sound of the world’s own pressure, the slow, immutable grinding of basalt against root, and the silent, shimmering weight of a creature that has decided, aeons ago, that it has reached the perfect place to stop.
