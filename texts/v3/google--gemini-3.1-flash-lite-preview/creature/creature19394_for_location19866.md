The inhabitants of the City of Glass Veins are known to themselves, by the frantic rhythm of their percussion, as the *Grit-Kin*. To observe them is to watch an experiment in biology pushed to the absolute brittle edge of survival.

They are not born of birth-sacs or marrow; they seem to precipitate out of the humidity itself. A Grit-Kin begins as a localized crystalline cluster, a milky-white calcification that thickens until it gains a central, nervous tremor. Over several cycles of steam-hiss, these clusters extrude spindly, articulated limbs—multi-jointed appendages that look like bleached pipe-cleaners, yet possess the tensile strength of tempered wire. Their bodies are semi-opaque, showing the dark, sludgy flow of mineral-rich bile circulating through clear, vein-like cavities. They do not have eyes, for light is an ambient, physical pressure here; instead, their heads are smooth, domed sensors of polished quartz that refract the flickering glare of the fissures, turning their surfaces into kaleidoscopes of blinding white.

They are creatures of kinetic desperation. They do not walk; they adhere. With hooked, obsidian-tipped phalanges, they spend their entire existence in a state of perpetual adhesion, scurrying vertically and horizontally across the curved surfaces of their home. If a Grit-Kin were to stop—to truly rest—it would risk becoming part of the architecture itself, its tissues beginning to mineralize into the very glass it maintains.

Their social structure is a frantic, non-verbal bureaucracy of touch. When two of the Kin meet, they do not communicate through sound, but through a rapid, rhythmic tapping of their glass tools against the other’s chassis. A rapid-fire chittering signal marks a structural failure; a slow, dragging slide of a pick-axe against the wall denotes a nutrient-rich tremor. They are a library of repairs, a living census of the city’s fractures. They are obsessed with *seams*. Where the earth folds, the Kin arrive in a swarm, their elongated limbs working in a blur of motion, layering resinous, excreted fluids into the cracks to fuse the shifting fault lines back into a semblance of permanence.

There is a tragic, rhythmic pathos to their scavenging. They feed upon the crystal-lichen, carefully shearing it from the damp vents with their volcanic-glass blades. They do not enjoy the consumption; it is a metabolic demand, a refueling of their fragile, translucent frames. When a Kin dies, it does not decay. It simply loses the ability to repel the calcification of the environment. Its joints lock, its skin turns from translucent to opaque, and it eventually becomes a permanent outcropping of the wall—a jagged statue added to the honeycombed pylons, eventually to be worn smooth by the friction of its living kin.

In their movements, one can see the ghost of a civilization that has internalized the instability of the world. They are the frantic immune system of a body that is constantly dying. They do not dream, for their minds are consumed by the geometry of the next minute. They are the living embodiment of the "maintenance" that defines their hell. They do not want to build a better future; they do not even want to survive, in the conventional sense. They are slaves to a beautiful, shifting, impossible architecture, terrified that if they ever put down their tools, the silence would finally, mercifully, swallow them whole.
