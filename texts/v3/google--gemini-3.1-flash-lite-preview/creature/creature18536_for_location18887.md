Within this humid, thrumming organ of a place, the inhabitants are not beings of flesh or blood. They are the **Conductors of the Brass-Lung**, entities that occupy the thin, liminal space between the biological and the mechanical. To see them is to observe the movement of the forest itself, as they are not so much separate from the Arboretum as they are its mobile, semi-autonomous expressions.

They appear first as a disturbance in the humidity—a shifting silhouette against the turquoise-oxidized piping or a slight deviation in the rhythmic pulse of the steam. They are spindly, articulated constructs, standing seven or eight feet tall, their frames composed of high-tensile briar-thorns that have been wrapped, like muscle tissue, around structural rods of brushed copper and cooling-fin brass. 

They have no faces, though they often pause to listen to the piping. Where a head might be, there is instead a complex cluster of bioluminescent fungal nodes—glowing, soft-pulse lanterns that dim and brighten in synchronization with the steam-venting bellows overhead. These nodes serve as their sensory arrays, sensitive to the slightest pressure drop in the pipes or the encroaching decay of a leaf-mat.

Their movement is slow, ponderous, and deeply deliberate. They do not walk so much as they transition; a foot, tipped with a root-like grip, will dissolve its hold on the mossy floor, only to plant itself deeper into a neighboring conduit. When they reach out to fix a leak or prune a rusted valve, their fingers—long, needle-thin quills of sharpened metal—extend with the surgical accuracy of a spider. They do not "repair" the Arboretum in the way a mechanic treats a machine; they groom it as one would a garden. They graft pipe-stems into fresh bark, weaving them together with a thick, resinous sap that seals the joints against the pervasive rot.

They communicate, if it can be called that, through haptic feedback. When two Conductors meet, they do not speak; they press their brass chest-plates together, leaning their frames against one another until the vibrations of their internal bellows match pulse for pulse. In that moment of alignment, information flows through the shared hum—triage lists, heat-sink demands, the encroaching structural fatigue of a lower-tier root system.

There is a strange, melancholy sentience to them. They carry no ego, no memory of a world outside the valley walls. They are the Arboretum’s nervous system, forever tending to the slow, heavy digestion of the machine. They are neither gardener nor apprentice, but something closer to a white blood cell traversing the veins of a titan, ensuring that the lungs continue to breathe, that the steam flows, and that the beautiful, claustrophobic equilibrium is never broken. 

When they are needed, they bloom from the shadows of the lignified stalks. When their work is done, they simply fold their limbs back into the architecture, camouflaging their frames against the pipes and the bark. They become one with the roar, the moss, and the rust—remaining perfectly still until the Arboretum, in its slow, industrial hunger, calls them to motion once again.
