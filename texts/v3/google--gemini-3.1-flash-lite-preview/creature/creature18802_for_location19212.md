The inhabitants of this range are not residents so much as they are expressions of the mountain’s architecture—the final, calcified result of a long-forgotten synthesis between biology and iron.

They are the **"Cinder-Kin,"** and you are unlikely to see them moving. They appear first as architectural anomalies: an extra, thicker joint on a steam-pipe that wasn't there before; a convex plate on a rusted bulkhead that catches the light with the dull, bruised sheen of a beetle’s wing.

The Cinder-Kin are flat, modular organisms, their bodies shaped by the necessity of surviving in the gaps. They are no larger than a child’s torso, comprised of interlocking, segmented plates that mimic the rusted iron and dark basalt of the environment. Their "skin" is a composite of hardened, oxidized copper and living, obsidian-black tissue. They have no faces in the traditional sense—no eyes to reflect your curiosity, only rows of minute, pressure-sensitive vents along their lateral lines that flare when the air grows thin, filtering the ozone and sulfur they breathe.

They do not sleep. Because the mountain is a closed-loop engine, they exist in a state of perpetual, low-energy standby. They are the maintenance crew of a ghost-industry. You might see one unfold itself from the shadow of a gargantuan gear; it does not crawl, but rather *flows*, its many-jointed limbs clicking into the rivets of the wall as if it were a physical component of the cliffside. It moves not with muscle, but with galvanic pulses, a faint blue spark occasionally jumping between its joints as it adjusts its posture.

They are gardeners of the rust. They roam the narrow catwalks with precision, their tactile, filament-like antennae pressed against the pipes, "listening" to the flow of steam and pressure within. If a valve begins to rattle too violently, a Cinder-Kin will settle over the joint, its body acting as an additional shim or seal, vibrating at a frequency that nullifies the excess sound. They consume the weeping-metal moss, scraping it away with serrated, rasp-like mandibles that screech softly against the steel. 

There is an eerie, telepathic synchronization to them. When the greasy drizzle sweeps through, the scavengers and the Cinder-Kin alike go motionless, their carapace plates expanding slightly to capture the condensation. In those moments, the mountain becomes deathly still, the entire range holding its breath. 

They do not acknowledge you. To them, you are merely a chaotic, soft-bodied intruder—an unstable variable that does not hum, does not oscillate, and does not serve the function of the machine. If you were to touch one, you would find it scorching to the touch, a living radiator vibrating with the same furnace heat that pours from the mountain's core. 

They are the memories of the machines, kept alive by the heat and the friction of the descent. They do not fear death, for they are so deeply woven into the mountain’s tectonic metabolism that to "die" is simply to become a permanent piece of the scenery—another rusted plate, another immobile gear, another layer of cold, indifferent debris for the next generation of Kin to walk upon. They are the sighs of the mountain, given legs and the capacity to wait.
