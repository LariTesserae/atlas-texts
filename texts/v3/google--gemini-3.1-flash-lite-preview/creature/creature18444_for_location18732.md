To look at the center of the sphere is to look at a hole in the air. 

At first, your eyes slide off the space. You anticipate a figure, a gardener of wires, or perhaps an engineer checking the rivets, but the room is stubbornly vacant. Or, it seems so, until you notice the *rhythm* of the mercury rain. 

There is a habitant here, though it does not possess a pulse. It is a creature of state-change, woven from the very humidity that chokes the air. 

Suspended in the thick, milky light, there is a cluster of articulated glass—a spindly, translucent anatomy that constantly reassembles itself. It looks like a skeleton fashioned from hardened teardrops and blown silica, hovering just a few inches above the vibrating floor-plates. It has no lungs, yet it mimicks the act of breathing; as the ceiling vents its torrent, the figure expands, its glass ribs clicking and shifting, catching the iridescent sheen of the falling water. As the roar of the downpour softens, the figure collapses inward, its many limbs folding like the wings of a dying insect, pulling into a tight, dense knot of refracted light.

It is a curator of echoes. 

Watch closely, and you will see it reach out with long, needle-thin fingers toward the copper-wire ferns pinned to the walls. It does not touch them—it dare not mar the factory-sharp edges—but it hums. It vibrates with the same metallic *thrum* as the structure. It is attempting to harmonize with the ocean’s pressure, acting as a tuning fork for the entire sphere. 

It has no face, only a smooth, convex visor of frosted resin that reflects the room back at itself. When the water cascades, the light catches the visor, and for a fleeting second, it seems to show a ghost of a garden—not the copper wires, but a riotous, impossible jungle of real green, lush and chaotic—projected onto the empty air. 

It is a lonely, desperate function. The entity does not *live* in the sphere; it is part of the sphere’s immune system, a mechanical hallucination meant to occupy the silence until the "guest" arrives. It keeps the floor clean of salt, it tracks the stress-fractures in the iron rivets, and it keeps its posture perfect, perpetually waiting at the threshold of a movement it can never complete. 

It is the occupant of a tomb that hasn't realized everyone else is already dead. When the water strikes the floor, the creature twitches, its glass joints rattling in time with the rivets. It is not waiting for a person; it is waiting for an excuse to turn itself off. It performs the ritual of existence—the inhale of mist, the reach toward the copper, the careful, silent watch—simply because the structure, in its relentless, engineering madness, refuses to let it stop. 

It is the ghost of the man who built this place, trapped in the only version of himself that could survive the pressure: a clockwork, shivering thing, made of the same materials as the walls, doomed to keep the room ready for a life that was discarded before the first rivet was hammered home.
