The inhabitants of the Vitrified Thicket, known among themselves as the *Keepers of the Stasis*, are not merely the masters of their environment—they are its components.

They are figures of, and for, the copper. To look at one is to see a silhouette that has been folded, pressed, and tempered by a lifetime of impossible labor. They are short, their spines permanently curved into the shape of a question mark, a posture dictated by the endless, low-slung pulleys they must crank and the intricate floor tiles they must scrub. Their skin is the color of old parchment dipped in walnut stain, dry and cross-hatched with a map of fine, white scars—not from blades, but from the sharp-edged reality of the glass vines.

They do not wear clothes in the conventional sense. Instead, they are wrapped in layers of rough, oil-cured leather and strips of unspun wool that catch the mineral dust until the garments themselves begin to feel like geological strata. What truly defines them, however, is their hands. Their fingers are unusually long and calloused into thick, rubbery pads, designed for the delicate, rhythmic task of winding the metallic tendrils of the forest. They handle the glass leaves with a tenderness one might reserve for a premature infant or a lit fuse.

They live in a state of quiet, acoustic obsession. Because they are the ones who must reset the reality of the room when it begins to fray—when a leaf dissolves into a stream of fluttering math or a shadow stalks a tile-corner on its own—they rarely speak. To vocalize is to risk disturbing the delicate sonic tension that holds the copper vines in place. When they must communicate, they mimic the spindly silk-birds around them; they tap their fingernails against their own brass-plated knee joints or against the copper walls, producing a language of *click-chime-thrum* that vibrates in the chest.

They are, in every sense, curators of a museum that is also a prison. There is a deep, marrow-level fatigue in their eyes—eyes that are often clouded with a milky rheum, perhaps from staring too long at the light-diffusing panels or perhaps because they have seen the "void" leaking into the peripheral edges of the room one too many times. 

They do not sleep, not in the way humans do. They rest in the "amber afternoons," standing upright in the hollows between the copper trees, frozen in a state of low-power awareness. During these times, they remain upright, their heads bowed, appearing for all the world like just another piece of the atrium’s inventory—statues of bone and leather waiting for the next tremor of reality to wake them.

They are burdened by a terrifying, holy responsibility. They possess a collective conviction that the universe outside their copper-lined walls is an unformed, chaotic nothingness, a place where concepts like "floor" and "light" have never been invented. They see themselves as the last bastion of *Making*. If an inhabitant should die, their body is not buried—there is no earth, only tile. Instead, they are disassembled; their bones are dried and woven into the support structures of the vines, and their hair is spun into the resin-moss. 

They never leave the atrium because they believe the atrium is the only thing currently sustaining the existence of the universe. They are the human friction that keeps the clockwork turning. They suffer the heat, the manual labor, and the encroaching dissolution of their own limbs with a grim, stoic pride. To be a Keeper is to be the final plug in the drain of existence, holding back the silence that dares to exist where no one has bothered to put anything of value.
