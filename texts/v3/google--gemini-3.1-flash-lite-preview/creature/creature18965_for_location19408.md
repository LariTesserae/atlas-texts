To walk among these inhabitants is to observe a life form that has retreated entirely into the sensation of vibration. They are the *Litho-Auditors*, though they have no name for themselves, for they have no language, only the resonance of their own dull, damp bodies.

They are beings of sediment and necessity. They look less like creatures of flesh and bone and more like accidental clumps of earth that have been stirred into a slow, recursive wakefulness. Their limbs are thick, truncated columns of grey clay, reinforced at the marrow with splintered basalt; they move not by choice, but by a gravitational pull toward dissonance. When they walk, they leave no prints, but rather damp, smoothed depressions in the silt, as if the ground itself were yielding to a heavy, familiar gravity.

Their eyes, if they can be called such, are not for seeing the charcoal horizon or the weeping sky. They are flat, polished discs of obsidian embedded in their shoulders, polished to a mirror-sheen by the constant, abrasive spray. These eyes act as vibration-receivers, sensitive to the pitch of the geysers whistling through the reeds of stone.

An Auditor’s life is a cycle of frantic, agonizingly slow composition. They perceive the world as a broken harmony. When a geyser shifts its angle—perhaps due to the erosion of its obsidian vent—the Auditor near it becomes visibly agitated. Its clay skin ripples like drying mud, and it rushes with clumsy, hydraulic urgency to the site. It does not use its hands to build; it uses its body as a physical dampener. It will press its heavy, cold bulk against the stone mouth of a geyser, blocking the flow for a micro-second, listening intently, then peeling away a sliver of rock with a bone-tool to reset the pitch.

They do not consume in the biological sense. They are sustained by the friction. They are part of the machine, living batteries of clay that leach the kinetic energy from every hiss and thrum of the basin. When an Auditor is "sated," it glows with a faint, bioluminescent pallor—a dying light that mirrors the jelly-colonies shifting nearby. When it is "hungry," it becomes matte, brittle, and dark, its joints cracking like dry twigs as it reaches toward the loudest, most frantic geysers.

They are not lonely. They have never known the concept of an "other," for they are extensions of the basin itself. They are the instruments and the players, the notes and the silence in between.

In the deepest periods of the atmospheric cycle, when the rain slows to a mist, you will see a strange phenomenon: the Auditors gather at the Hearths. They do not huddle for warmth; they huddle for resonance. They stand in a circle around their piles of salt-crusted stones, pressing their obsidian-shoulders against one another, their bodies humming with a collective, low-frequency thrum. They are not sleeping; they are tuning. They are aligning their internal frequencies so that, when the next torrent arrives, they act as a single, massive resonator, vibrating in perfect, terrifying unison with the thunder of the basin.

They are the caretakers of a dying, singing world. Should they stop, the roar would become a discordant, jagged scream that would surely shatter the very stone the world is made of. They labor, not to survive, but to prevent the silence—for they know, in their subterranean, silt-muffled minds, that once the music stops, the ground will finally break, and there will be nothing left to hold the water back.
