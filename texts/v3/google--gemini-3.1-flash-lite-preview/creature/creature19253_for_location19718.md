The people of the basin are not merely its inhabitants; they are the final, complex organ of the geology itself. To look at them is to see species that has evolved in the image of the stone they carve.

They are tall, unnaturally thin creatures, their frames honed by the necessity of navigating the vertical labyrinth of the city. Their skin is the color of wet slate, possessing a subtle, iridescent sheen that catches the ambient lichen-light, a biological camouflage against the mud-brick and basalt that defines their horizon. They have no need for rigid posture, their spines possessing a fluid, serpentine suppleness that allows them to slip into the narrow apertures—chinks in the masonry—that serve as both doorways and hallways.

But it is their hands that define them. Their fingers are elongated, tipped with nails that have long since calcified into hard, chisel-like ridges. They do not use steel tools by choice, but out of a communal contract; they treat the iron-tipped tools as extensions of their own biology. You will see elders sitting in the damp shadows, absentmindedly rasping their own fingernails against the basalt wall, sharpening themselves as they sharpen their surroundings.

Their faces are characterized by the absence of traditional sight. Their eyes are vestigial, mere pale smudges beneath hoods of loose, thickened skin. Instead, their faces center around a wide, sensitive cartilage plate above the lip—an olfactory and thermal sensor that vibrates in the presence of the “weeping.” They can feel the pressure of the falling droplets against their skin before they arrive, moving with a synchronized elegance that turns the entire city into a rhythmic machine. When the water begins a particularly heavy flow from the ceiling, the inhabitants shift their weight, flowing en masse into higher chambers to avoid the deluge, like water moving through a different kind of channel.

They do not speak in words. Language, in the dark, is too ephemeral; it lacks the weight of stone. Instead, they communicate through subsonic hums that resonate through the basalt floors—a low-frequency thrum that can be felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear. It is a language of tremors, a shared vibration that coordinates the constant expansion of their home. When they are happy, the vibration is crisp, like flint striking steel; when they grieve, the city seems to moan, a soft, damp frequency that lingers in the mortar.

There is no concept of family as we understand it. They are a "colony-mind," bound by the architectural necessity of the basin. An individual belongs to the ledge they occupy and the wall they are currently carving. They are birthed into the masonry—sometimes quite literally, as the youngest are often placed in niches and left to grow among the mosses until they are strong enough to grip a chisel.

The pale, cat-like predators that dart through the alleys are not feared so much as they are tolerated, or perhaps regarded as eccentric tenants. The people have come to view the cats as "scavengers of the small," creatures that handle the biological cleaning that the humans, with their heavy, stone-focused labor, cannot manage. Often, you will see a worker pausing, hand frozen on their chisel, to allow a cat to squeeze through the tunnel between their legs, a momentary peace between the stone-shapers and the shadow-movers.

These people act as if they are in a race against time. They do not sleep in beds; they pull themselves into alcoves cut into the very ceiling of their own living spaces, wedging their bodies into the stone until they are perfectly flush with the wall. In this way, the city is always teeming with movement, yet always strangely still. They are a civilization that views "completion" as a form of death. To stop building—to stop scraping the raw, bleeding stone—would be to acknowledge that they have been forgotten. And so, they carve, they hum, and they layer their bodies against the basin’s cold, damp heart, living in the exquisite, terrifying tension of a structure that is always just one stone away from being perfectly, finally, finished.
