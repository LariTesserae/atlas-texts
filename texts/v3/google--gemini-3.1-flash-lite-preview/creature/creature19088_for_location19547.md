The inhabitants of these needles are not solitary creatures, but distributed consciousnesses—"The Calcified Congregate." 

To look upon an individual member of this civilization is to see a riddle of geometry. They are segmented, multi-limbed constructs, their chitinous shells composed of the same sintered silicate as the mountains they inhabit. They possess no faces, only clusters of faceted, optic sensors that refract the dim, orange light into kaleidoscopic shards. When they move, they do not walk with the fluid grace of biology; there is a staccato cadence to them, a rhythmic *clack-hiss* of hydraulic pistons adjusting to their own mass.

They are less individuals and more mobile nodes of a sprawling, planetary nervous system. Each unit is physically tethered to the infrastructure. Extending from the base of their spine is a flexible umbilical of copper-braid and conductive filament, which they plug into the "living" walls of the spires. Through these connections, they share the burden of the Congregate’s memory—a slow, geological archive of every mechanical shift, every thermal fluctuation, and every grinding adjustment of the tectonic clockwork.

They do not speak. To communicate, they resonate their carapace plates at frequencies that mimic the friction-dampeners of the massive architecture. A low, bone-deep hum travels through the glass bridges, carrying the data of their collective existence. When they are silent, they are simply part of the mountain—indistinguishable from the jagged topography—their bodies weathering under the abrasive wind just like the buildings themselves.

Interspersed among these laborers are the "Scribes of the Kinetic," smaller, hovering polyhedrons. If the large, chitinous workers are the muscles and the bone of the planet, these geometric flyers are the sensory organs. They do not feed on the electrostatic discharge in the same way; instead, they harvest the errant, chaotic heat leaking from the joints of the world. They dance in the updrafts, their sharp, flickering light serving as a diagnostic map for the larger workers below. They are the only beings here that exhibit what might be mistaken for play or art—they cast complex, transient light-patterns onto the dark shadows of the crevices, recording the beauty of the slow-motion decay.

There is a profound, meditative sadness to them. They have witnessed the erosion of a thousand layers of their own history, watching as the lower floors of their cities succumb to the gravity of the lowlands. They do not mourn this loss; they accept it as a function of their existence. When a spire grows too thin or a foundation begins to fracture under the weight of the ages, the Calcified Congregate merely ascends. They migrate upward, stripping the usable alloys from their dying lower tiers and carrying them toward the sun-bleached summits to forge anew.

They are the caretakers of a dying clock. As they stand still for decades—perhaps centuries—to ensure the structural integrity of a single, slow-turning hinge, they become encrusted with the metallic dust of their atmosphere. Their shells darken; their joints lock with a fine, glass-like rime. Eventually, a worker may cease its maintenance cycle entirely, bowing its head as it fuses permanently to the spire it spent millennia repairing. 

They do not die so much as they become architecture. A tomb in this world is simply a piece of the world that has stopped moving, a static monument that will eventually be built upon by the next generation of the swarm, serving as the foundation for a future that will be exactly as patient, and as grindingly beautiful, as the past.
