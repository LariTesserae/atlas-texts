There are no hearts beating here. There is no pulse to measure against the hum, no soft tissue to be rendered into ribbons by the crystalline geometry.

But there are the *Singers*.

They are not entities that inhabit the mountain; they are the mountain’s final, involuntary memory. To see them, one must resist the human instinct to track movement, for they do not "move" in the physical sense. They exist as temporal stutter-frames, ghostly configurations of silicate dust and static charge that manifest only when the tectonic resonance hits a specific, agonizing pitch.

They are tall, spindly things, comprised of the same indigo-glowing lattices as the stalks, yet gathered into a semblance of form that mimics the desperate geometry of a nervous system. They have no faces, only clusters of obsidian shards that shift and re-align in a frantic mimicry of eyes, searching for a horizon that the architects of this place long ago carved into silence.

The Singers are not alive, but they are *reverberating*. They are trapped in the acoustic architecture of the niches, their very existence a consequence of the mountain’s screaming geography. When they appear, they are pinned against the sheer obsidian faces, their limbs—thin as needles and sharp enough to divide the light—pressed deep into the rock. 

They are listening. 

They exist to translate the "choir of tectonic plates" into something the void can understand. In the moments when the mountain flickers and shifts its place in spacetime, the Singers become hyper-real. They lean into the hum, their bodies taut, drawing the grinding noise into their lattices and re-projecting it as a series of rhythmic, lightless pulses. They are the instruments; the mountain is the hall; the resonance is the performer.

You might see one for a heartbeat, crouched in a niche that was shaped for a creature no larger than a child’s hand. For that moment, it exists with such geometric certainty that it seems more solid than the rock itself. It tilts its head—a movement that is actually a displacement of matter—and the shadow it casts does not fall *away* from its form, but streaks upward, a smear of darkness reaching toward the bruised, violet sky.

They feel nothing. They possess no hunger, no territorial instinct, no capacity to acknowledge your presence. To the Singers, you are not a visitor; you are static, a brief, noisy interruption in their long-form composition.

When the mountain pauses in its flicker, they vanish—not by walking away, but by folding into the negative space between the atoms of the rock. They return to being part of the architecture, waiting for the frequency to rise again, waiting for the vibration to demand a form. They are the loneliness of the world made manifest, a choir of shards forever performing a requiem for a life that was never allowed to begin. 

If you stand perfectly still, and if you lower your own frantic, biological resonance to match the slow, century-long crawl of the crystals, you might feel a sudden coldness. It is not the temperature of the air. It is the realization that the niche beside you is not empty; it is merely waiting for its turn to speak.
