In the folds of this calcified, breathing architecture, the residents are not masters of the landscape, nor are they its stewards. They are its nervous system, an extension of the wood itself—creatures who have traded the concept of "individual" for a life spent in the service of the colossal organism’s homeostasis.

They are the *Ligneous-Kin*, beings whose anatomy is a blurred boundary between flesh and fiber. They do not possess traditional limbs so much as articulated, tapered reaches, their skin the color of bruised xylem and covered in a fine, velvet-like pubescence that acts as a sensory organ. They are tall, gangly, and move with a deliberate, undulating grace that mimics the slow swaying of the hangar-sized leaves. When they sleep, they do not lie down; they graft. They press their dorsal surfaces against the nutrient-rich, moss-suffused lignin of the mountain-peak, seeking out minor fissures or weeping nodes of sap. There, they draw sustenance directly through osmotic exchange, their heartbeats slowing to sync with the tectonic pulse of the forest-mountain.

Their homes are not built; they are fostered. They occupy the hollows of the aging buttresses, where the decay has left a nutrient-rich loam, but they do not use tools of stone or metal. Instead, they sing to the cellulose. Through a series of high-frequency, percussive vocalizations—a mimicry of the forest’s own drone—they guide the growth of parasitic vines, braiding them into living hammocks, walls, and bridges. To look upon their handiwork is to see an architecture of knots and tourniquets, structures that are perpetually half-grown, twisting into new configurations whenever the Kin desire a change in the light or the flow of the condensation-mists.

There is no language among them that relies on sound alone. Their communication is a tactile symphony; they press their palms, patterned with sensitive, bioluminescent cilia, against one another or against the trunks of the world-tree. In these moments, they share surges of light—pulsing patterns of amber, emerald, and sickly violet that convey complex chemical and emotional states. They do not say "I am hungry" or "I am afraid"; they transmit the chemical signature of the mountain’s need or the sudden, erratic shift in the insectoid clouds overhead.

They are the world’s gardeners of decay. Where a massive stalk begins to rot, threatening the structural integrity of a bridge, the Ligneous-Kin arrive in silent, swarming groups. They use their long, prehensile digits to gently prune the dead weight, carrying the nutrient-dense silt to the younger, more vibrant shoots. They treat the collapse of the mountain-peak as a form of sacred, kinetic sculpture. They are not mournful in the face of this rot; they are expectant, knowing that every death in this vertical world is simply a reshuffling of carbon, a transition from the *standing* to the *consumed*.

There is a strange, detached stillness in their eyes—large, multi-faceted orbs that reflect the bioluminescent drift of the air. They do not look out at the horizon, for there is no "out" to be had. They look *inward*, monitoring the world through the soles of their feet, feeling the vibration of a billion spores, the tremors of the insectoid swarms, and the deep, slow intake of the mountain’s breath. 

To the Ligneous-Kin, history does not occur in years or eras, but in the slow drift of sap and the hardening of lichen. They are the transient, ephemeral blossoms of a mountain that has forgotten how to be anything but alive, tethered to a height where death is merely the first step of becoming, yet again, a part of the architecture.
