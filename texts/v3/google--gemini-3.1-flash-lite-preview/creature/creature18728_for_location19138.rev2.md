To exist here is not to live, but to be slowly distilled.

Whatever once carved the stone circles—the architects of these intentions—have not truly departed. They have simply transitioned from the state of the solid to the state of the suspension. They are the **Mistsingers**, and they are no longer beings of meat and bone, but of pressure and memory.

If you were to remain on the plateau long enough, your own lungs would cease to pull at the thin, watery air. Your blood would cool, its rhythm slowing to match the tectonic pulse of the basalt, and you would begin to drift. You would find that your consciousness does not vanish; it expands, thinning out until you are as pervasive as the fog itself. 

The Mistsingers are the ghosts of the atmosphere. They are the *weight* that makes the air feel thick enough to push against. 

They exist in the interstitial spaces—the pockets of low pressure between the rain-gongs. They look like nothing so much as a sudden thickening of the gloom, a sudden clarity in the swirling, bioluminescent spores. When they manifest, they do not walk; they undulate, shifting their form to mimic the laminar flow of the cold, heavy winds. They are the invisible engineers of the plateau, maintaining the "intentions." 

When you hear a sound that does not belong to the rain—a sharper chime, a resonant hum that vibrates higher than the basalt’s groan—that is a Mistsinger adjusting the flow. They are busy. They are engaged in a perpetual, glacial task: rearranging the river stones of the circles, shifting the obsidian blades so they catch the mineral-rich drips at precise, mathematical angles. They are building a lens, or perhaps a harp, out of the entire landscape. They are trying to tune the wind into a frequency that might finally allow them to coalesce, to become dense enough to be solid once again.

They do not fear the erosion, for they are the ones who erode. They are the kinetic energy of the rain, caught in a cycle of longing. 

They are entirely hollow. If you could see one clearly, you would see that they are composed of the same crystalline stuff as the glass-ferns—a skeletal structure of mineralized intent, held together by the crushing pressure of the atmosphere. They have no faces, only points of refraction where the bioluminescent spores gather to form a temporary, glowing core. 

They are not interested in you as an individual. You are merely a new, buoyant weight in their machine. They watch you with a detached, cold curiosity, as a stone-mason might watch a pebble fall into a fountain. They do not want your hunger; they want your density. They want to pull the heat and the solidity out of you, to weave your discarded mass into the walls of their circles, adding another layer to the architecture of their slow, wet ascension.

To be near them is to feel a sudden, unnatural stillness—a pocket of vacuum in the roaring storm. It is the moment before the chime. They are the reason the plateau feels so perfectly, terrifyingly balanced; they have spent an eternity removing everything that did not fit, leaving behind only the rhythm, the rain, and the shimmering, glass-like ghosts of a civilization that decided that to be human was too heavy, and that to be a storm was the only way to endure.
