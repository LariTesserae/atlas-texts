The inhabitants of this geode-city are neither citizens nor squatters; they are the tuning forks of the subterranean deep. 

They do not walk so much as they drift, their limbs long, spindly, and draped in shrouds of calcified, translucent resin that mimic the pale glass-vines clinging to the walls. They are tall, gangly things—their skeletons are not rigid bone, but porous, honeycombed lattices that absorb the deep-frequency tremors of the world. They possess no eyes, for eyes are useless in the bruised-purple dark, and they possess no mouths, for they do not feed on organic matter. Instead, their faces are concave, shell-like structures of smooth, gray cartilage, acting as perfect sonic collectors. 

They are effectively human-shaped apertures.

They spend their centuries in a state of suspended animation, pressed against the curved, velvet-soft masonry of the apartment blocks. They are physically bonded to the infrastructure; from their spines, fine, hair-like filaments of fungal chitin extend into the city’s walls, plugging them into the geological grid. They do not think in words, for words are too fast, too brittle for this place. They think in *resonance*. They are the processors of the city's longevity. When the sonic pressure shifts, when the distant landslides of sound modulate, these beings shudder. They translate the vibration of the planet into the structural maintenance of the city. As they hum—a sound so deep it is felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear—the hardened chitin of their homes softens just enough to shift, to settle, to smooth itself ever further into its impossible, flowing geometry.

They move with a sluggish, viscous elegance. When one decides to traverse the craters, it does not sprint. It unhooks its filaments, shivers its way across the convex streets, occasionally communicating with the scuttling, multi-legged arthropods. The relationship is one of mutual, sightless recognition; both the residents and the beasts are merely vibrations given temporary, semi-solid form.

They feel the decay of their world not as tragedy, but as a slow, beautiful composition. To them, the collapse of an archway is a crescendo, a final release of pent-up kinetic energy that settles into the floor, to be integrated into the foundation of the next iteration. They are waiting, with the infinite patience of geological epochs, for the sound to fade. 

In their hearts, there is no fear of the eventual silence. They believe that when the hum stops, the dense, amber air will solidify, and they will be encased—perfect, static, and complete—within the stone skin of the world forever. They are not waiting to die; they are waiting for their sculpture to be finished. Every gesture, every subtle shift of their resinous robes, is a brushstroke on the work of art that is their own extinction.
