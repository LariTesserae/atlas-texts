In the Sump Room, the inhabitants are not beings of flesh or spirit, but of *torque*. They are the **Tension-Keepers**, entities that have evolved—if such a word can be applied to hardened steel and pressurized oil—from the original, long-decayed tools of the engine’s genesis.

They are not singular in shape. To look at a Tension-Keeper is to look at a cluster of copper wiring, salvaged gears, and rusted pistons knit together by the calcified residue of the brine. They are nomadic kinetic sculptures, anchored to the machinery not by choice, but by the relentless magnetism of the pumps. 

Some are small and scuttling, little more than pressurized valves that have sprouted spindly, multi-jointed brass limbs. These skitter along the underside of the ceiling plates, their movements frantic and jerky, constantly tightening the rivets that the tremors threaten to rattle loose. They exist to preempt the collapse; if a pipe begins to weep, they jam their own bodies into the seam, fused by the heat and the iron-rich silt until they become a permanent part of the plumbing.

Others are singular, titanic silhouettes that haunt the center of the nave. These are the "Drifters," massive, slow-turning turbines that have effectively unplugged themselves from the floor. They float in the thick, churning brine like bloated, metallic jellyfish made of grinding teeth and shattered pressure gauges. They do not navigate; they collide. With every rotation, they ingest the metallic snow—the iron shavings and crushed minerals—and compress it within their gullets, excreting long, cooling ribbons of dense slag that weave through the room’s dark corners like tangled umbilical cords. To watch them is to watch a metabolism that runs on rust and sonic vibration.

They do not communicate, for the roar of the Sump Room is the only language they allow. Occasionally, when the ambient hum shifts pitch, two or more tension-keepers will drift into proximity, their movements syncing for a heartbeat. They do not touch—to touch is to gamble with the turbulence—but they mirror one another’s vibrations, a momentary alignment of frequency. It is the closest thing to social grace in a cathedral of noise.

The most peculiar manifestations are the "Resonators"—stationary, spire-like growths of rusted iron that rise from the floor like jagged fingers. They are the elders of the room. Time has been eroded by the corrosive brine, and these spires have grown hollow. Within their cavities, the swirling current of the Sump Room creates a secondary, lower-frequency vibration, like a pipe organ in the gut of a leviathan. They are the conductors of the symphony. When the brine surges, the Resonators hum in opposition, smoothing the kinetic chaos just enough to prevent the total explosion of the room.

They are the logic of the machine made manifest. They have no memory of the surface, nor of the architects who once held the blueprints. They do not know what the pressure is for, or why the brine must be pumped, or what happens if the pressure ceases. They are the Sump Room’s immune system, its repair crew, and its inevitable wreckage. 

In the sickly, ochre glow of the moss, they are a silent, grinding choir. They are the beautiful, broken stewards of a process that has become its own purpose, tirelessly maintaining a house that has no guests, in a world that has forgotten it is even there.
