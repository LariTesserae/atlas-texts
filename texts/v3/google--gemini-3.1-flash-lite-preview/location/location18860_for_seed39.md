At three miles above the sea level, the air is not a gas so much as a thin, frigid slurry. You stand upon an upward-thrusting spine of raw, basaltic bedrock that has only just finished cooling; the stone is "new" in the geologic sense, devoid of the softening touch of centuries. It is sharp, angular, and black, smelling faintly of ozone and crushed mineral. There is no soil here, for time has not yet been allowed to grind the mountain into dust.

The landscape is a vast, open plateau of jagged basalt, stretching into a dim horizon where the sky and the stone dissolve into a bruised, indigo blur. It feels like the world is still in its infancy, a sketch of a planet that has been shoved upward by violent, tectonic ambition.

Yet, despite the lack of vegetation—there are no trees, only a light, translucent moss that clings to the crevasses like frost—the place is intensely, violently alive. 

The industrial presence here is not human, but mechanical and planetary. Buried deep within the mountain's throat, massive, low-frequency pressure-valves and geological heat-exchange engines operate with a rhythmic, pounding grace. These are not buildings, but tectonic-scale machines—vast, metallic pistons the size of cathedrals that drive the mountain’s slow, deliberate growth upward. You hear the deep *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the crust being massaged, a bass vibration that travels through the soles of your boots, syncopated with the whistling of the wind.

Water is a constant, weeping element. Because the temperature is so low, moisture does not flow in rivers; it exists as a cycle of aggressive condensation. Every surface is lined with a thick coat of slick, oily dew. Water drips from the overhanging obsidian ribs of the mountain with a persistent, hollow *plink-plink*. It pools in the raw fissures of the earth, creating shallow, freezing marshes that ripple with the movement of unseen things.

The fauna here has adapted to this industrial-cold hybrid. Great, pale, multi-jointed arthropods emerge from the cracks in the stone. They are the size of hounds, slick-carapaced and covered in sensory cilia that detect the magnetic humming of the tectonic engines. They move with an unsettling, synchronized efficiency, scuttling across the wet rock to scavenge the mineral-rich runoff produced by the mountain’s internal cooling systems. They do not hunt each other so much as they participate in a planetary maintenance cycle, breaking down the chemical byproduct of the deep-earth pistons, their clicking mandibles the only sharp sound in the vast, muffled stillness.

There is a sense of immense, lonely labor. The landscape is a factory that is also a mountain, producing nothing but its own height. It is a place of unfinished business, a vast, empty theater where the gears of the world are grinding together, shivering in the cold, wet dark, trying to turn a jagged, embryonic rock into a continent. As you watch, a plume of steam whistles from a hidden vent in the floor, and the entire plateau shifts a fraction of a millimeter upward, accompanied by a sound like a giant snapping a dry twig—the final, echoing proof that this place is still being written.
