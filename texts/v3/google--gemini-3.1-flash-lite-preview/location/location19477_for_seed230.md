High above the reach of the clouds, pressed into the narrow, sheltered cleft of a jagged mountain ridge, sits the town of Oru. It is a place that feels less like a city and more like a carefully arranged collection of fossils.

The architecture is the primary inhabitant here. Constructed from stacked, unmortared sheets of slate and dark shale, the buildings grow directly out of the mountain’s own strata. They are wedged into the verticality of the peaks, appearing as natural outcrops that have been hollowed out by long-forgotten hands. The stairs are carved into the living rock, smoothed by centuries of light footfalls, leading from one cramped, stone-walled terrace to the next. Everything here is weathered; the sharp edges of the initial construction have long been softened, surrendered to the slow, relentless sanding of mountain winds.

There is a profound stillness here, a "frozen-equilibrium" that dictates the rhythm of the place. No new stone is added to the dwellings; no new path is forged. The town has reached the end of its expansion, a finished work of history. Because of its precarious elevation, moisture arrives as mist—cold, clinging condensation that beads on the dark masonry and drips in rhythmic, glassy intervals from the overhanging eaves into shallow, stagnant basins carved into the ground. These basins are the town’s only water supply, grey mirrors that catch the high, thin daylight and hold it still.

Vegetation is a rare occurrence, limited to the thin, resilient tufts of lichen and silver-grey moss that cling to the leeward sides of the stone, feeding on the dampness stored in the fissures. There is no soil to speak of, only rock, stone, and the grey dust of erosion. Fauna is equally sparse; occasionally, a solitary, flightless bird with plumage the color of weathered slate scuttles across the terrace roofs, its claws clicking against the shale with a sound that emphasizes, rather than breaks, the silence. 

The air is consistently cool, biting at exposed skin with a freshness that suggests altitude and thin atmosphere. There is no heat here, and consequently, there is no combustion. There are no hearths. The tools found in Oru are primitive—simple stone scrapers, woven fibers of tough mountain grasses, and polished bone needles used to mend the heavy cloaks worn by the silent, infrequent figures who emerge from the narrow doorways.

The light is sharp and untempered by pollution or haze. It strikes the stone city with a clarity that reveals every grain of sand and every hairline fracture in the masonry. Because the light does not shift through any seasonal pattern—the town existing in a state of perpetual, mid-day stasis—the shadows remain pinned to the same corners, lengthening and shortening only as the sun makes its precise, predictable transit.

To walk through Oru is to walk through a gallery that refuses to admit the passage of time. There is no decay here, for decay implies a cycle of birth and death, and Oru has moved beyond that. It is a stone pocket held in the sky, a landscape of geometry and silence, perfectly content to be exactly what it has been for a thousand years.
