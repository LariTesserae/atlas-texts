The entire world is a single, vertical column—a gargantuan, rusted pipe or chimney, miles high and perhaps only a few hundred meters across. You are standing on one of its many internal tiers, a scaffolding of synthetic, lichen-crusted steel that hums with a deep, industrial resonance. 

The air here is thick, a heavy, warm mist that sits somewhere between a gas and a liquid—water is not merely ambient; it is the medium. It clings to your skin with the weight of an ocean, yet it remains breathable, oxygenated by the frantic, churning turbines that spin somewhere deep below. Everything is saturated. The "rain" here is a constant, slow-moving drift of massive, water-heavy spores drifting downward in slow motion, like jellyfish through a vertical current.

Everything is built. There is no soil, no original bedrock. The entire environment is an ancient, sprawling construction that has long since ceased to be maintained by whatever creators birthed it. It has entered a stage of "primordial reconstruction." What was once flat, clinical plating is now warped and buckled by eons of growth. Thick, emerald-colored vines, their leaves translucent and glass-like, have fused with the oxidised metal, drawing nutrients directly from the ambient mist and the electrical hum of the decaying conduits. 

The scale is paradoxical; while the pipe itself is an architectural titan, you are embedded in a tight, intimate section. You can see the curvature of the world—the wall of the pipe is always there, curving upward and downward into the infinite. Because the density is sparse, you are alone on this particular level of the scaffolding. There is miles of space between your ledge and the next humming platform of vegetation.

Despite the sparsity, the landscape is loud. It is not the sound of wind, but the sound of heavy machinery breathing. Rhythmic *thumps*—the deep, subsonic pulse of ancient pumps—vibrate through the soles of your feet. The water-drenched air carries the shrill, mechanical chirping of metallic insects. These aren't silicon constructs, but biological-industrial hybrids: creatures with chitinous carapaces that click like assembly-line gears, skittering across the slick, wet metal to feed on the damp, sprawling vegetation. 

The light is the light of a perpetual, humid afternoon. It filters in through the high, distant vents at the top of the column, diffused by the thick water-vapor until it is a soft, bruised amber. 

This place has reached its "final form." Erosion has smoothed the harsh edges of the machinery, and the biology has softened the geometric rigor of the steel. It is a world that has stopped expanding and started cycling. Everything is active, yet perfectly locked in a cycle of decay and rebirth. The turbines push the water up, the vegetation cleans the water, the insects grind the vegetation into chemical sludge that drips back down to feed the turbines. 

To look out from your ledge is to see a vertical tapestry: the glint of oxidized copper, the deep, dark green of water-bloated moss, and the relentless, cascading spray of the atmosphere itself. You are not on a planet; you are living inside the lungs of a giant, ancient engine that has forgotten its purpose and decided, instead, to become a garden.
