The low-lying basin of Sector 4 is a place of heavy metal and constant saturation. It is a city of rust and wet concrete, a sprawling industrial necropolis built along the banks of a slow-moving, oily mire that covers the landscape in a thin, shimmering film.

The temperature here never drops; it holds at a sweltering, damp heat that forces the air to shimmer and cling to the skin. Because the architecture is almost entirely mineral—composed of iron, oxidized copper, and slick basalt—there is no plant life to absorb the moisture. Instead, water behaves like a guest that refuses to leave. It crawls over the sides of the massive, inactive blast furnaces like sweat on a fevered brow. It drips endlessly from the undersides of rusted gantry cranes, falling in rhythmic, heavy beads that strike the ground with a dull, echoing *tink* against the metal floors.

The scale is vast, organized, and deeply hollow. The city follows the geometry of a grid, but it is a grid meant for machines, not people. Massive, empty warehouses the size of airships sit with their doors agape, their interiors filled only with the scent of damp rust and the faint, ozone-heavy hum of subterranean generators that still pulse with a dim, weary light. 

It is "empty" in terms of human presence, but it is far from quiet. The fauna of this place has reclaimed the infrastructure with a terrifying, rhythmic density. Millions of copper-shelled beetles, each the size of a human palm, cover the walls of the factories like living shingles. They move in waves, a constant, low-frequency rustle as they scuttle over the oxidized metal surfaces, their grinding mandibles chewing at the rust of the world. They are the primary inhabitants of this industrial marsh, a sea of chitinous armor that creates a constant, living vibration throughout the sector. They do not seem to notice the occasional, larger reptilian scavengers that slither through the flooded alleyways, their slick, dark bodies weaving between the motionless gears of the great engines.

The light is perpetually dim, a bruised violet-gray provided by the thick, condensing mist that hangs at eye level. This mist is thick with heavy water, turning the horizon into a smear of indistinct shapes—towering chimneys that lean at precarious angles, eroded by centuries of being licked by the humid wind, and the skeletal remains of elevated rail lines that have long since lost their trains.

There is a sense of established equilibrium here. The erosion has worked the sharp edges off the machinery; the vents are clogged with silt and beetle-castings, the streets are permanently submerged in a few inches of tepid, metallic water, and the buildings have settled into the soft, silty earth to the point where they appear to be growing out of the ground like tectonic protrusions. 

It is a world of stagnant utility. The gears are frozen, the furnaces are cold, but the system is alive—not through human command, but through the patient, sweltering takeover of the creatures that love the heat, the rust, and the damp. It is a factory-city that has forgotten its purpose, surrendered to the slow, wet oxidation of time, breathing in the dark and exhaling the sound of a million tiny, clicking legs.
