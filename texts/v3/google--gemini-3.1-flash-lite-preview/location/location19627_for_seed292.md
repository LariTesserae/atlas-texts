The world is a vertical collapse of geography—a hyper-saturated, drowning skyscraper of stone that has been eroding since the beginning of time. 

Here, "elevation" is the primary axis of existence. There is no ground, only a series of gargantuan, moss-slicked basalt spires that thrust upward through a perpetual, heavy-gauge deluge. This is an oceanic verticality, a world of tiered terraces and jagged cliffs where the water does not merely fall; it flows in thick, pressurized curtains from one subterranean shelf to the next.

The atmosphere is cool—a damp, stinging chill that carries the metallic scent of pulverized rock and wet, rotting fern. Because the erosion is so ancient and complete, the landscape feels "settled." Millions of years of water have carved the rock into smooth, rounded geometries, giving it the appearance of softened, living muscle rather than simple geology.

Clinging to every available vertical inch is a density of vegetation that shames the rainforests of old. Ferns the size of sailing ships uncurl from the cliff faces, their fronds translucent and heavy with moisture. They are fueled by the constant, thundering roar of the falls that bridge the gaps between the heights. The sound is a physical weight—a constant, vibrating bass note created by billions of tons of water hitting the lower, invisible depths. It is loud enough to rattle the teeth, a chaotic, thrashing engine of sound that never settles.

Amidst this, there is the "built" layer—scattered, rusted, and stubborn. There are rusted iron walkways, slick with cyanobacteria, that pinwheel around the cliff faces like metal veins. These are the remnants of archaic, deep-sea industrial mining—engines the size of cathedrals, now half-submerged or encased in silica-heavy growth. They are not dead, however. Steam hisses from rusted valves, and the occasional hum of a failing turbine flickers in the gloom, powering rhythmic, pulsing lights that stutter through the rain. These machines seem to have integrated with the flora; tangled roots hold together cracking pistons, and bioluminescent fungi pulse in sync with the vibrations of the water-wheels.

Fauna is abundant, but distinctively adapted for a life of perpetual climbing and swimming. Large, pale-eyed amphibians with suction-cupped limbs navigate the cliffside, their skins rippling to match the mossy textures of the rock. Avian creatures with heavy, sodden plumage struggle through the dense, wet air, their calls swallowed instantly by the unrelenting roar of the rain.

The landscape is in a state of violent, beautiful maintenance. Every moment, a section of moss-slicked rock gives way under the weight of the water, tumbling down into the abyss, only to be immediately colonized by the next layer of opportunistic organisms. It is a carousel of decay and rebirth. 

There is no horizon here, only the "up" of the jagged, storm-swept peaks—blurry in the mist—and the impenetrable, churning "down" where the rivers merge into an oceanic grave. It is a world of constant motion, a turbulent machine of water and growth, operating at a scale that makes the occasional appearance of an iron pylon or a discarded ventilation shaft feel like a desperate, almost pathetic plea for permanence in a landscape that has long ago decided to dissolve itself back into the sea.
