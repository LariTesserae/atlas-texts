The world is a vast, low-slung basin of smoothed, slate-grey silt and polished obsidian debris, worn down by eons of torrential, mineral-heavy rain into a landscape of near-perfect flatness. There is no green here; the horizon is a bruised, charcoal smudge where the low, weeping clouds meet the ground. The atmosphere is saturated, a thick, cold vapor that clings to your skin like a wet shroud, condensing into rivulets that carve microscopic canyons into the silt.

The silence you might expect from a lifeless, barren waste is not present. Instead, the landscape is defined by a roar—a constant, thrumming hydraulic cacophony. Everywhere, water is being forced upward from shallow, pressurized aquifers. It erupts in stuttering, rhythmic geysers of cold spray. Because the ground is so ancient and eroded, these eruptions act as natural flutes and reed-pipes against the pockmarked stone, creating a deafening, multi-tonal whistle that rises and falls like the breathing of a giant.

The ecology—if one can call it that—is entirely liquid and mineral. There are no plants. Instead, the dominant lifeforms are translucent, gelatinous colonies that look like spilled oil slicks or globs of living glass. They slide slowly across the wet slate, feeding on the kinetic energy of the rain and the rhythmic vibration of the geysers. They are abundant, scattered across the expansive basin like shimmering, drifting constellations. They hold no intelligence, only a frantic, reflexive need to stay within the splash-zones of the rising water.

Amidst this, there are the ruins—or perhaps the unfinished beginnings—of a primitive civilization. Scattered at wide intervals are "The Hearths": low, dome-like structures made of stacked, unrefined volcanic glass and heavy, water-logged basalt. They are not dwellings so much as anchors. Faint, hand-etched runes mark the threshold of these huts, worn almost smooth by the relentless grit of the rain. Inside each, one finds only a pile of salt-crusted stones and simple, hollow-bone tools used for carving channels into the ground to direct the flow of the geysers.

There is a strange, cold geometry to the way the water moves. The inhabitants—creatures who appear to be semi-solid constructs of clay and silt, moving with a sluggish, manual determination—spend their cycles redirecting the streams with their crude tools. They are not building a city; they are curating a soundscape. They widen a channel here to sharpen a note, or pile stones there to dampen a hiss. 

The environment is a closed, self-sustaining instrument, older than the stars that might be hidden above the cloud cover. It is a world of low-light, high-frequency kinetic exhaustion. To walk here is to feel the constant vibration of the earth in your teeth, a persistent, chilling hum that makes it impossible to think of anything but the next step. You step over the glass-like slicks of the jelly-colonies, watching them pulse with the rhythm of the rain, moving through a landscape that has forgotten the sun and is content to simply exist as a monument to the cycle of falling water and the slow, grinding death of stone.
