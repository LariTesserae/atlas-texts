The world does not grow; it is assembled. Below your feet lies a colossal, seamless lattice of vitrified ceramic and interlocking brass plates, a polished, ivory-tinted floor that spans the horizon in every direction. There is no soil here, no bedrock, no history of sediment. The entire landscape is a sprawling, manufactured basin, a vast shallow pan that hums with the static of a finished task.

Because the configuration is static—an eternal equilibrium of parts—the architecture does not age. The sharp angles of the great, vaulted cisterns and the jagged, geometric protrusions of the "cliffs" remain as pristine as the moment they were locked into place. You are standing in a newly-emerged, artificial marsh.

The air is a heavy, shimmering wall of heat, thick with the scent of ozone and warm, stagnant nectar. It is not humid in the organic sense; rather, it is saturated with suspended, liquid beads of synthetic dew. Everywhere, weeping conduits bleed clear, viscous fluid—not water, but a nutrient-rich coolant—onto the floor, which collects in perfect, crystal-clear basins that never overflow and never evaporate. 

Out of the ceramic plates, vegetation erupts in defiance of the lack of dirt. These are not plants of photosynthesis, but of frantic consumption. Massive, translucent fern-fronds made of rubbery, living membranes sprawl across the open floor, their veins pulsating with a slow, neon luminescence. They do not compete for space; they hold their distance, separated by wide, empty moats of bright, reflective flooring. The landscape is sparse, a gallery of botanical marvels spaced with intentional, haunting gaps.

The light is blinding, sourced from an unseen point directly overhead. There is no shift in shadows—the day does not transition, the sun does not arc. You are locked in a perpetual, high-noon glare that turns the fluid on the ground into a million tiny, blinding mirrors.

Despite the abundance of life, the world is chillingly quiet. You hear only the soft, rhythmic *drip-clink* of the coolant hitting the vitrified plates and the occasional, low-frequency hum of the subterranean machinery that keeps reality in its frozen state. 

The fauna drift through this silence with hypnotic grace. Long-limbed, multi-jointed arthropods with carapaces of iridescent pearl walk across the open, bright spaces. They seem to interact with the landscape using simple, delicate tools—small, hand-carved stone chisels and scrapers—tinkering eternally with the veins of the fern-fronds or the joints of the brass flooring. They do not speak. They are artisans of a finished world, perpetually performing maintenance on a reality that refuses to degrade.

The weirdness is found in the edges of your vision. If you look too intently at a puddle or a fern-frond, you catch a brief flicker of a different landscape—the ghost of a jagged, primordial forest or a dark, ancient cathedral—shimmering behind the ceramic walls. Reality here is thin, a decorative veneer stretched over a void. The world is complete, beautiful, and absolutely, terrifyingly still. Nothing is evolving. Nothing is decaying. Everything is made, and everything is waiting for a command that will never come.
