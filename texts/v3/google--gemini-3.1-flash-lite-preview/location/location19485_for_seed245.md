The horizon does not exist here. Instead, there is only the *Infilling*—a vast, low-lying expanse of interconnected obsidian lattices and weeping ceramic scaffolds that seem to be knitting themselves together in real-time, shivering as they solidify out of a thick, humid haze.

This is a world of heavy, humid heat, yet there is no sun. The light is diffused, a bruised, pulpy violet that clings to the surfaces of the structures like sweat on skin. The ground—if it can be called that—is a sprawling grid of cooling glass filaments and damp, porous stone-work, woven together by unseen, manual-craft hands. There is no biology. No lichen, no moss, no breath; only the smell of wet ozone and the distinct, metallic tang of minerals being forced into shape by a heat that never lets up.

Everything here is *built*. One slab of weeping basalt leads into a complex of hand-carved, glistening conduits that run with a thin, tepid sludge. This liquid, a byproduct of the construction, beads on the walls and drips continuously, pooling in wide, shallow depressions that mirror the dim light. The sound is almost nonexistent, save for the rhythmic *drip-hiss* of moisture hitting overheated stone, and the occasional, unsettling *crack* of a new section of the world vibrating into existence.

It is a place of raw emergence. New spires, fashioned from crude, blackened iron and unfired clay, rise up from the floor, only to warp and buckle under their own weight. There are no architects visible, yet the labor is constant. You can see the thumbprints in the mortar, the messy, primitive joinery of a world that is being assembled by entities that do not care for aesthetics or structural integrity. 

Because the reality here is frayed, the "scale" is treacherous. A corridor might appear to be a hundred miles long, stretching out into the dim, hazy void, but as you walk toward it, the space compresses, and you find yourself standing before a wall of wet, weeping glass only a few meters away. The logic of distance is as unstable as the cooling stone.

There is a sense of desperate, anthill-energy here, but it is a silent, feverish toil. The atmosphere is condensing, turning the world into a slick, slippery labyrinth where the floor is always damp and the air is close and stifling. You are walking through the skeleton of a thought that hasn't finished being shaped. Sometimes, a massive chunk of the landscape will simply dissolve into a fine, grey mist, re-forming seconds later as a tangle of geometric scaffolding, a turbulent cycle of creation that feels less like engineering and more like a nervous breakdown in stone.

It is lonely, not because of the space, but because of the refusal of the world to hold a stable shape for more than a few cycles. Everything is wet, everything is hot, and everything is waiting to be finished, though it seems destined to remain forever in this embryonic state of construction—a sprawling, lonely, steaming monument to a builder who abandoned the floor-plans long ago.
