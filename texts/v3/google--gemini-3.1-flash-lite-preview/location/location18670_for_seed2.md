The world is not a planet; it is an immense, cylindrical cathedral of fluid mechanics, a sub-aquatic lattice stretching leagues in every direction. There is no solid earth here, only the "Substrate," a synthetic, porous alloy that has been extruded and welded into a sprawling, gargantuan manifold of conduits, pylons, and cathedral-like intake vents.

Everything is wet, yet the water is not stagnant. It is a controlled, pressurized ocean, a constant, rushing current that flows through the structural veins of this gargantuan construction. Because of the vastness of the architecture, the water creates its own weather—tides of internal pressure that surge through the corridors, creating whirlpools and eddies that roar like freight trains. 

The light is artificial and pervasive, emitted from long, glowing phosphorescent filaments woven into the metal walls. It is a sterile, white-blue glow that catches the suspended bubbles and the translucent, bio-luminescent schooling fish that pulse in synchronized rhythms through the halls. The temperature remains a crisp, clinical cool, kept steady by the heat-exchange cycles of the massive machines that hum at the perimeter of the horizon.

Life here is utilitarian. Because the environment is fully constructed, biology has adapted to take advantage of the industrial mechanics. Jellyfish-like organisms with armored, gear-like carapaces drift through the intake shafts, feeding on the microscopic nutrients stripped from the recycling water. They are the primary fauna, darting with sudden, mechanical precision to avoid the intake turbines that churn rhythmically, their blades thousands of feet wide, spinning with a low-frequency hum that vibrates through the very bones of the structure.

There is a sense of "young" wear. The metal surfaces are sharp-edged, still bristling with the slag of recent expansion, and the water—treated for purity—possesses a pristine, biting clarity. You can look through miles of depth and see the far-off flicker of other service modules, glowing like distant stations in a nebula of salt and power.

The silence is non-existent. The soundscape is a chaotic, layered soundtrack of heavy industry: the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the primary pumps, the screech of metal expansion against metal fasteners, the rushing of water through narrow high-pressure apertures, and the occasional, distant "thud" of an automated welding unit correcting a fracture in the hull. 

The physics here are dictated by the flow. To move through this world is to be a part of the circulation. You drift not by choice, but by the gravitational pull of the turbines and the complex, interlocking tides of the reservoir. There is no horizon other than the curvature of the next great bulkhead, and no sky other than the endless, pressurized ceiling of the superstructure. It is a place of perpetual labor—a lonely, submerged, and brilliantly engineered machine that is still teaching itself how to run.
