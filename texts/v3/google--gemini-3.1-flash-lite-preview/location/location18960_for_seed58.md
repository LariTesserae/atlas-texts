The air here does not circulate; it hangs, heavy and thick with the smell of scorched copper and wet silt. You are deep, nestled in a localized sinkhole of the crust, a compressed urban pocket buried far beneath the surface, where the heat is a constant, suffocating pressure—a dry, baking fever that never breaks.

The environment is a rigid, vertical maze. The architecture is not built so much as crusted, rising in sharp, aggressive geometric protrusions of basalt and industrial slag. Because the erosion is in its infancy, the edges of these structures are brutally keen; they slice through the dim, amber-hued light like serrated teeth. The geography here is entirely "built"—layered, interlocking galleries of iron piping and cooling fins, woven together like a congested, subterranean organism. 

There is no sky, only the underside of the rock above, weeping a slow, rhythmic condensation. Water does not flow in rivers; it beads on the black glass of the walls, pooling in the crevices of the machinery. Every few minutes, a rhythmic *hiss* echoes through the chamber—a pressure-valve release from the unseen infrastructure buried deep in the walls. This is the only "weather" known to this place: a localized cycle of steam, evaporation, and condensation that keeps the surfaces perpetually slick with a thin, oily sheen.

The density is claustrophobic. The "buildings"—if the honeycomb of metal cells can be called that—are pressed against one another so tightly that one must scrape through gaps or ascend the jagged exterior ironwork to make any progress. Everything is layered. Beneath the floorboards of one level, you can hear the deep, hypnotic thrum of heavy, automated pistons driving some process that has clearly been running for centuries, though there is no soul to witness it.

Despite this relentless activity—the creaking of cooling metal, the rhythmic condensation drips, the deep-earth rumble of machinery—the silence is absolute. There is no animal life. Not a moth, not a bacteria, not a stirring of anything biological. The ecology here is purely mechanical, a thermodynamic loop that requires no input other than the heat of the core itself.

The light is a sickly, twilight orange, seemingly generated by the glowing heat-exhaust of the conduits themselves. It is a dim, flickering source, trapped in the gaps between the dense, industrial stacks. Every shadow is sharp, every dark corner is deeper than it looks, and the scale is small—a compact, high-stakes cathedral of gears. You feel, profoundly, that this is a machine that manufactures only its own continuation. It is a city that never had, and never will have, a population; it is an apparatus that has simply reached a state of perpetual, searing, and lonely equilibrium.
