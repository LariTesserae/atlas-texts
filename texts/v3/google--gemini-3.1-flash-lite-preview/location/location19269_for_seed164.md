The world is a jagged, sky-piercing archipelago of obsidian spires, suspended in an atmosphere so saturated with moisture that the air itself moves like a translucent, rhythmic liquid. It is cold—a biting, sharp temperature that does not lower the humidity but instead turns it into perpetual, clinging frost on every surface.

Because of the high elevation, the vista is continental in its scale. You are standing on a precipice of basalt, looking out over a void of thousands of miles where cloud-seas churn between needle-thin mountain peaks. There is no animal life here; no wings break the air, no breathing disturbs the silence—yet the place is deafening.

The sound is industrial, a constant, low-frequency thrum that vibrates through the soles of your feet. It is the sound of a vast, continent-spanning engine that has been running since the world’s birth. Everywhere, the landscape is scarred by "young" geometry: sharp, crystalline outcroppings, fractures that have not yet been smoothed by the rain, and structures that appear to have erupted from the rock rather than been built upon it.

The "urban" element here is not organic or pedestrian. There are no streets, only colossal, electrified conduits—vast ribbons of gleaming, conductive silver alloy—strung between the peaks like spiderwebs. These ribbons are constantly vibrating, emitting a high-pitched, harmonic whine that interweaves with the deep thrum of the ground. Great, box-like automated factories the size of cities cling to the leeward sides of the cliffs, their soot-blackened stacks perpetually exhaling plumes of condensing vapor. They do not work; they never change. They are locked in a state of eternal, frozen equilibrium. The conveyor belts are loaded with components that will never be assembled, and the pistons are raised halfway in an eternal, motionless breath.

There is a profound loneliness to the density here. You could walk for a thousand miles across the sky-bridges and never see a sign of intention, only the evidence of process. The light is a flat, filtered gray that illuminates nothing but the moisture. Every rock is perpetually weeping; water beads on the cold metal of the conduits, aggregates into heavy globules, and drips into the blue-black abyss below, only to be vaporized by the heat of the factories before it hits the ground, creating a permanent cycle of rising fog.

The physics here are unsettling. Distance operates on a different scale—the horizon seems to bend inward, making the vastness feel like the interior of a hollow, crystalline skull. Time is not experienced as a progression; because the machinery is static and the environment never decays or matures, the world exists in a "perpetual now." It is a factory that has forgotten why it was built, a city of gears and silver wire holding up the clouds, waiting for an operator who has been absent for an eternity. The only thing that exists is the repetition of the vibration, the cold bite of the dripping air, and the sheer, terrifying geometry of a world that is too sharp, too loud, and too large to ever truly be known.
