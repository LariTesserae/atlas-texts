The air here is warm, thick, and tastes vividly of wet minerals and metabolic heat. You are standing in a space no larger than a cathedral, a vertical throat of stone that climbs toward a ceiling you cannot see. The walls are not made of raw, jagged mountain, but of "established" rock—limestone and shale worn into smooth, undulating concavities by aeons of rhythmic condensation. Everything is slick. There is no standing water, yet nothing is dry; a constant, fine mist clings to the porous surfaces, condensing into droplets that swell and slide in slow, patient rivulets down the vertical faces.

It is oppressively dense. The geometry of the space is crowded not by plants, but by breath. The life here is small, numerous, and tactile.

Because the light is near-nonexistent—a deep, bruised indigo that filters down from some far-off aperture—the visual sense has been decommissioned in favor of the haptic and the auditory. The sound is a constant, droning *thrum*. It is the sound of thousands of tiny, rasping appendages moving over stone; it is the rhythmic friction of carapaces. It is loud—a low-frequency oscillation that vibrates in the marrow of your teeth.

The ecology is overwhelming. The vertical walls are carpeted with layers of near-microscopic organisms. They form a biological tapestry, a living wallpaper of pale, translucently armored things that feed on the mineral condensation and the warmth of one another. They do not scurry; they creep with a geological deliberation. Occasionally, the crowd shifts—a slow ripple that travels through the density of bodies, a wave of motion that takes minutes to pass from one side of the chamber to the other.

Every surface is a skin. If you were to reach out—though there is barely room to extend an arm—you would feel the squelch of soft, damp clusters and the tick of chitin against stone. There is absolutely no evidence of "built" life. No tunnels, no nests, no architecture. The organisms exist in a state of primitive equilibrium with the terrain, their bodies perfectly shaped to the natural flutings and erosion-hollows of the stone.

The warmth is constant, radiating from the deep earth, and it smells of damp peat and copper. There is no wind, only the slight, pressurized shifts of air caused by the movement of the mass. Time feels viscous here. You are in a pressurized, vertical box of life, where the scale is small, the density is absolute, and the only rule is the slow, grinding persistence of survival—a friction-heavy, communal pressing of flesh against cold, ancient earth in the quiet, humid dark.
