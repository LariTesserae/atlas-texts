The world is not a planet of rock, but a vast, suspended lattice of hydrous cellulose—a continent-sized nervous system of xylem and phloem, floating in a dense, cool, atmospheric ocean. Gravity here is a suggestion rather than a law, allowing the giant, fern-like structures to reach thousands of miles into the sky, their roots trailing like nerves into the humid, liquid haze below.

There is no solid ground. One stands, if one can be said to stand, on the porous, spongy skin of a leaf the size of a mountain range. The terrain is a permanent, fixed geometry; the branches of these titanic organisms do not sway, and the rainfall does not descend in drops but hangs as thick, shimmering curtains of gelid liquid that refract the pale, cool daylight into static, eternal rainbows.

The "industrial" nature of this place is not a human imposition, but a biological one. The ecosystem functions like a vast, cold-blooded machine. Deep within the heart-tissues of the gargantuan flora, rhythmic pulses of pressure drive fluids upward with the roar of a thousand turbines. This is the sound of the world: a continuous, low-frequency thrum, the sound of atmospheric fluids being filtered, pressurized, and ejected through the porous, metallic-tasting pores of the bark. It is an industrial efficiency achieved by evolution, a plant-based factory that has been running for aeons.

Nothing here ever changes. The weather is a static installation—the rain falls at a constant velocity, the light hits the translucent canopy at a fixed angle of refraction, and the temperature hovers at an equilibrium point that never deviates by a fraction of a degree. It is a frozen, high-functioning mechanism.

Life is sparse but distinct. Where the density of the vegetation opens up, leaving vast, empty apertures of misty blue space, strange creatures drift. They are translucent, balloon-like entities, trailing long, conductive filaments that harvest the static electricity generated by the "factories" within the wood. They move with the predictability of clockwork, navigating the same currents they have navigated since the beginning of time. They are silent, save for the faint, high-pitched hum of their filaments interacting with the liquid atmosphere.

There is no decay here, for there is no death that disrupts the cycle. Everything is a piece of infrastructure. The leaves are living solar arrays; the sap is a high-grade coolant; the stems are conduits for the vast energy of the world’s internal tides. It is a world of pure, unadulterated process, a massive, cool, and wet engine of biology that has reached a state of perfect, unchanging grace. One looks out across the vast, misty expanse—the sheer, sheer scale of it—and feels only the terrifying, silent, loud vibration of a machine that requires no operator, and no end.
