Deep beneath the crust, in a hollow carved by ancient, retreating geothermal activity, lies the Siphon-Groves. It is a cathedral-sized pocket, roughly the dimensions of a buried village, where the air is thick with a heavy, pressurized warmth that sits on the skin like a wet wool blanket.

The terrain here is defined by "The Weeping Sediment." The walls of this subterranean dome are not rock, but vast, layered banks of weathered, compacted silt and calcified peat, sculpted by cycles of condensation that fall not as rain, but as rhythmic, slow-motion oozing. Moisture beads on every surface. It collects in the hollows of the soft, spongiform ground, forming stagnant, dark pools that smell of sweet rot and deep minerals. Though the space is largely dry, there is a constant, suffocating humidity—a dampness that has been here for millennia, undisturbed by the world above.

Because the space is small, the density of life is staggering. It is a vertical labyrinth of bioluminescent vines and thick, rubbery ferns that reach greedily toward the dim, amber light casting down from weeping fissures in the ceiling. The flora here is parasitic and interconnected; trees do not grow from soil but bloom out of the carcasses of older, fallen, petrified trunks. Everything is layered, stacked, and crowded. There is no empty space; every square inch of the dome is occupied by something soft, moist, and growing.

Movement in the Siphon-Groves is subtle. Because the environment is so dense, life has adapted to remain still to avoid conflict, only shifting when necessary. This results in a profound, heavy silence. You do not hear birds or wind; you hear the granular sounds of biology: the wet *thwip* of a fern unfurling, the slow, tectonic creep of thick moss climbing a wall, the rhythmic drip of condensation hitting the surface of a stagnant pool. It is an active, restless ecosystem, yet it unfolds with the agonizing patience of a long-settled landscape.

The ecology is governed by the "Heat-Siphon." The fauna—multi-legged, pale crustaceans and gelatinous, slug-like creatures—move in slow, predictable patterns dictated by the fluctuating temperature of the chamber. They are high-saturation, vibrant things—translucent purples, deep ochres, and bruised oranges—that crawl over one another in a delicate, non-violent dance for the most humid patches. 

Amidst this, there are the "Mementos." Scattered throughout the grove are remnants of a forgotten time: small, smooth, oval stones arranged in precise arcs, or thin, porous columns of bone-white material that appear hand-carved but are actually just the naturally weathered remainders of extinct, structural fungi. These formations feel like architecture, but they are purely biological, a series of ancient, calcified intentions left by a species that never developed a tool. They serve only as perches for the insects and anchor-points for the creeping vines.

Light is a premium here, filtering down in weak, dusty shafts that highlight the floating spores, which hang in the air like suspended grit. When a creature moves, it disturbs these spores, setting off a slow-motion shimmer that moves through the density of the room like a ghost. There is no urgency in the Siphon-Groves. The heat is constant, the moisture is persistent, and the life is eternal in its slow, circular decay. It is a self-contained heart, beating deep under the surface, perfectly balanced in its own, claustrophobic grace.
