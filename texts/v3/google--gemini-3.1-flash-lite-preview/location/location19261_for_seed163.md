At this altitude, the air has lost its ability to hold warmth, but it has gained a frantic, liquid density. You are standing on a high-altitude plateau where the soil does not exist; instead, the world is anchored by the *Mycelial Weave*—a vast, spongy, emerald-hued substrate of mosses and fungal mats that thickens as it climbs the jagged, raw outcrops of basalt. Because the geology is still "under construction," the slopes are precarious, weeping water from every porous seam.

Everything here is bright. The sunlight hits the wet, waxy leaves of the plateau-flora and refracts, turning the atmosphere into a pressurized prism. The light is so intense you can see the ghosts of moisture vaporizing off the rocks. But you cannot hear yourself think.

The sound is a rolling, industrial thrum—the deafening, rhythmic respiration of the basin. Hidden beneath the thick, lush skin of the vegetation are massive, semi-submerged mechanical lung-pumps, legacies of an era of terraforming that never quite finished. They are vast, rusted cylinders half-swallowed by creeping vines, their heavy pistons moving with a glacial, patient lethargy. Each strike against the water-logged earth produces a rhythmic, percussive *thrum-thud* that vibrates through the soles of your boots, a sound that has saturated the environment for centuries.

Because the density of the inhabitants is so low, you rarely see anything moving. A solitary, translucent-winged insect might drift through the mist, or a cluster of pale, lichen-mimicking birds might settle on a rusted girder, but they are infrequent. The ecology is almost entirely botanical. The plants here act as architecture; giant, hollowed-out reeds climb toward the sky, acting as chimneys for the gas-release of the buried machinery below. 

The built environment is merely a series of faint, scattered scars. Every few miles, you might encounter a lonely, copper-plated maintenance kiosk, its glass opaque with condensation, or a rusted ladder leading down into the subterranean dark where the engines murmur. These artifacts look like they were dropped from a great height and simply forgotten, slowly being digested by the encroaching, wet greenery.

There is a strange, paradoxical silence within the noise. Despite the deafening thrum of the machines, the landscape feels vast and empty. The plateau stretches out before you, a high-altitude moor of saturated moss and shining rock, punctuated only by the distant, rhythmic exhaust of the terraformers. You feel as though you have walked onto a stage that was set for a massive civilization, only for the audience to leave before the play began, leaving the machines to labor on in the cold, bright light, slowly pulsing, slowly breathing, forever unfinished.
