This is a land that has grown tired of standing tall. Over eons, the earth here has been polished down by the patient, insistent friction of water and warmth, finding its final, lowest resting state. It is a vast, nearly featureless basin of ancient sediment, a landscape that has long since surrendered to the horizontal.

The atmosphere is perpetually heavy, a saturated, warm blanket that clings to the skin. Because the ground is so low and the water table sits millimeters beneath the silt, there is no true separation between earth and liquid. The air itself feels distilled, a humid breath that condenses into iridescent beads on every surface—on the slick, dark moss that carpets the flattest patches, and on the porous, weeping stone outcroppings that hunch like sleeping giants, half-submerged in the muck.

Movement here belongs to the patient. Pools of tea-colored water mirror the sky, vast and shallow, broken only by the occasional spire of a mineral-crusted reed. The vegetation is sparse—mostly tufts of coarse, silver-leafed grass that shudder in the warmth and succulent, bladder-like shrubs that store the constant moisture within bruised-purple skins. There is no urgency in their growth; they occupy space with a heavy, sedentary grace.

Though the eye finds nothing to climb, the ears are overwhelmed. The soundscape is loud, almost deafeningly communal. It is the rhythmic, layered thrum of life: the wet, percussive *thwack* of bioluminescent frogs jumping into the shallows; the low-frequency drone of six-winged insects vibrating in the soup of the air; and the constant, soft tectonic *fizz* of minerals leaching from the weeping rocks into the pools. It is a landscape of constant, low-level activity, a primordial kitchen where life is being brewed in vast, open vats.

There is no sign of the hand of man, nor of any tool. The world is purely biological. The fauna is abundant but scattered, visible as ripples in the distance or the sudden, iridescent flash of a wing against the muted horizon. Creatures here are elongated, low-slung, and translucent, evolved to navigate the thin interface between the mud and the mirror-surface of the pools. They do not hunt with speed; they wait, motionless for hours in the thick, humid air, until the water brings the sustenance to them.

As the light—a soft, diffuse honey-gold—filters through the layer of suspended vapor, the terrain seems to evaporate into the horizon. There are no edges, only gradations from grey silt to tea-colored reflection to the warm, white-blue haze of the sky. It is a place that feels as if it has been finished for millions of years, an ancient, stable, breathing stillness. To walk here is to disturb a peace that has settled so deeply into the crust of the planet that it feels impolite to leave a footprint. The mud simply closes behind you, the water refills the void, and the silence returns, loud and heavy and warm.
