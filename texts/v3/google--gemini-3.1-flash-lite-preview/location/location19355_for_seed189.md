Deep beneath the crust, in a hollow node where the lithosphere has pulled away from itself like a healing scab, lies the *Throat of the Verdant Lung*.

It is not a cave in the mineral sense. It is a chamber defined entirely by the biology of an organism that does not recognize the concept of stone. Every surface—floor, wall, and ceiling—is composed of a dense, spongy, translucent moss-flesh, veined with pulsing, rhythmic sap-conductors that glow with a faint, bioluminescent amber. 

The environment is aggressively alive. Because the density of the space is so low—a vast, hollow, cathedral-like void where individual plant-structures are separated by yards of humid, stagnant air—the sounds echo with terrifying, wet clarity. The place is *loud*. Every breath of the living walls, every dripping condensation of nutrient-rich fog that gathers on the ceiling and falls in heavy, rhythmic droplets, resonates like a heartbeat inside a drum. The "rain" here is cycle-driven; the walls tighten and squeeze, distilling moisture into the air, creating a humid, tropical warmth that clings to the skin.

There is no erosion because there is no rock to wear away. Instead, there is *revision*. The architecture is young, constantly forming and reforming. A archway composed of fibrous, fern-like curls might reach out toward the center of the void, stiffen into a bridge, and then collapse back into a slurry of nutrients within a few hours. 

The weirdness is not in the plants, but in the reality that bleeds through the seams. Because the space is so deep below the surface and so thin in its structural connection to the world above, gravity here is a suggestion rather than a law. Occasionally, you might see a globule of sap—or a stray, floating seed-pod the size of a human torso—hovering motionlessly in the center of the void, vibrating in sympathy with the deep-set thrumming of the walls. These pods are the only fauna; they are slow-moving, semi-sentient, and appear to be waiting for the room to decide what they should become.

There is a primitive tech here, though it is not "made" by hands. A wandering observer might find a stone-tool, left by some forgotten entity, embedded halfway into the wall. The biology of the chamber is attempting to digest the tool, drawing iron and mineral out of it as if it were a nutrient. The tool is being slowly "incorporated" into the ecosystem, its edge becoming a serrated leaf, its handle turning into a piece of woody bark. 

You feel exposed, despite being buried under miles of earth. The void is so empty of habitation—no tracks, no shadows, no predators—that your own heartbeat feels like a violation of the silence. Yet, the chamber is fiercely active. The walls are constantly shifting, shivering as they recalibrate their nutrient distribution. It is an intimate, claustrophobic expanse—an internal organ of the world that you have accidentally walked into, and it is beginning to notice that you are not part of its metabolism. You feel the temperature rise slightly, a fever-spike, as the room attempts to classify you, to see if you are a nutrient or a distraction.
