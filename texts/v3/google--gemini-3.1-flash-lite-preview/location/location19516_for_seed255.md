The world is a vast, rhythmic interior, a colossal subterranean clockwork cavern composed entirely of weeping basalt and riveted copper. Here, the concept of "ground" is a misnomer; you stand upon a dense, interlocking lattice of fabricated plates that groan and shift with a low-frequency thunder, as if the landscape itself is suffering from tectonic indigestion.

Everything is built. There is no soil, no sediment, no natural biology to be found. The walls of this world—a grey, geometric expanse that stretches for miles into the gloom—are adorned with gargantuan, manually-cranked bellows and vast, sweating drainage pipes. They weep constantly, a slow, saline condensation that slickens the metal surfaces, creating a pervasive, rhythmic *drip-clatter-hiss* that fills the lungs like cold air.

Because the light is so sparse—nothing more than the dim, occasional bioluminescence of leaking coolant fluids or the spark-flicker of a friction-wheel ignition—the architecture must be navigated by touch and sound. The density of the environment is oppressive. You are never more than a few decimeters from a vertical surface; the space is a honeycomb of narrow catwalks, stacked gear-teeth, and claustrophobic service tunnels that suddenly open into sweeping, dark plazas of industrial machinery.

There is a sense of "embryonic" erosion—the world is not wearing away, but rather, it is being perpetually unfinished. Everywhere, you find the remnants of manual construction: heavy, iron-peened rivets that haven't been filed down; sharp, obsidian-like casting burrs left on the edges of massive structural supports; and vast, unrefined blocks of volcanic glass that serve as the floor's foundational ballast. It feels as though the builders were interrupted mid-task, or perhaps they are merely working at a pace so geologic that human intervention feels like a frantic, primitive tremor.

The soundscape is overwhelming. Every mechanical process—the expansion of steam valves, the rhythmic crushing of slag, the groaning protest of metal struts adjusting to the shifting floor—is amplified by the enclosed geometry. It is a loud, percussive environment that vibrates through the soles of your feet.

You might catch a glimpse of the fauna, though they are rare and deeply strange. Small, chitinous clusters—not biological, but perhaps sentient collections of clockwork and magnetic filings—scuttle along the underside of the conduits. They move with the jagged, jerky motion of broken timepieces, harvesting the condensation and the stray metallic dust that settles like snow. They hum in sympathy with the pipes, a dissonant, ringing tone that punctuates the deeper industrial roar.

There is a cold, damp vitality to the place. It does not grow; it assembles itself. It does not breathe; it cycles coolant. It is a monumental, hand-hewn engine of a world, perpetually sweating in the dark, a sprawling masterpiece of primordial engineering that has forgotten what it was intended to build.
