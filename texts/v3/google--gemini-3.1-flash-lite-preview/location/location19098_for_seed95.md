The world here has forgotten the ambition of height. It is a place of primordial exhaustion, an immense, plateau-like expanse that has been scoured by eons until it resembles the back of a sleeping leviathan. It is high—so high that the air feels thin and sharp in the throat—but the terrain itself is nearly flat, defined by gentle, agonizingly smooth undulations that speak of a billion years of soft wind’s labor. 

Because the ground is ancient, it is not merely dirt; it is a dense, compacted mosaic of lithified dust, shimmering minerals, and the calcified remains of structures that were never built by hands. It is a landscape of high-saturation detail. Every square inch of the surface is occupied. 

The vegetation is sparse in terms of height—nothing grows taller than an ankle—but the ground is carpeted in a thick, living felt of iridescent mosses, liverworts, and translucent, vein-threaded tubers that cling to the rock. These plants store the water. There are no rivers, but there are "sweat-pockets"—hollows in the stone that gather the constant, heavy condensation of the warm air. When you step near them, the earth sighs, releasing trapped humidity that clings to your skin like a second coat.

In this place, density is measured in the sheer, overwhelming presence of fauna. Though the landscape appears quiet—there is no rustling of trees or rushing of water—the air is thick with millions of small, drifting creatures. They are not insects in the recognizable sense, but rather soft, bioluminescent membranes that float and pulsate, hovering inches above the ground. You cannot walk without brushing against them; they move with the patience of slow-motion smoke, reacting to your heat with a gentle, rhythmic darkening of their hue. They possess a collective, sluggish intelligence, turning as one to observe the intruder, their surfaces shimmering with subtle, rhythmic bioluminescence that matches the slow cycle of the condensation-rain.

The "built" aspect of this world consists of obsidian-like shards, jagged and black, jutting out of the stone like teeth. They are not buildings, but ancient, natural outcrops that have been worn into shapes suggestive of geometry: lintels, arches, and smooth, circular windows. These are the "Ancient Marks." They do not serve a function, yet they are the focal points for the drifting fauna. The creatures gather inside these natural apertures, nesting in the coolness, their combined bodies creating a soft, humming vibration that you feel in your teeth rather than hear with your ears.

The light is permanent, a tepid, golden late-afternoon glow that never quite resolves into full sun or dusk. Because there is no technology and no metallic resonance across the plateau, the solitude is total. There is no urgency here, no predator-prey dynamic that suggests a chase. Everything exists in a state of suspended, heavy homeostasis. 

Time feels different here, as if the landscape is so deeply aged that it has reached a terminal velocity of patience. To watch the water bead on a stone takes an hour; to watch a cluster of floating fauna shift from one aperture to another takes the duration of a long, warm day. It is a world that has arrived at its final form, and it is content to simply exist, wet and warm and impossibly dense with the slow, pulsing life of its own skin.
