High in a jagged, young mountain range that has not yet learned to soften its edges, there exists a basin no larger than a village square. It is a place of sharp, volcanic glass and basalt, thrust upward by forces that have only just finished their work. Because it is surrounded by sheer, serrated peaks that trap the heat of the thin, high-altitude sun, the basin acts as a thermal crucible.

The air here is thick, pressing against the skin like a warm, wet towel. Despite the altitude, the water table remains at the surface in the form of porous "weeping" stones—obsidian monoliths that sweat beads of mineral-rich condensation. These droplets gather into slow, rhythmic pools of turquoise liquid that never seem to evaporate, despite the stifling heat. The water is heavy, viscous, and vibrates with a low-frequency hum that is felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear.

Vegetation here is sparse but startlingly vibrant. There are no forests, only singular, solitary stalks of pale, translucent ferns that grow horizontally, threading their roots between the hexagonal cracks of the cooling basalt floor. These plants do not photosynthesize; they appear to be conductive, their leaves trembling when the erratic, post-modern atmosphere pulses.

The "built" aspect of the site is faint, consisting of three or four freestanding geometric frames made of a self-repairing, iridescent alloy. These frames are scattered across the basin, standing like abandoned doorways to nowhere. They are not ruins, but rather diagnostic beacons—pieces of tech that defy current physics. If you press your ear to the cold metal of one of these frames, you can hear the sound of the future: a rapid, clinical sequence of high-speed data clicks that sound like ice cracking under a heavy boot.

Because the density is so low, you are always alone here. You can walk for hours in this small, enclosed space, stepping over patches of wet, iridescent moss that shimmers with a bioluminescence that flares blue whenever you approach. There is an active, dynamic cycle at play: the weeping stones drip, the pools churn, the alloy frames shift their alignment by millimetres to track something unseen in the star-dusted sky, and the warm, wet air circulates in a constant, humid convection.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, unless you count the "skimmers"—small, geometric slivers of chrome-like matter that drift a few inches above the marshy ground. They do not eat; they move in response to the magnetic fluctuations of the ground, darting from pool to pool as if verifying a calculation.

It is a quiet, breathless place, save for the occasional wet *thwip* of a fern unfurling or the distant, resonant groan of the mountainside settling into its new shape. Here, the world feels like a laboratory experiment that has been left running for a billion years, a cathedral of cooling stone and impossible engineering, eternally waiting for a signal that has yet to arrive.
