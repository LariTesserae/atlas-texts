The horizon is not a line, but a smear of bruised violet and ferro-fluid black. Here, at the low-lying basin of a world that has been worked to exhaustion, the ground does not consist of earth or stone, but of compacted industrial refuse—fossilized gears, pulverized copper wiring, and tectonic plates of rusted slag. 

There is no soil. There are no roots. Between the jagged, salt-crusted ridges of scrap, a thin, super-cooled brine wells up from the crust. It is a world of constant weeping. Water does not fall from a sky; it manifests as a dense, silver-gray condensate that clings to everything, weeping in ribbons down the sides of rusted monoliths. Every surface is slick, perpetually saturated, making the entire basin a treacherous, humming marsh of metal and moisture.

Gravity seems to hiccup here. Because the world is "packed"—dense with heavy, discarded machinery—and yet "open" in its vast, flat expanse, the air itself feels heavy, charged with a low-frequency hum. This is the sound: a rhythmic, wet thumping, like a massive hydraulic press striking a sponge. It is the sound of a world that never stopped its factory-cycle, even though the masters are long gone.

The ecology is not biological. It is mechanical mimicry. Swarms of "skitters"—multi-jointed, brass-limbed entities that resemble crab-like clockwork—scuttle across the sludge. They possess no nervous systems; they are run by stored kinetic energy and the friction of wet gears. You watch them tear at the landscape. They aren't eating; they are stripping. They consume the larger rusted plates, melt them down in internal, self-contained crucibles, and extrude newer, sharper shapes. They are a self-replicating engine of erosion, maintaining the landscape’s "final form" by constantly cannibalizing its past.

The light is a dim, sickly luminescence that emanates from the puddles themselves—a bioluminescence of chemical run-off and electrified sediment. It casts no shadows, but rather highlights the weird geometry of the place. Space behaves poorly here. If you look at a cluster of churning, grinding machinery across the valley, it seems to be twenty feet away. If you move toward it, the distance multiplies, the gears expanding into skyscraper-sized pistons that pulse with the rhythm of a heartbeat, before folding back into miniatures.  

There is the smell of ozone, wet iron, and something sharply electric, like a live wire dipped in stagnant water. 

In the center of it all, a fountain of pressurized oil erupts from a cracked, primordial basin, only to freeze mid-air, held in a statue-pose by the sheer intensity of the cold. The skitters swarm the frozen oil, chipping away at the rigid splatters, feeding their own metallic bodies with the chilled fuel. Everything is loud, everything is saturated, and everything is moving. It is a graveyard of industry that refused to die, choosing instead to become its own cycle—consuming itself, resetting itself, and weeping endlessly into the cold, metal dust. Reality here is thin; if you step too heavily on a patch of slick slag, the ground might ripple like silk, revealing a glimpse of a different, impossibly distant sky rotating beneath your feet, before snapping shut with the sound of a closing cellar door.
