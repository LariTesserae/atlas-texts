Deep beneath the crust, in a hollow carved by geothermic fatigue, lies the Foundry of Respiration. It is a chamber of cathedral scale, sunken and cradled in the stone, where the heat is a physical weight, pressing against the skin like a heavy, velvet blanket. The air is never dry, but it is never truly wet; it is a shimmering, constant haze of condensation that clings to everything in oily, lukewarm beads.

The environment is a masterwork of slow, geological erosion. The walls—pitted, smoothed, and undulating—are composed of a dark, porous obsidian-like rock that has been worn into bizarre, flowing shapes over aeons. There is no animal life here; the ecosystem is purely mechanical and botanical, a strange marriage of biology and industry that has reached a state of perfect, stagnant equilibrium.

Lush, thick-leafed vines—translucent and veined with pulsing, bioluminescent sap—climb the walls in dense, tangled mats. These vines are not natural; they are threaded through the rusted skeletal remains of industrial machinery. Great copper pipes, half-melted and fused into the rock, serve as the latticework for the flora. Steam escapes from fissures in the stone and from the joints of ancient, half-buried pistons, creating a rhythmic, percussive hiss that fills the cavern.

The sound is relentless. It is a low-frequency thrum, a subterranean vibration that seems to emanate from the very center of the earth. Occasionally, a massive, rusted gear embedded in the wall will groan—a sound like tectonic plates grinding—as it rotates a fraction of an inch once every few days. The process is excruciatingly slow, yet the roar of the steam and the mechanical groaning creates a localized wall of noise that makes thought difficult.

Scattered across the floor are the "artifacts": small, box-like dwellings made of oxidized brass, now almost entirely consumed by the encroaching, heat-loving vegetation. They look like forgotten canisters, their doors hanging off hinges, their insides filled with nothing but damp, clumping mold and tangled wiring that gives off a faint, electric ozone scent.

There is no natural sunlight here. The only illumination comes from the sickly, flickering amber glow of the vines and the intermittent sparks that fly from the friction of the grinding metal. The light is dim, catching the hanging moisture in the air and refracting it into oily, shimmering rainbows that drift across the dark stone.

In this place, time feels compressed and irrelevant. Everything is in a state of final, weary completion. The industry—the endless, grinding machinery—has finished its purpose centuries ago, yet it continues to operate, fueled by the intense heat and the constant, patient cycles of condensation. It is a garden at the end of a long, cold labor, where the machines have become trees and the vines have learned how to oil their own joints, all buried under miles of earth, existing only for the sake of the hum.
