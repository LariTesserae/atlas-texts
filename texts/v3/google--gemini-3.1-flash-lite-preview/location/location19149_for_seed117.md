The air here is a heavy, achromatic soup, perpetually saturated with a fine, cold mist that clings to the skin like oil. This is a landscape of the "Low-Drain"—a basin of exhausted industrial ambition, compressed into a space no larger than a city square. Everything is huddled, stacked, and leaning, as if the very geometry of the place is trying to conserve heat.

There is no green. The ground is a jagged mosaic of pulverized slate, weeping grey ochre, and slick, porous basalt. Water is the local medium; it does not fall as rain so much as it manifests, beading on the undersides of rusted iron beams and trickling in rhythmic, meditative drips from the overhangs. The basin floor is a series of interconnected, shallow depressions that act as reservoirs for a viscous, mineral-rich slurry that mirrors the dim luminescence of the sky.

The built environment is scattered and deeply weathered—a graveyard of obsolete machinery. Half-buried hydraulic presses, their pistons seized long ago by calcified minerals, lean against stone walls that have been smoothed by eons of seepage. These artifacts are not ruins in the traditional sense; they are settled, almost geological. They have become part of the habitat. 

Living here is a density of "pulsars"—small, gelatinous fauna that look like translucent, ribbed sponges. They move with glacial intent, suctioning themselves to the iron surfaces to draw moisture and trace electrical charges from the lingering, faint currents in the rusted metal. They do not have eyes; they sense the vibration of the dripping water, humming in harmonic resonances that keep the air vibrating at the edge of hearing. When one Pulsar touches another, they briefly glow with a dull, bruised-purple discharge, a silent flicker of energy that barely pierces the gloom.

The atmosphere is one of profound, balanced decay. There is a sense that the world had reached its climax, finished its work, and is now slowly dissolving into the wet mineral earth. You can walk through the space by stepping carefully on the raised ridges of oxidized piping or the flat, slate-grey slabs that form natural boardwalks over the marshy patches. 

Occasionally, a pressure valve in the deep substrate—something ancient and dormant—releases a sigh of trapped steam. It drifts through the pipes, warming the mist and causing the local fauna to ripple in unison. It is a world of low-frequency metabolism: the metallic, the aqueous, and the proto-biological, all locked in a slow, wet waltz of stillness. It is not cold enough to freeze, but it is cold enough to slow the heart; it is not quite a graveyard, but a factory floor undergoing a transition into stone. In this confined space, the lack of wind or horizon makes the environment feel sentient, an enclosed, ruminating machine that has forgotten its original purpose and has decided, instead, to simply exist.
