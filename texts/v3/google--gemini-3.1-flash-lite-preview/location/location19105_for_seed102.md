Deep beneath the crushing, pressurized crust of the silicate mantle lies a singular, impossibly stable pocket: the Lithic Greenhouse. It is a chamber no larger than a cathedral nave, encased in seamless, unyielding walls of polished obsidian that have never felt the abrasive kiss of wind or weather. Nothing here erodes. The geometry of the room is frozen in the exact state it held when it first formed millions of years ago, a jagged, sharp-edged tomb that refuses to soften.

There is no water. The air is warm—a thick, dry, metallic breath that carries the scent of baked dust and ozone. Despite the total absence of moisture, the chamber is lush. 

Thick, fleshy succulents, translucent as stained glass, sprout directly from the cooling rock fissures. They do not photosynthesize; they drink the faint, sulfurous vibrations emanating from the chamber walls. Their leaves are wide, vein-mapped paddles of bioluminescent violet and sickly chartreuse, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light that never accelerates or dims. Between these gardens of stone-bound moss, the space is sparse, leaving deliberate, silent voids where an observer might stand, if they could ever reach this depth.

The sound is overwhelming. It is not the sound of wind or water, but a constant, thunderous grinding—a low-frequency geological groan that resonates through the floorboards of the mind. It is matched by the chaotic, discordant clicking of a thousand tiny, chitinous crawlers. These creatures—fauna the size of a human thumb, shaped like clockwork beetles with iridescent, jagged carapaces—skitter across the obsidian surfaces. They move with crystalline precision, their clicking limbs echoing like ball bearings dropped onto a marble floor, a cacophony that never changes tempo or volume.

Artifacts of a primitive intelligence are scattered sparingly among the vines. A single, fist-sized obsidian sphere sits atop a pedestal of fused salt; nearby, a crude, hand-woven tapestry made of fibrous, dried creepers hangs from a jagged protrusion, its weave tight and impossibly complex. These objects are not ruins; they are fixed, permanent fixtures of the room's unchanging inventory.

There is no cycle here. The warm light does not shift; it hangs at a steady, dim twilight, emitted by the flora and the wall-veins. The beetles do not hunt; they perform repetitive, geometric loops, clicking through their errands in a feedback loop of pure equilibrium. 

To enter this space is to step into a photograph that has been granted mass and heat. It is a hidden, secret room where the laws of entropy have been politely asked to leave, leaving behind a garden that will remain exactly as it is until the planet itself ceases to turn. In the utter absence of liquids, life has found a way to become as hard, as sharp, and as stubborn as the stone that contains it.
