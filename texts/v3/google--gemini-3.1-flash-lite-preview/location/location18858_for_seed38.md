At this altitude, the air has forgotten the dry sting of oxygen. The atmosphere here is a thick, lukewarm emulsion of mist and scent, a perpetual condensation that clings to the skin like a second, velvet garment. You are standing upon the "High Mat"—a continent-spanning expanse of soft, vegetal biology that has long ago consumed the stone strata beneath it. This is not soil; it is calcified root-matter, compressed by eons into a spongy, yielding floor that breathes with a slow, planetary rhythm.

The erosion here is not the jagged scarring of wind or river, but the smooth, deliberate rounding of ancient death. Everything is rounded, softened, and fully integrated. The "landscape" is a single organism of fractal complexity. Gigantic, moss-like growths the size of ancient cathedrals arch overhead, their translucent fronds filtering the dim, eternal twilight into a mossy emerald gloom. There is no dry ground. Everywhere, moisture beads on the waxy surfaces of towering succulent-pillars, weeping in slow, rhythmic drips that sound like liquid clockwork.

Because the vegetation is the substrate, the world feels precarious yet immovable. If you press your hand against the floor, you feel a faint, pulsing heat—a deep-tissue circulation of nutrients circulating miles below. The light remains perpetually low, a bruise-colored dusk that never brightens into noon nor fades into true night. It is a world of shadows, yet those shadows are alive, teeming with small, chitinous presences—multi-legged things that navigate the vertical stalks with silent, purposeful movements. They are not predators; they are janitors, moving through the thick, humid air with a slow, patient grace.

Rarely, one encounters the "artifacts" of this place. They are not built, but grown-over. Rib-like structures of bone-white timber rise from the Mat, remnants of something ancient, pre-human, perhaps even pre-thought. They are soft now, covered in a thick patina of violet lichen, acting as trellis-work for the surrounding flora. These ruins offer no shelter, only a different texture of decay.

The sense of scale is dizzying. Because the vegetation blankets the curvature of the world, you cannot see a horizon. You only see the repeating arc of the Mat, stretching upward into the gloom, a vast, spherical organism that seems to have reached its terminal state of perfection. It is a finished world. There is no competition here, only synthesis. The plants do not grow *on* the planet; they *are* the planet’s skin, and the planet has been sleeping for a geological epoch.

The quiet is profound, punctuated only by that liquid respiration—the *drip-hiss* of moisture settling on leaf-surfaces and the low-frequency hum of a biosphere that has no reason to hurry. To walk here is to tread on a living lung. Every step triggers a soft, hydraulic sink, a sigh of displaced air from the roots beneath. It is a place of immense saturation, where the distinction between animal, plant, and climate has long since dissolved, leaving behind a warm, damp, and perfectly settled equilibrium that prefers, above all else, to be left to its own slow dreaming.
