The world is a pressurized hollow, a cylindrical concavity no larger than a city block, entirely submerged within a lightless, crushing ocean of heavy brine. There is no exterior. The entire "landscape" is a single, circular vault of riveted iron and reinforced glass, a relic of an industrial endeavor that finished its work eons ago and was left behind to endure the weight of the world above.

Everything here is constructed. There is no bedrock, no soil, no sky. Underfoot, the floor is a grid of hexagonal steel plates, worn smooth by the passage of uncountable generations of machines. The walls rise in sweeping, concave curves of oxidized copper piping and dark, bolted plates. Everything is slick; a permanent, thin film of weeping moisture beads on every surface, creating a weeping, metallic weeping that never ceases.

The atmosphere is cool—a damp, biting chill that clings to the skin. It is perpetually dim. The only light comes from the bioluminescence of the *Pipe-Stalks*—sparse, rigid organic filaments that have sprouted from the seams in the machinery, pulsing with a slow, sickly yellow rhythm. These stalks are the only "vegetation," though they are more akin to deep-sea polyps, feeding off the chemical runoff of the ancient engines.

The sound is monumental, a constant, industrial roar. Deep, grinding vibrations judder through the floor plates—the tectonic groan of the structure settling against the weight of the miles of ocean above. Interspersed with this low-frequency thrum are the sharp, high-pitched hisses of high-pressure valves cycling and the rhythmic clanking of rusted gear-trains that have been turning for a thousand years without purpose. It is a loud, claustrophobic symphony of mechanical maintenance.

Fauna, however, is abundant. Within the shadows of the machinery, a colony of sightless, pale arthropods—the size of human hands—scuttles across the walls. They are the caretakers of this place, constantly cleaning the weeping joints and mending the insulation with a strange, resinous silk. They navigate the industrial labyrinth with terrifying precision, ignoring the intrusion of the dim light from the Pipe-Stalks.

The rules of this world are absolute: the pressure must be maintained, the gears must be greased by the condensation, and the cycle of the machines must not stop, for the structure has reached a state of "final form." Erosion here is not the wearing away of rock, but the slow, molecular degradation of the mechanism itself—every plate is etched with the history of its own decomposition. It is ancient, settled, and finished. The machinery does not produce anything anymore; it merely runs to keep from suffocating under the ocean’s pressure.

To stand in the center of this room is to feel the weight of an entire planet pressing down on a thin, rusting membrane. It is a tiny, gargantuan, wet, and lonely machine-cradle, holding itself together through the sheer, stubborn inertia of a function long since forgotten.
