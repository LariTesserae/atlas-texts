The city of Orem-Vael clings to the spine of a continental-scale ridge, a sprawling, tiered labyrinth of dark, porous basalt and oxidized copper. At this high altitude, the air is perpetually heavy, thick with a warm, clinging mist that never quite coalesces into rain but keeps every surface—every gear-tooth, every walkway, and every chitinous limb—slick with a fine, dark sheen.

There is no soil here, and the concept of a "plant" is alien and forgotten. In their place, the geography is a dense, multilayered architecture of utility. The city is a masterpiece of slow-motion erosion; the structures were not built so much as they were sculpted by centuries of industrial grit and the weeping of the mountain rock. The basalt itself seems to sigh, releasing mineral-heavy beads of moisture that run down the sides of massive, rusted chimneys and gargantuan, slow-grinding piston housings.

The scale is impossible to fully grasp. Looking out from any one of the thousands of bridge-walks, you see endless tiers of machinery integrated directly into the cliff-face. The sound is a constant, ambient churn—a deep, rhythmic drumming of steam-valves and subterranean bellows that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet. It is a loud world, yet it suffers no frantic motions; the cycles of Orem-Vael are long and patient, governed by the slow expansion of heated metal and the gradual migration of the creatures that define this space.

The fauna is, quite simply, overwhelming. They are not biological in the sense of soft tissues or green-blood; they are dense, armored entities—crustacean-like workers and silken-webbed maintenance humours that crawl over every inch of the infrastructure. They move with a deliberate, haunting slowness. Some are massive, the size of houses, with legs like hydraulic struts that step gingerly over the exposed piping. Others are tiny, swarm-like, scraping the slick mineral deposits off the walls of the great boilers. They do not compete for resources in the traditional sense; they operate as part of the city’s metabolism. A creature might spend a decade polishing a brass pressure gauge, its multiple, multi-faceted eyes reflecting the dull, warm ambient light of a sun perpetually diffused by the damp haze.

There is a sense of "built" that has transcended the need for builders. The industry here—the soot-stained glass furnaces, the rotating turbines that catch the warm updrafts from the valley below—is self-sustaining. The heat comes from the earth itself, a geothermal pulse that keeps the air warm and the mist dense, nourishing the mineral-crusts that coat the walls like geometric moss. 

Movement here is a heavy, tactile business. When you walk these corridors, the texture changes from smooth, wet glass to rough, weathered stone, to the metallic vibration of a floor-grate. There is no urgency. The city is in a state of settled equilibrium. It does not grow; it endures. When a gear finally seizes after three hundred years, the fauna—a cluster of chitinous menders—swarms it, vibrating in a soft, low-frequency chorus as they slowly disassemble the component, replacing teeth and rings with parts scavenged from the city’s own inexhaustible stores of refuse.

It is a world of infinite, wet density. Nothing is ever dry, yet nothing rots. The atmosphere carries the sharp, metallic tang of ionized copper and the heavy, sweet scent of warm mineral rain. In the distance, the city stretches until the mist swallows the horizon, revealing only the faint, rhythmic glow of furnace-light pulsating through the dark, weeping geometry of the cliff-side metropolis. It is a slow, loud, and impossibly crowded monument to the idea that existence does not require a bloom of life to be complete.
