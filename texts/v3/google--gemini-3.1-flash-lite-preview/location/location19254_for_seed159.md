We are beneath the crust, in a vast, vaulted geode-grotto that feels less like a cave and more like the interior of a colossal, humming lung. 

The scale is immense, a sprawling cathedral of raw geology that has not yet hardened into stillness. The world is in a state of hyper-gestation; the walls are not static stone but thick, curdling layers of silicate slurry and cooling mineral lace. Because the elevation is so low, the weight of the world above translates into a constant, localized pressure that keeps the air thick, warm, and tasting faintly of ozone and wet copper.

There is no vegetation here—not a thread of green or a leaf—but the place is far from barren. It is crawling, teeming, and vibrating with an overwhelming population of non-biological entities. These are "gestalt-mites": crystalline, segmented creatures the size of human fingers, faceted like volcanic glass, moving in swarms that flow over the cavern floor like spilled liquid mercury. They are the architects of this realm. They do not eat; they rearrange. They scrape the mineral walls with primitive, obsidian-tipped tools, carving intricate, shallow channels into the cooling rock, then sealing them with a thick, viscous condensation that beads on every surface. 

This moisture—the 1.4/3 water parameter—is not a gentle mist but a heavy, rhythmic sweating of the cavern ceiling. It falls in erratic sheets that hissed as it hits the warm floor, feeding the localized condensation cycles.

The atmosphere is a riot of noise. It is not the sound of wind or water, but the rhythmic, millions-strong *click-clack-grind* of the mites. The sound is deafening, a dense, constant clatter that suggests a vast clockwork mechanism being assembled in real-time. It is a turbulent, anthill-energy, where the mites are constantly clambering over one another, shifting the focus of their labor. One moment they are building a spire of raw agate, building it with frantic, uncoordinated speed, and the next, they are tearing it down to pack the material into a different configuration along the cavern floor.

Because the erosion is so low (emerging/under construction), the terrain features have no history. A pillar might erupt from the ground in an hour, stand for three, and then be dismantled by a tide of mites who have decided the space would be better served as a shallow basin. 

Scattered throughout this chaos are the "Anchors"—the built structures. These are primitive, tripod-like frames made of fused obsidian, standing about four feet tall. They don’t seem to be habitations, but rather primitive magnetic stabilizers. They are caked in the condensation, shining with an oily, trapped light. When a swarm of mites passes an Anchor, the sound momentarily deepens, the clacking shifting into a low, resonant thrum that vibrates in your chest.

It is a world of constant becoming. Heat radiates from the seams in the floor, and the air stays humid, thick with the smell of wet minerals. Every square inch of the cavern is occupied by motion. You feel the floor shivering underfoot, shifting as thousands of facets carve the world into a shape that will be forgotten by tomorrow. Nothing is finished. Nothing is allowed to settle. It is a loud, crowded workshop of proto-reality, suspended in a warm, subterranean cradle.
