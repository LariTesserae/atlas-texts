The vessel is a vertical labyrinth of copper and worn basalt, a high-altitude cistern suspended above a world that has been forgotten. Here, the water is not merely ambient; it is the fundamental medium of existence. It is a warm, pressurized darkness—a fluid that feels thick, almost oily against the skin, swirling with a low-frequency hum that vibrates in the marrow rather than vibrating the air.

There is no sky. There is only the ceiling of the cistern, a vaulted dome of riveted stone and oxidized metal that curves inward, forcing the world into a claustrophobic, intimate scale. It is a cathedral built for creatures that do not need to breathe, encased in a liquid weight that keeps the silence absolute.

Everything here is constructed. There is no biological arc of growth, no green thing to break the monotony of the mineral and the manufactured. The walls are lined with hexagonal tiles of volcanic glass, etched with primitive, labyrinthine carvings that serve no discernible purpose other than to define the space. The carvings are soft, eroded by eons of slow, rhythmic circulation—the water itself has smoothed the edges of man’s ancient labor into something nearly geological.

The density is suffocating. Within the room-sized compartments, thousands of small, translucent entities drift in the warmth. They are not animals in any traditional sense, but clusters of soft, bioluminescent filaments tethered to crude ceramic weights. They pulse with a rhythmic, ghostly luminescence—a rhythmic light that provides the only illumination. As they drift, their filaments catch on the carved stone walls, snagging and pulling away with a sound like tearing silk.

There is no wind, no current greater than the slow, agonizingly steady convection that circulates the water through the basalt pipes. One cycles through the basin in a span of decades, feeling the temperature shift by a fraction of a degree. The technology is stark, heavy, and final; it possesses the grace of tools made by hands that never intended to innovate, only to endure.

In this place, time is not measured by the rise and fall of celestial bodies, which have long since been erased by the deluge. Time is the accumulation of minerals on the tile. It is the slow thickening of the water as it leaches the salts from the walls. It is the steady, patient drift of the luminous filaments as they map the boundaries of their final, submerged prison. Everything is held in a state of suspended animation—a warm, dark, permanent embrace where the only change is the infinitesimal wearing away of the walls that keep the ocean at bay.
