The sky here is not a void, but a ceiling of heavy, bruised moss. You are walking upon the *Aethel-Stalks*—a lattice of gargantuan, calcified vines that cross each other at vast intervals, creating a web that stretches into the dim, amber-tinted haze. 

This is a world of total construction, yet there is no stone, no steel, no smelting. The entire continental architecture is grown. The inhabitants, hunched-over weavers with translucent, spindly limbs, have spent eons coaxing the "living-substrate" into shape. They use primitive obsidian blades and braided root-saws to prune the architecture, directing the growth of structural veins into amphitheaters, bridges, and arching vaults that have long since calcified into a semi-stony, bioluminescent wood.

Because the density is so low—the architecture is a skeletal web suspended in a high-altitude mist—each "room" is a cavernous, empty kilometer of space. You walk for hours across exposed, moss-covered ribs before finding another structure, another hollowed-out bloom where someone resides. It is a vast, echoing emptiness, filled with the constant, rhythmic dripping of warm condensation.

The atmosphere is heavy, saturated, and perpetually weeping. Water beads on the underside of every architectural overhang, falling in slow, rhythmic droplets that blur into a constant mist at the lower tiers. Despite the warmth, the lighting is abysmal—a low-slung, diffuse orange glow that emanates from the pores of the plants themselves. It does not clarify, but rather obscures, creating shadows that seem to detach themselves from the walls and drift away.

This is where the reality breakdown manifests. The geography is not constant. Because the entire world is a living, wet organism, the connections between structural points are fluid. If you walk toward a bridge that sits a kilometer away, the bridge may pulse—contracting or expanding—as the vegetation "digests" the space between. You might step off a walkway and find that the gravity has shifted ninety degrees, as the plant-substrate prioritizes the alignment of its internal sap-flow over the convenience of its inhabitants.

There is almost no sound. There is no machinery here, only the soft *thrum-thrum* of the plant's internal hydraulic pressure and the wet, squelching footsteps of the fauna. The fauna are strange, amorphous things—gelatinous, slow-moving pods that hover in the humid gaps, drifting like clouds. They don’t hunt in the traditional sense; they absorb moisture from the air, trailing long, gossamer filaments that snag the floating pollen of the architecture.

You feel a profound sense of "settled form." The world is ancient, weathered by its own sap, its surfaces smooth and polished by millennia of slow-moving humidity. It is a place that feels finished, yet it is never static. It is a high-altitude, continental garden that has forgotten how to be wild, and in doing so, has become a labyrinthine monument to a species that exists only to trim the edges of a reality that is slowly dreaming itself into decay.

You stand on a ledge of fused, amber-hardened leaf, looking out into the mist. A kilometer ahead, a spire of woven thorns begins to slowly untwirl, uncoiling like a waking snake until it reaches for the ceiling of the world. It makes no sound as it moves, save for the wet, tearing noise of fibers sliding against one another. In this world, you are never lost, because the architecture moves to accommodate you—or perhaps, it moves simply because it has nowhere else to grow.
