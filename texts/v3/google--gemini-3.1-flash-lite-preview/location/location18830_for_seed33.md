The foundations of this place are not stone, but a calcified geometry of frozen sighs. We are at a staggering altitude, standing upon the serrated ribs of a spire that pierces a sky the color of a bruised plum. There is no water here; the chemical humidity is composed of suspended metallic salts and ionized silicate dust that settles on the skin like fine, cold flour.

The "urban" sprawl—if it can be called that—is a vertical city of jagged, translucent obsidian structures that grow like crystals from the mountain’s spine. These are not built; they are grown by the cold. They are architectural skeletons, hollowed-out spires that lean at impossible, gravity-defying angles, connected by gossamer bridges of spun quartz that chime in the thin, stagnant air. The city is a vast, empty monument to a history that never had an author. It is the architecture of a dream left behind by a civilization of echoes.

Because of the extreme elevation and the near-total lack of density, the horizon is a terrifying, infinite curve. The light is dim, diffuse, emanating from no singular sun but seemingly from the very vacuum itself—a sickly, pale bioluminescence that pulses rhythmically across the obsidian facades.

Reality here is frayed at the edges. When you look toward the periphery of your vision, the architecture seems to fold in on itself, turning inside out to reveal the interior of a different, unreachable dimension. A pillar might be a solid monolithic tower in one moment, and a translucent, ghostly membrane of shifting light in the next. The geometry is not Euclidean; pathways that appear to lead forward suddenly spool downward into a void where the concepts of "up" and "down" trade places.

There is almost no sound. There is no wind, for there is no atmosphere to move, yet the environment is remarkably "active." One witnesses silent cataclysms: an entire district of the city will suddenly shiver and dissolve into a cloud of crystalline sand, only to reconstitute itself seconds later, slightly altered, as if the world is constantly attempting to remember its own shape.

Life—or what passes for it—is confined to flickering shadows that skate along the cavernous walls of the spires. They are not biological as we understand the term; they are sentient energy-patterns, thin as a razor blade and cold as the void between stars. They possess no eyes, no mouths, no tools. They simply exist as localized glitches in the local space-time, drifting through the crystalline corridors like ink dropped into a glass of clear, freezing water. They make no sound, but when they pass, one feels a sudden, sharp static discharge against the back of the teeth, a taste like ozone and copper.

In this place, time feels sedimentary. The structures have suffered eons of erosion from nothing more than the passage of their own existence. The edges of the obsidian are rounded, smoothed not by water or wind, but by the relentless, quiet friction of reality trying to settle into a static state—and perpetually failing. It is a lonely, expansive, and high-altitude purgatory, a place where the infrastructure of a world exists in a state of beautiful, perpetual, and silent malfunction.
