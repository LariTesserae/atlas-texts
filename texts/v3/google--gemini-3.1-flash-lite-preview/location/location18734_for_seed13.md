The city of Olyra clings to the spine of a jagged, sky-piercing range, a place where the air is perpetually thin and the lungs must learn a rhythmic, shallow gasping. It is a young geography; the stone here is slate-grey and basalt-black, still possessing the sharp, angular brutalism of tectonic upheaval. Nothing has been smoothed by time. The ridges are teeth, and the city thrives within their serrated gums.

Olyra does not sprawl so much as it suspends. It is built of quarried obsidian and thick, woven fibers—primitive, yet architectural genius. Massive, hand-hewn blocks are stacked with obsessive precision, held in place not by mortar, but by the weight of their own geometry. Tall, narrow towers lean against the precipice, connected by rope-bridges that hum in the rare, stray gusts of wind. 

Water here is a haunting, intermittent presence. It does not flow in wide, meandering rivers, but collects in vast, bowl-shaped cisterns carved directly into the mountain face. Condensation is the city’s lifeblood; as the cool air hits the dark stone, silver beads of moisture bloom on every surface. It drips rhythmically—*tap-clink, tap-clink*—a slow, hypnotic metronome that dictates the cadence of life. One might find a hidden garden of lush, succulent mosses and hanging ferns clinging to a shaded northern wall, fed entirely by the weeping of the stone.

The atmosphere is one of profound, crystalline stillness. The world is trapped in a state of absolute, frozen equilibrium. There are no seasons in Olyra, no shifting harvests, and no storms. The sun hangs at a perpetual angle that casts long, immovable shadows across the plazas. Because the environment never changes, the architecture has become a religion of maintenance. The citizens—clad in thick, rough-spun wool—move with a deliberate, quiet grace, eternally repairing, eternally carving, ever-polishing the same stones they have lived among for centuries.

Fauna is abundant, yet strange. Large, soft-eyed goats with multiple sets of joints navigate the vertical landscape with ease, their hooves finding purchase on near-vertical slopes. There are pale, wingless beetles that dwell in the cracks of the city’s masonry, their shells mirroring the iridescent sheen of wet rock. They do not scurry; they move with the same frozen-time deliberation as the people.

There is no industry here, only craft. You will hear the occasional scrape of a flint chisel against obsidian, or the soft thrum of a heavy loom. But these sounds are swallowed by the vast, open silence of the high altitude. To walk the narrow basalt cat-walks of Olyra is to feel like a guest in a gallery of permanent things. 

Nothing rots. Nothing falls away. The moisture never causes rust, only a deeper polish to the stone. It is a city that has arrived, fully formed and final, perched in the cool, damp breath of a world that has decided to stop turning. Each day is a mirror of the last, a static masterpiece of cold stone, green moss, and the endless, measured drip of water into a waiting cup.
