Deep beneath the crust, in a hollow carved not by tectonic violence but by the slow, humid expansion of root-networks, lies the Hydro-Loom. 

It is a world of total saturation. To walk—or rather, to squeeze—through this space is to inhabit the interior of a swollen, subterranean lung. There is no soil here, only the flesh of the plants themselves. The walls are ribbed with calcified vines as thick as cedar trunks, polished to a slick, dark sheen by the relentless condensation that coats everything in a beaded, tepid sweat. The air is thick, weighing on the skin, holding the temperature constant at a stifling, feverish warmth.

The architecture is entirely botanical, yet it functions like an engine. Great, leathery bladders the size of cottages are suspended from the ceiling-vaults, pulsing with a slow, agonizingly patient rhythm. These are the hearts of the grid. Inside them, clear viscous fluids are pushed through a capillary network of translucent pipes that snake along the floors and walls. The sound is inescapable: a wet, rhythmic thumping—*thrum-shhh, thrum-shhh*—overlapping in a constant, industrial drone that vibrates through the very soles of your feet.

There are no animals. The ecosystem is a closed-circuit of vegetation and mechanics. The "town" is a series of stacked, organic chambers where the architecture grows into the necessary shape to house the plant-machinery.
