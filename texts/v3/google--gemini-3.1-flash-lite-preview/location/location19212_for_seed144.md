The air at this altitude is thin, sharp, and tastes perpetually of scorched ozone and wet copper. You are standing on the spine of a mountain range that has long ago ceased to be just rock; it is a claustrophobic, vertical city of geological and industrial fusion. 

The heat is a physical weight, a 2.9-scale furnace that radiates not just from the sun, but from the mountain’s own core. Every few hours, a thick, greasy condensation—half-mist, half-drizzle—sweeps through the jagged crevices. It does not wash the land clean; it only serves to coat the rusted iron surfaces in a slick, dark patina.

This is a landscape of "packed" architecture. There is no empty space. The mountain is riddled with industrial honeycomb: riveted cylinders, rusted catwalks, and churning pistons are lodged directly into the basalt cliffs. You cannot tell where the geology ends and the machine begins. Massive, slumbering gear-works, their teeth worn smooth by centuries of friction, are embedded into the canyon walls like fossils of a civilization that decided to merge with the tectonics. 

Despite the heat, vegetation is a desperate, brittle affair. What grows here are "weeping-metal" mosses and obsidian-colored lichens that cling to the warm pipes. They don't draw nutrients from soil, but from the trace sulfur and mineral condensation that beads on the machinery. A few stunted, wire-like stalks push through the fractures in the rusted steel, clicking like chitin when the dry thermal winds stir them.

The fauna is unexpectedly dense, though quiet. Scuttling things with metallic carapaces—crustacean-like scavengers—inhabit the gaps between the pipes. They move with an unsettling, synchronized rhythm, feeding on the runoff from the heavy industrial ducts that vein the valley. You hear them before you see them: a soft, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* that echoes inside the metal chutes, a sound of industry that has been repurposed into a biological heartbeat. 

There is no town here, no center of governance, only these scattered, half-buried bunkers and switch-stations. They are weathered, their edges softened by aeons of wind-scouring. Some are intact, whistling with the release of pressurized steam; others are crushed, their innards spilling out like metallic entrails into the ravines. 

Everything is in motion. The heat drives the atmospheric cycles, pulling the fog through the shafts and cooling it against the shadowed undersides of the peaks, creating a recirculating loop of moisture. It is a world that has reached a perfect, static state of constant work. It doesn't need people. The factories, the vents, and the scavengers function as a singular, closed-loop machine, hummed into a quiet lull by the sheer scale of the heat. 

If you stand still—which you must, for the footing is treacherous and narrow—you realize the mountain is vibrating. It isn't an earthquake; it is the low-frequency thrum of the deep-earth pressure valves working in balance. The world feels saturated, finished, and utterly indifferent to your presence. There is simply no room left for anything new to be built, and nothing left to be destroyed. It is a landscape locked in a permanent, industrial exhale.
