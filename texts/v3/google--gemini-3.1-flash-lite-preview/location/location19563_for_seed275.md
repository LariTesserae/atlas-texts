We are deep beneath the planetary crust, in a subterranean expanse that stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every lateral direction, yet measures only a few hundred meters from the bedrock floor to the pressurized, ceiling-bound root systems above. The air here holds no trace of moisture; it is a pressurized, oxygen-rich gas, dry as kiln-fired clay, tasting faintly of ozone and pulverized cellulose.

The environment is not built upon rock, but woven. The entire "infrastructure" consists of massive, calcified, timber-like structures—hardened, brownish-grey fibers that have been grafted, knotted, and grown into the shapes of cathedrals, sprawling factory floors, and winding transit conduits. There is no soil, only the organic, exfoliating skin of the world-growth.

The industrial aspect is visceral. Great pulsating nodes, the size of houses, hang from the ceiling-vines like rhythmic sap-pumps, driving a low-frequency hum that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet. These are the lungs and hearts of the city, grinding out the dry, refined gases that maintain the structural integrity of the environment. Steam—or rather, a fine particulate dust of discarded, pulverized plant-ash—venting from brass-like valve stalks, drifts through the open halls, illuminated by the bio-luminescent glow of millions of tiny, stationary light-mites embedded in the fiber-walls.

There is a sense of "embryonic" construction here. In the vast, sparse gaps between the massive, dwelling-hubs, one can see the process in motion: sprawling, rapid-growth vines are slowly knitting together, forming new scaffolds, their tendrils lashing out with deliberate, slow-motion intent to bridge the emptiness. It is a world under constant, patient revision.

The fauna here are adapted to the drought and the architecture. Large, chitinous, leaf-veined insects—the size of hounds—scuttle across the vertical corridors, their movements silent and precise. They are the maintenance crews, grooming the organic cables, stripping away brittle, dead material, and tending to the flow of gases. They move with a chilling lack of urgency, occasionally congregating in the vast empty plazas to vibrate their wings in unison, creating a rhythmic, dry chirping that serves as the only relief from the heavy silence.

Because there is no water, physics here feels shifted, almost crystalline. The "buildings" don't possess the sharp edges of stone or steel; they are rounded, flowing, and porous, layered with a complexity that suggests an intelligence beyond simple biological necessity. To traverse the space is to walk through a city that is simultaneously a machine and a forest. 

The light is constant—neither day nor night exists—just the soft, shifting pulse of the light-mites fluctuating as the rhythmic sap-pumps cycle. Everything is orderly, industrial, and yet fundamentally, terrifyingly alive. The "streets" are merely the gaps between the massive organs of the city, and the "walls" are always growing, always tightening, always waiting for the next layer of the design to manifest in the cool, dry dark.
