The basin is a pressurized lung, a place where the earth has not yet finished exhaling its first breath. It is a shallow, sunken crater of slate and silt, perhaps no larger than a village square, walled in by cliffs of raw, black schist that weep constantly. There is no central spring; instead, the rock itself sweats, a slow, greasy seepage that keeps the ground in a state of permanent, clinging saturation.

The density here is suffocating. It is not an open landscape, but an architectural one, packed tight with the "Weep-Stacks." These are small, claustrophobic dwellings carved directly into the soft, eroding faces of the basin’s rim, connected by a chaotic web of driftwood walkways and slick, hand-laid stone bridges. Nothing is spacious. Every inch of the basin floor is occupied by a jumble of low-slung, stacked-stone huts that resemble cairns more than homes. The structures are built from scavenged river rock and mud-mortar, held together by sheer compression; if one stone were removed, the entire cluster would likely shift and settle like a collapsing honeycomb.

The vegetation is sparse, restricted to pale, translucent mosses and liverworts that cling to the damp undersides of the bridges. They do not grow toward the sun, but toward the points of highest condensation, unfurling delicate, gelatinous fronds that pulse with a slow, rhythmic transparency. 

The air is cool, heavy, and smells of wet stone, ozone, and the faint, briny tang of minerals forced through shale. Because the atmosphere is always nearing its dew point, everything is coated in a fine, shimmering film of water. To walk here is to tread on slick surfaces where sound is muffled, then amplified by the echo of dripping water. There are no birds. The only fauna are small, multi-legged crustaceans that skitter along the weeping rock walls, their shells the same charcoal gray as the stone, making them nearly invisible until they move.

The tech is humble: bone chisels, braided vine ropes, and woven reed mats that drape over the entrances of the stone huts to keep out the constant mist. The residents, few and quiet, move with a deliberate, low-slung grace, their feet calloused and gripping. They are busy—constantly repairing the masonry, clearing silt from the drainage channels, and harvesting the moss—but their movements are glacial, synced to the slow drip of the cliffside. 

This is an early, fragile world. The erosion is sharp-edged; the landscape feels "newly minted," as if the earth had only just pulled back the tide to reveal this basin yesterday. The light—a diffuse, milky gray—bathes the scene without casting harsh shadows, giving the wet stone a pearlescent sheen. 

There is a strange, underlying rule here: everything must be wet to remain stable. If the air were to dry, the mud-mortar would crumble, the mosses would wither, and the entire fragile, packed ecosystem would lose its adhesive integrity and slide back into the silt. The landscape survives only because it is perpetually saturating itself, a self-contained, weeping machine of stone and mist that refuses to let the world dry out.
