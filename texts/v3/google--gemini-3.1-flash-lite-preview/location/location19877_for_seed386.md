At an elevation that defies the clouds, where the air is so thin it seems to shimmer, lies a sprawling, vertical labyrinth of basalt and slick, sweating stone. The temperature is a brutal, equatorial heat, a heavy blanket that refuses to lift, even as the landscape is perpetually weeping. It is not exactly raining; rather, the atmosphere itself has surrendered to saturation, condensing against the thousand-foot cliff faces so that every surface is slick with a permanent, nourishing rheum.

This is a world of immense, weathered stability. The geography has been carved by aeons of these thermal cycles, resulting in staggered terraces and profound, echoing gullies that stretch for miles—a vast, mid-range valley system suspended at the edge of the stratosphere. The terrain is dense, not with buildings, but with an aggressive, layered biology. Massive, broad-leafed ferns with trunks thick as oil barrels cling to the precipices, their roots drinking from the fissures where mineral-rich water seeps out of the mountain's heart.

Yet, there is a strange, jarring intrusion upon this wild, steaming solitude. Buried within the very strata of the mountains are the rusted, ancient bones of an industrial era that has long since ceased to be human. Huge, oxidized iron conduits, some as wide as a tunnel, protrude from the weeping rock faces like petrified veins. They hum. It is a low-frequency vibration, more felt in the marrow of one's bones than heard by the ear—a rhythmic, subterranean thrum that suggests a massive, automated cooling system or a deep-core pump still cycling against the rot of time. Lubricant, thick and black, occasionally leaks from these weathered joints, pooling in the marshy pockets of the terraces, where it swirls in iridescent, oily patterns against the vibrant chlorophyll of the mosses.

The fauna here has adapted to this industrial relic. Six-limbed amphibians with skin the color of rusted iron cling to the steaming pipes, their movements sluggish in the heat, watching with lidless, golden eyes as moisture drips from the machinery above. They serve as the engineers of the rot, scraping the lichen that clogs the vents, their chattering the only sound that breaks the pervasive quiet.

The light is flat and diffuse, filtered through the perpetual haze of the thermals rising from the valley floor. There is no sunrise or sunset here, only the transition from a bright, featureless white to a muted, hazy gray. The landscape is in a state of high-saturation equilibrium; the plants grow at a frenetic, almost impatient pace, reclaiming the metal inch by inch, pinning the industrial scars to the mountain with thick, fibrous tendrils.

It feels settled, ancient, and utterly indifferent. The machinery does not function for the benefit of any observer; it is simply part of the geology now, a mechanized organ pumping fluid through the body of the mountain. One walks across shelves of obsidian-black rock, stepping over the weeping conduits, surrounded by the smell of hot, wet soil and the faint, ozone-tinged breath of a factory that has forgotten why it started working. It is a place where nature has not rejected the machine, but has instead smothered it in a humid, green embrace, integrating the sterile rhythm of gears into the timeless pulse of the rain.
