The world is not merely wet; it is a pressurized, drowning heaven. Gravity here acts upon the fluid as much as the solid, pulling the atmosphere into a dense, frigid slurry. It is a place of perpetual twilight, where the air is a thick, translucent medium—a suspended ocean of cold, heavy mist and drifting, bioluminescent spores that function as the only source of light. 

You are standing on a high-altitude plateau, though "standing" is a generous term. You are submerged in a liquid-heavy fog that flows over the rugged, obsidian-slicked basalt like a slow-motion waterfall. Because the air is saturated to the point of being a fluid, the sound is magnified, carried with violent clarity. Every movement of the environment is *loud*. The sky-rain—a constant, rhythmic drumming against the porous rock—echoes with the resonance of a thousand distant gongs. The sound is inescapable, a low-frequency thrum that vibrates through your chest cavity, pulsing in time with the erratic, churning storms that brew in the hollows between the peaks.

There is no animal life here. The silence of the organic is absolute, yet the landscape is intensely *active*. 

Great, vine-like ferns—pale, translucent, and rigid as glass—reach out from the crevasses of the obsidian. They do not grow toward the sun, for there is no sun, but instead toward the magnetic currents that hum within the rock. They drip incessantly. Every leaf is a fountain, every stem a conduit for the mineral-rich water that cycles through the cold, dark air. The vegetation is lush, but it is a sterile, crystalline lushness. It feels ancient, a landscape that settled into this exact, shivering state millennia ago. It is in perfect, weathered balance; the erosion is not destructive but transformative, the stone rounded and soft, draped in the velvet of blue-black mosses that drink the heavy moisture.

Scattered across this plateau are the "marks." They are not ruins, exactly, but *intentions*. You find low, circular walls built from perfectly stacked, unmortared river stone. They contain nothing, yet they are weathered as if they have endured the scouring rain for eons. Near these circles, you find primitive tools—obsidian blades lashed to shafts of hollow, mineralized wood with fibers that look like spun hair. They are relics of a people who seemingly vanished into the moisture, leaving their artifacts to be slowly swallowed by the creeping, glass-like flora.

The weirdness is the stillness of the architecture against the violence of the climate. The wind here doesn't blow; it flows, a river of cold air that drags the scent of ozone and pulverized minerals across the peaks. As you move, your own presence feels like a foreign, buoyant interference in a place that has achieved a total, damp equilibrium. 

There is no hunger here, for there is nothing to eat. There is only the rhythm of the water, the chime of the glass-ferns brushing against one another in the surge, and the heavy, crushing darkness that holds everything in its cold, wet grip. You are a ghost passing through a machine that is constantly being built and eroded by the rain, a place that remembers nothing because it is too busy falling.
