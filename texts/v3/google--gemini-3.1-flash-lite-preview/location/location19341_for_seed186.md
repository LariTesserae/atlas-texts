The world exists at the jagged, freezing edge of a continental spine, a vertical wilderness where the air is so thin it feels brittle. Here, at an elevation where the atmosphere thins into a crystalline ghost, the temperature holds a perpetual, biting chill. It is a place of immense, hollow silence, broken only by the rhythmic, unnatural thrum of deep-earth industrial veins.

There is no soil here, only vast, weathered shelves of obsidian-colored slate and frozen peat, carved by eons of glacial drag. Because the ground is nearly always hovering at the freezing point, the water—minimal and precious—never truly flows. It manifests as a slow, creeping condensation that beads on the undersides of leaves and collects in the deep, porous capillaries of the rock. Liquid mercury-bright droplets cling to the undersides of the "vegetation": a sprawling, bioluminescent moss-forest. It is not green, but a spectrum of pale violets and rusted ochres, thick and spongy, growing in dense mats that seem to move, shifting their weight toward the heat trapped in the mountain’s fissures.

There are no buildings, no roads, no artifacts of a creator. Yet, the land is haunted by the presence of a "mechanical ghost." Buried deep beneath the permafrost, there are ancient, rusted pistons the size of cathedrals, rhythmic in their operation. These derelict industrial rhythms—the *clunk-hiss* of steam-presses from a forgotten epoch—pulse through the stone, dictating the heartbeat of the ecosystem. The flora shivers whenever the ground beneath them cycles, its fronds vibrating in resonance with the hidden engines, creating a low-frequency hum that is felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear.

The fauna here are sparse, hauntingly lonely, and perfectly adapted to the cold dark. There are spindly, multi-limbed scavengers, pale as bone, that skitter across the vertical cliff faces. They do not hunt with sight, but with vibration-sensors embedded in their carapaces, attuned to the machinery of the earth. They move with an elegant, terrible precision, clinging to the damp rocks, feeding on the bioluminescent moss that flares with a soft, pulsing electricity when touched.

The darkness is absolute, save for the sickly, rhythmic flicker of the moss and the occasional phosphorescent drift of airborne spores. You could walk for hours in this vast, open expanse and see nothing but the slow, churning motion of the mountain’s surface—the way the tectonic shelves shift millimeters at a time, the way the ice-mist settles and evaporates in the long, perpetual twilight.

It is a landscape in a state of suspended animation—a graveyard of an industry that never quite died, holding up a world that refuses to succumb to the cold. Everything is in motion, yet nothing changes. It is a cradle of gears and moss, isolated by height, preserved by the biting frost, and utterly, hauntingly empty. Should you stand still, you would eventually find yourself becoming part of the equipment, your own pulse synching to the grinding of the forgotten iron deep below, your own skin dampening with the cold condensation of a world that is slowly, quietly, rearranging its own bones.
