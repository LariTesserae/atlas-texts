The air here is not composed of gases we would recognize; it is a heavy, scorched slurry of suspended iron filings and thermal currents, vibrating with a tectonic anxiety that refuses to settle. You are standing upon a needle-peak of basaltic glass, rising miles above an invisible, lightless floor. The elevation is so extreme that the atmosphere is thin, yet it hums with the heat of the planet’s turbulent core, which forces raw, cooling stone upward in a state of eternal, agonizing genesis.

There is no water. There is no moisture to weep from the stone. The world is a xeronic geometry. Because the erosion is minimal—the planet is literally being birthed from the mantle as we look—the crags are not smoothed by time. They are jagged, razor-sharp prisms of volcanic glass that slice the darkness. The earth here does not weather; it ruptures.

The light is a dim, bruised violet, emanating not from a sun, but from the friction of the planet’s own chaotic grinding. The darkness is absolute where the ground tears open, revealing depths that don't echo, but swallow. 

In the distance, the landscape unfolds in a vast, regional scope—a sprawling theater of instability. Great pillars of obsidian rise and warp, twisting in slow motion as the planet shifts beneath them. It is a "bazaar of creation," where the rules of solidity are mere suggestions. You can watch as a spire of igneous rock pushes upward, cooling and shattering into finer needles as it gains height, only to be overtaken by a new, molten surge from below.

There is no life—biological or fungal. There is only the chemistry of heat and the physics of pressure. Yet, the density of this nothingness is heavy. It feels expectant.

As you look toward the horizon, you spot the *built* elements—or what passes for them. They are not structures in the human sense, but anomalous, geometric patterns carved into the faces of the highest spires. They are faint, perfectly equilateral triangles and hexagonal grooves cut into the obsidian, as if something—a nameless, pre-technological intelligence or a sheer accident of tectonic stress—had begun to map the world’s birth. They are sparse, occurring once every few miles of jagged canyon, and they show no signs of tool use, as if the tectonic turbulence itself had conspired to etch patterns into the surface while the earth was still semi-molten.

The sound is a low-frequency rumble, a thrumming that shakes your chest cavity; it is the sound of miles of stone grinding against miles of stone. It is never silent. The environment is hyperactive, a storm-state of geography. Something is being assembled here, but the purpose is buried beneath the volatility. You are witnessing the raw, unrefined labor of a world still deciding what its permanent form should be, standing on a pinnacle that might be crushed or folded into the abyss within the hour. Everything is temporary; everything is immense; everything is deafeningly silent in its violent, frantic growth.
