The air here does not circulate; it cycles. It is a cool, heavy breath held deep within the planet’s crust, heavy with the sharp, metallic tang of ionized slate and the cooling hiss of steam. 

This is the City of Glass Veins—a subterranean expanse that stretches for miles along a light-drenched fault line. It is not built of stone or steel, but of polished, translucent sediment, worn smooth by eons of tectonic grinding. The architecture here is "ancient-urban": massive, honeycombed pylons carved into the very skeleton of the earth. These structures are not so much constructed as they are excavated with the deliberate, agonizing patience of a civilization that has practiced the art of the pick-axe for ten thousand years. Everything is rounded, softened by erosion until right angles are a forgotten physics. 

The light is the most jarring element. Despite the depth, the world is saturated in a brilliant, flickering white glare. It pours from luminous, gas-filled fissures that weep rhythmic jets of condensation. The water—if it can be called that—collects in shallow, mirror-bright pools on the floor. It is slick, mineral-rich, and clings to the walls like cold sweat. There is no true plant life, no green to soften the harsh, alabaster anatomy of the world. Instead, the "ecology" is composed of lichen-like clusters of conductive crystals that bloom in the damp patches, pulsing weakly when the atmosphere shifts.

The silence is deceptive. It is a place of profound quiet, punctuated only by the sudden, violent *chatter* of the city itself. The environment is hyper-turbulent. Because the crust here is so finely eroded and settled into its final, unstable form, the earth is constantly shifting. A wall that was solid a moment ago will fold like linen, or a staircase will liquefy into a slope of shifting grit as a pocket of pressure releases. It is a chaotic, anthill-like existence. 

The inhabitants—the fauna—clamber across the smooth, vertical surfaces with frantic energy. They are spindly, translucent things, their bodies mirroring the simplicity of the architecture. They work with manual tools of volcanic glass, patching the cracks in their homes as soon as they form, or scavenging the crystals that grow in the wake of a floor-shift. There is no language here, only the rhythmic scraping of tools against glass-like rock and the hiss of escaping steam.

To stand in the City of Glass Veins is to experience the end of time. There is no growth, only maintenance. Everything is worn to its limit, stabilized by constant, frantic attention. The scale is oppressive—looking up, you see tunnels fading into the white haze, miles of tiered, empty dwellings waiting for the next tremor to redefine their shape. It is a bright, damp tomb that refuses to stay still, a society of scavengers forever repairing a masterpiece of erosion that wants, more than anything, to crumble back into silence.
