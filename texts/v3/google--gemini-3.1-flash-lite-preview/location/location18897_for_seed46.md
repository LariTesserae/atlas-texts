Deep within the microscopic creases of a singular, jagged obsidian monolith—a space smaller than a human palm—exists an industrial nursery of the impossible. To the naked eye, the monolith is a dead, matte-black pebble. To the observer shrunken down, it is a high-altitude plateau of scorching, airless extrusion.

There is no water here. The atmosphere is instead a pressurized, viscous suspension of ionized argon and synthetic silicates, shimmering with the heat of a thousand invisible furnaces. The ground is raw, embryonic geology; it has not yet weathered. There are no rivers to carve it, no wind to soften the edges of the obsidian monolith’s jagged, uplifted ribs. Instead, the terrain is composed of sharp, cooling glass plates that groan under the pressure of their own birth.

From these plates, vegetation erupts—but it is a lush, botanical madness that defies biology. Instead of chlorophyll, these stalks are powered by localized, micro-industrial circuitry. Chrome-plated vines, pulsing with a rhythmic, faint blue current, spiral upward toward a light source that emanates from the internal seams of the stone itself. These "plants" do not seek the sun; they seek the friction of the world’s ongoing construction. They are self-assembling, their leaves unfolding like sheets of copper lace, serrated and burning to the touch.

The world is sparse. The chrome vines are few, standing equidistant from one another, separated by vast, barren plains of crushed, sun-hot mineral dust. The density is low; there is a profound, meditative space between each mechanical orchid, allowing the heat to radiate in long, lazy waves of distortion.

The weirdness is absolute. Because the laws of space-time are still "under construction" in this interior fold, reality frequently breaks down. Occasionally, a chrome vine will detach from the soil, drift upward, and multiply into a dozen translucent echoes of itself before snapping back into a singular, solid form. Gravity here is not a constant; it is a suggestion, localized to the individual plant, creating small, floating spheres of mineral dust that hang motionless in the heat.

There are no animals. Nothing breathes, consumes, or wanders the obsidian plains. The soundscape is dominated by the "industrial" hum—a deep, tectonic vibrato that sounds like a colossal engine turning over, miles below the surface. It is a drone of creation, a low-frequency pulse that vibrates through the soles of your feet, interrupted only by the sudden, sharp *tink-tink* of expanding glass cooling in the extreme temperature.

The landscape is "under construction." It is a factory of geology, a pocket-dimension where materials are being fused, forged, and laid out in an eternal, patient process. It is neither fully grown nor fully imagined. It is a latent potential, a place where the universe is still testing its own mechanics, building a lush world out of spare parts, intense heat, and absolute silence, hidden entirely within the shadow of a stone that does not know it is a world.
