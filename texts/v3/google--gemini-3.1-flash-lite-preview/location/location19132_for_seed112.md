The world is a singular, calcified lung. There is no water here; moisture is a structural heresy that the planet rejects, exhaling it instantly into the void. Instead, the entire globe is a continuous, continental weave of iron-wood and silica-flesh, a botanical sprawl that has grown so dense and so high that it pierces the thin, frigid upper atmosphere.

It is a world of **The Great Tangle**. Vast, sprawling root-buttresses the size of mountain ranges grip the crust, weaving together in a latticework that contains entire internal climates. The "soil" is not dirt, but the compacted, fossilized dust of billions of years of shedding leaves, turned to gray, metallic silt.

The environment is aggressively industrial, though there is not a single tool-wielding hand to be found. The plants themselves have evolved metabolic processes that mimic heavy machinery. Colossal, bulbous flowers function as piston-chambers, rhythmically compressing gases to drive internal circulation; they throb with a low-frequency hum that borders on a mechanical vibration. Vine-cables, braided with conductive mineral veins, crackle with bio-electric arcs as they pulse through the canopy. The sound is a deafening, constant cacophony: the shriek of high-pressure air venting through floral valves, the rhythmic clanking of woody joints shifting against one another, and the high-pitched harmonic whine of static electricity building in the dry air.

The scale is sickening. You can stand on a petal the size of a province and look out over a horizon that curves upward, swallowed by the hazy, electric-yellow light of a permanent sun that never sets, only shifts intensity. 

Despite the vastness and the density of life, it feels sparse. The architecture of the plants is so colossal that it creates voids of staggering emptiness—miles-wide cathedral-apses of translucent, translucent vein-work where nothing moves but the dust. Fauna exists, but it is mechanical in movement: metallic-scaled insects with whirring gossamer wings that sound like chainsaws, and hexapedal stalkers made of hollow, bamboo-like bone that glide across the surface with unnerving, twitchy speed. They are part of the turbulence; they do not live *on* the landscape, they live *in* the machine gears of it, pruning and scarring the environment as they compete for the high-voltage nectar that surges through the vines.

The logic of this place is one of pure, accelerated kineticism. Everything is in a state of rapid maintenance. Something is always being crushed to fuel something else. A flower withers and instantly, the ground-tissues erupt into a frenzy of decay and recycling, the debris being hauled away by swarms of chitinous harvesters. It is a world that has been eroded by its own frantic growth—the mountains of wood are weathered into smooth, streamlined shapes, polished by the constant friction of wind and internal shifting.

There is no rest. The air tastes of ozone and dry, pulverized pollen. To walk here is to tread upon the moving parts of an organism that thinks in centuries but acts in milliseconds. The light is blinding, reflecting off the polished silica of the giant leaves, turning the entire continent into a mirror-bright, clattering, roaring engine of total, living industry. You look up and see no sky, only layers of canopy moving like cogs in a clock that has long since forgotten why it was built, yet refuses to stop ticking.
