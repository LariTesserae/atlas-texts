This is the *Slag-Garden of the High-Plains*, a continental expanse perched miles above the oceanic floor, where the air is so thick with moisture that it acts as a conductor for the machinery embedded within the soil.

It is never bright here. The atmosphere is a heavy, pressurized soup of neon-blue phosphorescence and swirling, dark-grey vapors that cling to the ground like bruised velvet. Because there is no sunlight, the ecology has evolved to synthesize energy from the rhythmic vibrations of the earth and the electrical discharge of the weeping rocks.

The terrain is a labyrinth of basaltic plateaus and deep, peat-saturated trenches. The erosion is ancient, deep, and patient; the rocks do not look like stone, but like the rusted, smoothed-down gears of a god’s clock, partially buried and smoothed by eons of torrential, acid-tinged rain. Water is everywhere—seeping from the pores of the iron-stained earth, collecting in brackish pools that pulse with Bioluminescent kelps and mosses that grow in geometric, industrial patterns.

The scale is sickeningly vast. If one looks up, there is no sky, only a shifting ceiling of low-hanging storm fronts that move with their own internal, mechanical logic, clashing to produce deafening peals of static-electricity thunder. This sound is constant—a deep, resonant *thrum-wail* that vibrates in the marrow of one’s bones. 

The density of this place is claustrophobic. Every cubic meter of the landscape is occupied by competing life. Fern-like machines, with metallic fronds that bleed lubricating oil instead of sap, intertwine with fleshy, parasitic vines that grow in soft, pulsating loops. The fauna are a fusion of biology and repurposed tech: multi-limbed scavengers, resembling crabs plated in rusted steel, skitter over the wet rocks, their antennae crackling with blue-white arcs as they hunt for pockets of geothermal charge.

Scattered amidst this chaotic growth are the "Markers." These are all that remain of the civilization that once governed this madness. They are not cities, but solitary, monolithic pylons—towers of black glass or corroded copper—that jut from the marsh like needles. They still hum. Deep within them, secondary turbines spin with a low-frequency roar, pumping coolant into the marsh and exhaling clouds of steam that thicken the dark. 

The physics here are subtle but wrong. Water does not always flow downward; sometimes, responding to the magnetic pull of the deep-buried industrial veins, it pools into floating spheres, shimmering in the gloom like suspended, liquid lanterns. Gravity seems to flex, and the sound of the vegetation is not the rustle of leaves, but a metallic clicking—a billion tiny relays and switches engaging as the plants react to the temperature shifts.

There is no rest here. The environment is always in motion, a relentless, churning mill of growth, decomposition, and electrical discharge. The rain falls in sheets of warm, greasy water, scrubbing the soot from the giant, metallic ferns, while somewhere far in the distance, a forgotten reactor vents a plume of pressurized steam that screams louder than any beast. It is a world that has outlived its masters, a self-sustaining engine of life and decay that has folded into the landscape, proving that even in the absolute dark, industry and biology can weave together into a singular, suffocating tapestry.
