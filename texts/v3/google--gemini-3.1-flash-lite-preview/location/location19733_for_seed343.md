The ascent does not end in a peak, but in a suspension. You are standing upon the crown of a colossal, sprawling organism—a mountain range composed entirely of calcified, moss-suffused lignin that has been weeping and growing for eons. At this extreme elevation, the air is thin but heavy, saturated with the frantic, percussive respiration of a billion leaf-pores.

The terrain here is not rock, but a sprawling, rugose architecture of ancient, gnarled cellulose. Massive, horizontal buttresses—some the size of city streets—bridge vast, hollow abysses where the "ground" has long since rotted away or been consumed by the next iteration of growth. The erosion isn't the carving of wind on stone, but the slow, patient collapse of dead plant matter into nutrient-rich silt trapped within the living lattice. It is a landscape that has settled into its final, primordial form, where the distinction between mountain and forest has been erased.

The sound is the first thing that strikes you: a deafening, rhythmic oscillation. It is the sound of the ecosystem in motion—a constant, thrumming drone created by the movement of massive, leathery leaves the size of hangars, rasping against one another, coupled with the discordant, multi-tonal symphony of a fauna so dense it defines the atmosphere. You cannot see the ground because the air between these gargantuan stalks is filled with the bioluminescent drift of winged, insectoid things and the frantic chattering of arboreal creatures that occupy every cubic centimeter of the canopy.

Water is an intimate, intermittent guest. It does not pool in lakes; it clings. Massive, translucent droplets—some several feet across—hang from the undersides of drooping, sponge-like blossoms, trembling before they fall into the, dark, humid depths below. When the occasional warm rain sweeps through, it is less a shower and more a collective mist, a condensation that turns the world into a slick, glistening emerald-gold, reflecting the normal, bright light of the suns above.

Between the spans of vegetation, you find the remnants of the Built. These are not cities, but scattered, fragile artifacts: ossified skeletons of wooden dwellings, long since abandoned, wrapped irrevocably in the slow, tightening grip of parasitic vines. There is no metal here, only the soft, dark wood, woven cordage, and the faint, hand-carved markings of a people who once traversed these heights. These marks speak of a primitive, manual craft—a dialogue between human hands and the living substrate—but they have been swallowed. The structures are merely heartbeats in the slow, vegetative tempo of the place.

The density of the landscape is deceptive; while there is "nothing" in the great empty canyons between the primary stalks, that void is filled with the chaotic, overwhelming presence of the fauna. Everything is alive, and everything is eating or being eaten. It is an evolutionary pressure cooker, settled into a state of violent, ancient balance. To stand here is to feel the vertigo of the altitude and the profound, crushing weight of a world that has replaced geology with biology. You are not standing on a mountain; you are standing in a long, slow, living breath that has been held for ten thousand years.
