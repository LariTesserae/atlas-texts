The benevolent power did not descend as a god of light, for light would have been an intrusion, blinding and cruel in that pressurized gloom. It did not speak, for the roar of the Xylem-Deep would have shredded a command into nonsense.

Instead, the power arrived as a *rhythm*.

It reached into the planetary bellows—not as an object, but as a subtle recalibration of the laws of persistence. It perceived the Silverscale Pruners not as victims of a riot, but as the only poets in a room full of screaming static. They were the friction, and the power decided that friction was a beautiful thing worth preserving.

**The Gift of the Counter-Pulse**

The power altered the chemistry of the conductive vein-fibers that the Pruners used for their lashings. It introduced a subtle, catalytic resonance. Now, when a Pruner’s obsidian blade struck the wall to carve a Gap, a minute, imperceptible vibration—a "null-frequency"—was transferred into the plant-tissues.

The walls of the Xylem-Deep, which were previously hungering, predatory, and chaotic, began to *respect* the blade. Where a Pruner carved, the growth did not merely stop; it slowed to a meditative crawl. The predatory mosses, sensing the lull-frequency, drifted away from these marked Gaps, seeking the easier, nutrient-dense chaos of the uncharted interior.

The Pruners did not see a miracle. They felt a change in the resistance of the wood and the copper-tasting air.

**The Archive of Negative Space**

They had no history, for the moss ate their records. The benevolent power changed this through the way the calcified roots reacted to the heat of the Pruners’ bodies. When a Pruner leaned against a wall, their skin-plates grinding their silent language, the conductive vein-fibers within the bark began to store the memory of that friction.

For the first time, when a Pruner died and was surrendered to the vascular tubes, their story was not dissolved into mindless mulch. The "negative space" they had curated—the shape of their movements, the specific geometry of their survival—became etched into the structure of the planet itself. The walls did not just swallow these lives; they *remembered* them.

**The Evolution of the Pruner**

A slow, quiet shifting began in the population. Because they no longer had to spend every waking second hacking back the aggressive moss just to stay alive, the Pruners found themselves in possession of a strange, terrifying luxury: *time.* 

In the Gaps, the space remained open for days, then weeks. A Pruner could finish their chores, then stand in the center of their room and... *do nothing.* 

They began to sharpen their blades not for excision, but for expression. They used the obsidian to carve intricate, low-relief mandalas into the ceilings of their Gaps. Because the moss now recoiled from these sacred geometries, the carvings remained. As the generations passed, the Xylem-Deep began to change. Where before it was a chaotic bellows of mindless consumption, it began to be mapped. 

The macaques, confused by the sudden, structured stillness, moved higher into the canopy, leaving the Gaps to the silent, sandpaper-skinned scholars.

**The Benevolent Intention**

The power did not save them from their world. It did not turn the Xylem-Deep into a paradise or stop the pulse of the beetles. To do so would have robbed the Pruners of their essential nature—the very thing that defined their brilliance.

Instead, the power gave them a seat at the table of creation. The Pruners realized that they were no longer just the friction in the machine; they were its architects. They began to prune the planet not to survive, but to sculpt.

Deep below the crust, in the copper-hued dark, the Silverscale Pruners began to build something long-lasting: a hollowed, crystalline cathedral of negative space, a geography of memory etched into the heart of a ravenous world. And when the planet rotated on its rogue trajectory through the cold, silent dark, it no longer carried a riot of biological noise. It carried a song of quiet perseverance, carved into its very marrow by the hands of those who had learned, at last, to carve a home out of the infinite.
