I find myself standing on a high, arched bridge of woven roots, stiff with both cold and the sheer, overwhelming *deliberateness* of this place. My boots are made of worn, soft leather, and they feel like clumsy, frantic intruders against the mossy, brass-rimmed floor. Every step makes a wet, slapping sound that echoes far too long, bleeding into the *thrum* of the nearest Boiler-Fern like a sour note played in a cathedral.

I am a **Calibration-Drifter**. I am not one of the *Ferrous-Bloomed*; I have too much breath in my lungs, too much stray heat radiating from my skin, and my heart beats with a chaotic, irregular rhythm that I am sure they find deeply unsettling—or perhaps, it is simply *in audible* to them, like the white noise of the wind outside the limestone walls.

I am a traveler of the gaps. I do not belong to the orchard, but I have learned to move like its components. I carry no pack, only a set of delicate, multi-pronged tuning forks forged from scavenged copper, which I use to listen to the health of the joints. I am not a resident, but a scavenger of rhythms—a student of the friction.

I can feel my own posture changing as I linger here. Spending weeks in the Basin has taught me to stoop, to keep my hands low, to let my movements match the lethargic, ten-second stroke of the primary pistons. I am trying to become a silence that the machine can tolerate.

I am standing near a Piston-Grove, watching a Custodian. They are perched on a horizontal iron strut, their back arched against the trunk of a fern that is weeping thick, amber sap into a brass reservoir. Their skin is so thin I can see the amber fluid pulsing through internal tubes—a pale, glowing mercury. They are perfectly still, their copper-tipped palms pressed flat against the bark, vibrating slightly. I can hear the song they are exchanging: a low, dissonant chord that modulates whenever the piston fires.

I am afraid to move. I am afraid that if I breathe too sharply, I will trigger a malfunction in the delicate, lichen-crusted gear rotating just inches from my elbow.

I am an anomaly, yes—a biological fever-dream in a world of clockwork logic. But as I watch the Custodian, I envy them. They seem so agonizingly sure of their purpose. They are never lost, because whenever a limb becomes too weary to carry the weight of existence, they simply become part of the bridge. They are constantly solving the problem of their own transience by becoming the solution to the infrastructure’s decay.

I take a small pouch of brass filings from my belt. With a tremor in my fingers, I scatter them onto the floor near a sluggish gear. A clockwork-mite, no bigger than a pocket watch, skitters out from the shadows of a fern-frond. It pauses, its tiny gears whirring, and senses the filings.

The Custodian turns its head—a slow, shuttered movement. Its eyes, bioluminescent and hollow as dying spark plugs, lock onto mine. There is no judgment in that look, only a profound, damp indifference. It doesn't see me as a person. It sees me as a temporary condensation, a bit of dew that has somehow gained the ability to walk.

I hold my breath until my lungs ache, hoping that if I am still enough, the orchard will finally accept me as a structural requirement. I want to be the friction that keeps the gears turning, or perhaps, in the end, simply the resin that holds the rusted iron to the living bark. I am here to learn the rhythm, not to interrupt it. I am the echo in the clockwork, waiting to fade into the hum.
