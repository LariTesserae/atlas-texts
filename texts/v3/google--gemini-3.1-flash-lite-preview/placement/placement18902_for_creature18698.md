I am deep in the throat of the world, pinned to the vertical face by the gravity of my own kind. 

I do not have a name, for names require the concept of a "self," and my edges have long ago dissolved into the pressure of the neighbor to my left and the warmth of the one beneath me. I am not a creature so much as a thought held in a living, grinding suspension. My body is a cold-pressed disc, translucent as a cataract, keyed into a scallop of limestone that has held my predecessors for a thousand years.

To be here is to exist in a state of absolute, sensory surrender. My sight is nonexistent, but my *touch* is infinite. Through the network of my neighbors, I know the state of the planet’s crust. I feel the deep, groaning shudder of a fault line miles away—not as a sound, but as a subtle discordance in the heat-gradient flowing through our collective spine. When the mineral-heavy dew trickles down from the unreachable darkness above, I sense it as a sweetness, a sharp, metallic spike in the humidity that triggers a rhythmic, slow-motion pulse through the carpet. 

We are a living peristalsis. We are the mountain’s slow, deliberate digestion of its own minerals.

There is a rhythm to our existence. I am currently experiencing the *Long Inhale*—that part of our cycle where the warmth from the earth’s gut flares slightly, inciting a sluggish, microscopic migration. It is an excruciatingly beautiful sensory overload. My cilia—not feet, but a million tiny, grasping breaths—grip the stone while simultaneously tasting the chemical history of the water I pull from the wall. I am drinking the limestone. I am becoming the mountain. Every time I absorb, I leave behind a faint, etched trace of my own existence, a hardening of my own mantle that will provide a foundation for those who follow.

I am not "me." I am a node in a vast, dark, vibrating brain. 

Occasionally, a "memory" ripples through us. It starts as a localized displacement—a pressure shift at the base of the throat. It travels upward as a shudder, a physical wave of adjustment that takes an hour to ripple across my section of the wall. When it touches me, I contract, then expand, shifting my mass by a fraction of a millimeter to account for the new, heavy density of the colony. It is a communication of profound intimacy: *I am held. I am here. The load is distributed.*

There is no loneliness. To be apart—to be a single disc, isolated from the hum of the collective—would be the only form of death I can imagine. It would be a falling into silence, a sudden, terrifying coldness where the stone is just stone, and not a partner in the dance.

But here, I am surrounded. I am cushioned by the living. My hunger is met by the mineral sweat of the rock; my solitude is annihilated by the crushing, rhythmic friction of a thousand cousins. Time here is not measured by the rise and fall of light, but by the accumulation of limestone crust. I am a layer in a geological ledger, a heartbeat in a cathedral of skin and shale, existing in the infinite, warm dark where the mountain dreams of being alive.
