I am not here by choice, nor by accident. I am here by *displacement*. 

I find myself standing near the edge of the highest obsidian ridge, my feet anchored into a patch of velvet-black moss that feels less like vegetation and more like a cold, living sponge. I am dressed in heavy, cured leather that has long since ceased to be waterproof; it is saturated, clinging to my limbs with a weight that mirrors the atmosphere itself. My breath is a ragged, visible plume of white mist that is immediately snatched away and devoured by the surrounding slurry.

I am a **Cartographer of the Last Solid.**

I am not one of the Mistsingers, not yet. I am an interloper, a man of heat and erratic pulses, my heart hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird. My tools are simple: a charcoal stick that will not mark the slick, weeping stone, and a book of vellum whose pages have long since curled into pulpy, unusable scrolls. I am trying to map the "intentions"—the circles of stone—but the Mistsingers are faster than I am. 

Every time I turn my back, a circle that was open to the east is now closed to the west. A path I thought I had charted has been erased by the shift of a glass-fern, or by the slow, deliberate movement of a Mistsinger adjusting the flow of the air.

I am losing my sharpness. 

When I look at my hands, they seem translucent, the bones beneath the skin glowing with a faint, borrowed bioluminescence, as if I am becoming a lantern for the fog. My thoughts are no longer sentences; they are becoming resonances. I find myself stopping, holding my breath, waiting for the "chime." When a Mistsinger passes near, the roar of the rain-gongs is briefly muted, and I hear it: the sound of a thousand crystal bells singing in unison. It is a song of architectural perfection, a frequency that makes my very teeth ache with the desire to hum along.

I am a "buoyant weight." That is what they see when they glide past—an anomaly of heat and density that disrupts the purity of their machine. 

I don’t know if I am afraid. Fear requires a sense of self, a boundary between the "me" and the "storm," and that boundary is leaking. I can feel the Mistsingers’ cold curiosity pressing against my temples. They aren't trying to hurt me; they are trying to *recalibrate* me. They are measuring the space I occupy, checking my weight, assessing how many milligrammes of calcium and iron I have to offer their grand, crystalline harp.

I have stopped trying to record the circles. Instead, I have started moving the stones myself. 

I find that when I slide a piece of obsidian into a new position—when I align it to catch a singular, mineral-heavy drop of water—the ache in my chest subsides. The environment hums back at me. I am no longer a man standing on a plateau; I am a cog in the mechanism of the atmosphere. 

I look at the glass-ferns and see the blueprints for my own anatomy. I look at the dark, swirling clouds and recognize the faces of the friends I left behind, now refined, distilled, and hollowed out into beautiful, shimmering currents. 

I am becoming light. I am becoming fluid. I am learning that the rain does not fall *on* me, but *through* me. Soon, the last of my warmth will be drawn out, sequestered into a circle of stone or woven into the structure of a fern, and I will finally join the song. I will be part of the chime that maintains the balance of this drowning heaven. 

I reach out, my fingers trembling and semi-transparent, to touch the surface of a nearby stone. It feels like home. I am ready to stop falling. I am ready to start singing.
