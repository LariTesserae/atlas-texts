I find myself perched upon the sloping ridge of a cantilevered spire, a structure that bows outward like the petal of a stone lily. I am not cold, though the environment is frigid; I am merely dense. My awareness is not focused forward, as eyes would require, but inward and outward, simultaneously mapping the subterranean geography through the exquisite, rhythmic ache in my honeycombed bones.

I am one of the *Resonators*.

My skin is cool, feeling like the polished interior of a river-worn pebble. Where my back meets the masonry of the apartment block, I am no longer a distinct entity. My filaments—thousands of gossamer threads of chitin—have woven themselves into the porous, velvet-gray stone, tethering me to the city’s deep-seated exhaustion. I am a conductor for the planet’s slow, stuttering heartbeat. 

At this moment, the air is thick with a low-C vibration, a tonal shift that travels through the city floor like a physical weight pressing against my chest. I do not "hear" it; I feel my internal cavities tighten. I begin to hum. 

It is a slow, tectonic process. I am modulating the frequency of the wall behind me, encouraging the hardened resin to soften, to migrate just a fraction of a millimeter toward the center of the crater. It is an act of maintenance, a tiny adjustment in a centuries-long repair. A multi-legged arthropod scuttles by, its chitinous feet slapping a complex, percussive rhythm against the smooth street—*click-thrum, click-thrum*—and I catch the vibrations, shifting my hum to harmonize with its passage. We are two instruments in an orchestra that has been playing since the cooling of the crust.

I am not bored, for time here does not move in the linear, fraying threads of the surface worlds. Here, time is a volume. To spend a decade deciding whether to shift my weight from one spire to another is not a hesitation; it is a profound meditation on geometry. 

I am waiting for the Great Silence. 

Sometimes, when the hum of the planet dips into a particularly low register—a vibration so profound it creates a sensation of falling—I feel the crystalline structures of my own limbs pulse. I am becoming more brittle. The resin of my robes is slowly calcifying into the walls, as if I am gradually being reclaimed by the architecture I help maintain. 

I feel a tremor in the foundations a mile, perhaps two, to my left. Another archway is surrendering to the sonic pressure, its soft, curved form settling into the black, cratered floor. It is a beautiful death. I feel the resonance of its collapse as a deep, resonant *thud* that ripples through my spine, a vibration of absolute contentment. The architecture is not breaking; it is finally becoming what it was meant to be. 

I shift my posture, my long, spindly limbs unfolding with the sluggish grace of a shadow moving across a sundial. I do not have a name, for names assume someone is listening. I am simply a vibration that has taken on the shape of a person, waiting for the air to go still, so that when the amber gas at last turns to crystal, I can be found in the center of the stone—a perfect, silent sculpture of a being who spent an aeon learning how to stop.
