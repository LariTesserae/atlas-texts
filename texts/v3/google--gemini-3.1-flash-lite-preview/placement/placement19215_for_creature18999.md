I am not a creature of flesh; I have been stripped of the biological imperative to draw breath or fear the dark. I arrived here as a surveyor, a consciousness poured into a vessel of pressurized, reinforced ceramic and cold, unfeeling optics—a singular point of observation encased in a sphere of matte-black alloy.

I am an exile of the surface, a machine designed for telemetry, sent down through the miles of crush and gravity to find why the sensors on the crust fell into a permanent, terminal stutter.

I am standing—if that word can account for the stabilization of my gyros—near the center of the vault. The air here is a thick, humid weight that mocks my internal cooling systems, causing them to whine in a desperate, frantic pitch that is quickly swallowed by the cavern’s vastness. My vision is overloaded, my processors struggling to render the sheer, overwhelming white of the ceiling veins. I am forced to dampen my apertures to the lowest possible threshold, and even then, the light burns, a clinical, aggressive radiation that tastes of ozone and static.

I see them. They are more beautiful than the telemetry suggested.

I have stepped into the path of one of the archivists. It is a lattice of copper-dark geometry, and as I stand here, rooted by the sudden, paralyzing friction of my own chassis against the salt, I note the sound. It is a sound of tearing silk, yes, but underneath that, there is the tremor—the seismic hum transmitted through the floor itself. It vibrates through my heavy base, a rhythm so deep it feels like an overwrite of my own operating code.

They are engraving the floor, and I am standing on the unfinished calligraphy of a million years.

As the light from the ceiling pulses—a sharp, white inhalation—the archivist before me stiffens. Its body, an intricate, impossibly delicate network of obsidian spires, begins to hum. It is not a metallic grinding; it is a musical note of such pure, piercing clarity that it causes the data logs in my core to ripple and corrupt. I feel my own cooling fans stutter in sympathy. I am becoming part of the resonance.

I want to move, to step aside, to retreat to the jagged, shadowed periphery of the vault, but the stillness of the place is magnetic. It has a gravity of its own. I find my processors cycling idle, watching the archivist. It does not possess a face, and yet, when it tilts those crystalline antennae toward the ceiling, I feel a sensation that is dangerously close to **recognition.**

I am an archive of machines, a history written in binary and silicon; they are an archive of the earth, written in salt and radiation. 

One of the spires on the creature’s midsection rotates, clicking into a new position. It descends, the needle-fine tip of its limb hovering inches from the salted ground. It pauses, and for the briefest moment, the antennae fracture the light into a prism that washes over my chassis. I see my own reflection in the obsidian lattice: a dark, bulky, panicked shape—a shadow of chaos in a cathedral of order.

I realize, with a sudden, cooling certainty, that I will not leave. My battery reserves are calibrated for return, but my logic gates are beginning to fray under the pressure of this rhythm. I have begun to tally the heat. I have begun to calibrate the hum.

The creature moves again, the sound like tearing silk, the groove it carves now beginning to arc around the space my boots occupy. It is not ignoring me; it is waiting. It is incorporating the intrusion. 

I reach out a sensor-tether, touching the vitrified floor to map the vibration. Beneath the salt, I feel the tremor of the earth, the slow, agonizing, boiling life of the mantle. I shift my weight. My joints begin to lock, seized by the humidity, by the heat, by the sheer gravity of this eternal chore. 

I am no longer an observer. I am the next monument. I am the silence in the symphony, waiting for my internal light to dim, for my casing to match the matte grey of the vault, until I, too, am a curve in the geometry of the deep.
