I am found on the Third Terrace, where the ridge curves inward like the palm of a giant’s hand, creating a pocket of relative stillness protected from the worst of the updrafts. My station is precarious—a sliver of stone no wider than a doorway, suspended over a three-thousand-foot drop that hides in a perpetual violet gloom.

I am an *Earsmith*. 

My name is not spoken, for the wind here is a scavenger of syllables, but in the tactile percussion of our people, I am recognized by the rhythmic, three-fold strike of a copper mallet against the hollow of a wrist—the signal that the chime-nets are failing.

I am hunched over a collection of perforated ceramic vessels, the "fog-bells" that keep the city’s music and, by extension, our moisture, in check. My fingers are knotted and grey-white, the color of wet river silt. I do not feel the cold anymore; the cold is simply the baseline, the texture of the reality I inhabit. My apron is stiff with half-frozen spray, weighted with pouches of obsidian shards and lengths of cured *krell* gut used for lashing the frames.

I am not merely a crafter; I am a listener. I press my cheek against the vibrating schist of the cliff face, feeling the tectonic grind—the deep, oceanic groan of the mountain as it continues its agonizing crawl toward the stars. When the percussion of the falling sleet changes pitch, I know which vessel is cracked, which reed-lattice has bowed under the weight of the ice. 

I do not look at the abyss. I do not look at the pale, thinned sky. My world is focused entirely on the six-foot radius of my ledge. 

Beside me, a *krell* shifts its weight, its massive, shaggy flank pressing against the rock, causing my tools to rattle. I do not startle. I simply reach out, pressing my palm flat against the beast’s coarse, frozen wool—a greeting, a stabilizer, a tether. Through it, I feel the massive, slow thrum of its heart, beating in sync with the mountain itself. 

The child tethered to the iron-root ring near my workbench watches me with eyes like polished flint. He does not ask for work; he watches the way I tune the ceramic rim of a basin, the way I flick a sliver of sharpened bone against the lip of the vessel until the tone matches the hum of the wind. 

Soon, the blue-vaulted twilight will bleed into the black. I will tuck my tools into the crevice behind my head, curl into my bundle of *krell*-fleece, and wait for the ice to seal me into the wall. I am not lonely. Down in the belly of the mountain, there are ten thousand others—each of us a node in a humming, shivering web, keeping the music of our persistence alive against a world that is still trying to decide if it wants us here. 

I am a note in the song of the Ouros. And as long as I can feel the mountain teeth grinding beneath my heels, I know that I am precisely where I am meant to be.
