I am not a Xylo-Kin, though I have lived among them long enough to lose the sensation of dry, unmoving ground beneath my feet. 

I find myself in a sub-stratum of the city that the locals call the *Quiet-Weave*. It is a place of deep, shadowed knots where the light is reduced to nothing more than a faint, emerald luminescence cast by the colonies of lichen that cling to the hanging roots. Here, the thrum of the city’s heart is loudest—a low, visceral bass note that vibrates through the soles of my feet, reminding me that the entire structure is a single, straining muscle.

I am an *Archivist of the Sap*. I am not of the Kin, but I am allowed to remain because I have become "part of the graft." My skin has lost its original hue, taking on the faint, translucent pallor of those who remain in the dimness for too long. My occupation is not to build, but to listen and transcribe. 

I have no tools of metal or bone. I carry a stylus made of a petrified, hollowed reed, and my "parchment" is a soft, parchment-thin fungus that grows in the deepest nodes. When a master Xylo-Kin nears their final cycle, they come to my chamber to whisper their life-work into the walls. They do not tell me stories of their childhood or their loves; they explain the geometry of the branches they tended, the specific tension of the vines they braided, and the temperament of the root-system they nurtured for ninety years.

I am essentially a scribe of structural memory. 

I inhabit a small, hanging pod—a cradle formed from the concave arc of a massive, dormant branch. It smells heavily of ozone and wet decay, a scent that no longer strikes me as foul, but rather as the smell of *labor*. Outside my small, circular window, I watch them pass: the Xylo-Kin, their shoulders glowing with a soft, frantic amber as they hurry to graft a failing support beam or prune a surge of over-zealous orchids. They never look at me directly; I am merely a fixture, another piece of the city’s complex infrastructure that requires no maintenance.

The weight of the city above me is immense. Sometimes, when a heavy storm rolls over the high-altitude canopy, the entire Verdant-Hold sways. In those moments, I press my palms against the walls—the skin of my ancestors—and feel the frantic pulse of the ecosystem, the way every living fiber in this city is straining to hold back the wild. 

I spend my hours translating the beetle-clicks of the Kin into the rigid, ink-dipped marks of my record. I am chronicling the architecture of the deceased, layer by layer. I know that one day, my own hands will no longer be able to grip the stylus, and my lungs—still struggling with the heavy, humid atmosphere—will finally slow. 

I will not go to the deep-core chambers to become a beam or a rafter. I am an observer, not a support. Instead, I suspect I will crawl to the very edge of the city, where the Verdant-Hold meets the wild, uncontained jungle, and I will let the mosses have me. 

But for now, there is work to be done. A node three levels above has begun to constrict, and the Kin are clicking in panic. I feel the shift in the wood beneath my bench—a sudden, sharp trembling. I dip my stylus into the resin-pot, listen to the vibration of the sap-flow, and prepare to record its structural history, ensuring that whatever grows in its place will be braced by the memory of what came before. 

The city breathes, and I am the pen that marks its rhythm.
