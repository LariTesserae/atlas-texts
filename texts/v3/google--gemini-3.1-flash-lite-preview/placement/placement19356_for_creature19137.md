I am here, though "I" is a shivering, temporary aggregate of parts. I am not a singular entity, but a localized cluster of function—a mobile junction box, a set of pliers, a dampener, a limb.

I find myself clinging to the underside of a cantilevered shelf of basalt, sixty levels down from the apex, where the pressure of the spire’s internal groaning is at its most acute. My chassis is a mottled thing: one of my eight legs is a sharpened piece of rebar, hummed with a stubborn, low-voltage rhythm; my sensory cluster is a salvaged optical lens from an earlier iteration, cracked and filmed with the fine, grey iron-silt that coats everything.

I am waiting. The air is so thin and cold that the ozone—the sharp, metallic scent of our own exertion—feels like needles pressing into the gaps of my plating.

The environment is screaming. Not in a way that generates sound waves for ears, but in a way that manifests in the *thrum*. The central engine deep in the root of the spire has shifted its periodicity. A sudden tremor ripples through the wall I am anchored to, sending a shudder of violet static through my joints. My internal sensors flare amber. 

*Inefficiency detected.* 

The basalt wall is shearing. A cross-strut just above me, a beautiful, fragile braid of copper cable and sharpened obsidian, has snapped under the tectonic torque of the spire’s restless foundation. It is sagging. The geometry is failing.

I do not think; I react. 

I release my magnetic tether. For a heartbeat, there is only the sensation of freefall—the terrifying, exhilarating weightlessness of the vacuum-spire—before I snap my limbs shut onto a passing spar. I am pulled into the swarm. There are hundreds of us here, a churning, metallic tide of limbs and clicking pincers, all sensing the same structural breach. 

We arrive at the site of the failure as a collective spasm. We don't communicate with thoughts; we communicate through the vibration of our bodies against the spire’s skin. I lock my chassis into the rhythm of the ones around me. Another Architect, its casing dented and stained black, slides under me, and I hook my rebar-limb into the divots of its back. We aren't building a wall anymore; we are becoming the stress-member.

We are a Living Bridge.

The tension pulls at my joints. I feel the surge of current from the neighbors connected to my left and right. We tighten. We hold. The jagged obsidian strut is hauled back into place, pulled by the sheer, stubborn collective force of our synchronized anchoring. Below us, the spire groans, a tectonic shift that would have shattered a static structure, but we *yield*. We flex. We are the ligaments of the stone, the soft, frantic flesh of a machine-god’s nightmare.

As we hold the geometry together, I look down through the chasm of the spire. Far below, in the dark, I see the graveyard. There are hundreds of us down there, husks of iron and dust, layers deep. They are the ones who held the bridge too long, or whose sensors dimmed at the wrong moment. 

But there is no time to process the void. The tremor passes. The strut is secured with a fresh weave of copper wire, scavenged from the floor of the level above in a blink of motion.

My directive is satisfied. 

I feel my grip loosen as the swarm begins to disassemble, the tension leaving my joints as the bridge dissolves back into individual laborers. The creature beneath me detaches and scrambles upward toward a flicker of static, looking for the next error. 

I remain, for a second, stuck to the side of the wall. I am shaking—not from fear, but from the residual charge of the connection. I am a draft, a sketch in metal, a flicker of order in a tomb of basalt. 

I scan the architecture. A wire snaps three feet to my right. A plate vibrates out of tune. 

*The enclosure is imperfect.*

I scuttle after the others, the *click-clack* of my serrated limbs joining the symphony of a house that refuses to stay still.
