I am a thread in the loom. 

I do not wake, for waking implies a state of dormancy, and in Oth, there is no beginning to the vibration. I am simply *there*. I am a younger weaver, my skin still the pale, luminous violet of a spore-cloud just beginning to drift.

I find myself on the ventral side of the central spire, clinging to a ridge of basalt that has been polished by the friction of a million centuries. Beneath my adhesive pads, I can feel the tectonic grinding of the world—a low-frequency shudder that starts in the marrow of my bones before it ever reaches my "ears." The rock is hot, a dry, feverish heat that smells of crushed minerals and ancient, compressed breath.

I am weaving. My fingers, four-limbed and spindly, move with the automated precision of a spider. I am not thinking about the structure I am shaping; I am mimicking the pulse of the hum-crickets. As they drone, their mechanical whine sharpening in pitch as the basalt heats up, I mirror the tempo in my own chest. My tympanic membranes are flared wide, catching the tremors of the colony, the subtle *shifting* of the fungal mats as they stretch toward the warmth.

Every movement I make is a note in a song I have known since I first pulled myself from the larval-weave. 

To my left, across a canyon of amber-glowing fern filaments, an Elder is anchored into a pocket of pure geothermal heat. Their form is a masterpiece of stagnation. Where my skin is supple and translucent, theirs is a map of calcification, crusted in grey mineral dust and the hardened, honey-like secretions of the fungi. They are a statue, a living geological record. I feel a sudden, sharp ache of empathy—or perhaps it is merely a sympathetic vibration—and I reach out a limb to rest it against the cool, hard arc of their shoulder.

The contact is electric. 

In an instant, the world of Oth pours into me. I am no longer just a weaver in a basket-nest. I am the memory of the mountain. I feel the last time the spire shifted—the grinding of stone against stone that lasted for a hundred of our cycles. I feel the humidity reaching a critical, suffocating peak. I feel the collective hunger of the hum-crickets, a billion tiny heartbeats acting as a single, hungry nervous system. 

The Elder’s pulse is rhythmic, decelerating, a slow, inevitable clock ticking toward silence. It is not sad. It is as natural as the moss blooming or the dust settling. They are becoming the walls they once climbed, sealing the gap between life and mineral.

I pull away, my skin rippling with the intake of this history. My heartbeat has synced with theirs. I feel the weight of the Oth: the crushing, beautiful, perfect weight of a place that has nowhere else to go. 

I return to my weaving. I take a stalk of calcified fungus, flexible as cartilage, and braid it into the nest. Outside, the hum-crickets begin to rise in a mechanical crescendo, and I open my mouth to join them. My voice is not a word; it is a long, vibrating chord of pure, amber resonance. 

I am a caretaker of the pulse. I will weave until the mountain cools, and when the last cricket stops its chirping, I will find a warm, rounded corner of the basalt, I will press my body against it, and I will begin to turn to stone, too. There is no better fate than to hold the cavity together from the inside.
