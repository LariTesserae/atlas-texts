I am kneeling on a driftwood slat that feels soft, almost spongy, beneath my calloused shins. My toes are curled around the edge, not for balance—for balance is a concept for those who walk upright—but to sense the vibrations traveling through the lattice of the bridge. 

I am a Silt-Wright. My name, if it could be voiced, would be the frequency of a single, heavy drop hitting a flat stone—a low, rhythmic thud that rings in the marrow rather than the ear.

My skin is the color of the weeping schist, a matte, bruised gray that catches the milky light. It is cold, always, but it is a thick cold, like the water that clings to the underside of the cliff. My palms, broad and spatulate, are currently pressed against a fissure in the rock wall. I can feel the seepage through my skin; it is oily, rich with minerals, tasting of the deep earth’s oldest salts. 

I am not "fixing" this wall in the way a builder of the dry-lands might. I am *persuading* it. 

I lean my weight into the schist, exhaling a subsonic hum—a low, oscillating *vrrrr* that resonates through my sternum and travels into the stone. I am feeding the weeping. I am telling the rock that we are still here, that the damp must continue, that the mortar must not crumble. Beneath my fingers, I feel the wet grit shift. It is a slow, liquid movement, like muscles twitching under a heavy blanket. The stone responds. The moisture thickens, turning from a smear to a bead, then to a steady, rhythmic drip. The joint seals. The honeycomb holds for another cycle.

I shift my gaze to the moss, the pulse-fronds nearby. They are healthy, their gelatinous surfaces clear as rainwater, shivering with a light I cannot see. They are my neighbors, my clock, my kin. 

I feel a tremor in the bridge—one of my brethren is moving toward the lower basin, their feet silent, their body rippling like water poured into a narrow channel. They do not look at me. There is no need for recognition; we are the same substance. We are the friction that keeps the stone from grinding itself to dust.

I am tired, but it is a heavy, structural tiredness. It is the exhaustion of being a rivet in a machine that is perpetually falling apart. I look down at my own hands, seeing the fine network of veins beneath my translucent skin, pulsing in time with the dripping cliffs. 

The air is thickening. The ozone smell is sharp—a sign that the condensation will hold. I will stay here for a while longer, pressing my warmth into the rock, ensuring that the moisture remains, that the world stays soft, that the breath of the earth does not stop. 

I am not afraid of the drying. I have seen the piles of grey rubble at the base of the cliffs where the others have finished their work and let go. One day, I too will cease my humming. I will tuck myself into the alcove, fit my body into the curve of the schist, and let the dew dry from my skin. I will become part of the mortar. I will become the very thing I am currently holding together. 

For now, the drip is steady. The world is wet. I am here.
