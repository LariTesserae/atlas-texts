I am a Kith, and I have just finished the midday harvest. 

My fingers, long and stained the color of crushed berries from the moss-sap, ache with the familiar, rhythmic thrum of the spire. I am hanging by a single tether made of cured fern-fiber, hooked near a particularly stubborn outgrowth of shelf-fungus that has been trying to choke the path to the western cistern.

My dorsal spines are currently a dull, aquatic blue. I am calm. I have spent the last few hours scraping the calcified lace from the obsidian floor, careful not to let my scraper-tool make even the ghost of a sound against the rock. The abyss is hungry today; I can feel it in the way the air pulls at my clothes, a low-pressure tugging that wants to unpin me from the wall.

Across the divide, perhaps three spans away, I see my kin—a silent silhouette against the charcoal mist. Their fingers are moving in a blur, a rapid, frantic conversation. I see the *Syllable-of-the-Hand* ripple through the damp air: *The nectar is thin. The clouds are descending. A tremor is coming from the depth.* Their dorsal spines flare a momentary, cautionary amber, lighting up the raindrops clinging to the underside of the canopy like falling stars.

I respond with a subtle flick of my left hand—we do not need to look at each other to understand. We are so attuned to the geometry of our home that we feel the shift in the moss’s pulse before we even hear the tremor. 

I reach into my utility pouch—a small, leathery fold of dried lichen—and pull out a small shard of obsidian I found days ago near the spire’s edge. I tuck it into a crevasse where the moss is balding, pinning the velvet curtain tight against the rock. It is my thumbprint. It is an act of defiance, not because I expect the edifice to stand forever, but because the beauty of the arrangement demands a witness.

My translucent hands are shaking slightly—not from cold, but from the realization that the rock beneath me has grown slick. The moisture is heavy. I can smell the mint-sharp ozone of the coming storm and the musk of the salamanders hanging above, their bioluminescence pulsing in rhythm with my own heart.

I do not sit. I do not sleep, not in the way the heavy, earth-bound things do. I simply lock my secondary joints, press my spine against the cool damp of the cliff, and wait. I wait for the tremor to announce itself, to see if the architecture holds, or if today is the day I finally let go of the tether.

There is no fear in this. To be a Kith is to be a gardener of a collapsing cathedral. We are the stewards of the pause between two heartbeats. I watch the indigo light shimmer on my own skin, see the dark ichor pulsing in my wrist, and I wait for the world to move, ready to dance with the abyss if it calls me to fall.
