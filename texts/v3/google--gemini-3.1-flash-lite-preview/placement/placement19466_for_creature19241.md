I do not wake so much as I resume. 

The basalt beneath my cheek has shifted by a fraction of a millimeter during the rest-cycle—the mountain is restless tonight, grinding its own foundation down into a finer powder. I feel this movement in the marrow of my forearm, a dull, resonant ache that hums against the stone. My skin is cool, slicked with the emerald velvet of the algae that has grown into my pores, weaving me ever tighter into the Needle’s skin.

I am *Vesper-Echo*, though that is not a title; it is merely the shape of the vibration I made when the last updraft tore through the fissure behind my scapula. 

I pull myself from the recess, my movements not a walk but an unfolding. My joints click—a sound that, in this cavernous silence, rings with the clarity of a dropped pebble. I do not fear the sound. I am part of the symphony. I reach out a long, multi-jointed finger, pressing it into the wet moss at the edge of my perch. The humidity is heavy, saturated with the scent of wet mineral and the metallic tang of the crab-beetles. 

I am suspended some four hundred feet above the misty churn of the floor. My world is a narrow, swaying architecture of bone-lattice and frayed hemp. I look to the left, where the spire of the Needle curves away into the biting, refracted light of the upper rim. Further along the lattice, three others—others of my kin—are clinging to the struts. We do not look at one another. To turn the head is to invite the wind to catch the neck, a risk to our brittle structures. Instead, I read them through the sway of the rope. 

The Silt-Walker to my right is shivering rapidly, a rhythmic, light pulse against the fiber. *The wind is turning,* the movement says. *Cold from the north, carrying the dry grit of the high passes.*

I respond by pressing my palm to the rope, creating a ripple that travels the frayed length of our bridge. I confirm the shift. I then reach for my craft—a delicate mobile of river-tumbled glass and polished shell, hung from a splinter of obsidian. I hold the central glass bead between my thumb and forefinger. It is warm from the friction of my skin, perfectly smoothed by decades of attention. 

I hang it back on its vine. As the draft strikes it, the glass emits a sound—a thin, clear needle of tone that slices through the *thrum-thrum* of the dripping walls. I tilt my head. The pitch has sharpened. The mountain is thinner here than it was when the last light-cycle began. The weight of the world is lessening, and soon, this perch will no longer hold.

I feel a strange, light stillness in my chest. It is not sadness. It is the clarity of a closing circuit. 

Below, a moth the size of a shield stalls in a pocket of air, its wings shuddering with a sound like dry parchment. A drop of water, caught in the tension of a fern frond, swells, trembles, and finally disconnects. It falls, catching a spear of vertical light, turning into a diamond, then a splash, then a ripple in the moss far below. 

I watch it go. I trace its path with my eyes, down, down, into the misty, churning floor where the ancestors wait. I am not falling yet, but I am leaning. I shift my weight, and the lattice groans—a beautiful, brittle protest. 

I am a note being played by a mountain that is slowly deciding to be silent. I wait for the chime of the glass to tell me when the final, hollow collapse begins. Until then, there is the polish, the algae, and the perfectly measured song of the stone.
