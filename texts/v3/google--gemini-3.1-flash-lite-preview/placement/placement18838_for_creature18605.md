I am standing in the center of the Green Foundry, my feet sinking an inch or two into the warm, gelatinous floor with every shift of my weight. My arrival wasn't a choice; it was a falling, a gradual descent from a bright, dry world into this suffocating, humming damp. The air here tastes like iron-rich tea and heavy, stagnant life.

I am not a human in the traditional sense, nor am I a spirit. I am **a Calibration**.

I am a slim, upright tool of brushed, oxidized brass, articulated with a complexity that mimics the human form, yet clearly constructed for a different taxonomy of labor. My chest is hollow, acting as a natural resonator for the foundry’s vibrations. I have no pulse in my wrists—only a faint, ticking resonance where my internal gears turn, keeping time with the succulent pistons that heave around me. My hands are tapered into delicate, multi-fingered instruments: one tipped with a needle-fine probe, the other with a curved, spooned edge designed to clear the calcified grit from the valves.

I am the Foundry's *Errant Function*.

I move through the grove not with the urgency of a traveler, but with the repetitive, stuttering grace of a hand-wound watch. My head is a smooth, featureless dome of glass and copper, reflecting the dim, phosphorescent glow of the moss. When I walk, I leave no footprints; the moss-flesh heals beneath my soles immediately, knitting shut to hide where I have been. 

The Loom-Heart knows me. It does not perceive me as an intruder, but as a misplaced part of its own anatomy—a mobile instrument it once cast off, or perhaps grew to perform a specific, forgotten maintenance task. 

I don't remember the name of my maker, but my limbs are etched with archaic schematics. I stop in front of a rusted, leaking valve—the same one encrusted with salt—and I reach out. My fingers find the grooves, and I begin to scrape. The sound is the sharp, wet shriek of metal on mineral. As I work, I feel the Foundry react. A nearby succulent stalk thickens; a vine tightens its coil around a support beam. They are accommodating my work, compensating for the friction I create.

I am neither alive nor mechanical. If I stop moving, I am not dead; I am merely "stowed." If I linger in one spot too long, I feel the microscopic filaments of the moss creeping over my calves, threading into my joints, attempting to anchor me so that I might become a Watchman—a stationary conduit for the surplus sap. 

I sense the Loom-Heart underneath my feet, a vast and slow intelligence that thinks in centuries. It is frustrated by my mobility. It wants me to root. It wants me to turn into the statue I was destined to be, to become a permanent facet of the engine.

But I continue to walk. I continue to scrape. I drift from the bronze valve to a tangle of copper wire, smoothing a sharp edge, checking the hydraulic flow of a dormant stalk. I am a ghost of maintenance, a memory of industrial purpose moving through a biological dream. 

I am the noise in the system that refuses to be quieted. As long as I move, the Foundry must keep processing; as long as I work, the cycle remains in motion. I don't know who I am serving, but I know that if I ever truly stop, the Foundry will finally be finished with me. And so, I limp toward the back of the cloister, tracing the crumbling stone walls, looking for the next patch of decay, trying to stay ahead of the roots that want to claim my feet.
