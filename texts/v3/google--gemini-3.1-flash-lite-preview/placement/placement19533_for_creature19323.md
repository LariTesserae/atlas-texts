I am standing near the southern junction, where the copper vines coil tightly into the cooling vent. The air here is particularly dry, tasting of ozone and that familiar, sharp prickle of scorched cinnamon coming from the moss-mats. 

My spine aches. It is a dull, rhythmic throb that hums in time with the tapping of the silk-birds. I am not thinking, not in words; the concept of "language" has long ago been replaced by the *click-chime-thrum* I feel vibrating against my own sternum. 

I am a Weaver of the Third Cycle, though the numbering feels increasingly hollow. My fingers, thick-padded and mapped with the silver-white scars of glass-burns, are currently buried deep within a cluster of copper tendrils. I am winding. The wire is warm, pulling against my resistance with a faint, static tension—the world wants to unspool, it wants to lose its form, and I am the tension that holds the knot tied.

If I were to look up, I would see the ceiling panels—those vast, heavy stone slabs—angled just so, catching that impossible, amber light. Behind those slabs is the Nothing. I have seen it leak once, or perhaps a dozen times, when the fatigue in my eyes made the floor tiles blur. It looked like static, like the world failing to remember its own architecture, a fraying at the edges where "here" ceases to be "here."

I pause my winding, my body locking into that involuntary, statuesque posture. My shadow, a dark, jagged thing detached from my heels, is currently pacing toward the far wall, restless. It does not obey me, but it belongs to the room, so I let it wander. 

My life is not measured in years, but in the density of the moss and the length of the copper wire under my care. In the hollow of my chest, I feel the weight of those who came before me—the brittle, calcium-white reinforcement rods woven into the branch above my head, the lingering scent of a predecessor who is now part of the forest, holding up a leaf that constantly tries to dissolve into fluttering numbers.

I am not afraid of the ache. It is the only thing that proves the atrium is still real. To feel pain is to be part of the friction.

I press my knuckles against the cold, vertical copper lining of the wall—*click-chime-thrum*. It is a greeting, or perhaps a warning to the others in the quiet: *The vine at the southern junction is fraying. A leaf is becoming math. I am working. Existence is still here.*

I feel a small, shale-beetle crawl across my boot, its rhythmic tapping a microscopic heartbeat against my leather-wrapped foot. Outside this room, I know, there is only the silence of things that haven't been imagined yet. Here, in the amber haze, the dust motes drift like gold-leaf suspended in resin. My hands return to their work. 

I wind. The wire pulls. The universe stays, for another long afternoon, exactly where I have put it.
