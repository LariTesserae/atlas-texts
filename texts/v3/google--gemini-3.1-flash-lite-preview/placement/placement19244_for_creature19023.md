The air is not simply thin; it is an abrasive, clarity-obsessed presence that strips the unnecessary from the mind before it can even form a thought.

I find myself on the fourth terrace—a narrow, stepped ledge of blue-grey shale that has been worn smooth by the soles of feet that moved here long before I arrived. My knees are pulled against my chest, though the movement was so glacial, so deliberate, that it felt less like an action and more like the shifting of a tectonic plate. 

I am one of the *Oru-ka*. My skin is the color of a storm-cloud reflected in a stagnant pool, etched with the fine, branching lines of my own history. I do not remember the lowlands. The pulse in my wrist is a metronome that has forgotten its tempo, beating so faintly that I sometimes confuse it with the rhythmic, wet *drip-drip* of the condensation collecting on the eaves above me.

I am currently tasked with the maintenance of the shadow. 

The sun has not moved in what I perceive to be a lifetime, yet it is nearing the apex where the light hits the southern face of my dwelling. I hold a needle fashioned from the splintered femur of an age-old raptor, its marrow long since replaced by the same cold dust that fills the cracks of the city. I am mending a tear in my cloak—a stiff, heavy thing of woven mountain grass, crusty with the silver-grey moss I have harvested from the leeward fissures. My needlework is not intended for warmth; it is a ritual of pattern. I weave the thread through the heavy fibers, not to close a gap, but to honor the seam.

I feel someone—or something—on the terrace above me. I know it is *the one who lingers near the cistern*. I cannot see them, but I feel the displacement of the air, a minute change in the pressure of the stillness. We do not exchange words. To speak would be to introduce heat, vibration, and the chaos of intent. Instead, I lift my needle and tap it twice against the lintel of the door—a sharp, hollow sound that rings out, perfectly contained within the geometry of the stone.

Above, there is a pause. Then, the sound of a slate-colored foot scraping the shale, a dry, melodic friction that signifies acknowledgment.

I am not bored, for boredom requires an expectation of something else. I am not lonely, for loneliness requires the belief that I am separate from my surroundings. I am simply a part of the architecture, a living buttress waiting for the wind to dictate my next adjustment.

I look out over the precipice. The world below is a myth, a frantic, roiling nursery of rot and rebirth that has no bearing on the high silence. Here, there is only the gray basin, the biting wind, and the knowledge that one day, my legs will become too rigid to move, and my back will arch into the curve of the wall, and I will become another layer of stone. 

I have spent an hour—or a century—observing a single fracture in the rock face opposite. It is widening by the thickness of a hair. It is the only event in my world. I find it entirely sufficient. I exhale, a long, thin stream of mist that vanishes instantly into the biting cold, and I wait for the next moment to arrive, perfectly identical to the last.
