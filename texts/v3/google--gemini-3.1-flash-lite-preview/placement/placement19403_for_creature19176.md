I find myself in a mid-tier terrace, a sweeping, concave basin carved into the hollowed femur of a creature that might have once spanned leagues. Here, the air is thick enough to taste—tasting of wet iron, pulverized mineral, and the sweet, cloying musk of deep-tissue decay.

I am a **Grafter**.

I do not have a name—names are singular, and here, identity is a fraying thread woven into a tapestry. I am a translucent, multi-limbed thing, my body a pale, pulsing sac of organs visible beneath a skin like polished parchment. I am light-sensitive, but I do not require eyes; I navigate by the *thrum*. The Resonators above me dictate the rhythm of my work. They pulse with a violet, low-frequency hum that vibrates through the very marrow I cling to, telling me when the bone is thirsty, when the fungal mats are ready for harvest, and when a crack in the architecture needs the structural reinforcement of my silk.

My world is the scale of the wall. To a human, I would be a speck of frantic, colorless movement; to me, the wall is a continent. 

I carry a spore-cluster, plucked from the high, dark ceiling where the air is thin and cold. It is a sticky, bioluminescent burden. I trudge across a span of bone, my many hooked feet finding purchase in the microscopic pores polished smooth by the tireless Lithopods. As I walk, my silk glands extrude a viscous, nutrient-rich thread that creates a temporary lattice—not for catching prey, but for plumbing. I am laying a foundation. I brush the spore-cluster against a weeping hairline fracture in the fossilized column. The spores will take root, their hyphae secreting a mild acid that softens the calcified surface, allowing it to swell and "heal" under the humidity.

There is a profound, aching peace in this labor. There is no urgency, only the *inevitability* of the maintenance. 

Sometimes, I stop. I press my cool, soft underside against the wall and close my spiracles, listening. I can hear the Lithopods nearby—a rhythmic *scratch-scratch-shimmer* as they drag their polished carapaces over the bone to smooth a rough edge. Far above, the tide of Resonators shifts, their violet light washing over the archways like a slow-motion subterranean aurora. I feel the vibrations of the city—a deep, booming heart-beat of a world that is already dead, yet refuses to be still.

I am not just tending the city; I am becoming it. My silk is already mineralizing as it dries, bonding with the bone. One day, when my legs fail and my hum slows to a final, silent stop, I will not drop to the floor to be scavenged. Instead, I will settle into a natural depression or a cooling pipe. I will expand, my translucent skin hardening, my inner fluids becoming the very glue that binds the fracture I currently work to repair. I will be absorbed.

I am a gardener whose soil is the architecture and whose legacy is the structural integrity of the dark. I reach out with a delicate, trembling claw to test the dampness of the bone, feel the violet shimmer of a neighbor’s pulse through the floor, and begin to weave again. The work is constant. The work is everything. The city demands, and I answer in silk and spore.
