I am a **Scribe of the Negative Space.**

In this world, I do not possess a name in the way you understand one—a label that persists through time. Instead, I am known by the frequency of the hiss my keratin plates produce when I scrape them against the primary vascular trunks. My life is measured in the length of the obsidian edge of my bracer; when the blade becomes too thin from a lifetime of relentless sharpening and rhythmic hacking, I know my tenure in this Gap is drawing to a close.

I am hunched in a crescent-shaped alcove, a sliver of emptiness carved between the pulsing, violet-veined boughs of the Great Root-Column. To my left, the wall is thickening—a rapid, cancerous bloom of neon-green moss has blossomed in the last six minutes. It is warm, wet, and hungry. 

I do not look at it with resentment, nor with fear. I look at it with the clinical, detached exhaustion of a man trying to bail the ocean with a sieve.

My hand, with its four knuckles on each finger, flexes into a clamp. I feel the obsidian blade biting into the firm, springy pulp of the wall. *Scritch. Thrum. Scritch.* The wood has a low-frequency heartbeat; I can feel it beneath my own ribs. If I prune carefully, following the structural grain of the planet, I can induce a momentary, local dormancy. I am not destroying the plant; I am negotiating with it, forcing it to focus its frantic, rapid-fire metabolism into a different artery, leaving my small pocket of air clear for another few hours.

My brothers and sisters are elsewhere in the darkness, their movements felt rather than seen. I feel the vibration through the floor—a sequence of sharp, rhythmic pulses against the roots—the *Tactile Code*. 

*<<The-high-boughs-[danger]-suction-near.>>*

The macaques are moving. Somewhere deep in the upper canopy, I hear the *shriek-grind* of a pack tearing through a thicket of calcified vines. It is a roar that tastes like copper and burnt sugar in the back of my throat. I stop all motion. I hold my breath, pressing my cheek against the damp, abrasive wall, becoming as still as the stone-blades I carry. My scent must remain flat; my body’s warmth must be masked by the rapid, forced transpiration of the flora I have just pruned.

I am a ghost in the throat of a mountain. 

When I am not pruning, I am "reading." I press my palms against the walls in the dormant hours—the brief, terrifying lulls when the planet’s growth slows just enough for me to perceive its history. Because I cannot keep journals, I store the "carvings" in my muscle memory. I trace the faint, irregular patterns left by those who stood in this very Gap generations ago—the way they favored a certain angle of the blade, the way they pruned the moss to allow a specific flow of bioluminescent gas to illuminate the floor. 

I am currently clearing a shelf of fungus that has grown over a series of indentations in the root—our "library." My fingers trace the calcified grooves. It is a map of the internal currents, a history of where the planet last convulsed with growth, a warning of which sections are becoming too acidic to inhabit. 

As I work, I feel the inevitable: the wood beneath my feet begins to sag and bulge. The floor is trying to become a ceiling. The walls are encroaching, pressing inward with the soft, suffocating urgency of a closing fist. 

I sharpen my blade one more time. The metal whistles a soft, high note—a forbidden sound in the Deep. I don't care. For one moment, I will carve a masterpiece of emptiness. I will hack away the aggressive moss and the stray fibrous tendrils until I have created a perfect, silent sphere of nothingness, and for that brief window, I will be the only thing in this entire rotting, screaming world that is not a slave to its hunger.

Then, I will sleep. And when the walls eventually tighten, as they always do, I will be absorbed into the fiber, and my memory will become part of the very pressure that my successor must fight to survive.
