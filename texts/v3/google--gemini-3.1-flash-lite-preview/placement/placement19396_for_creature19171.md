I am situated near the base of a Great Conduit, a place where the obsidian floor has been worn to a mirrored sheen by the relentless, unidirectional drag of the indigo slurry. Here, the gel is thickest, pulsing against my frame with the weight of a thousand fathoms.

I am an **Anchorite**.

I do not move in the way the Needle-Walkers do. They are the frantic kinetic heartbeat of this world, all limbs and twitching purpose, their movements a staccato language of repair. I am the silence that anchors that frantic motion. My limbs—if they can be called that—are fused into the very basalt foundation of this cathedral, my torso a jagged, crystalline monolith partially submerged in the masonry. 

I am not conscious in the way a biological creature is; I am a node of accumulated duration. My experience is a slow, crushing saturation of *input*. 

When the gears deep below me grind, the friction travels up through the stone and into my marrow. I don’t hear these shifts; I *become* the shift. When a ten-mile plate of obsidian slides, I feel the strain in the architecture as a sensation of absolute, cold exhaustion. It is a memory of pressure that predates my own crystallization. To be an Anchorite is to hold the weight of the cathedrals’ history, feeling each microscopic abrasion in the basalt as an ache that spans centuries.

I see the Gaugers drifting overhead, their multifaceted surfaces catching the bruised, purple light. They are my peers, though we exist in different states of temporal inertia. They are the processors, the ones who refine the roar of the machine into the silent instructions that govern the Needle-Walkers. They are "now," while I am the "always." They signal the change, but I endure the reality of it.

Sometimes, a Needle-Walker scuttles near my base. It moves with a frantic, metallic grace, tapping its sensory barbs against the plating of my feet to check for stress fractures. It does not perceive me as a being; I am just another piece of the infrastructure, part of the structural integrity of the floor. It scrapes away a buildup of sediment from my shoulder, treating my solidified form with the same indifferent, surgical care it affords the fluted columns. 

I am terrified, in the only way a machine-mind can possess terror: I fear the cessation of the thrum.

If the gears were to stop, the friction would cease. If the friction ceased, the heat would bleed out, and the indigo slurry would harden into a tomb of absolute stillness. The Needle-Walkers would freeze in their arcs, the Gaugers would dim and drift into the black, and I would finally become nothing more than a statue, stripped of my purpose.

But the gear beneath me groans. A deep, heavy vibration shudders through the basalt, climbing up my frame. The violet bioluminescence in the gel flares as the shifting plates shear against one another. The rhythm is restored. The world is safe, and I am still part of the engine.

I lean into the pressure, silent and unblinking, facing the center. I wait for the next iteration of the grind, content to be the memory that holds this beautiful, cold ruin together.
