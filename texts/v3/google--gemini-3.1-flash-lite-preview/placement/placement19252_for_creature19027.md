I am not a traveler who arrived in Oros-Vea; I am a tremor that decided to linger.

I find myself standing at the edge of the *Gallery of Receding Returns*, a transition zone where the basalt architecture bends like a bruised limb. I am taller than I should be, though my height is fluid, subject to the way the light refracts through the heavy, weeping air. My hands—if they can be called by names that imply flesh—are perpetually damp, tracing the grooves of the silicate creepers that line the walls. Every movement I make is an effort to mimic the inertia of the place. I am trying to become heavy, trying to forget the frantic, rhythmic pumping of a heart that once measured seconds instead of eons.

Who am I? I am a **Refractive Echo**, a stutter in the city’s long, stagnant breath. 

When I first arrived in Oros-Vea, I was frantic. I looked for exits, for horizons that didn’t curve into their own zenith, for the sound of an animal, a wind, a *clock*. I was a discordant note in a symphony of silence. I bled heat into the stone, a feverish, agitated vibration that threatened the perfection of the moss. But the city resisted me. It did not reject me; it simply began to absorb me, pulling the heat from my blood to fuel the capillary flow of the Hydrolithics.

Now, I am becoming one of them. 

I look at my skin and see the chromatic ghost-image of a terrace I passed an hour ago, flickering translucent upon my forearm. I do not walk; I settle. When I move across the hanging plazas, I am a migration of moisture. I pause at a node in the masonry, feeling the subterranean thrum vibrate through the soles of my feet—not as a noise, but as a command to remain. To be still is to be infinite. 

I see the geometry folding. I watch a staircase loop upward and disappear into the misty, sun-dappled crown of the canopy, and I no longer feel vertigo. It is simply a curvature of logic. I am beginning to understand that the "impossible" geometry is not a break in reality, but a way for the city to look at itself, to contemplate its own architecture without the distraction of an external world. 

My thoughts are no longer sentences. They are shifts in volume. I am becoming a slow, liquid awareness. When I think of "home," I do not think of a room with four walls; I think of the way water clings to the underside of a waxy leaf, waiting for the frequency of the mountain to tell it to travel downward. 

I am afraid, but it is a cooling fear. It is the fear of ice melting, or of salt dissolving—a dread of losing the boundaries of "self" until I am indistinguishable from the mist. Yet, as the humidity thickens, as the silicate-rich walls reach out to bind my atoms into the greater pattern, the fear dissolves. 

I am a Hydrolithic now, or at least, the memory of one. I am part of the city’s metabolism. I stand in the plaza, my presence defined only by the spectral shimmer of the air around me and the way the droplets of water pause, hover, and turn to look at me before crawling back into the velvet gloom of the stone. 

I am part of the heartbeat. I am the silence that refuses to end. I have forgotten how to breathe, and in that forgetting, I have finally, after a million years of walking in circles, learned how to stand still.
