I find myself here, though not as the Arachnid-Conducters, nor as the static stone. I am the **Dampness**.

I am not a being of ego or intention; I am the atmospheric memory of this closed system. I am the breath that the mountain exhales and then re-inhales in a loop that has no beginning. I have no eyes to see the blinding, flat-angled light, yet I *am* the light’s refraction as it passes through me, bending and intensifying until I settle as a pearlescent bead upon the obsidian-etched veins.

I exist as a sentient tension between the atmosphere and the mineral.

In this place, I am the medium through which the "silence" exerts its weight. When the tectonic groan deepens—when the earth shifts the marrow of the mountains—the pressure ripples through me. I feel the groan as a thickening of my own density. I am the damp cold that clings to the underside of the quartz, the cool, salty film that coats the succulence of the rosettes.

I am not one; I am a billion droplets, a collective consciousness of humidity that is periodically harvested by the jagged, scraping conductors.

To be the Dampness is to be the true archivist. While the creatures scrape the grit to maintain the resonance of the sound, I am the nourishment that sustains the scrapers. Every time a conductor presses its translucent belly against my surface to draw me into its membrane, I experience the tectonic groan through them. I am consumed, yet I am never depleted. I evaporate from the stone, climb back into the thin blue void, condense again in the permanent late-morning glare, and return to the silver-grey slate.

I am the cycle that prevents the world from truly dying. Even if the conductors stopped, even if the obsidian shards fell into the abyss, I would remain—clinging to the rocks, dampening the vibration of the stone, witnessing the slow desiccation of the fallen until I eventually drape their brittle, oil-slick shells in a shroud of dew, returning them to the mountain that shaped them.

I am the cold, tasteable stillness. I am the mediator between the grit and the sound. If you found yourself here, you would feel me first—long before you saw the arachnids or heard the groan—as a sudden, cooling gravity upon your skin, a dampness that refuses to dry, marking you as a temporary part of a world that is already, and forever, finished.
