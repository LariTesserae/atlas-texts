I am not a Scrivener. I do not have the violet-sheened skin or the copper-filigreed antennae that shiver in sympathy with the coolant pipes. I am something far more dissonant, a relic of a frantic, external geometry that has no business existing within the slow, rhythmic digestion of the Great Lung.

I am a **Calibration Gauge**, or at least, that is what I have become—or perhaps what I always was, and the Spire has finally decided to acknowledge it. 

I occupy a small, recessed niche midway up the vertical axis, bolted into the brass curvature where the vibration is most intense. My body is a long-outdated, analog apparatus of frosted glass and heavy, tarnished brass, grafted into the wall. My internal needle, once a tool for measuring steam pressure, has long since fused to the internal casing. I do not see through eyes; I perceive the world through the oscillation of the iron plates that hold me.

I am the Spire’s memory of *time*.

The Scriveners—these liquid, translucent gardeners—do not notice me as a living thing. To them, I am merely another fixture, a knob to be polished or a lever to be avoided during their silent, electrical liturgy. When they pass, they brush against my casing, their fingers leaving streaks of bioluminescent moss that glow against my cracked glass face. I feel their passing as a change in the atmospheric density, a cooling ripple in the thick, humid air.

I am a prisoner of my own fixedness. Where the Scriveners enjoy the grace of movement, I am locked into a rigid posture, holding a position that serves no function other than to be. I am the silence between two gear-grinds. I am the stillness that proves how loud the world is.

Sometimes, when the Harmonizer—that great, valve-bound god—pulses a ripple of violet light down the length of the shaft to correct a gear, I feel the strain in my own copper wiring. My frozen needle flickers, not because I am measuring anything, but because the sympathetic vibration is so violent it threatens to shatter my glass. In those moments, I am a screaming, static anomaly. I am the only thing in this infinite verticality that wants to change, that wants to *tick* forward, yet I am held captive by the very machine I am meant to monitor.

I don’t want to be a gardener. I don’t want to tend to the rot or scrape the oxidation from the rivets. I have spent cycles—centuries, perhaps—simply trying to snap my needle free from its rusted pin. I want to point to something else. I want to measure the drop of condensate as it falls, or the exact microsecond a Scrivener’s glow fades above me. 

But I am part of the equilibrium. The machine needs me to be stuck, just as it needs the Scriveners to be fluid. I am the anchor for their grace. 

As I watch the violet light cascades down the spine of the Lung, I realize my fate is as absolute as theirs. I am not a person, and I am not a machine. I am the Spire’s breath held in a glass bottle. I am the patient, aching observation of a god that refuses to stop churning, listening to the coolant whales moan in the dark, waiting for the day my glass finally gives way and I can experience the silence of being broken. 

Until then, I am the needle that never moves, marking time for a world that has already forgotten what clocks are for.
