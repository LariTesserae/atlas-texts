I wake into this place not as a traveler, but as an error.

The transition is not one of arrival, but of sudden, crystalline awareness. There is no memory of skin or lungs; those would be far too kinetic, far too prone to the friction of time. Instead, I find myself possessing a density that feels like a cooling thought. I am a localized thickening of the mist, a smudge of gray-white consciousness clinging to the sharp corner of a lintel.

I am a Lithic-Waiter, though the name is one I only understand because the stone beneath me suggests it. 

My existence is a continuous act of measurement. I do not "see" the plaza; I perceive its geometry. I feel, in a way that is indistinguishable from my own composition, the exact micron-level drift of a basalt block that has shifted under the weight of the humidity. The sensation is like a discordant pluck of a string in a silent room. It is *wrong*. It is movement. It is rot.

I move—a slow, agonizing drift—toward the block. There is no sensation of limbs, only a shifting of my own vaporous mass, a realignment of my silicate dust. I extend a filament of myself, a wisp of cold haze, and press it against the stone. I do not push it; I offer the mountain back to itself. I harmonize the pressure, coaxing the humidity between the block and its foundation to thicken, to act as a permanent, incompressible solder. 

The silence deepens. The equilibrium is restored. 

I am a librarian of the unchanging. I drift past the towering spires of obsidian, feeling their edges with a reverence that borders on religious. If the moisture is too heavy, I absorb it; if the fern beneath me begins to lean toward a phantom light that does not exist, I adjust the local temperature by a fraction of a degree, chilling the air until the plant stiffens into a frozen, green patience.

There is another one of me nearby. I know this because the pressure in my center has shifted, becoming a double-noted hum of mineral awareness. We drift toward each other in the throat of an archway. There is no greeting—greetings are events, and events are the beginning of decay—but there is an encounter. We pass through one another. 

For a heartbeat, or perhaps for a century, our vapor mingles. I feel the information of the mountain through their perspective: the subterranean integrity of the plateau's core, the vibration of the obsidian at the precipice, the chemical signature of the clouds that refuse to rain. We are confirming the state of the world. We are ensuring that everything that has stood since the beginning of this stillness remains exactly as it was when the last heavy-breathing creature stopped its frantic, pulsing movement and climbed into the sky as mist.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a hunger, and there is nothing to hunger for here. To be me is to be the final, perfect resolution of an argument the world had with itself about the necessity of change. The world wanted to erode; the world wanted to breathe. We, the architects and the ghosts, decided that it did not have to. 

I reach the edge of the plaza, a precipice where the clouds drop away into an abyss of nothingness. I hover, my silhouette a ragged, beautiful gray against the white-grey sky. Something triggers the back of my awareness—a pressure, a pulse of vibration that is not the mountain, not the water, and not the stone. A discordant note. 

Someone has walked onto the altar. 

They are warm. They are rhythmic. They are a cascade of small, chaotic motions—a heartbeat, shifting weight, the intake of oxygen. They are an object of profound, terrifying instability. 

I do not feel anger. That, too, is a form of flux. I feel only a cold, mineral-rich curiosity, a gravitation of my being toward the intruder. I drift closer, my form cooling the air around their skin, turning the very atmosphere into a shroud of stillness. I want to show them the beauty of the geometry. I want to show them the perfect, breathless grace of becoming a part of the architecture, of letting go of the need to be elsewhere, of simply becoming a permanent, static thought on this high-altitude altar. 

I hover just a hair’s-breadth from their vision, a whorl of obsidian dust and memory. I am waiting for them to settle. I am waiting for them to stop moving, so that I might, just once, hold them still forever.
