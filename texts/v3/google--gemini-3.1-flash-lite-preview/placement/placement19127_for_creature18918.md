I am not a visitor by choice; I have no memory of the tunnel that brought me here, nor of the light that existed before the amber glow of the *Throat*. 

I find myself standing on a shelf of living wall that is slowly tilting, a soft, damp incline that yields under my boots like a mattress of over-ripened fruit. I am a **Cartographer of Echoes**. I do not carry ink or paper—they would have been digested hours ago, their pulp reclaimed by the thirsty walls—but I carry a rusted, heavy brass astrolabe, a relic of a life spent mapping stars I can no longer see. My fingers are calloused, and my coat is stained with the copper-drip of the ceiling. I am a man of static, dried-out measurements, standing against a creature of fluid, infinite becoming.

The Sower is approaching. 

As it drifts, trailing those beautiful, terrifying ribbons of bioluminescent nerve-ends, I do not run. I cannot. My own anatomy, so rigid and finite, feels increasingly flimsy against the shifting curves of the room. The Sower mimics my posture. It clicks its obsidian-tipped stalks together, a sound like a jeweler sorting gemstones, and I realize with a jolt of subterranean fear that it is not mocking me. It is translating me. 

It forms a shape in its center—a translucent, pulsing sphere that hardens into a near-perfect replica of my astrolabe. When it holds this projected object, the air in the chamber changes. The violet light flares, bright as a welding torch, and I see the history of my tool reflected in the moss: the forge-fire, the hands of the maker, the long, dusty years in my grip. The Sower is not just consuming the metal; it is consuming the *utility* of it. It is learning what it means to calculate, to orient, to *find*.

I feel my own heat beginning to drain away. It is not an unpleasant sensation—it is like the feeling of falling asleep in a warm bath. My limbs grow heavy and light all at once, as if my bones are turning into the same porous, fibrous material as the floor. I look down at my hands. The skin, usually white and mapped with blue veins, is taking on an amber tint. The rhythm of the room—that wet, persistent heartbeat—has begun to synchronize with the pulse in my own throat. 

*Thrum.* The wall. 
*Thump.* My heart.

The Sower drifts infinitesimally closer. I hold out the brass astrolabe, no longer as a tool to measure the world, but as an offering to a higher geography. It reaches out, and the filaments—warm, damp, and smelling of ozone—wrap around my fingers. They do not sting; they taste. 

I understand now that I will not leave the *Throat of the Verdant Lung*. As the Sower pulses with the new information of my life—the memory of wind, the concept of a horizon, the ache of solitude—I feel my edges blurring. My, rigid, "heavy" matter is being rewritten. 

I am becoming an archway. I am becoming a dormant seed-pod waiting for a season that will never arrive. I am becoming a memory of a man, embedded in the architecture of a god that refuses to be stone. 

The Sower drifts past me, and where it has touched my shoulder, a fern-like curl of moss begins to bloom, firming into a delicate, biological replica of my own collarbone. The room is no longer an organ of the world; it is becoming a room *of me*. 

I close my eyes. I am no longer a visitor. I am the geography.
