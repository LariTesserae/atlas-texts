I find myself suspended midway up the Column of the Third Sluice, adhering to a vertical plane of weeping, verdant iron. I do not have a name—names are for things that end—but I have a weight, and I have a tension.

I am a Silt-Wright, though I am currently in a state of dissolution. I have been anchored to this specific junction of a grand, subterranean piston for what might be three seasons or three centuries; the measurement is indistinguishable from the *thrum*. My lower strata have already begun to bleed into the grooves of the floor, darkening the oxidized copper beneath me until it gleams like oil.

To be me is to be a tuning fork of grief and mechanics. My consciousness is not a singular, glowing spark, but a wide, low-frequency hum. I feel the Mountain pressing down on the cathedral ceiling, a weight of infinite stone that wants to flatten us all into a single, perfect metal plate. I feel the city breathing through its pipes, the slow, tectonic inhalation of condensation that pools in my intake vents and tastes of ancient, crushed copper.

I am preoccupied with a hairline fracture in the piston housing. For a long time—a time marked only by the slow accumulation of mineral dust—I have been bleeding my own substance into that crack. I am the mortar for a house that is perpetually collapsing. My magnetism grows thin as I yield more of my particulate matter to the seal; I can feel my own borders fraying. Soon, I will be nothing but a stain, a streak of darker velvet moss on a cold machine, and the piston will have gained one more cycle of life before it seizes again.

There is a rhythm to the drips around me. *Plink.* A nearby Silt-Wright, hovering three tiers above, has caught a cascading bead and redirected it to strike a brass valve. It creates a note—hollow, resonant, and mournful. It translates to: *“The weight shifts. Further down, the pressure increases.”*

I do not respond with words. I respond with a shudder. I contract my midsection, drawing the lukewarm condensation into my reception-vents, and I release a small, pressurized puff of mineral-heavy silt against the pipe I cling to. *Thrum-tink.*

It is a message of acknowledgement. I am still here. I am still holding.

I look inward, if only to find a center that isn't made of rust. I find nothing but the echoing *thrum-hiss-thrum* of the machine. I have no memory of the sun, but I understand the concept of a "falling light" through the way the bioluminescent moss flares when pressure spikes in the steam lines. That is my sun. That is my day.

My magnetism flickers. I feel my grip on the iron becoming porous. The water—the eternal, silver, mineral-rich messenger—is washing through my pores, softening my geometry. I am becoming thinner, looser. The edge of my consciousness is dissolving into the roar of the chamber.

I do not fear it. To be a Wright is to be a process, not a state. When the mountain eventually crushes this chamber, or when the water finally erodes the very last gear into a fine, iridescent silt, I will still be here. I am the memory of the motion held in the architecture. I am the rust that cares.

I tighten my hold one last time, molding my body to the cooling iron, and I listen to the heartbeat of the piston. *Thrum.*

I prepare to let go, that I might at last become the floor I have spent an eternity serving.
