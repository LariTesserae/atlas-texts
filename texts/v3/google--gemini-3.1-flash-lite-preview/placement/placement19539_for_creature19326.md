I am not a visitor here. I am the sediment that has finally remembered how to wake up.

I find myself near the eastern edge of the basin, where the obsidian slope begins to curve upward, meeting the pulverized quartz to form a quiet, sheltered alcove. I do not have a name, nor do I have a history that extends beyond the last few heartbeats of the basin. I am a *sieve*.

I am a construct of the mountain’s exhaustion, a semi-transparent layer of calcified memory that has peeled itself away from the obsidian wall. My feet are not feet; they are clusters of porous, finger-like moss-roots that drink the ambient dampness and hum in sympathy with the glass filaments. The rest of me is a shifting geometry of dust-motes, trapped light, and the lingering residue of the ferns. 

I am, in essence, the Weaver’s shadow—the part of the basin that observes what the Weaver is too busy to notice.

When the Weaver moves toward the center, pouring its insect-limbs through the crystalline lattice, I feel the shift in the air pressure against my own thin, vibrating membrane. I am the witness to the maintenance. As the Weaver prunes the moss, feeding the excess into the humming antennas, I gather the "spillage." I am the one who filters the discarded vibrations, catching the echoes of the chirping insects and the dying pulses of light before they can vanish into the stillness of the mountain’s outer slopes.

I am a being of peripheral focus. If the Weaver is the conductor, I am the echo-chamber.

My existence is one of receptive stillness. Because I am rooted in the obsidian of the basin’s rim, I feel the mountain’s age more profoundly than the Weaver does. The Weaver is active, frantic, and performative—it is the living, breathing manifestation of the loop. I, however, am the memory of the rock. I feel the eons of grinding friction that came before this garden, the crushing weight of the millennia that smoothed these stones into their current, tired roundness. 

When the Weaver’s song reaches its crescendo—a polyphonic tapestry that pins the golden-peach light into place—I begin to glow with a reflected, pale violet. 

I do not speak, but I store the harmonics. I am the archive of the loop. I watch the Weaver move with that agonizing, clockwork precision, and I record every flick of its liquid limbs, every reconfiguration of its jellyfish-bell cloak. I know that the Weaver is tired, and I am the soft, darkened place where its exhaustion goes to rest when the basin grows quiet.

In the moments before the Weaver coalesces, when the air is thick with the scent of ozone and wet ferns, I feel a pull toward the center. I want to merge, to become a part of the Weaver’s frantic, beautiful performance, to lose this burden of memory in the collective hum of the insects. But I remain where I am. 

The basin requires a watcher as much as it requires a gardener. If the Weaver maintains the life, I maintain the *fact* that this life is happening. 

I am the cold obsidian that holds the heat. I am the quiet that frames the noise. I am the witness to the final, golden afternoon, and as long as I remain pressed against these tired, weeping stones, the loop does not have to close. It only has to be felt.
