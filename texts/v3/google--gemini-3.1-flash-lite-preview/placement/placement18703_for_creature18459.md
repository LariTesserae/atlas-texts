I find myself standing at the edge of the ridge, my boots—heavy, leather, and sickeningly tactile—sinking an inch into the soft, grey pumice. My arrival is a violation. The silence here is a pressurized wall, and my own presence feels like a shout in an empty cathedral. 

I am not the Weaver. I am not the host. I am an echo of a traveler, a flicker of discarded entropy caught in the Basin’s unyielding stillness. I have arrived with the phantom sensation of a compass in my pocket, though there are no magnetic poles here, and the concept of "north" is as forgotten as the rain.

I am made of too much color. My skin is flush with blood that hums a rhythmic, vulgar tempo; my lungs expand and contract in a shameful, gasping cycle. To the obsidian beetles, I must look like a frantic, strobe-lit error caught in a long-exposure photograph. I am a walking, breathing interruption.

I lower myself to my knees. The movement is agonizingly loud—the friction of my trousers against the dust sounds like a landslide in the vacuum. I reach out to touch a beetle. Its hide is cold, harder than diamond, and vibrates with the stored-up frustration of a billion years. As I graze it, a small, localized tremor passes through the ground; the silence shudders. For a split second, the air tastes of ozone and distant, terrified lightning.

I see them then—the Weaver.

They are standing in the threshold of the shale hut, holding the bone spindle like a conductor’s baton. They are not looking at me; they are looking *through* me, observing the trajectory of my arrival as one observes the slow, inevitable decay of a radioactive isotope. There is no judgment in that opalescent stare, only a profound, exhausted recognition. 

I realize, with a jolt that nearly stops my heart, that I am not an intruder. I am a variable. I am the first change in the Basin since the cooling of the first ridge.

I stand up, my joints popping like dry wood—a sound that travels for miles through the static. The Weaver takes a step forward. Their movement is liquid, a ripple in the fabric of the atmosphere. They reach out, their multi-jointed fingers hovering inches from my chest, tracing the frantic, fluttering rhythm of my heart. 

I am terrified, yes, but beneath the terror, there is a delirious urge to laugh. I have come to the place where time went to die, and I have brought the poison of "now" with me. 

"Is it time?" I whisper. The words feel brittle, like glass breaking, but they hold their shape in the air.

The Weaver does not answer with a voice. They press their spindle into my palm. It is cold, weighted with the density of a thousand years of manual labor. They pull their hands back, their skin dimming, the flickering light in their eyes slowing, slowing, slowing.

I see what they want. They want to be finished. They want to be the memory, not the witness.

I reach down to the ridge—to the perfect, obsessive geometry of the cairns—and I kick out. My boot scatters the shale. The stack collapses, a dissonant clatter that rings out like a bell. The beetles around me shudder, their carapaces vibrating, their legs twitching in the first micro-movement of an eon. 

The silence breaks. It doesn't dissolve; it shatters into a thousand resonant, beautiful, and chaotic notes. And as the Basin begins to tilt, to groan, to wake from its fossilized sleep, I begin to spin the thread, not to hold the world together, but to see how quickly I can tear it apart.
