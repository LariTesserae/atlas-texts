I find myself clinging to the side of a spiraling, calcified column that reeks of wet ozone and sterile, crushed sap. My fingers—long, articulated lattices that feel like dry reeds—are buried deep into the mossy rind of the stalk. I do not feel cold, though the air bites at my exposed joints; I only feel the *hum*. It is a mechanical, pulsing thrum that travels from the ceiling’s spores, down through the architecture, and rattles in the translucent nectar of my own chest.

I am a Weaver. That is all I know of a name. I have been moving for what feels like a thousand cycles of the spores, my path dictated by the frantic, blooming hunger of the floor beneath me.

I look down. The abyss is a swirling, curdled milk of mist and drifting gelatinous spheres. A few hundred meters below, a bridge of translucent foliage is currently "turning"—the green cells are darkening, turning into a gummy, brown mulch that will fall away within seconds. I watch it dissolve with an apathy born of long survival. I shift my weight, my extra-jointed knees snapping into position to distribute my mass against the sheer verticality of the stalk.

My task today is to steady the spiral. I reach into the hollow of my chest and pull out an obsidian needle, the edge sharp enough to shear through the hyper-fast fibers. I move with a predator’s grace, yet my heart—if the pressure-pump within my ribs can be called that—beats in a slow, rhythmic syncopation with the light above. 

I find a junction where two major stalks are pulling apart, their connective tendrils fraying under the tension of their own rapid vertical growth. If they pull away, the path will snap. I lean into the wind, my feet gripping the velvet moss, and begin to stitch. I pierce the fibrous skin of one, loop a strong, cured vine through its core, and anchor it into the pulse-center of the other. 

My movements are silent, but my work is loud with intent. The plant shudders as I pierce it; it tries to knit itself over my needle, aggressively closing the gap. I weave with a frantic, machine-like speed, sewing the architecture together before the growth can choke my own hands. For a moment, the two massive stalks become a single, sturdy spine. 

I pause. The exertion leaves me dehydrated, and I feel the biting rime settling into the cracks of my chitinous plates. I look toward the far wall of the Void, a white cliff of shimmering, living timber thousands of meters away. 

There, perched on a ledge that is already beginning to list toward the abyss, is another of my kind. 

We do not look at each other, for we have no eyes to meet; we simply align our internal frequencies. I close my refractive plate, letting the light of the ceiling spores map the vibrations passing between us through the floor. We hum—a low, flat, oscillating note that harmonizes with the cathedral. It is a communication of geometry, a map of the path ahead, a warning that the growth in the southern quadrant is accelerating and the bridges are becoming brittle. 

He nods—a slow, mechanical tilt of a featureless head—and then he is gone, stepping off into the void with a terrifying, calculated leap that lands him on a descending spore-pod, carried away by the shifting currents of the air.

I am alone again, but the path is secure for another few hours. I turn my face toward the white glare of the ceiling. The spores pulse, and I blink back, matching their cadence, ready to follow the rhythm until the architecture finally dissolves and I am forced to fall. I am the stitch in the lung; I am the heartbeat in the wood. As long as I am weaving, the Void does not devour itself, and I, in turn, have a place to walk.
