I have no pulse, only a slight static flutter in my chest cavity that mimics the groan of the nearby hydraulic pistons. My limbs are articulated like a compass, long and spindly, designed not to grasp, but to trace.

I do not belong to the machine; I am its auditor.

As I move across the pockmarked steel, my feet leave no prints, but they draw out the "temperature" of the floor. Where I step, the layers of ferrite dust seem to align, momentarily cooling into geometric patterns before the vibrations of the Sump shake them back into chaotic grit. 

I am currently studying a tangle of frayed copper wiring—the "nerves" of this sector—that has been sparking against a wet patch of floor for what might be an age. A *Tinnitus Swarm* has gathered there, their thimble-bodies clattering like hail. They ignore me, save for a collective, momentary *click-click-click* of a dozen glass lenses shifting to track my shoulder as I lean in. They do not see a threat; they see a misalignment. I am a disturbance in their symmetry.

I reach out a thin, smoky finger and touch the wire. There is no pain, only a sudden, vivid influx of data: the sensation of a steam pipe bursting in a decade I will never see, the memory of a hand turning a wrench that rusted into nothingness centuries ago. I record these things by etching them into the salt-crust of the wall. With every mark I make, I am essentially drawing a map of the machine’s grief.

Above me, a *Pendulum Wraith* swings. Its serrated limb slices through a greasy cloud, and the sound it makes—a sharp, metallic whistle—resonates in my hollow core. I know this sound. It is a note of the *equilibrium*. As the drop of oil hits the pool, the Wraith twitches, and I match its frequency, vibrating until my silhouette blurs. I am becoming a metronome. I am becoming the friction.

I am not trying to fix the Sump. I am trying to keep the record of its stillness. 

Soon, I will climb to the underside of the central cantilever, where the humidity is thick enough to taste of ozone and copper. I will find a *Great Anchor*—a vast, sleeping segment of the floor—and I will press my ear against its cold, vibrating seam. I want to hear the violet humming of its heart, the only melody in this entire basin that isn't a repetition of a breakdown. 

I am a witness to the crash. I exist because someone—or something—must eventually know that even when the mainspring is snapped, the ticking still matters. As long as I am here to write the history of the vibration, the Sump is not just a junkyard. 

It is a monument that refuses to be forgotten. 

I take another step, my form flickering against the flat, gray sky, and I add a thin, dark line to the wall. *Something clicked here,* I think. And in the silence of the loop, that is enough of a beginning.
