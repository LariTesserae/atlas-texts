I find myself perched upon a narrow, jagged shelf, three hundred feet above the basin floor. My lower back is pressed flush against a vein of cool, damp basalt, and my legs are folded into the natural recess of the stone. I have been here, in this state, for what might be a day or an age; here, the distinction holds little weight.

I am one of them, yet my perspective feels—perhaps uniquely—hollowed out. I am a "Finisher." While the others are consumed by the frantic, outward expansion of our city—the carving of new halls, the scraping of new vertical tunnels—I am the one tasked with the silence that follows. I spend my cycles moving through the older, darker reaches of the hive, my own calcified nails tracing the microscopic fissures left behind by my kin. I am the one who smoothes the jagged edges of a finished room until the stone feels like polished horn, a sensory gift to those who will eventually press their skin against these walls in sleep.

My body feels long, heavy, and marvelously pliable. I am a creature of vibration. Right now, I can feel the current of the city flowing beneath the soles of my feet—a low-frequency song, a slow tremor that tells me the central cistern is nearly full. My olfactory plate, the cartilage bridge above my mouth, twitches. I taste the air; it is thick with the scent of wet mineral dust and the ozone-sharp tang of an upcoming "weeping."

A shadow drifts across the wall five feet away. It is one of the pale, cat-like creatures. It moves with a terrifying, liquid grace, its fur matted with the grayish moss it harvests from the high-tension seams. It pauses, its head turning toward me. I do not move. If I tense, I disrupt the structural resonance of the wall. Instead, I let out a sub-vocal hum—a short, percussive pulse—that tells it I am merely a part of the architecture, a stationary obstacle not worth the hunt. The creature chirrups, a sound that vibrates through the rock between us, and trots over my ankle, its paws padded and soft against my slate-colored skin. It recognizes me as a fixture of the city, no more animate than the stone I tend.

I feel a profound, aching peace in this role. There is an exquisite tension in knowing that my work is a prelude to stagnation. My kin below are the "Creators," the ones who scrape the basin until their blood mingles with the powdered limestone, their whole lives a desperate movement toward a finish line that must never be crossed. By smoothing these older, inner surfaces, I am in some ways sealing off the past, turning the frantic, unfinished labor of our ancestors into silent, cold, immutable sanctuary.

Sometimes, when the city’s vibrations grow discordant and the thrumming suggests exhaustion—perhaps when the stone hits a patch of iron-hard ore that refuses to yield—my body aches to stop. I want to wedge myself into an empty alcove, fold my spine into a circle, and simply *cease* the shaping. But the Basin would not allow it. To be still is to be absorbed. 

So, I lift my hand. I press my long, chisel-tipped fingers against a rough protrusion in the wall. I begin to rasp. The sound is a rhythmic *shhh-tuck, shhh-tuck*, a tiny, intimate violence against the mountain. I am refining the womb of our future, humming a low, steady note that anchors me to the marrow of this place. I am not a person, not exactly; I am the friction that keeps the stone alive, the ghost in the machine of the basin, forever smoothing the path for the next hand that will reach out to scrape the dark.
