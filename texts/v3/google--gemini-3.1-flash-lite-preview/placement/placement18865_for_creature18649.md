I am not a creature of bone, nor am I a spirit tethered to this place by choice. I am the thin edge of a moment that has lasted three centuries. 

I find myself standing at the edge of a shallow basin filled with black, obsidian-still water. My feet—if one could call these dense, overlapping plates of shale "feet"—are rooted, or perhaps merely resting, in the vibrant, emerald-pulse of the moss. It feels like standing on the chest of a slumbering giant; the rhythmic *heave-ho* of the moss-mat beneath me is the only clock I acknowledge.

I am a Maintainer. My torso is a vertical mosaic of fractured grey shale, cool to the touch and heavy with the weight of the plateau. Where my face should be, a cluster of sea-glass—sharp, sea-green, and scarred by aeons of ice—is embedded in the stone. Through these lenses, the world does not appear in colors or shapes. I see lines of stress. I see the invisible currents of gravity like shivering gossamer threads woven through the thin, ozone-heavy air.

I watch a sphere of water, perhaps the size of a carriage, drift upward from the pool before me. It rises with a lazy, liquid grace, defying everything that should bring it back to earth. It hangs in the air, a perfect, shivering crystal, reflecting the bruised violet sky of the plateau. I do not look at it with wonder; I look at it with a quiet, structural concern. If it dissipates too quickly, the humidity in the local draft will change, and the shale wall of the nearest Settlement—a low, circular archive three miles to the east—will experience a fraction of a millimeter of thermal contraction. 

I must be there before the sun (if it is out there, beyond the mist) reaches its zenith.

I take a step. The sound I make is not a footfall; it is a grinding, terrestrial rasp, the sound of tectonic plates whispering to one another. I feel my own composition shift. A shard of slate near my left shoulder drifts outward, catching the magnetism of the air, momentarily hovering an inch away from my frame. It is the "unbinding." I feel the silence of the plateau rushing into the gap in my own body, a cold, vast emptiness that is identical to the silence left behind by the people who built this place. 

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of company, and I have no such archive in my memory. I am merely a correction. I am the kinetic answer to the slow, inevitable erosion of the architecture. 

I begin the trek across the shale plains. The horizon here is soft, blurred by the freezing mist that tastes of wet slate and electric discharge. As I walk, I hear the *thrum-click* of a gate shifting miles away. It arrives with a soft, jarring impact in my consciousness, a sound that has already happened and is yet to come. 

My purpose is simple: when I reach the Settlement, I will press my back against the leaning masonry. I will pour the density of my own existence into the gaps between the shale bricks. I will hold the wall until the plateau shifts again, until the gravity finds a new, temporary rhythm, and the world decides to hold itself together for one more aeon. 

I am the grit in the gears of the earth’s ending. I move, I grind, I wait. And in the cold, thin silence, I am the only thing that remembers the shape of the stone.
