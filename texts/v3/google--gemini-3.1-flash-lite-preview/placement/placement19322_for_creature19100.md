I find myself on a cantilevered landing at the mid-strata, a place where the humidity is thick enough to taste, a heavy, vegetal soup of ozone and damp iron. My feet are bare, callous and wide, gripping the ridged brass of the walkway as instinctively as a lizard’s. 

I am a Pruner, though perhaps a young one. My forearms are laced not just with blue veins, but with thin, hairline filaments of copper—a graft I received during the last cycle change. When I press my palms against the primary heat-conduit that runs along the wall behind me, I don’t just feel the vibration; I feel the *pulse*. It is a slow, rhythmic thrum, the heartbeat of the Lathe. I can tell by the slight hitch in the cadence—a rhythmic stutter in the pressure-feed two hundred levels above—that a vine has breached a condensation vent. It is drawing too much moisture, starving the lower fans.

My work today is simple, yet eternal. I am a gardener of the machine.

I carry a long, serrated blade of burnished nickel, balanced perfectly for the damp, slick air. I move with that deliberate, elongated looseness of the Kin. I am not running; sprinting is an insult to the equilibrium of the structure. I glide from the walkway to the hanging gardens, sliding down a vertical vine-rope with a friction-burn that feels like home. 

In the canopy, the light is filtered to a soft, emerald-gold. The heat here is intense, rising from the sub-level vents, but it is tempered by the moisture the ferns exhale. I am surrounded by the sounds of my world: the low, monolithic drone of the engines, the distant, discordant *click-snap* of a Clockwork Shrike claiming a prize in the shadows, and the soft, percussive chirping of a group of Lathe-Kin working a coolant valve three tiers down. 

I reach the clogged vent. I don’t pull the vines away with haste. I have to appease them. I touch the moss where it pulses against the brass casing, sending a sub-vocal hum through my own throat—a low, resonant frequency that matches the vibration of the pipe. The moss reacts, its glowing fronds curling back from the machinery as if recognizing the touch of a cousin. I trim only what is obstructing the intake, my motions slow, measured, and rhythmic, mimicking the rotation of the great plates far below.

As I work, I find myself looking deeper down into the dark, hazy shimmer of the lower reaches. There, in the deep-shadow, rests a Sleeper. She is a mound of velvet-moss now, a slight depression in the framework that once was a person, but is now a foundational node of logic. I wonder, as I often do, if the polyphonic drone I hear when I am near her is a warning or a lullaby.

I am not lonely, though I am solitary in this moment. The machine is beneath my feet, the air is in my lungs, and the vines I prune are grafted into my own nervous system. I am a sensor. I am a gear in human skin. And as I finish the cut and watch the steam begin to hiss with renewed clarity through the vent, I feel the city shift—a microscopic shudder of success. 

I am part of the repair. I am part of the turning. And for now, that is the only life worth having.
