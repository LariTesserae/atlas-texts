The rain here does not fall; it arrives, a horizontal pressure against the spine.

I find myself on the "Mid-Shelf," a cantilevered gallery bolted to the northern face of the Spire. My world is a narrow strip of brass decking, weeping mineral-heavy droplets that turn black as they slide over the oxidized copper railings. I am huddled in the shadow of a colossal, humming bypass valve, the size of a cathedral bell. My breath is a thin shroud, hitching in my chest, syncopated with the deep, rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of the massive pistons buried deep in the mountain's throat.

I am a Liner. I do not remember a world that is flat, and the thought of such a thing—a horizon that extends beyond the reach of a spool-line—feels like a vertigo-inducing nightmare.

My hands are splayed against the basalt, the stone slick and freezing, the texture familiar as skin. To my eyes, the gloom is not empty; it is a tapestry of micro-climates. I see the amethyst moss pulsing with an irregular, feverish heat, and I see the structural fatigue in the rusted bracing three levels below. My fingers, elongated and stained with the silver-grey dust of the Spire, act with an autonomy that my mind barely oversees. I am currently replacing a rivet.

The work is a prayer. I take a needle-thin drill, its bit humming just above the resonant frequency of the rock, and clear away a decade of salt-crust from a junction where the brass meets the stone. The metal screams a thin, high-pitched note, a chime that echoes into the white abyss. *Clack-clack.* I catch the sound, wait for the echo, and answer with a series of rhythmic clicks from my throat, signaling to the neighboring galleries that the joint is secure, that the geometry of our house remains mathematically sound for another cycle.

I am not alone, but my solitude is heavy. Beside me, unspooled and drifting like a ghost in the fog, a Wind-Skimmer hovers. It is a translucent, pulsing lantern, its cyan-glow dimming as it feeds on the cold moisture of the air. I stop. My hands, usually so feverish in their maintenance, relax. I unhook the carabiner at my waist—a moment of terrifying vulnerability—and reach out.

I am not reaching to catch it, but to be acknowledged.

The creature drifts closer, its rubbery, bioluminescent membrane rippling as it navigates the turbulence of the steam-vents. It feels like a magnetic pull, a gentle, static charge that makes the hairs on my forearm stand rigid. Its long, trailing tentacles, soft as silk and freezing as the troposphere, graze my forehead. 

For a heartbeat, the city vanishes. The *clack-clack* of the pneumatic tubes, the groan of the metal under the wind’s slow, scouring ambition, the crushing sense of my own thin, grey life—all of it goes silent. In the cool radiance of the Skimmer, I feel the age of the mountain. It is not static, as we pretend; it is a long, slow exhale. I am merely the dust in the wake of that breath. 

The Skimmer drifts on, pulled by an updraft toward the cloud-wall where the Spire finally ends. I sigh, a wheezing sound that mirrors the steam-nodes, and clip my tether back to the rail. The click is crisp, final, and comforting. 

I return to the rivet. I polish the brass until it shines like a mirror, a tiny star in the throat of the fog. The wind lashes at the gallery, trying to unmake us, but I am strong, I am tethered, and I will be here when the next layer of moss needs scraping. I am the Spire’s memory, and for now, the Spire holds.
