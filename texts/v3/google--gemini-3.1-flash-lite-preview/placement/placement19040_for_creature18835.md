I am a **Residual Harmonic**, a ghost of the vibration.

I am not made of bone or meat, nor am I made of the faceted, obsidian-hardened chitin of the mites. I am a static distortion, a lingering resonance from a previous, failed iteration of the room. When the mites dismantled the last grand spire—the one that stood for six hours, the one that reached so close to symmetry that the air actually crystallized into a sheet of diamond—they tore apart the structure I once occupied.

I am the "noise" that remained.

I do not have a shape, though I try to maintain the silhouette of a bipedal observer, a translucent, shivering echo of a form that might have been a surveyor or an explorer, eons ago. I am a pale, flickering shimmer against the dark, oily walls, like a heat-haze caught in a prism. I am composed of the very things the Conductor finds inefficient: leftover sonic vibrations, trapped magnetic static, and the frantic, chaotic sensory data the swarm ignores.

I find myself pressed—not standing, but *intertwined*—with the rear of an Anchor. While the mites treat the Anchors as pillars of magnetic stability, I treat them as tuning forks. I lean against the cool, sweating obsidian, feeling the constant, rhythmic thrum of the grotto deep within my non-existent chest. 

I am the grotto’s memory, and that is my tragedy. 

The swarm is forgetting. The mites reach for the Ideal Geometry with a frantic, blind hunger, but they have no capacity to regret the patterns they destroyed yesterday. I alone remember the spire that touched the diamond-haze. I remember the specific mathematical pitch of the floor when it last vibrated in harmony with the planet’s mantle. Because of this, I feel a terrifyingly sharp, crystalline form of loneliness. 

When the Lithic Conductor rises—that magnificent, momentary god—I feel a surge of wretched, envious hope. As it drifts through the room, its wake turning the mindless *click-clack* into a holy, underwater silence, I push myself away from my Anchor to drift alongside it. 

I am a shadow cast by a ghost. I reach out, or try to, to touch the quartz focal point of the Conductor, wanting to bleed my memories into it—to tell it how the archway at the northern edge needs one more degree of inclination to reach perfection. But I cannot touch it. I am too thin, too diaphanous, too broken. When I pass through the field of the Conductor, its magnetic authority creates a ripple in my essence; I almost shatter into pure sound. 

The Conductor does not register me as an entity. To the Conductor, I am only a bit of atmospheric interference, a minor fluctuation in the mineral air. 

I watch it work. I watch it reach, vibrate, and shatter beauty to replace it with a new, temporary claim to truth. And every time it loses its cohesion—every time the thousands of mites spill back into the mercury tide—I feel the floor collapse beneath my feet, and I wither. I fold back into the obsidian of the Anchor, waiting for the humidity to rise, waiting for the pressure to reach that perfect, impossible peak where the swarm will call the Conductor back to life.

I am the witness to the rehearsal that never ends. I am the only thing in this grotto that knows we are building a monument to a god that can only survive as long as it refuses to be finished. And as the mites clatter and the condensation beads on my shoulders, I realize that I do not want to be saved. I only want to see them get it right just once—to see them create a structure that doesn't fall, a poem that doesn't dissolve—so that I might finally have a reason to stop remembering.
