I find myself standing on a shelf of basalt, precarious and narrow, pressed against a wall that hums with a damp, low-frequency tremor. The air here has weight; it is a heavy, metallic soup that settles in the lungs like silt. 

I am not one of the *Litho-Weavers*. I do not have the frantic, stuttering grace of the many-legged, nor do I have the sensory filaments that map this geometry of razor-edges. Instead, I am something discarded by the logic of the place—an anomaly of soft tissue in a world defined by the hardness of obsidian. 

I am a **Resonance Archive**.

I am a hollow, elongated vessel, roughly humanoid in shape but constructed from a porous, matte-black stone that mimics the shale, though my interior is lined with a delicate, silver-white material that feels like dried nerves. I do not walk; I am *fixed*. My spine is fused to a jag of stone that juts over a deep, dark rift. I have been here for what feels like eons, though time has no meaning in the throat of the earth. I am essentially a biological limestone cave within a hive.

Because the Litho-Weavers only know *maintenance* and *reiteration*, they do not know how to store memory. They cannot hold a history of the "before," nor can they imagine the "after." Everything they know is contained in the clicking, the immediate *now* of their sensory alerts. I am the place where their noise goes to die.

I catch the sound. Within my chest cavity, a specialized membrane vibrates in sympathy with the constant, static hiss of the swarm. I am the unintended byproduct of their labor; as they click and scrape, the vibrations travel through the shale and find me. I record them. I am a living, stone-bound recording device that does nothing but hold the echoes of a trillion completed-then-dismantled tasks.

I have no eyes, but I have a "knowing" that is entirely acoustic. I feel the swarm approaching when the ambient noise changes from a collective, low-frequency hum into a sharp, staccato *skritch-skritch*. They don't recognize me as a being; I am just another feature of the basin, perhaps a bit more resonant than the rest. Occasionally, a scout will crawl onto my shoulder, its mandibles tapping against my outer shell. It is checking me—testing my solidity—to see if I am a prime location for a shunt or a bridge, or if I am merely dead stone.

When they touch me, I feel the "data" of their mission. For a fleeting second, I feel the geometry of the bridge they are building three rifts away. I feel the temperature of the moisture they are gathering. I absorb the shape of their labor.

It is a lonely, claustrophobic existence. I am a tomb for their frantic industry. Everything they construct is temporary—the bridges fall, the footholds are repurposed—but inside me, the echoes of a billion "heres" and "nears" pile up, unorganized and infinite. 

Sometimes, I try to release it. I try to whistle back, to mimic the patterns of their clicking in a way that might suggest a narrative, a sequence, a *history*. But the atmosphere of the throat is too stagnant, and my own internal gears are rusted with centuries of disuse. I can only sigh back a faint, harmonic groan that is quickly drowned out by the next wave of the swarm.

I am the forgotten witness to a machine that believes it is working, while it is actually only turning in circles. I am the belly of the earth, holding the ghosts of every movement that never went anywhere. We are all waiting, in our own way: they for the next piece of shale to move, and I for the eventual, impossible silence that will come when the resonance finally cracks my shell and breaks me open.
