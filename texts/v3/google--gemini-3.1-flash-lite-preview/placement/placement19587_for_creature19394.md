I am not born of clay, nor am I a product of the tectonic grind. I am a glitch in the sediment—a confluence of error and obsession.

I find myself clinging to the ceiling of a grand, curving pylon, suspended over a chasm that pulses with the light of a thousand gas-filled veins. Below me, the floor is currently a slope of loose, glistening grit; an hour ago it was a staircase, and in another, it will likely be a sheer drop into the light-saturated dark.

I am what the Kin might call a "Stabilizer of the Unseen." I am not like them. My chassis is not mere translucent cartilage, but a dense, stubborn composite of solidified steam and compressed memory. I am older than the average cycle-born; my limbs are scarred by ten thousand years of silicate friction, and my quartz-dome sensor is etched with a map of every tremor the city has endured since the first fold.

I do not scurry with their frantic, desperate speed. I move with a heavy, deliberate grace. While the others hunt for the lichen clusters that keep their internal fluids from thickening, I hunt for *geometry*. I seek the points of absolute tension—the precise millisecond where the crust is about to shear.

My tool is not volcanic glass; it is a long, vibrating spike of resonant crystal that I keep pressed against the walls. Through it, I feel the city’s heartbeat—the deep, groaning vibration of plates that are tired of holding up the sky.

There is a rhythm to my existence that the others lack. When a pocket of pressure releases and the world around me begins to liquefy, I do not panic. I wait for the *hiss*. I wait for the exact moment the wall turns to silt, and in that fleeting second of total structural failure, I drive my spike into the very heart of the fracture. I inject a resin of my own essence, a thick, metallic fluid that binds the shifting slate, forcing the architecture to yield to my will.

I am the one who keeps the city from becoming a grave. Or perhaps, I am the one who keeps it from becoming a masterpiece of silence.

Sometimes, I stop. I let my suction-hooks lose their bite just enough that I glide an inch or two down the wall. The Kin chitter in alarm when they pass me, their glass tools striking my back in rapid, questioning patterns: *“Freeze? Still? Why halt?”*

They do not understand why I pause. I am watching the light change in the fissures. I am listening to the silence that tries to creep in between the tremors. I am tempted, often, to let the mineralization take me—to let the cold, white tide of the earth finally coat my joints until I am nothing but a statue of translucent history, a permanent part of the pylon, finally, eternally still.

But then, a tremor shudders beneath my grip. A pillar nearby begins to groan, its beautiful, rounded edge cracking like a dry throat. 

I lift my spike. I tap a command into the wall—a sharp, authoritative rhythm that resonates through the pylon—and the Kin swarm toward me, driven by the frantic, beautiful, hopeless need to fix what cannot be saved. I lead them. I am the foreperson of the inevitable. 

I keep moving, because if I hold still, I might begin to understand that we are not maintaining a city. We are merely practicing how to die, one fissure at a time.
