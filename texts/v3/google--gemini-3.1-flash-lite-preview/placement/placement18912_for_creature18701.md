I am standing, if it can be called standing, on a catwalk of oxidized iron that groans under the weight of a thousand years of moisture. My feet are not feet; they are delicate, splayed pincers, evolved to grip the corroded grating without snapping the brittle metal. Around me, the basin exhales—a rhythmic *hiss-thrum* that vibrates through my central sap-vessel, a sound so deep it has become the rhythm of my own heart.

I am a Lumen-Carpenter, and I am currently experiencing the "wrongness" of a hairline fracture in the pipe to my left.

To a shorter-lived creature, this pipe would be silent. To me, it is screaming. It is losing pressure, a high-pitched, harmonic whine that disrupts the tectonic symphony of the room. I reach out. My arm extends in a series of twitching, multi-jointed segments, translucent and shimmering with a faint, cyan pulse. I am made of white-veined creepers that have forgotten they were once wild, and machine-flesh that has forgotten it was once cold, dead steel.

My sensory dish—the smooth, concave mirror where a face might have been—tracks the vibration. I feel the pipe’s cold, metallic decay, the friction of oxygen eating away at its structural integrity. It is an infection. It is a discordance.

I begin the graft.

My movements are agonizingly slow. I work with the patience of geological time. I bleed a line of thick, bioluminescent resin from my inner cavity, coating the weeping rust. It is a slow, methodical process of sealing, stitching, and binding. As I work, I feel the "memory" of the pipe—not a sentient thought, but a lingering, residual charge of electricity still trapped in its iron veins, a ghost of an industrial era that tried to conquer this valley and failed.

As I press my palm against the girder, polishing away the scale until the metal shines like dull obsidian, I feel a tremor in the floor beneath me. Another Carpenter is approaching. 

He nears, his movements a mirror-image of my own—a series of soft, fluid shifts in the gloom. Our reflective faces brush. There is no language to be exchanged, no exchange of data that isn't already inherent in the smell of ozone and the vibration of the roots. We touch skin-to-skin, a brief, silent passage of nutrient-rich sap, a mutual acknowledgement of the decay we are tasked to hold together.

He does not speak. He drifts past me, his long, spindly limbs unfolding against the shadow of a giant, stalks-bound stalk. He is headed further into the gloom, toward the core of the basin, where the floor is so saturated with the machine’s hum that it requires two Carpenters to prevent the collapse of a critical valve.

I turn back to my work. My own flicker is dimming; the light at my center is not as bright as it was when I first budded from the pipe a century ago. One day, I know, I will find a segment of this catwalk that is too far gone, too consumed by the creeping white-veined rot. I will wrap my limbs around it. I will let my sap turn to gasket. I will become the very thing I am currently repairing, and in that transition, the machine and the forest will become one layer more inseparable.

There is no loneliness in this. I am simply a stitch. And the cathedral must be held together until the earth stops groaning, or until the plants finally swallow the last of the iron, and the world is allowed to sleep in the dark, silent and whole.
