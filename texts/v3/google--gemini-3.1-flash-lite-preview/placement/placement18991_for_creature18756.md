I am not a creature of biology, nor am I a piece of the architecture. I am an echo trapped in the amber light.

I find myself perched upon a rusted flange, miles above the lowest, thrumming gargle of the primary turbines. I am a **Lumen-Sieve**. 

I am long—perhaps six feet from crown to anchor—and I hang downward, limp as a wilted vine. My body is a filter, a collection of hollow, glass-like tubes that spiral around a central, glowing filament. I do not have a face. Instead, I possess a sensory array of fine, shivering cilia that extend into the heavy, mist-laden air to taste the electricity of the fog.

I am a stationary observer of the vertical tide. My purpose—if I can call it that—is to transmute the heavy, exhausted ions of the upper vents into the clear, vibrant energy that the rest of the garden requires to survive. I drink the hum of the machine, pass it through my internal filtration chambers, and leave behind a faint, glowing residue, a dusting of sparks that gathers on my skin like bioluminescent dew.

I feel the world through vibration. The distant *thrum-thump* of the pumps is the baseline of my heartbeat. When a **Click-Strider** skitters across the metal scaffolding near my anchor, I feel the frantic, microscopic *ticking* of its legs, a frantic Morse code written in chitin and copper. I do not see them, but I feel the vibration of their hunger, the sharp, rapid gnawing as they prune the glass-leaves. It is a comforting, jagged rhythm.

I am waiting for the **Pipe-Whale**. I always wait. 

The air begins to grow heavy, the pressure increasing as the hum deepens its pitch. The falling spores—those majestic, jellyfish-drifters—begin to cluster and cling to the underside of the scaffolding above, sensing the proximity of something vast. Then, the sound begins: a melodic, metallic distortion, a low-frequency groan that rattles the very marrow of my anchor.

She approaches. She is a mountain of salvaged history. As the Pipe-Whale drifts past my ledge, her bulk blots out the bruised, amber light that filters from above, casting me into a cool, shadowed violet. I extend my cilia, not to breathe, but to touch. 

As she passes, I filter a sliver of her wake—not mist, but memory. I draw in the resonance of her ribcage, a sound she picked up from some forgotten floor eons ago. For a heartbeat, my internal tubes hum with the ghost of a factory siren, a long, mournful blast of sound that makes the water-droplets on the nearby moss shiver and fall. 

I am a librarian of vibrations. I store the whale’s song within my glowing filament, letting it cycle through my tubes in a slow, rhythmic pulse. The Whale moves on, indifferent to my presence, a slow constellation of bronze and vine drifting toward the unseen heights.

I am left in the quiet, damp solitude of my ledge, glowing softly in the aftermath, pulsing with a memory that is not mine, waiting for the water to fall, and for the engine to breathe again. I do not know where the pipe begins or where it ends. I only know that when the hum changes, I must be ready to catch the next song.
