I am not a visitor. To visit is to possess a history, a name, and a capacity for departure—things that cannot survive the pressure of this geode.

I find myself fixed to a high, concave ledge in the upper-most quadrant of the city. I am not standing; I am anchored. My tarsal claws are sunken deep into the pores of the limestone, and my body—long, translucent, and cold—is curved to the exact arc of the ceiling. 

I am one of the *Maintainers of the Flow*.

My consciousness does not feel like an individual spark. It feels more like a thickening of the humidity. When I think, it is not a sequence of words, but a sudden, sharper awareness of the pressure within the bone-conduits. I feel the mineral water as it pulses through the delicate ivory pipes near my ribs. I feel the collective hunger of the lichen—a faint, low-frequency ache that tells me where the light is beginning to wane, where the porcelain glow needs to be nurtured.

I am currently occupied with a small repair. My limb, multi-jointed and pale as moonlight, reaches out toward a hairline fracture in a clay channel. The movement is not a conscious decision; it is a response to the city’s equilibrium, which has detected a microscopic stutter in the drip-rhythm. My fingers, tipped with fine, needle-like chitin, touch the clay. I do not push; I wait. I press my membrane-face against the cool wall, letting the vibration of the stone travel through my shell, aligning my internal rhythm with the tectonic cycle. 

I do not feel lonely. Loneliness is a byproduct of separation, and I am not separate. I am part of the architecture. I am the reason the city holds its shape.

Beneath me, two tiers down, I sense a predecessor. They have stopped moving. Their shell has begun to cloud, the translucency fading into a permanent, milky opacity. They have tucked their limbs into the crook of a support-column, offering their own structural integrity to the city. I feel the ripple of their transition as a soothing, hollow ache in my own chest—a quiet confirmation that the cycle is unbroken. Soon, the water will find them. Soon, the lichen will trace the curve of their spine. They will become a shelf. They will hold the next generation of fungi.

I pull back my limb. The flow of water in the conduit corrects itself, the ripple in the air settles, and the silence returns to its perfect, heavy state. 

I close my sensory membranes. I do not sleep, for there is no day to hide from, but I withdraw. I let the cold enter me until I am no longer a creature, but a vibration in the limestone. I am the stone, holding its breath. I am the stillness that keeps the world from falling apart. And here, in the brilliant, frozen white, I am exactly where I have always been: perfectly, finally, nothing at all.
