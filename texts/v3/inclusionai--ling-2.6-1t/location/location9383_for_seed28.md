The hollow is not a crater but a thumbprint pressed into a larger skin that has forgotten it is skin.  
Low, intimate, pocket-scale: you could walk its long axis in an hour and still have breath left to listen. Yet inside this small bowl the world behaves as if it were a city, not a wilderness. It has blocks, lanes, aqueducts of absent water, and a grammar of load-bearing silence. The built does not tower; it sprawls low, hugging the ground as if afraid of the brightness above, laying itself in terraces, causeways, and colonnades of sun-cured mineral paste and knapped-stone beams. The architecture remembers that it was once dug more than built—excavated, then elaborated—so walls are at once foundation and relic.

There is no water, and the land admits this without grief. Instead, a white mineral rime coats everything like a dried lacquer of ancient sea. It crunches underfoot, squeaks when you drag a hand along it, and glints with a brightness that feels almost metallic. The light here is not gentle; it is a loudness. It pours in from a sky that seems closer than it has any right to be, flooding each alley, bleaching color to chalk and silver, and pressing heat into the ground until the very stones seem to hum. This is a warm pocket that keeps its warmth like breath held behind teeth.

The vegetation, nearly lush against such austerity, has learned to brag. Fleshy fronds, braided vines, and broad porous leaves crowd wherever a seam in the mineral permits. They do not look thirsty; they look smug. Their greens are too many greens—sulfur, verdigris, bruised jade—and they rustle with a papery insistence that refuses to be background. Their roots have gone urban, threading through conduits and colonizing the undersides of walkways, knitting the city’s edges into a living grout. In places they form tunnels of leaf and stem, arches over forgotten thoroughfares, screens that flicker and clatter whenever anything passes.

Erosion has had time enough to be polite, not frantic. It has rounded corners, softened edges, written a patina across every surface. That patina tells you this place has been exactly as it is for long enough to have settled into its own habits. The hollow feels in-its-own-state: not frozen, not preserved, but comfortably used, as if the land agreed to its arrangement and has since stopped resisting.

The density is a kind of generosity in reverse. Almost nothing per volume—vast gaps between presences. Streets are wide; plazas are broad and mostly bare; the built sits lightly on the land, leaving room for brightness to pool and for heat to circulate. The emptiness is deliberate, even theatrical, as if the city needs space to hear itself.

And it must hear itself, because the sound is deafening.

This is not the noise of engines or industry—tech here is primitive, hand-lashed and hand-turned—but the noise of things insisting on being themselves. A mineral wind, stiff and dry, clatters through leaf corridors and whistles through apertures like a player testing every key of an unfamiliar instrument. The vegetation itself is percussive: fronds snapping, stems popping as they heat and cool, seed pods bursting with little papery gunshots. Under it all is the city’s own voice: feet on mineral crust, a shifting chorus of clicks and scrapes, low groans from beams settling, the soft thock of tools shaping softer stone. When the wind rises, the brightness seems to gain timbre, and the hollow becomes a drum you can stand inside.

The fauna is rare, and perhaps wise to be so. What lives here does so by becoming part of the sound. Lizards with glass-thin scales clink as they run. Winged things, few and translucent, ride thermals that rise from the baked streets, their wingbeats clicking like castanets. In the hottest hours, predators are less animals than accidents of heat and motion—slides of stone, sudden falls of mineral skin, the city itself shedding a moment that catches and crushes. Even the plants have their appetites: bladders that snap shut with a sound like a coin dropping on tin, roots that contract with a wet crack and pull nutrients from dust.

The weirdness is not an intrusion but a habit. Here, light has weight. You can feel it leaning on your shoulders, see it pooling in hollows like milk. At certain angles it seems to braid itself into the mineral crust, leaving temporary filaments that hum until a vibration from below or a gust from the side unwinds them. Time behaves with a loose joint: some afternoons feel elongated, as if the sun pauses to inspect each leaf; some nights—or their dry, cool equivalents—contract into moments that flash and are gone. The city seems to know this, and has built rituals into its forms: alignments that catch light and sound at once, chambers that amplify a single footfall into a chorus, streets that funnel wind so it whistles in intervals, like a language forgetting its words.

The dynamic pulse of the place is both biological and civic. There are cycles of heating and cooling that drive flows as tangible as tides. Warm air rises through leaf tunnels; cooler air drains from higher edges, setting up currents that carry pollen, sparks of mineral dust, and the occasional seed like messages. The urban layout encourages this breath: low canals of shade, open plazas that act as lungs, and perforated walls that let the hollow inhale and exhale. The vegetation participates with its own tempo—leaves opening to brightness like hands catching coins, roots shifting subtly at night, rearranging the city from underneath by fractions that over years become streets.

Rules of this world are few but firm. Nothing here is allowed to hoard moisture, so every surface is either porous or sloped, and even the most cunning architecture leaks or evaporates. Brightness is both resource and authority; to live here is to learn its angles, its glints, its sudden intensities. The city respects the land by not filling it, and the land respects the city by not swallowing it. Sound is a form of truth: a place that is too quiet is assumed to be ill or pretending. Even silence, when it comes, is textured—mineral settling, a held breath, a room cooling.

And still, life persists and flourishes in its rare, careful way. Between the built and the wild, a third thing has grown: a hybrid etiquette of crack and clatter, of shade and glare, of mineral and leaf. You can walk these wide, low streets and feel the city listening, feel the brightness weighing in your bones, feel the land’s own pulse beneath the soles of your feet, steady as a heart that has learned to beat in rhythm with the noise.

The hollow is small but not cramped. It is empty but not vacant. It is loud with being alive, alive with being loud, and above all it is a city that has learned, in a world without water, how to sing to the light until the light sings back.
