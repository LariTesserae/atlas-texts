The room is older than rooms were meant to be, a pocket scraped into the world and left to settle into its final, flattened form. It sits at the bottom of everywhere, hardly rising above the datum of memory, yet it is dense with presences: bodies stacked, breathing, clicking, shedding, crawling over one another in a living crust that never thins. There is no water to speak of, only a dry mineral tang, like licked stone, and the air carries a coolness that settles on flanks and antennae without chilling the deeper furnace of motion. Vegetation is absent or reduced to mineral cousins—brittle films that look like frosted schematics, glass-veined crusts that never unfurl. Life here is fauna, and it is overwhelming.

The space is not large. You could nearly span it with your arms if you were shaped for such a gesture, but it refuses to feel small because it is packed so thoroughly. Layers upon layers: shells upon shells, chitin upon chitin, industry upon industry. The floor is a mosaic of worn alloy and fossilized tread, scored by loops and grooves that have eroded into near-horizontals, smoothed by ages of traffic that never intended to be gentle. Walls lean inward slightly, not from weakness but from the patient pressure of bodies that have, over epochs, learned to use every vertical inch. The ceiling is a lattice of pipes, conduits, and vents that exhale warm drafts at odd intervals, stirring clouds of fine metallic dust into slow cyclones.

Light is normal, which here means a bruised brightness that spills from shielded lamps bolted to ribbed struts, from indicator panels blinking in languages of amber and bone-white, from the occasional flare of a furnace mouth behind louvered grates. Shadows do not pool; they are carved out and hurried along by movement. The atmosphere hums with a loudness that is never simply noise: it is the overlay of engines ticking through their cycles, of shearing gears, of pumps that have forgotten how to pause, of a million claws and pads drumming out rhythms older than intention. There is a language in the sound—threat, courtship, maintenance, warning—stacked so thick that it becomes terrain itself.

Ecology is a churning engine. Predator and prey have worn each other into complementary shapes, their interactions now industrial in regularity. Creatures with faceted eyes and too many joints traverse scaffolds, carrying fragments of machine and mineral to nests that are also foundries, repair yards, larders. Some secrete coolants that bead and roll like misplaced rain, harvested by others who lick the droplets from polished plates. There are scavengers born to graze on oil-slick and oxidized filings, and hunters who ride the drafts between stacks, dropping in spirals like weighted thoughts. The weirdness is gentle but persistent: organisms that incorporate salvaged lenses into carapaces to see themselves seeing; colonies that build chimneys not for smoke but to tune the room’s resonance; symbioses in which a partner’s heartbeat is used as a metronome for a mill.

Built things are scattered but significant. A single tower of riveted plates rises from the center, its purpose long reduced to landmark and roost. Around it, partial arcologies cling like barnacles, their doors sealed or swung open to reveal interiors that have been colonized down to the bolt. Markings remain—serial numbers scoured nearly smooth, warning stripes faded into pale ghosts, directional arrows that now point only to more crowding. There are engines that run without fuel, driven instead by the compression of motion itself, their cycles turning because the crowd insists they turn. Factories have been swallowed by their own output; conveyors loop and knot into braids that support bridges and perches.

Rules of the world are few and stubborn. Gravity points down with a patient insistence, but so does attention: everything tends toward contact. Momentum is conserved in social form; to stop is to be incorporated. Temperature is cool at the margins and warm at the core, regulated not by climate but by density, by the sheer friction of existing together. Time is erosion; the room has settled into its oldest-stable-state, and change happens only as ornament, as variation within a theme that will outlast every participant.

Movement is constant. Fauna pour along canyons of machinery, spilling from maintenance hatches, cascading down worn inclines. Wings beat in synchronized bursts, not for flight but to stir the air into useful currents. There are moments when the entire room seems to inhale—a lull in the clatter, a collective intake of coolness—before erupting again into the business of being alive. In this pocket, survival is a matter of calibration: of finding the pressure that holds you intact, of learning which sounds mean danger, which silences mean opportunity.

If you stand long enough, you begin to feel polished by it, your edges rounding to fit the crowd, your senses attuned to the pitch of industry, the weight of watchful eyes, the cool kiss of ancient stone and newer steel. The place is not hostile, exactly; it is insistent. It has room, but only by refusing to leave any. It is loud with life, and the life is loud with purpose, even if that purpose has been worn down to a hum, a single note sustained by many throats, many gears, many bodies pressed together in the dark, cool heart of the world.
