The world here is a city in the process of drowning slowly, a high basin of warm, breathing water that has not yet decided whether to be sea or sky. Elevation means little in the usual sense; height is measured in fathoms of bright air still trapped between rooftops, in the thin ribs of bridges that have not learned to surrender. The flood came not as a breaker but as a permission, seeping upward through joints and foundations, filling streets like ink into unsqueezed cloth. What remains above the surface is a town stitched together from primitive means—knotted vine, cured hide, bone-riveted wood, simple copper clasps—arranged with an urban insistence: districts, thoroughfares, plazas half-roofed in amber thatch, towers whose lower halves are already pillars for algae.

Water stands at nearly three, but it is not the ocean’s blunt force. It is atmospheric-water, a heavy, luminous presence that pools in cups, cisterns, hollowed stones, and the cupped hands of statues whose features have begun to drift. The temperature is a patient warmth, like breath held behind skin, and it keeps the flood from turning cold or cruel. Because erosion is near zero, edges resist blur: new stone still holds the memory of the chisel, wood still carries the scent of resin, mud is fresh enough to remember the hand that pressed it. The landscape is under construction even as it sinks; scaffolds made of bound reed lean into currents like questions, walkways extend to piers that stop shy of the water, as if waiting for the tide to offer its opinion.

Vegetation is lush but not riotous, a green insistence that has learned to share. Gardens float on rafts of woven root; orchards are trained along nets so that fruit hangs suspended between air and brine. Leaves are large, waxy, cupped to collect the dim light that filters down as though through gauze. Fauna are rare and quiet, slipping between pilings or gliding beneath walkways with a languid curiosity. The density of the place is high: layers press close, balconies overlap, laundry brushes laundry, ladders braid into ladders, and every surface seems to have been asked to carry one more thing. The urban plan is evident but soft, an agreement rather than a decree, as if the town were still negotiating its own shape with the water.

Scale is regional without being boundless; you can glimpse the neighboring ridge of houses across a canal the color of old honey, hear faint bells from a district you could reach by rowing if you had the patience. Weirdness lives in the small adjustments the world makes to keep itself whole: columns that widen at the top to cradle pools, staircases that descend only to rise again in a different district, doorways framed by shells that click like teeth when the tide changes. Water does not behave the way it was told; it gathers in rooms and remembers conversations, or rises in slender columns to touch ceilings and leave maps of moisture that fade by morning.

Light is dim, not from cloud but from the water’s own absorption, as if brightness has agreed to linger rather than arrive. The sky is a pale ceiling streaked with cloud-rifts that hold the color of bruised fruit. Sound is mostly quiet, but not silent: the soft knock of timber against timber, the hush of water threading through gratings, the low thrum of pumps made from hide and hollowed gourd, operated by hand, tended by people whose hands are etched with fine lines of mineral and time. There are songs, too, short and circular, taught to keep the water friendly.

Tech is primitive yet exact: rope knowledge, joinery, the geometry of weight and counterweight, simple metal plates sealed with pitch, ceramic pipes that carry water uphill by capillary patience. There is no engine, only repetition and leverage and careful listening. The dynamic of the place is slow; change arrives in increments too small to alarm, like the almost-imperceptible rise of water that makes a step into a threshold over the course of a season, or the slow silting of a plaza that turns market-stones into islands.

In this submerged high town, life has not retreated so much as adjusted its posture. People move along causeways with jars strapped to their backs, trade in small boats carved from single trunks, sleep in lofts where the floor is sometimes wet and sometimes not. The water is not an enemy; it is a collaborator, still learning its manners, and the town is learning to be partly fluid. The result is a dense, layered, embryonic city of warm water and patient wood, dim-lit and softly strange, holding itself together long enough to become something older, or perhaps something newer, or perhaps something that belongs to neither category but to the slow, thick middle where building and flooding converse without either winning.
