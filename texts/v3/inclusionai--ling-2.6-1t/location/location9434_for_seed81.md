The city does not rise from ground so much as sit inside a bowl the sky has been persuaded to hold. At nearly three kilometers above the ancient datum, the air is thin, bright with heat, and yet it clings like wet gauze—condensation sweating from every cold-born seam, beading on ferroceramic, dripping in slow, deliberate rhythms from eaves that have never known rain as anything but a hiss. The water is everywhere and almost nothing: thin skins on metal, briny seep from weeping stone, capillary films that map the city like a second, invisible plan. It beads, it slides, it leaves salt-veins that bloom like frost on the hot side of a pan.

There are no plants. In their place, the mineral world has learned to stand in for forests. Cliffs of magnetite and slag hold the pose of trunks; towers of folded basalt and vitrified ash wear canopies of iron-lattice and tensioned glass that catch the dim light and refract it into bruised purples and dull coppers. The city is industrial in its bones—belts, gantries, catwalks, and causeways stitched together with rivets and sealant and the patient math of load-bearing patience. Districts are stacked like geological accidents that decided to be architecture: one layer for filtration, one for heat-exchange, one for the slow turning of engines that do not roar but hum, a sound that settles in the teeth and behind the eyes.

The temperature presses in, almost three degrees of heat on a scale that allows no forgiveness, yet the place does not burn. Something here conducts, radiates, absorbs in an arrangement that keeps flame at the respectful distance of a kept secret. Surfaces are matte, dark, eager to drink photons, then careful to let them go as long-wave sighs. In the hollows between built forms, steam ghosts rise, condense on cooler underbellies, and return as thin, warm drizzle that hisses on the walkways and leaves the air tasting of copper and ozone.

Erosion has had its way, not with softness but with insistence. Corners have learned to be blunt. Facades wear the gloss of handled dust and the matte of old soot. Water has cut runnels that do not look like cuts so much as decisions the city agreed to over centuries. Everything is in balance because everything has been worn to a compromise that fits the bowl and its heat and its thin, wet breath. The scale is open enough that you can see, beyond the next rise, another neighborhood’s smokeless stacks, another cantilever with its train of slow cargo, and beyond that the rim where the sky thins to violet and the horizon tilts slightly, as if unsure which way is down.

The density is near-void. Between the built islands, the space is wide and spare, crossed only by infrequent causeways and the thin, bright paths of aerial trams that drift on magnetic threads. The emptiness is not vacant; it is reserved, like the silence between notes that lets you hear the city’s hum. That sound is mostly quiet—a hush of pumps, the whisper of exchangers, the click of thermal joints expanding and sighing, the far-off thump of something heavy settling into its own heat. Fauna are rare: a few arthropod-analogues armored against the hot wet, their shells the color of cooled piston-rings, and the glint of something with too many legs skating on a film of condensate across a cooling fin. Most life here is industrial, or mineral, or the brief, bright flare of microbial mats in the warm brine that gathers in the lower basins.

Weirdness is the city’s truest citizen. There are rooms that do not fit the outside shape of the building that contains them, corridors that smell of cinnamon and static, water that runs uphill for a stretch before remembering gravity and flushes itself in a sudden, warm torrent. Gravity here is not a law so much as a habit that the city encourages but does not enforce; in certain chambers, one feels lighter near the walls, as if the iron bones are tugging with a faint, polite insistence. There are signs that change their message depending on how long you look, and windows that show not the outside but a plausible memory of somewhere cooler, somewhere quieter.

The place is active, not frantic but busy in the way a body is busy when it keeps itself alive. Heat cycles turn over like lungs: day-swell, night-purge, the basins exhaling warm vapor that the upper intakes drink and cool and send back down as motive force. Engines pulse in staggered rhythms, not for show but because the city needs to keep its blood moving, its metal from dreaming itself into brittle sleep. The water circulates, the heat redistributes, the strange geometries of the place adjust themselves by fractions of millimeters, groaning like a ship finding a new cargo.

And yet for all its industry and strangeness, the city feels like it grew from the bowl rather than being set upon it. The built and the mineral blur where the stone has been coaxed into load-bearing ribs that look organic, and the manufactured has rusted into patterns that look like lichen if you do not look too closely. The dim light, never generous, lends everything a sense of confiding, as if the place is telling you its secrets in a language of temperature gradients and the direction in which water chooses to run.

If you stand at the rim at the hour when the heat softens and the wet air thickens, you can hear the city’s heart in the clink of cooling fins, see its breath in the haze that drapes the lower streets like a wet shawl, and feel, in the slight, warm tug at your boots, that the place is alive in a way that does not need green to prove it. This is a high, hot, empty, industrial city of wet stone and strange hums, where water is a visitor that has overstayed its welcome and stayed anyway, and where the silence between the machines is the loudest thing of all.
