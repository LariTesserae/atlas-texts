The place is a below-ground city grown rather than dug, and the distinction between root and rivet has long since been forgotten. It lies in a broad, shallow bowl of old stone where groundwater seeps persistently through every seam, so that walls never fully dry and the air carries the cool metallic taste of wet iron and crushed chlorophyll. The ceiling is low enough that you can touch it with a raised hand, yet the city extends outward in a labyrinth of rooms and tunnels no larger than a grove or a hamlet, each passage branching into tighter braids until the whole mass feels like one continuous organ.

Everything is built from vegetation that has become structure. Canes as thick as beams arc overhead and knit together into vaults, their surfaces rippled with the scars of decades of slow swelling. Leaves have thickened into shingles, overlapping like fish scales to shed the constant condensation that beads and drips from above. Roots thicken into walls, then hollow into corridors; sap hardens into amber gutters that guide slow runnels of water along the streets. The city is packed to choking: every span, every pocket, every alcove is occupied by another living brace, another lattice, another breathing partition. There is no room for accident; the place has long since filled its own outline and settled into a balance of pressure and growth.

The temperature is warm, the kind that encourages patience. Heat rises from vents where subterranean springs meet long-buried engine cores, and it mingles with the breath of the city itself, which exhales moisture at a steady rate. Surfaces are weathered into soft reliefs, edges rounded by time and the mild acidity of running water. Erosion here is not loss but conversation: stone yields, wood accepts, and together they negotiate a shape that has not changed meaningfully in generations. Even the waterways have established their own cadence, pooling in broad, quiet basins before slipping away through channels lined with resilient mosses that knit themselves into filters.

This is an urban place in the sense of density and function. Districts are defined less by purpose than by rhythm: some quarters thrum with the low grind of pumps and generators, their vegetal towers braced by iron bands and threaded with cables that hum with current; others are given over to slow factories where resins are distilled, fibers pressed into boards, and bioluminescent cultures coaxed into steady, dim glow. Machinery is not foreign here but symbiotic, bolted to trunks and slung beneath vaults, oiled by the same dampness that swells the wood. Smoke, when it comes, is thin and mostly ceremonial, caught by the wet air and dragged down into gutters to be swallowed by root mats that metabolize soot into darker growth.

Light is scarce and carefully husbanded. Most illumination comes from panels of cultured algae set into frames of hardened leaf, casting a greenish, watery luminescence that pools on slick floors and glints off rain-slick cane. In corners, phosphorescent fungi bloom in slow pulses, marking thresholds and junctions. The dimness encourages sound to travel, and the city is loud with it: water ticking and slapping against stone, the creak and groan of living architecture adjusting to its own weight, the steady percussion of pumps and flywheels, the hiss of steam released into moss-lined vents. There are no animals here; the city belongs to the vegetal and the mechanical, and the absence of fauna only deepens the sense that this is a world of process without spectators.

Movement through the place is slow. Corridors open into chambers where water gathers in broad, trembling mirrors, reflecting the heavy tangle of growth above. Bridges of woven vine span these basins, damp ropes swaying almost imperceptibly. Staircases spiral around trunks, steps worn concave by generations of bare feet and padded soles, each descent accompanied by the soft plash of runoff finding its way downward. At the heart of the city lies a reservoir, more root than pool, where water rises and falls by fractions of an inch over weeks, its surface dimpled by falling drops and the occasional pulse from a pump.

The rules of this world are those of integration and endurance. Nothing is wasted; everything is cycled. Waste heat warms beds of propagating shoots; runoff feeds walls that thicken in response; metal rusts into beds for fungi that feed machines that pump water that feeds walls again. Time here is not urgent. Growth is incremental, repairs are constant, and the city expands only by fractioning itself into finer and finer channels, always dense, always packed. To live here is to accept that you are living inside a metabolism that includes iron, water, wood, and current, all working at a volume that never demands attention but will not be ignored.

In the end, the place is less a settlement than a single, long-lived breath held in the dark: wet, warm, crowded, loud, and deeply patient, with every surface alive and every echo belonging to the city itself.
