The world begins as a brightness that does not come from a sun but from the fact that the ground has forgotten how to be dark.  

It is an end-of-erosion plain, flattened by epochs until altitude is less a height than a rumor. The elevation is low, yet the scale is continental, so that standing anywhere feels like standing inside the belly of a slow, polished planet. There is no water, and what passes for moisture is a memory of damp that has been baked into the mineral lattice and then glazed. The surface is made—total, without apology—not of rock in the old sense but of grown mineral skins fused with circuitry, memory-glass, and nameless alloys that have outlived their patents. The ground is dense with presences: strata-thin veneers stacked like pages that refuse to separate, each tuned to a different hum, each bearing the scar of a billion footsteps that never quite arrived.

Vegetation is sparse, not because life is absent but because it has learned to live in seams. Along fractures that look like cracks but are more like deliberate stitches, filaments rise—glass-brush, silicon moss, filaments that drink light instead of rain. They are pale, almost shy, and they withdraw when touched by shadow, though shadows here are uncommon. The light level is high and even, so even the sparse plants glow with a borrowed luminescence, their chlorophyll proxies tuned to wavelengths that would bleach ordinary leaves. Fauna is rare, and when it appears it is more suggestion than body: a glint that resolves into a small, six-legged courier made of folded foil and intent, or a distant smear of motion that might be wind but behaves like hunting.

Because the world is ancient and built, its weather is not water-based but material-based. The air itself is thick with suspended motes—metallic dust, photonic pollen, the shed skins of machines that have become landscape. These particles catch the brightness and create their own micro-climates: halos that drift like slow clouds, pressure fronts made of static charge, rivers of warmth that pool in valleys of glass. The dynamic score is high: the place is alive with activity, but it is the activity of maintenance. The ground shifts almost imperceptibly, plates sliding with the patience of glaciers, realigning circuits, polishing fault lines, erasing and rewriting its own history in light.

Sounds are quiet to the point of silence, but that silence is textured. There is the soft click of expansion, the almost-subsonic murmur of energy threading through veins beneath the surface, the faint rasp of glass on glass when the ground breathes. These sounds do not travel far; they are absorbed by density, turning into heat, into more light.

Rules here are stitched into the substance. Time moves but wears down slowly; cause and effect are polite with each other, never rushing. To break something is to invite reconstruction, not because the place is alive with malice but because it is alive with purpose. The weirdness is moderate—a sense that angles are slightly more patient than they should be, that distances shrink when observed steadily, that reflection sometimes precedes the thing reflected. Technology is beyond modern: it does not announce itself as tools but as texture. Surfaces compute by being surfaces. Navigation is done by letting the ground read you and offering paths that feel like memories you never had.

At dusk, which is more a dimming of brightness than a departure of light, the plain offers its final courtesy: the dust settles into patterns that resemble maps of places that never existed, and the sparse vegetation pulses once, as if counting heartbeats across the planet. Then the brightness returns, and the constructed world, ancient and dense and fully itself, continues its slow, luminous work of being finished.
