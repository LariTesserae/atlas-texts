The world begins as a seam rather than a surface.  
At low elevation it does not sit; it is still being laid down, as if the ground were a ribbon of cooling solder unspooling across a continent-sized forge. The heat is not merely high—it is structural, a working temperature that keeps edges pliable. Everything you see has been poured, pressed, or riveted into being, yet the making is not finished. The landscape is total artifice and embryonic at once: a manufactured crust that still remembers it was once liquid.

Water is a guest that refuses to leave. At 1.7 it is neither lake nor river but a persistent sweating of the made world. Condensation beads on corrugated metal plains and gathers in gutters that were never meant to hold so much. Rivulets trace the fault lines between plates that have not yet locked, silver threads wandering across copper-skinned valleys, pooling in shallow basins that hiss when they meet hotter seams. The wetness does not nurture; it negotiates, carrying heat away grain by grain, polishing edges that are still trying to decide their shape.

There is no vegetation, only suggestion of it. What might have been lichen or moss is instead mineral frost—salt efflorescence and borax flowers that bloom overnight and crumble by dawn. In places the surface wears a skin of glassy slag that cracks like thin ice, revealing underlayers of folded alloy the color of cooled forge-scale. From a distance these look like lichen on stone, but up close they are tessellations of absence, geometries of not-life.

Temperature reads 2.6: bearable only because the air is thin and the humidity steals breath. Heat shimmers do not rise; they crawl, thick and deliberate, as if the atmosphere itself is a viscous fluid trying to decide whether to be gas or metal. The sky is a low vault, pressed down by the scale of what lies below, its light dimmed to the color of a banked fire. Clouds, when they form, are not water but condensate of lofted particulates—dust from grinding, filings from cutting—drifting like ash that forgot it was ash.

Elevation is modest, yet the scale is vast, and the two together create a horizon that feels close enough to touch and too large to comprehend. You can walk for days and never rise high enough to see the edge of the making. The place has its own weather, not of rain but of outgassing: plumes of steam and metallic vapor that drift and condense into curtains, then fall back as warm drizzle that smells of ozone and old solder.

Erosion is almost nonexistent, which makes the wearing that does occur feel deliberate. Each scratch, each pock, each warped beam looks like a first draft of damage. Surfaces are fresh to the point of embarrassment, as if the world is ashamed of its own roughness. Wind does not smooth; it polishes in patches, leaving islands of matte finish surrounded by skin still glossy from the pour.

Builtness is total. There are no boulders, only shaped masses; no soil, only packed aggregate and grates. The ground is a composite deck that buckles where it was laid too hot, rising in blisters that split to reveal ribs of conduit, still warm, still humming. Bridges do not span—they stitch, their undersides latticed with hand-forged braces and guy-wires that sing when the wind finds them. Towers are not built to stand but to be stood upon while work continues, their flanks cratered with landings and scaffolds that have fused where metal met metal in the heat.

Technology is simple in means but grandiose in effect. Tools are hand-cranked winches, mallets of layered ceramic, tongs big enough for a door. Fasteners are rivets, clamps, wedges—solutions that admit they may need to be undone. There are no engines, only conduits that channel pressure, vents that sigh when the load shifts. The craft is pre-industrial in gesture and colossal in execution, as if a village of smiths had been given a planet to finish before morning.

Light is scarce and hoarded. The dimness is not dark so much as deferred brightness, a glow held behind a scrim. Sources are few: gaps in the canopy of vapor, seams where heat leaks as visible red thread, and the occasional phosphor strip hammered into a joint to mark where one maker ended and another began. At night—or what passes for it—the land cools just enough to reveal the colors it carried all along: bruised purples, liver-reds, the blue of a bruise on a bruise.

Fauna are absent. Movement is mechanical and slow: the settling of a deck, the groan of a girder finding its set, the skitter of a rivet-head rolling downhill. What sounds emerge are mostly quiet, but the quiet is textured. It is the sound of a vast workshop in which every worker has paused at once, leaving only the materials remembering what to do. A drip strikes metal and rings. A plate shrinks and ticks. Somewhere far off, a length of pipe cools and splits with a sound like a snapped cable.

Weirdness is the rule, not the exception. Reality does not break here; it frays in controlled bursts. Lines that should be straight develop sympathetic curves. Gravity forgets its name in patches, so water runs uphill until it remembers where down is, then hurriedly pretends it never left. Distances elongate when you are not looking; a journey that took an hour yesterday takes three today, and the landmarks have not moved—only the space between them has stretched, thin as taffy.

The landscape is turbulent without motion. It churns in place, reconfiguring its own logic. Sections of floor rise to become temporary walls, then slump back into pathways, still warm enough to tack your boots. Bridges invert to become gutters. Towers lean into each other and fuse, creating arches that were never planned but feel inevitable once they exist. There is no master plan visible, only a consensus of making that changes its mind as fast as the metal can cool.

And through it all runs the water, patient and practical, learning the new streets as they are born, filing down the sharp corners of a world that is still trying to be solid. It does not shape the place so much as witness it, bead by bead, drop by drop, teaching the made world how to be weathered before it is even finished.

In the end you realize you are standing not on a map but inside one that is still being drawn. The heat, the wet, the dimness, the total made-ness—they are not qualities of the place but its pulse. The place is alive only in the sense that a forge is alive: hungry, unfinished, holding its breath until the next rivet is set, the next seam poured, the next patch of sky dimmed a little further by the steam of its own becoming.
