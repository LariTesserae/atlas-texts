The place does not lie below you so much as hold you at its own chest level, because elevation here is a polite fiction of altitude: the ground is high, yes, but it is high the way a lung is high in the body, lifted by breath it refuses to exhale. Warmth settles like a cloth that has been wrung out and hung to dry—still damp, still holding heat in its threads. Light arrives in the ordinary manner, as if the sky had simply forgotten to turn it special, yet the brightness behaves strangely, pooling instead of spreading, as though it too were a liquid waiting for permission to run.

Water is the second grammar of this place. It is not a river, lake, or sea, but a memory of wetness that never quite decides to become past. The substrate does not show itself because there is, properly speaking, no substrate—only built, and built so thoroughly that the word nature has to be borrowed from somewhere else to even be said. Everything you stand on, lean against, or see stretching into the open distance is constructed of living plant-stuff that has been trained, coaxed, fused, and persuaded to harden into architecture without ceasing to breathe. Walls vein themselves shut then open again; floors flex when you step, not from softness but from etiquette. The space is total in its making: every beam is grown, every lattice is grafted, every roof unfurls like a leaf that decided to become a ceiling and stayed that way.

And yet the region is sparse. The built masses are scattered, leaving wide gaps of bright air between them, as if the place had been laid out according to a rule of respectful distance. The emptiness is not empty of meaning, though; it hums. Sound is loud here, not with machinery—for tech has never arrived, and no device scratches or clicks—but with the noise of tension being held in place. There is a low, continuous rasp, like bark on bark, and beneath it a liquid gurgle so constant it has begun to sound like a note held by something enormous. At regular intervals, something cracks, pops, or sighs, and the sound rolls away through the open gaps, bouncing back from invisible edges that define the town-or-valley-sized extent without ever quite revealing them.

Vegetation is total, but it has forgotten how to be scenery. It is the material of everything: stairs spiral up from fused roots that spiral up from nothing firmer than their own patience; columns thicken and thin like lungs practicing their alphabet; ceilings bloom with small translucent organs that catch the normal light and let it through, refracted into colors that shift if you stare too long. The wetness seeps from these surfaces continually. Water beads on leaf-roofs and runs in channels carved into living stems, weeping not from injury but from an excess of caring. The atmosphere condenses around you in thin veils, so that you are always walking through a kind of gentle, warm drizzle that never quite wets you and never quite dries.

Fauna is rare, and when it appears it does not behave as if it expects to be seen. A shape detaches itself from a wall that is also a tree and moves with the unconcern of weather. It has too many joints or not enough, depending on when you look. Its passing leaves no trace, as if it had been a ripple in the loudness itself. The ecology is therefore mostly performed by the place: sugars move in slow, deliberate currents beneath translucent skins; fragrances bloom and retract on schedule, as if time were a drum being beaten very far away. The dynamic is never changing; nothing here surges or recedes. The wetness is always just this wet, the warmth always just this warm, the brightness always just this bright. Equilibrium has been set like resin, and the place sits inside it without impatience.

Erosion is embryonic, as if the world had only just begun to consider the idea of edges. There are no scars, only suggestions of where edges might someday soften. Surfaces look new in a way that is not fresh but primordial, as if they had been born fully formed and have not yet decided to age. The raw geology of the place is botanical: layers upon layers of cambium and xylem and something stranger, pressed together with a patience that refuses to become stone.

Weirdness sits in the middle of everything like a guest who has forgotten to leave. It is not the weirdness of breakage but of insistence: that a building can also be a plant, that water can behave like light, that sound can be dense enough to lean against. Rules of the world are simple and absolute: nothing made here was ever meant to be inanimate; nothing wet here wants to stop being wet; nothing high here wants to descend; nothing sparse here wants to crowd itself. The built and the living have married so completely that their children look like rooms, and their grandchildren look like corridors that remember how to blossom.

As you walk, the place adjusts. A hallway you passed an hour ago is slightly more open now, its wall having relaxed. A bridge you have not yet reached is slightly lower, its curve having deepened in sympathy with your approach. The changes are so small they might be imagined, except that the loudness shifts its pitch to match, a choir tuning itself to a body moving through it.

The region extends past you in all directions, open but bounded, a territory sized like a thought you can almost hold. In the gaps between built things, the warm air hangs like a held breath, and the water that never falls and never rises keeps singing. You realize you have stopped looking for a ground beneath your feet, and in that moment, the place lets you know it has been holding you up all along, not with hands, but with a kind of green, wet, loud patience that has no need for tools, no hurry for change, and no intention of ever being anything other than itself.
