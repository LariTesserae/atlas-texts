The world is a drowned cathedral built by no one and inhabited by everything that can swim in cold ink. It floats high, or perhaps it only feels high because every other place you could compare it to has sunk lower, folded into heavier water. Here the surface is not a line but a lid, a skin of bruised glass that breathes. Rain is not weather here; it is architecture. It arrives in drifts and ribs, stacking itself into walls that hold for minutes or years, then dissolve. The air is thick with it, a second ocean hanging above the first, so that to breathe is to sip the storm.

Because nothing grows, the terrain is all edge and patina. Basalt that remembers being poured, iron that remembers being struck, carbon that remembers being squeezed. These materials have finished becoming. They have been scoured so long by water and pressure that their names have softened into adjectives: the slate-slick, the ore-hollow, the flint-fret. Erosion has done its final work and left the place almost polite, rounding promontories into suggestions, carving channels that hesitate before they remember themselves as rivers reversed. The result is a geography of courteous refusal—shelves that dip away just as you trust them, arches that do not collapse but whisper.

The cold is not sharp; it is a patient arithmetic. It subtracts heat in small, regular installments, so that motion costs more, thought slows, and even turbulence feels heavy, like cloth dragged through syrup. Yet the place is turbulent without end. Currents arrive from no visible source, shear against each other, braid into knots, and untie only to retie elsewhere. The water is not deep in the sense of a measurable bottom but deep in the sense of accumulation: layer upon layer of cold history stacked like unspoken rules. The high elevation keeps the pressure moderate, so the water is more like a heavy sky than a crushing depth, and the fauna have learned to fall upward as easily as down.

They are abundant. Shapes that should not be numerous are, as if abundance itself is a form of camouflage. They swarm in constellations, drift in councils, jostle in parades that have no destination. Some have bones like glass needles, some have skins like tarnished foil, some wear shells that look less like protection and more like punctuation. They feed on motion, on the turbulence itself, grazing the shear between two colliding flows, harvesting the static from the rain-lid above. Their sounds are small: clicks felt more than heard, frictions like seeds rolling over stone, the hush of fins parting water that is already parting. Mostly quiet, but busy quiet, the kind that hums in your teeth.

Because everything is built, there is no wild in the old sense. The substrate itself is artifact, seamless and ancient, as if the minerals decided to construct a place to live in and then forgot who they were pretending to be. The construction has no seams, no scars of assembly, only weathering that looks like embroidery. Patterns emerge from the making: grids that are not grids, spirals that refuse to close, symmetries that pivot slightly off center. These are not decorations. They are rules made visible, the logic of a place that has settled into its final form and now polishes that form like a word repeated until it becomes a thing.

Light dims by consent. It filters through the aqueous ceiling as if through felt, arriving in coins and crescents, pooling on the mineral floors and then sliding away. Shadows do not lie beneath things; they cling beside them, companions with their own weight. In this dimness, color withdraws to essentials: black that is only less black, white that is only less cold, occasional iridescence like a rumor passing through.

Weirdness is present but restrained, the way a stone can be unusual simply by being exactly where it is and not apologizing. Sometimes the rain falls up for a while, or the fauna line up to stare at a patch of water that has begun to think out loud, but these events do not feel like violations. They feel like the place remembering it is allowed to change, even at the end of time.

The scale is open without being empty. You can travel for a long time and never repeat a room in the drowned city, yet always feel you are inside the same thought. Space is generous, almost wasteful, with much left between presences, but the turbulence stitches those gaps together with motion. You are never alone, even when you are, because the water itself is crowded with history, with cold, with the quiet industry of things that have outlived their makers and decided to keep going anyway.

What rules govern this place? Simple ones, enforced by repetition. Water does not pool; it lingers. Stone does not break; it remembers. Cold does not kill; it preserves. Motion does not end; it redistributes. Life does not originate; it arranges itself. These rules are soft, like wool pressed against glass, but they are unbending in their softness.

And through it all, the sound: a hush that is full, a quiet that is busy, a storm that is careful. The rain-lid taps, the fauna brush, the currents argue in low voices, and the built world listens, because listening is all it has left to do. This is a place that has finished being made and is now making itself again, slowly, coldly, beautifully, in the dim high water where nothing grows and everything moves.
