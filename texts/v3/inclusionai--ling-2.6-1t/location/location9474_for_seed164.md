The world begins as altitude.  

You stand on a spine that is also a scaffold: a high plain lifted until it grips the lower atmosphere like a cold finger pressing into cloud-wool. The elevation is vast, but not empty in the way of deserts—rather, it is emptied of clutter, thinned of audience, so that every structure can be heard from everywhere. The ground is newly born, its bones still sharp, angles unrounded, as if the planet recently shrugged and brought this tableland up in a single night. Erosion has not yet had time to apologize; basalt and iron-slag cliffs stand with the polite violence of fresh cuts. Water weeps from them in steady threads, because water is present but not abundant—a wetness that hangs like breath on a mirror, sliding down cold stone and collecting in thin, glassy lakes that refuse to freeze completely, yet never warm.

Temperature is a kind of discipline. Cold does not bite; it clarifies. It keeps the air dense enough to carry sound, light enough to travel far, and turns every exhalation into a small lantern that rises and vanishes. The light is normal—whatever that means at this height—so shadows behave as if they belong to a world that runs on schedule, but there is a metallic cast to the day, as though the sun has been polished by industry.

Vegetation is lush in the sense of insistence, not softness. Mosses crowd in electric blues and near-black greens along seams where water seeps. Fern-analogues unfurl stiffly, armored with mineral veins, thriving in the wet-cold like factory gardens fed by runoff. There are no flowers that announce themselves to bees; there are no bees. Fauna is absent—not silent, but subtracted, as if evolution were asked to design a place where meat was unnecessary and noise was compulsory.

Because noise is compulsory.  

The land is loud. Not with living voices but with machinery that has become geographic. This is an industrial place folded into urban plans at continental scale, a city that chose to grow up instead of out, stacking itself into the sky until it became a plateau of its own physics. Towers of riveted alloy and soot-dark composite rise like stalagmites, their tops lost in low cloud. Conveyor belts arc between them, not carrying goods so much as potential, moving slurry that glows faintly from within. Pumps breathe in a rhythm that never falters, because the dynamic is frozen in equilibrium: nothing here is shifting toward an end; it is simply running, stable as a metronome set in concrete.

Strangeness blooms in the gaps.  

Pipes weep not water but a condensate that hums when wind skims it, turning the wet into an instrument. At the centers of the broadest basins, the ground thins into grates under which light smolders, suggesting fires banked for purposes you cannot name. The architecture is layered—cities inside cities, with bridges that lead nowhere except deeper into infrastructure, into districts where the walls themselves seem to have been grown in molds and then machined to look accidental. There are no streets, only causeways of perforated steel that ring underfoot like struck cymbals, amplifying every step into a public announcement.

Atmosphere condenses nightly into pearls of fog that cling to cables and rails, lit from below by sodium and something else, something sour and violet, industrial luminescence that suggests power being invented rather than consumed. The vastness of the place makes weather of its own: updrafts from warm machinery collide with the cold ceiling of the plateau and spin into small, predictable storms that circle the tallest stacks without ever touching ground, like satellites of rain.

You can feel the density of emptiness here—a lowness in the count of things, so that between every tower there is a gulf of air large enough to lose a town in, and yet it is filled with sound, filled with cold, filled with wet industry. The rules of this world are simple and unyielding: water seeks the edges, metal does not rust but sings, light tells the truth but in a dialect of glare and shadow, and time does not pass so much as repeat, a steady-state hum as durable as the stone beneath it.

And still the place grows, not by expanding outward but by thickening inward, adding more layers of structure, more channels for the weeping water, more cold light under the grates. It is a landscape that has decided to be itself forever, loud and high and freshly edged, with nothing soft to interrupt the beautiful, terrible machinery of being.
