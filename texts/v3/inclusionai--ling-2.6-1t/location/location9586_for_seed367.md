The place is a cup of warm dusk cupped in a fold of the world’s old skin. It lies high enough that the air is thin enough to sing through, yet low enough to be cupped like a palm around a basin that never held water. Instead of sea there is a floor of glass-bright mineral, frosted with salt-skin that cracks in tessellations like dried paint, and between the cracks the ground breathes. The whole hollow is barely larger than a town, but it is dense with being, stacked and layered as if every moment had settled and never left.

The temperature is a constant warm that does not scorch but insists, a low hum against skin and stone. Because there is no water to carry heat away, warmth pools and lingers, sinking into fur, into stone, into the marrow of creatures that have long since stopped looking for rivers. The elevation presses gently on ears; footsteps ring like small bells against the mineral skin of the world, and the wind, when it chooses to speak, has to climb to get here, arriving raspy with altitude and age.

Erosion has brought the land to its final grammar. Edges have surrendered to soft curves. Cliffs have slumped into braids of rubble that look as deliberate as braids of hair. Everything is in its oldest-stable-state, which here means not dead but deeply calm, patient to the point of ceremony. Time has scoured away the frantic and left only what can sit without apology.

Even so, the place is overwhelmingly alive. Fauna teem in every layer, on every face, above and below and threading through. Winged things with too many joints stitch the dim air in tight braids, clicking and humming like a loom that never stops. Burrowers with polished carapaces navigate tunnels they never dig, following tunnels that were there before the world cooled. Predator and prey have worn their chase into a choreography so practiced it looks like courtship. Nothing here wants in or out; the cup is enough.

Vegetation is lush in a way that defies the lack of water. Fronds and tubers and creeping mantles drink from the mineral itself, from breath, from condensation scraped from warm skins. Leaves are leathery and dark, with undersides that gleam like oil. Mosses form forests of velvet on the shoulders of boulders that have forgotten they were once peaks. Growth is dense—there is never a handspan without something living, and most things grow in company, stems braided together, roots knotting into cables, trunks bearing balconies for smaller lives.

Where the fauna and flora meet, the town sits—not imposed, but grown. Built things are primitive, yes, but they have been built so long and so often that they have absorbed the rules of the place. Walls are woven from cured stems, stiffened with resin, and laced with polished bone. Paths are laid with flat stones chosen for their warmth underfoot, fitted so tightly that nothing weedy can slip between them. Towers are not tall so much as many; the settlement expands inward and upward in layers, each level borrowing the one below for support, roofs doubling as terraces, gutters catching dust and skin and falling wings. There is no metal worth speaking of, only early metal that remembers being ore, hammered into shapes that apologize for their edges.

Light hovers at a dim level, not dark enough to hide but bright enough to sculpt. The sun or its substitute spends most of its time behind a lid of cloud or altitude, or perhaps the cup is simply turned away. In this dimness, color lives in scent and sound more than in sight. Things announce themselves by rustle, by thrum, by the particular pitch of scales sliding over stone.

Speaking of sound: it is loud. Not violent, but insistent. The place is a parliament of small noises that have learned to shout together. Wings shear the air in synchronized bursts. Feet drum in rhythms that echo off the ancient walls of the cup. There is a constant susurrus from the vegetation itself, leaves rubbing together like whispered arguments, mineral skins shifting with the slow breathing of the earth. Even the silence here has a texture, a hum left behind when the noise dips for a heartbeat.

Life is dynamic through and through. The ecosystem moves in overlapping cycles. Birth and consumption happen with the same casual frequency as chewing. Weather, such as it is, arrives as changes in pressure and scent, warm gusts that roll through the cup carrying news from higher slopes, setting the mineral skin to ticking as it cools and contracts. The fauna respond with migrations that are less about escape than about appetite, flowing from one thicket of growth to another, pursued and pursuing in turns.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute. Nothing comes in; nothing leaves without leaving a mark. Warmth must be shared. Density is a virtue. Erosion is a teacher; whatever stands long enough will be taught to sit. The built things are allowed because they mimic the living things; they do not conquer, they conjugate. Water is not missed so much as reinvented—sweat, condensation, sap, the moisture of breath and membrane.

Fauna wear their overwhelming presence like clothing. Some are small and many, moving as a single coat over stone and stem. Others are singular and magnificent, broad as bridges, slow as continents, their passage rearranging the town by accident and design. Their eyes are polished and patient; their teeth are worn to tools and talismans. When they die, they are taken into the thicket, dismantled with care, their parts returned to the weave of walls and walks and warm floors.

The settlement is alive with craft. Hands shape the world with tools that are little more than extensions of intent. Baskets are plaited from shed wing-casings. Threads are spun from softened stems and the belly-hair of certain burrowers. Resin is coaxed from cuts in trees that do not bleed but sigh. The work is loud, communal, rhythmic: stone on stone, stem on stem, breath on breath.

Above all, the place feels complete. It is a pocket, yes, but a pocket brimming. It has no need of water because it has learned to be its own sea of motion, of touch, of heat. The dimness makes closeness necessary, and the closeness makes the dimness warm. In this high, ancient, crowded cup, life has found a way to be loud without being reckless, dense without being crushed, primitive without being simple.

The landscape is not waiting for anything. It is occupied.
