The place has no childhood. It begins already thick, already crowded, already hot enough to keep memory from hardening into stone.

Elevation is an insult here: spines of basalt and welded tuff thrust two and a half, three kilometers above what used to pass for sea, but the ground does not feel high so much as congested. Ridges crowd against ridges like stalled trains. Cliffs lean inward as if to whisper, and every saddle sags with the weight of bridges, ducts, and scaffolded buttresses that were never removed, only paused. The landscape is not eroded; it is exhaled. Edges are still wet with cooling, still tacky where flows slowed and thought better of solidifying. You can see where the mountain tried to fold itself, changed its mind, and left pleats standing half-finished, mineral tongues lolling out like mistakes in a kiln.

This is mineral life, not its absence. No chlorophyll, but plenty of appetite. Veins of alabaster and sulfur stitch the rock as if the mountain had learned to suture itself. At night the ground glows faintly along those seams—not from fire, but from stored daylight metabolized into slow phosphorescence, a kind of mineral memory sweating its way out. Crystals bloom like mold along faults: amethyst whiskers, citrine fuzz, halite beards. They taste of copper and rust if you lick them, which you should not, because the air is already heavy with evaporated metal, the tang of ore thinking about becoming breath.

Water is a rumor that keeps its word twice a year. A brackish river, thin as a promise, braids through the city’s lower canyons. It arrives with a schedule it invents on the spot, slipping between sluices and evaporative cooling racks, pausing in catch-basins that have become public bathtubs for things that are not quite public. The water does not flow so much as accumulate, pool, hesitate, then decide to be groundwater again, rising as steam through the pavement’s pores. Condensation pearls on the undersides of catwalks, on the bellies of conveyors, on the eyelids of statues made of slag.

The city—because it has been one long town that grew too ambitious to remain modest—hangs in terraces. Walkways stitched with rivets span chasms that are still warm at the soles of your boots. Towers lean like drunks but stand like conspirators, lashed together with steel that has rusted into decorative lace. Factories yawn and exhale in long, tectonic sighs. Their chimneys do not smoke so much as leak light, a pale aureole that settles on rooftops like pollen. Inside, presses the shape of hammers that strike only when nobody is counting, manufacturing silence by compacting it into ingots that are then stacked in vaults labeled “reserve weather.”

Technology here is not a tool but a metabolism. Motors hum like molars. Conveyor belts creep like caterpillars that forgot how to hurry. Electricity arcs along wet rails in blue stitches, mending gaps that keep opening. There is a power station built into the throat of an extinct vent; it drinks heat and gives back a low, continuous thrumming that feels less like generation than digestion.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. You see them as interruptions: a fox-sized beast plated in mica, padding across a cooling slab, pausing to lap at a rivet’s sweat. Birds—perhaps lizards that grew feathers to apologize—glide above the smelters, banking off thermals that smell of almonds and old wiring. They do not sing; they click like switches thrown by distance.

Weirdness is the city’s zoning code. Gravity sometimes thickens in alleys, as if mass had pooled like runoff. Shadows detach themselves at noon and wander off to gossip with other shadows. Doors open into rooms that are slightly older than the buildings they sit in, furnished with chairs grown from slag, tables that remember being lava. Language here drifts. Words acquire patina. A greeting is often a warning about temperature. A farewell is permission to keep walking.

Atmosphere is a pressure-cooker’s polite cousin. The air is dense with possibility: powders, spores of glass, lint from insulation, the ozone ghost of overloaded transformers. You can chew it, almost. It tastes like standing too close to a forge that is proud of itself. The sky is never empty; it is busy with contrails that fray into feathers, with cranes that never land, with heat mirages that build phantom neighborhoods on the horizon—whole districts of longing.

Sounds are spare but deliberate. A hammer finding its rhythm. A pipe settling like a sleeping animal. The soft collision of thermal expansion, metal napping against metal. The city does not shout; it mutters, and its muttering is a form of architecture.

Change is glacial in human terms, but industrious in mountain terms. A wall slumps and is repaired with something brighter, less apologetic. A plaza slowly tilts, learning to catch rain better. The river changes its mind about which canyon to favor. These decisions take decades, and the city watches, patient as geology. It knows that being dense means having many ways to fail, and therefore many ways to persist.

To walk here is to feel crowded by height, heated by proximity, and lightly touched by moisture that is more intent than water usually is. The place presses in, not to crush, but to assemble—always assembling, never done. It is a settlement that refuses to choose between being a machine and being a mountain. It is hot, crowded, strange, and quietly building itself a future that will outlive its explanations.
