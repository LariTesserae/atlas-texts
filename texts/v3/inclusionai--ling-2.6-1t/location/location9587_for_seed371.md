The place begins as a throat.  

It is a cathedral carved not by piety but by hurry—a deep, narrow basin wedged between walls that rise like the ribs of something still breathing. The elevation is enough to thin the air, to make your chest remember it is a bellows, yet the temperature lingers in that cool band where metal chooses to sweat. What little water exists does not pool; it appears in shifts—film on stone, beads on the undersides of ferns, sudden curtains that fall and vanish before they reach the floor. The dampness is a rumor that keeps its promises just out of reach.  

The land is young. The scars of uplift are still white, striations that catch the dark like fresh handwriting. Erosion has not yet softened edges; ledges shear off in steps that would cut a careless hand. Soil, where it exists, is coarse and eager, still learning how to hold itself together. The scale is intimate: a single gorge and the rooms it has hollowed for itself, a place you could pace end to end and still feel the pressure of walls leaning in.  

And yet it is dense beyond reason. Vegetation has poured into every fault, a green tide that does not respect gravity. Mosses beards the ceilings; vines braid themselves into columns; bulbous fronds crowd shoulder to shoulder, leaves overlaid like scales, until the gorge becomes a lung clogged with chlorophyll. The density is acoustic as much as visual—a thicket that swallows light and throws it back as shadow.  

There is no animal life. No wing, no claw, no skittering pause. Only a kind of expectant vacancy, as if the place were holding its breath for something that has not been invented yet.  

What moves is made.  

From clefts in the upper walls descend conduits of riveted iron and lacquered copper, grown as much as built—industrial organs that were never meant for this altitude but have taken root anyway. They thrum. They sing. They scream. The sound is loud and granular, a thousand machines stacked into a single choir: hammers that never strike the same place twice, turbines that gulp the damp air and exhale it as pressure, pistons cycling in arrhythmic polyphony. The noise does not travel; it accumulates, layer upon layer, until the gorge feels like the interior of a working lung.  

Light is almost absent. What exists comes from the machines themselves—embers, arcs, the sour glow of overloaded filaments bled into mist. Shadows pool in the corners like sediment. The darkness is not empty; it is saturated, almost heavy, pressing against the edges of vision the way the walls press against the space.  

The world obeys rules written in turbulence. Stability is a courtesy the gorge withdraws as soon as it is noticed. Pipes burst and are rerouted within minutes; metal skins blister and split, then knit shut in new configurations; waterfalls of coolant reverse without warning, climbing as easily as they fall, as if gravity here has learned to hesitate. Vegetation reacts—leaves retract, stems stiffen, root crowns hiss when the machinery flares. The dynamic is furious and beautiful: a system that prefers becoming to being, creation and destruction braided so tightly they look like a single process.  

And through it all, the cool air carries the scent of wet iron and crushed greenery, the smell of something new learning how to endure. The place is small enough to know itself by heart and vast enough, in its noise and density, to forget where it ends. It is a gorge that is also a factory, a garden that is also a storm, and it holds you in its dark, loud, breathing mouth.
