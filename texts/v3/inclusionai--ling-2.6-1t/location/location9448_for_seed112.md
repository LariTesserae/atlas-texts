The spine of the place is altitude: a high plateau that forgets it ever had a sea, ringed by rims so sheer they look cut with a blade cooled in vacuum. Air here is cool enough to stiffen sap, but not cold enough to still the storm. The sky is not empty; it is crowded with itself—layer upon layer of wind shearing across different densities, so that cloud-streamers are braided, torn, and rewoven mile by mile. Light is a tyrant: broad-spectrum, high-altitude glare that glints off waxy surfaces and turns every edge into a blade of brightness. Shadows do not linger; they are chased across the ground like startled animals.

Water is a rumor, not a fact. There are no lakes, no rivers, only the ghost chemistry of salt-fields that crust in fractal plates and the dry breath of condensation that sweats from living tissue at night. Surfaces glisten as if lacquered by absence, sealed against evaporation, holding onto moisture the way vaults hold gold. The ground is less soil than scaffold: mineral ribs and porous stone threaded with rootways so dense they read as geology. Plants are not guests here; they are the host, the architecture, the load-bearing wall. Forests do not grow—they accumulate. Trunks fuse into buttresses, canopies laminate into decks, vines braid into cables that hum with tension. The biomass is total, but it is not crowded; it is sparse in the way a radio tower is sparse—much space between presences, each one vast, each one alone in its way.

Where the plants end, the industrial begins, but it does not announce itself with geometry. It announces itself with sound. The deafening roar is not machinery so much as the machinery of the living: pumps that are hearts, turbines that are lungs, belts that are tendons. Engines have been grown, not built—bronze-colored organs grafted into trunks, pistons that are seedpods slamming open and shut, exhaust vents that bloom like night flowers and exhale scented steam. Factories are hollowed trunks, cathedral-sized, their walls ribbed with growth rings that double as circuitry, veins threading copper and resin in vascular lines. There is no waste; every byproduct is metabolized, shunted into other chambers, pressed into service as signal or fuel or defense.

Fauna moves through this roaring green like sparks through a wire. Some are small and bright, darting along conduction paths, tasting the air for ion gradients. Others are vast and slow, their bodies encrusted with moss and lichen, drifting on thermal drafts like airborne islands, calling to one another in frequencies that rattle teeth. Their abundance is not chaos; it is choreography, a turbulence of bodies that feeds the instability of the whole. Predators are fewer but louder, their arrivals heralded by drops in pressure and sudden shifts in the scent of ozone and crushed resin.

Erosion is a settled fact. Everything has been weather-worn into shapes that remember their own endurance. Surfaces are pocked, fluted, rounded as river stones, yet nothing here was shaped by water. Wind and vibration have done the carving, along with the slow grind of living tissue expanding and contracting, secreting acids and resins, dissolving and rebuilding in the same breath. The landscape is in its own state, balanced on a knife-edge of establishment: old enough to have rules, young enough to still argue with them.

Weirdness is subtle but systemic. Plants communicate in shifts of hue and texture, signals that travel faster than sound through conductive sap. Time dilates in the canopy: a storm that passes in minutes below may last hours up there, branches thrashing like flags in a gale. Gravity seems courteous here, willing to let the colossal arches of fused root hold up the sky. There are zones where sound cancels into pockets of ringing silence, cathedral hushes that make the ears ache, and other zones where noise is so thick it feels solid, something you could lean against.

There is no built world, only the grown one. No roads, only routes scuffed into living bark by generations of passage. No towers, only spires that are branches, tuned to catch wind and turn it into power. At night, the industrial glow is bioluminescent, a cool luminescence that rides the underside of the canopy like a slow tide, mapping the arteries of the place in ghost-blue. The cool temperature keeps the light sharp, keeps the air biting enough to remind you that you are alive, that you are intruding.

The scale is continental, planet-sized in its indifference. You can stand on one rim and see another as a thin line, and beyond it another, each holding its own weather, its own roar, its own version of this green, bright, turbulent engine. Storms are not interruptions; they are conversations, arguments the land has with itself. Lightning, when it comes, is not fire but a sudden conduction, a branching vein of light that maps the nervous system of the place, then fades into the general noise.

In the end, the landscape is a living factory, an industrial forest that has outgrown the need for industry, a high, dry, deafening garden where every leaf is a piston, every trunk a tower, every breath a machine, and the emptiness between them is not absence but possibility, waiting to be filled by the next surge of growth, the next roar, the next bright, chaotic bloom.
