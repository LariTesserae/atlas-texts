The place is a high hall of fresh stone, small enough that its edges keep returning to you, yet lifted so far above whatever plains lie beyond that the air has thinned into a mild, persistent warmth. The elevation does not bite; it only presses gently on the lungs, a reminder that gravity here is still learning its manners. There is no true water, only memories of it—veins of salt crusted like lace across pale mineral skin, and hollows that might have held liquid once but now cradle instead a stiffer kind of damp, a resinous exudate from walls that sweat slow beads of waxen oil.

The terrain is young, which is to say it has not yet decided how to age. Ridges stand like uncut blades; faults are open mouths still learning vowels. Cliffs have not been rounded into familiarity, so every shoulder is abrupt, every lip a shelf. The stone is variegated—bands of rose, iron-gray, bone-white—cut by hairline fractures that gleam as if oiled. Where the rock has split, it has done so cleanly, revealing geodes that pulse faintly, not with light exactly, but with a kind of optical density, as if the inside of each crystal were a crowded room of smaller rooms.

And the place is dense—not in the sense of pressure, but of presence. Life has moved in early and in numbers. Vegetation is lush without being chaotic; it respects edges in a way that feels deliberate. Mosses form felted carpets that bulge and dimple like living upholstery. Ferns unfurl in ranks, their fiddleheads glossy as shell, and vines braid into curtains that hang between ribs of stone. Leaves are broad and waxy, holding onto the day’s warmth, margins often darkened to seal in moisture against the dry air. There are flowers, too, small and numerous, clustered in geometries that suggest conversation: spirals, coronets, rings that tighten and loosen as if breathing.

Fauna is abundant, and because there is no water, it has invented other fluids. Winged things glide on currents that pool in corners of the hall, riding the slow, patient breath of the space. Their wings are not feather or membrane but tessellated plates that click softly when they fold, though sound here is not a theme but a guest—thin, reluctant, almost imagined. Creatures with many limbs negotiate the stone with deliberation, their pads leaving faint scents of metal and citrus. Others cling in swarms, not alarming but companionable, like lichen that has learned to stir. Their bodies are glossy, compact, built for a world where touching is inevitable and space must be shared.

The atmosphere is warm and lightly perfumed, a mix of pollen, dried resin, and the faint ozone tang of recent formation. Sunlight, or something like it, enters from unseen high apertures and spreads across the stone in sheets, not beams—diffuse, even, as if filtered through a vast, unseen loom. Shadows pool in clefts and soften the severity of edges, giving the hall the feeling of a room that has been waiting for a long time and is only now beginning to be lived in.

Built things are present, though not made by tools. The urban quality lies in layering: platforms of stone worn into steps by unknown passage, rings of growth that suggest repeated return, walls within walls formed by root and rock together. There are clearings that feel like plazas, and corridors that feel like lanes, but all are emergent, agreed upon by the life here rather than imposed. The sense of town is subtle—an implication of gathering, of routes and nodes, of shared use.

Weirdness is mild but persistent. Sometimes gravity seems to hesitate before pulling something down; sometimes a plant leans a moment too long toward another plant, as if listening. Colors shift when not observed directly, and distances contract slightly in the corners of the hall, as if space is folding itself into a more convenient shape. There are no ghosts, but there are echoes that arrive before their causes, a kind of anticipatory resonance.

Rules of the world are gentle. Change is slow, not because nothing happens, but because everything happens many times before it settles. Erosion does not mean wearing away so much as revealing—a slow unveiling of patterns already implicit in the stone. Growth is layered, patient, cumulative. Conflict, if it exists, is muted, resolved by density rather than violence, by accommodation rather than conquest.

As you stand there, the hall seems to realize it is being seen. The moss softens its edges. The vines adjust their curtains by fractions. A creature pauses, its many eyes reflecting the hall back at you in miniature, and then resumes whatever it was doing, which is simply being part of the place. The absence of water does not feel like lack; it feels like a different kind of fullness. The warmth is not empty heat but a held breath. And in the quiet—the almost silence—you can hear, if you listen closely, the sound of the place becoming itself, slowly, surely, together.
