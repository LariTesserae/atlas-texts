The place is a city of breath and bone buried beneath a skin of world that has forgotten rain. It lies at a depth where sky is rumor, yet it is not closed in—space yawns outward, regional, a hollow that could hold a dozen valleys and still feel spare. The scale opens and opens, contours blurred by age, the architecture not imposed but accreted, grown into forms that now read as streets, plazas, thoroughfares without ever having been planned.

There is no water here, or almost none: only a ghost-moisture that clings to mineral like memory, a faint salt-rime on surfaces that never see flood. Where water ought to pool, other fluids have taken its place—resins, slow lipids, phosphorescent ichors—condensing on cavern ceilings and weeping down in threads that never reach the ground before they thicken and fall as glassy beads. The ground itself is mineral plate and compressed ash, a floor so ancient it has worn itself smooth, accepting footfall without resistance. Erosion has run its course until almost nothing is sharp; edges have surrendered, cliffs have slumped into benches, and the city’s bones have settled into a final, patient geometry.

Vegetation is lush where it occurs, but it occurs sparingly, as if rationing its own abundance. Towering fungal trunks, pale and ridged like old marble, rise in scattered groves. Fronds of translucent keratin unfurl from crevices, drinking dimness the way plants elsewhere drink light. There are mats of velvet moss the color of bruised plum, and climbing vines that are less plant than mineralized ligament, knotting through grillwork and lintel, turning masonry into living lattice. This greenery is not wild in the chaotic sense; it has learned the city’s cadence and echoes its layout, clustering where corridors widen, thinning where they narrow, a second city superimposed on the first.

Fauna is abundant and loud. The air thrums with wing, claw, and chitin, a deafening congress of clicks, scrapes, hums, and barks that bounce off hard surfaces and return amplified. There are no predators here in the usual sense, only inhabitants partitioned by frequency and hour, each species carving its own bandwidth of sound and space. Beetles with carapaces like beaten copper drum their abdomens in rhythms that feel architectural. Sky-fish—ribbed membranes stretched over light frames—glide between towers on thermal sighs, their passage producing a sound like tearing silk. Colonies of burrowers gnaw the margins of the city, keeping its edges nibbled and alive, redistributing minerals in a slow metabolism that spans centuries.

The city itself shows every sign of great age and continuous habitation without conquest. Streets are stratified: lower layers compacted to stone by generations of tread, upper layers still soft with recent growth. Walls are porous, shot through with chimneys and vents that breathe in and out in a tide driven not by wind but by the slow expansion and contraction of deep rock. There is infrastructure that predates memory—channels for thick sap, aqueducts for light, bridges that are really scaffolds for gardens—but it has all been absorbed by use, softened by moss, braced by vine, turned into another kind of geology.

Light is dim, a bruised twilight that seeps from mineral seams and the bodies of luminous fauna. It does not illuminate so much as reveal, picking out edges and leaving interiors to pool in velvety shadow. In this gloom color shifts its meaning: blues read as warm, reds as cool, gold as something almost dangerous. The dimness encourages closeness; voices stay near bodies, touch does the work of sight.

Temperature is cool, a steady, skin-tight chill that smells of iron and wet limestone. It sharpens scents: old spice, ozone, petrichor without rain, the musk of crowded life. The coolness is not hostile; it is preserving, slowing decay just enough to let complexity accumulate. Change here is slow, deliberate. Cycles stretch across generations; a building may shift an inch in a century, a garden may take a lifetime to cross a square. Yet the place is not static. It breathes. The deafening sound is proof: life is negotiating its terms in real time, even if the terms themselves are ancient.

Weirdness is low, but only in the sense that the place has worn its strangeness into familiarity. What once was impossible is now etiquette; what was once shocking is now infrastructure. The city does not announce its oddness. It offers it like weather.

Above all, this is a landscape of density and emptiness held in balance. Built forms are legible as urban, but they are sparse in placement, allowing the hollows between them to become rooms of their own—echo chambers, listening posts, nurseries, altars. The abundance of fauna moves through these voids, threading the city with motion and voice. The result is a place that feels at once inhabited and abandoned, finished and ongoing, a settlement that has aged into a kind of permanent becoming.

To stand here is to feel time thicken. The ground remembers every step. The air keeps every sound, refolding it into new sound, until the city speaks with many throats and none of its own. It is cool, loud, dim, ancient, and deeply alive—a final, open form in which life has arranged itself, carefully, without water, without hurry, into something that can last.
