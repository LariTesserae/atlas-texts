There is no water here, yet the place sweats.  
A resinous, mineral-heavy breath beads on every surface and immediately dries into a crust of glittering, glass‑thin sweat that cracks like old varnish underfoot. The air is hot enough to make metal think it is remembering being ore, and it tastes of hot copper and baked sugars that never caramelized, only blackened into a stable, aromatic soot. This is a high shelf, kilometers above a world that may or may not still exist below, and the ground is not ground but a continuous, built thing—an industrial crust grown old and absolute.

Erosion has had its way for so long that the original seams between tool and territory have dissolved. Nothing is assembled so much as settled, like ash that refused to stop falling and eventually decided to be a floor. The terrain is a flattened tumult: dunes of circuitry rise and subside in slow, tectonic breaths, their crests capped by lattices of petrified vine. Age has ironized the artificial into something geological, the industrial hardened into strata that behave like sedimentary rock except the grains are gears, filaments, and hull plates that have learned to weather.

Vegetation is everything, and it has taken the contract of architecture seriously. Stems the thickness of smokestacks weave together into vaults that arch over canyons of braided root-rubber and stem-cable. Leaves are plated with foil-thin alloy grown in place, chlorophyll working side by side with photovoltaic rust. Branches split into bronchial pipes that exhale warm, resinous air smelling of overripe orchard and hot grease. The plant-matter has calcified its own history: trunks are spiraled with bands of ceramic bark, knots bulge into control nodules, flowers unfurl as louvers that click open to release spores that are, on closer inspection, tiny printed circuits riding on thermals of their own making. There is no empty space; density is a rule of physics here. Every corridor is choked with tapestries of climbing matter that have braided themselves into ladders, scaffolds, and bridges, all humming with a low, interior tremor like a hive dreaming of engines.

Built-ness saturates the region from the molecular scale upward. Even the dust is manufactured, a silt of polymer flakes and ceramic pollen that settles in drifts against walls that were never poured but woven into existence. Foundations are absent because the whole mass has fused into a single slab of elevated artifact, its underside ribbed with heat sinks the size of quarries, their vanes furred with moss-metal hybrids that conduct and insulate in alternating rings. The heat keeps the whole assembly pliant in a dangerous, slow way, as though the landscape is annealing itself, relieving stresses by warping gently, continuously.

Industrial logic runs through it like a nervous system. Factories here have no roofs; they are clearings where plant-foundries exhale clouds of pollen that gleam with suspended metal. Conveyor belts grow from the ground, their surfaces ribbed like bark, and they carry fruit that are actually ingots wrapped in leaves that dissolve upon delivery into basins where biological acids leach and separate alloys in a broth that bubbles without boiling. The sound is almost always a subtraction: the quiet of distant pistons, the papery rustle of thermal expansion, the occasional chime of crystalline stress releasing in a beam the size of a street. Fauna never lived here, and nothing has arrived to take their place except the processes themselves, which scuttle and creep like invisible insects inside walls.

Darkness is not an absence but a pigment. The sky, if it can be called that, is a lid of bruised violet stretched low and luminous with a dull ember glow, as though the sun had been swallowed and compacted into a battery behind the horizon. What light there is comes from within—veins of phosphorescent sap that throb in arrhythmic tides, warning lamps grown like thorns along the stems of bridges, and the dull cherry bloom of overheated casings. Shadows pool in the hollows of root-pipes and collect in the bellies of leaf-cups, thick and syrupy.

Weirdness is a matter of proportion. A staircase may ascend for a hundred meters and then, without landing, invert into a culvert flowing with hot, viscous resin that smells of cinnamon and iron. Rooms exist that have no inside, only increasingly folded exteriors. Signage is written in bark that rearranges itself when not observed, encoding regulations that seem to forbid absence itself: *No gap shall remain unappointed.* The rules of the world feel feudal and thermodynamic: energy must circulate, structure must persist, change must not cease but must also never become rupture. The turbulence is geological in patience—a churning, roiling rearrangement of parts that slides and settles like a landslide slowed to the pace of bureaucracy.

This is a region in its final, restless form. It is a plateau of built, packed, vegetal industry that has aged into a single, breathing artifact, hot and high and humming with the quiet violence of a billion adjustments being made at once. It does not invite entry so much as it admits infiltration, the way a lung admits air, with friction, heat, and the certainty that what enters will be metabolized into more of the same. Nothing here ends; it only thickens. And in the dark, the place continues to grow itself, layer by layer, into whatever shape best endures its own heat.
