The place does not begin as ground but as a long pause.  
You step out of air into a mineral breathing that has no lungs. The surface is a cooled skin of once-molten stone, now fractured into plates the size of neighborhoods, tilted and locked at angles that refuse to lie flat. Elevation is not a single peak but a grammar of rims, scarps, and inverted bowls stacked so high that the sky feels borrowed, a thin lid pressed over a kiln that has forgotten its fire. At this height, coolness is less a temperature than a thinning, a subtraction of heat that leaves edges sharp and shadows dense enough to chew.

There is no water. Not as liquid, not as memory. Instead, the ground hoards dryness like a vow. Salt blooms in dendritic circuits across the plates, white as bone, conductive as thought, drawing fine powders into filigrees that map ancient seepages. Where the crust heaves, glassy volcanic sheets buckle into fluted columns, their surfaces rippled as if water had once run there and learned to harden. Between plates, gaps yawn, and the air in those gutters carries a metallic hush, as though proximity to mass has learned to whisper.

Vegetation is absent, yet something answers where roots might have been. Fungal analogues, pale and mineral-threaded, rise from crevices not to feed but to store—crystalline lattices grown into porous towers that hum with temperature shifts. They do not branch; they assemble. Their forms suggest mathematics more than biology, vaults and spirals that seem to have been solved into existence and left standing. They are not alive in any sense you can carry home, but they persist with the stubbornness of an equation still true.

The settlement is not built so much as exhaled. Towers of fused aggregate spiral up from the plates, not straight but following the grain of uplift, their walls pocked with windows that never look out the same way twice. Streets are less a plan than a weathering pattern: grooves worn by footfall and expansion, bridges of solidified slag that leap between tilted blocks, plazas that are really only flatter scars where gatherings have pressed the stone into memory. There are no machines. No moving parts. Only the city, shaped by the slow insistence of its own occupation, learning the shape of the heights it clings to.

Fauna, where it appears, is rare enough to be rumor: six-legged silhouettes that skitter along magnetic seams, bodies like folded wire and glass, feeding on the static charge that builds before a slab settles. They do not hunt; they calibrate. Their passage leaves trails of fine dust that align into transient dunes, rearranged by each tremor. You might see one, or you might invent it later, and the difference is slight.

The atmosphere is thin and bright in a dull way, a dimness that does not soften but clarifies, making distance precise. Light comes from no single source, as if the altitude itself has become luminous, a glow of cold reflection off salt and glass. Sounds are scarce, but not absent: the clink of cooling plates, the soft grind of settlement settling into its own weight, the almost-musical ping of a crystal spire releasing heat. Together they make a quiet so textured it could be mistaken for voice.

Erosion is not destruction here but conversation. Wind, laden with powdered glass, has scoured faces into fluting. Frost, when it comes, does not melt but sublimes, stitching cracks with hoarfrost that glues stone to stone in temporary unions. The processes are many and simultaneous: uplift, tilt, dissolve, accrete, cool, and creep. The landscape is active with balance, always in negotiation with its own extremity.

Scale opens outward past the ridge. A valley is visible, not as a dip but as a wound filled with layered scree, each sheet a calendar of collapse. Beyond that, another city-scab on another plate, smaller, paler, repeating the pattern. There is no horizon in the sense of an end; only transitions, one kind of height becoming another, all of it thinly inhabited, widely spaced, almost empty.

Weirdness seeps in where the rules fray. At dusk, the angles of the city sometimes refuse to sum. A street you walked by an hour ago now lies atop a tower you have not climbed. Gravity, never malicious but often distracted, allows brief sideways rains of pebbles that fall at an angle you cannot correct. The mineral fungus, if watched too long, seems to count in a base that hurts to follow. The silence, when it deepens, does not feel like absence but like a held note, as though the place is waiting for a permission it will never ask for.

And yet the place is not hostile. It is exacting. It asks you to move slowly, to learn the weight of your steps, to notice how the city has grown into the land rather than over it. Here, urban and wild are not opposed but braided, each shaping the other through the long, cool patience of height. The result is a landscape that is settled but not finished, active but quiet, empty enough to hear itself think and strange enough to remember that thinking is a kind of erosion too.
