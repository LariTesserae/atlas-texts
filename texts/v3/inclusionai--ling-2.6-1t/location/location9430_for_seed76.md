The world begins as altitude.  

You step onto a plateau so wide it forgets it is a plateau—continent pressed against sky, its edges lost to a horizon that looks like a property of light rather than distance. The ground is high enough that the air tastes of metal and faint ozone, cool enough to raise gooseflesh despite the brightness pouring down. This is not the cold of ice but the cool of stone that has long since traded its heat for patience.  

There is no water, and yet the place refuses to read as barren. Instead, the dryness has become a kind of resin. The surface is a mosaic of mineral sheets—mica, feldspar, veins of hematite—polished by eons of wind into broad, pale mirrors that catch the light and sling it onward. Where the sheets fracture, the breaks are rounded, as if the planet had the time to heal its own wounds, filing sharp edges into smooth lips. Erosion here is not a wound but a grammar: long-established, fluent, balanced. The terrain has settled into its own posture and learned to stand that way.  

And yet it is lush.  

Vegetation does not crowd; it attends. Forests of silica-barked stalks rise spaced widely enough that each one reads as a deliberate punctuation. Their trunks are pale, almost translucent, banded with layers that record seasons of light rather than water. Leaves, where they exist, are not green but iridescent—thin sheets of mineralized tissue that flutter like moth wings when the air moves. Their roots do not seek moisture but pressure, threading into cracks to feel the slow pulse of the planet’s cooling skin. Between them, carpets of low, crystalline mosses grow in strict geometries, reflecting the sun in such a way that the ground seems to breathe brightness.  

The brightness is almost aggressive. At high elevation, the light has less atmosphere to apologize to, and so it arrives as a physical weight, casting shadows with knife-edge clarity. Halos crowd the stalks where dust motes and mineral fines hang suspended, turning every breath into a glitter. This is a place that photographs itself constantly, etching its appearance into itself.  

Fauna is rare, but not absent. You might glimpse, at great distance, a shape like a lizard woven from stained glass, moving with a deliberation that suggests it is less hunting than conversing with the land. Its passage leaves no tracks—its feet distribute weight so evenly that it only rearranges the light. Higher still, something like a kite made of bone and thin membrane rides thermals that are more current than wind, banking with a silence that feels like listening. Life here is not abundant; it is precise.  

Sounds are sparse and soft. There is the tap of mineral expansion as day warms—a ticking like a clock made of pebbles—and the low shiver of those iridescent leaves, which make a noise like fingernails drawn across a comb of crystal. When the wind finds a hollow in the mineral sheets, it produces a long, almost vocal tone that holds steady until the air changes its mind. Silence is not an absence but a material, dense and negotiable.  

The weirdness of the place lies in its chemistry. Without water, other rules have swollen to fill the space. Certain patches of ground exhibit slow effervescence, exhaling trapped gases that form temporary halos above the soil like breath held and released. Other zones display capillary action not of liquid but of fine, charged dust, which rises along invisible channels to seed miniature storms of sparkle that drift and settle according to electrostatic moods. Gravity here feels politely insistent, as if it is willing to compromise.  

Despite the vastness and the brightness, the density remains sparse. Space is left between things as a courtesy. You can see how weather might invent itself here: not rain, but tides of temperature that scour the mineral plains, or pressure fronts that align the crystalline leaves into choirs that sing only once a day. The place is active, but its activity is patient—predator and prey relationships replaced by negotiations of light and angle, of shadow and reflection.  

There are no built things, and no tools. The land is pre-instrument, but not primitive. It is complete. To walk here is to feel your own edges blur, your heat becoming another small wind, your shadow another temporary tenant among the bright, cool, enduring forms. The plateau does not ask you to stay, but it is in no hurry to let you leave.
