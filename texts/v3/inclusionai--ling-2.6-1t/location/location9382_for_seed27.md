The world begins as a lifted plain that refuses to end.  
It sits so high that the air is less a medium than a rumor, cool enough to slow thought, thin enough to make the blood sing. There is no water here—not as liquid, not as memory, not even as vapor in any quantity that pools or flows. Instead, the ground is a mineral desert baked into silence-slabs of salt-white crust, glassed basalt, and porous stone that drinks light without keeping it. At this elevation, the horizon bends gently, an old curve that has been allowed to settle into its final, patient shape. Erosion has finished its argument long ago; edges have been filed to velvet, scarps have slumped into themselves, and the land looks as though it has been waiting in the same posture for epochs.

And yet it is entirely alive.

Vegetation does not grow here—it occupies. Plants have become the substrate, the architecture, the gravity of the place. Forests in the old sense would feel like scaffolding beside what rises across the plain: braided trunks that knit into lattice-cities, canopies that fuse into continuous ceilings miles above the ground, root-masses that have mineralized into vaults and pillars. The biomass is total and unapologetic, a green-brown-gold flood that has filled every gap it could find, climbing until it met the sky and then deciding to hold the sky up. Between these living cathedrals, space is kept sparse, almost courteous; gaps open like plazas, clearings where the cool air can gather and speak. The density is low in saturation but high in intention: every stem placed as if it knows its neighbors by name.

The settlement that humans—or what came after them—built into this green monolith is subtle and persistent. It does not sit atop nature; it is threaded through it. Bridges of ceramic and woven light span between trunks, conduits pulse with cool luminescence beneath bark that has grown to tolerate them, and platforms rest on thickened branches that have learned to bear weight as a habit. Infrastructure is layered, nested: ventilation spirals up through hollows that smell of ozone and bruised leaves, transit lanes glide without sound along magnetic vines, and towers—part mineral, part petrified wood, part engineered storm-glass—rise like questions posed to the thin air. This is beyond modern engineering: materials remember their history, surfaces heal with the patience of seasons, and pathways rearrange when they sense a need they have not yet been told.

Light is dim, as though the sky above is made of felt. The sun, or whatever stands in for it, is a pale coin that casts long, cool shadows, and most illumination comes from the place itself—bioluminescent veins in leaves, phosphorescent mosses on stone, the faint pulse of tech embedded in the living walls. Colors are muted, almost monochrome, but when they appear they are distinct: the violet of sap under pressure, the slate-blue of mineral veins, the copper smudge of aging alloys embraced by root.

The sound is loud.  
It arrives in layers. There is the constant, low roar of wind combing through millions of leaves that have evolved to vibrate, a susurrus like a crowd speaking just below comprehension. Beneath that, the thrum of machines that have borrowed their voices from insects: turbines that chirp, pumps that click like beetles, relays that sing in overtones. Fauna adds its own density to the noise—wings that rasp like shaken paper, calls that bounce off mineral and living wall alike, the percussion of bodies moving through hollow trunks. The result is a cacophony that does not feel chaotic; it feels like the landscape clearing its throat.

Life here is abundant and strange. Creatures glide on membranes between towers of plant, their bodies jeweled with symbiotic lamps that flicker in code. Others crawl across the undersides of bridges, polishing surfaces that would dull if left alone, maintaining the sheen of the world. Predators are patient, their metabolisms slowed by the cool air; prey moves in flickers, quick and bright. Nothing is in a hurry, but everything is moving. The ecosystem is active in ways that hum: spores ride engineered updrafts, seeds are launched by mechanisms older than memory, and nutrients cycle through mineral, machine, and muscle without ever touching water.

Weirdness saturates the place without breaking it. At dawn, the tallest trees lean toward something that is not the sun, adjusting in increments too small to see until hours have passed. In certain clearings, gravity softens; dust rises in spirals that look like handwriting. There are doors in trunks that open only when a particular rhythm of breath is matched, rooms that have folded themselves into four dimensions to fit more space than the forest should allow. The tech is not merely applied; it is grown, trained, cajoled into usefulness, and in return it has taught the plants to dream in circuits.

Rules of the world are gentle but absolute. Here, nothing is wasted, not even absence. Emptiness is a resource, a relief, a note of rest in the loud score. Change happens, but on a timescale that respects stone and settles for slowness. To live here is to accept that you will be heard, that you will be seen by leaves that have learned optics, that you will walk paths that remember your steps and adjust to soften your tread.

The landscape continues past the edge of attention, an open expanse of high, cool, ancient green and quiet stone, humming with life and louder with purpose, where the oldest earth and the newest thought have agreed to share the sky and split the silence between them.
