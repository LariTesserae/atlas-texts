The pocket is a husk the size of a market town pressed into a continent whose bones have long since ceased to complain. Elevation is low, almost apologetic—a bowl that forgot how to hold anything but itself. There is no water, not even as memory: the floor is a mineral salt-field calcined into plates that ring like old bronze when stepped on. Yet vegetation is total, a green that has eaten the idea of emptiness. Plants do not grow here; they occupy, entrench, legislate.

The city is plant-as-architecture. Towers are fused trunks braided into columns, their outer skins cured into a leathery bark that holds the scribble of early metal brackets—wrought-copper clamps and pinions hammered into place by hands that knew only how to persuade, not command. Bridges are living arches trained across voids, roots braided into cables, cambium wound into rope. Streets are not paved; they are compressed moss and leaf-litter that exhale a cool breath at every step. Temperature settles at a consistent chill, the kind that keeps things from rushing, that encourages patience.

Erosion has had its say and gone to sleep. The place wears its age as a soft polish: edges blunted, corners swallowed, surfaces mapped by fine cracks like the script of a language no longer spoken. The layout is urban in its logic—districts, thoroughfares, plazas—but the geometry has been allowed to slump. Walls lean into one another for support. Gates are veils of woven vines that must be parted like curtains. The density is almost empty: vast pockets of air between presences, hollow chambers inside colossal stems, alleys that open into sudden clearings where nothing happens but the slow business of being.

Light is absent, not as a cover but as a fact. The city manages without it, or with a substitute that is more rumor than glow. Phosphor-molds bloom along seams and rivets, their spores drifting like dust, settling on copper and skin alike. The result is a visibility that comes in pulses rather than streams, a place perceived in increments. In this quiet dark, sound is loud—not from voices, for fauna is nearly absent, but from structure itself. The city creaks and groans, a chorus of fibrous tension, of bark contracting in the cool, of sap shifting like slow tides. Metal ticks as it cools, hollow stems hum at pitches that feel less heard than felt in the sternum. Wind, when it comes, is a visitor, and it moves through with the insistence of a guest who knows it is invited too often.

Weirdness lives in the seams. Some thoroughfares dead-end into organs that thrum on their own schedule, bulging and relaxing as if remembering a purpose they outgrew. Fountains exist but do not spill: sculptures of porous stone that drink the air and weep brine so slowly that each drop outlasts the day. At the center is a hollow mound, the town’s quiet heart, where roots knot into something like a throne, upholstered in soft rot and wrapped in copper filigree that no longer remembers how to conduct. The rules here are simple and severe: nothing is removed without permission from the whole, and permission is given only when the whole is certain it will not notice the loss. Growth is permitted only where it can be persuaded to serve. Decay is not failure but redistribution, a civic duty carried out by molds and beetles with tools of bone and grit.

Ecology is a negotiation. Lichens arbitrate between bark and salt-floor. Tiny filaments lace the ground into a mat that drinks dust and returns firmness. High above, leaf-canopies overlap into a vault that keeps the cool from ever becoming cruel. There are no animals to trample or chew, so the city grows with minimal opposition, expanding in hesitations. Infrastructure is hand-worked: gutters carved into living trunks, levers of antler and knot that open reservoirs of resin, stairways spiraling up the interior of a single stem, their steps polished by generations of bare feet.

Time is slow, a gradient almost flat. A change in the angle of a bridge might take seasons. A shift in the taste of the air might indicate that a distant wall has finally surrendered to its own weight. The place is ancient enough to have become its own geology, young enough in its habits to still care where the footfalls fall. In the darkness, the city breathes without noticing, a loud, cool, hollow, leafy place that holds itself together by being careful not to ask too much of anything—least of all of itself.
