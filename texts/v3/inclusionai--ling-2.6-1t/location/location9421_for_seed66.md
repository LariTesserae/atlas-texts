Water here is not absent; it is a commuter. At 1.7, it lives in the seams—sweating from warm stone, pooling in the low bellies of avenues, weeping from the undersides of bridges like slow, mineral tears. The elevation, 1.6, lifts the city into thinner, clearer air, so that water behaves differently: it condenses on cold metal at dawn and refuses to leave, gathering in brassy cataracts that drip in metronomic, industrial time. Rain does not wash so much as oil the place; it slicks the gutters and turns the runoff channels into bright, sluggish serpents that remember every gradient they have ever learned.

Vegetation, at 0.3, has surrendered its claim. This is a mineral world: basalt and iron, slag and calcined bone, glass that cooled too fast to remember being sand. Where something green might have anchored itself, there are only mineral crusts, lichen-like films of oxidized metal, and fungal-mineral hybrids like pale coral growing in the margins of steam vents—simple, not alive so much as persistent. The absence of plants does not feel barren; it feels polished. Every surface has been asked to carry weight, and so it has grown dense and articulate.

Temperature, 1.5, is the city’s steady hum: warm enough that metal expands with a soft complaint, cool enough that sweat does not become tyranny. Warmth here is manufactured as much as given—exhausts, condensers, the slow breathing of geothermal sump-pumps sunk deep into the high rock. The warmth rises in layers, trapped between canyon walls of construction, so the air moves like a tired animal, circling its own heat.

Erosion, at 2.5, has had time to negotiate with the city. Nothing is sharp anymore; edges have been surrendered to wind, water, and the soft abrasion of things rubbing neighbor against neighbor for centuries. Stone steps show the shine of millions of feet. Iron balustrades ripple like cloth where wind and rain have filed them. The city is in balance with its own decay: rust blooms in orderly gardens, and cracks are filled with mineral lace that the city learned to call mortar.

Scale, 2.6, is continental in patience. The place sprawls across a high plateau whose rim is lost in haze. Districts repeat like geological strata, each one a slightly different answer to the same problems of altitude and wet. Weather forms here as it does over seas: fronts collide along the long ridge, and the city’s canyons channel wind into choral howls. The horizon is a rumor, glimpsed only from the highest girders, where the built world yields to a sky that feels too big to be owned.

Density, 1.5, is a matter of insistence. Structures lean together, balconies kiss across alleys like gossip, pipes braid into thickets, and catwalks knot themselves into webs. The city grows inward as much as outward—tunnels beneath tunnels, basements below basements, cisterns that remember being quarries. Light is refracted and trapped in these close ranks; brilliance splinters into a thousand softer glints.

Built, at 2.3, this is a city that thinks in infrastructure. Streets are rivers, bridges are machines, walls are dams. There are districts devoted to lifting water up and others devoted to letting it down. Staircases spiral like screws; entire blocks can be raised or lowered on ancient, oiled gears to accommodate seasonal flow. The city does not sit on the land; it converses with it, offering geometry in exchange for passage.

Tech, at 1.9, is industrial without being alien. Engines cough in steady rhythms, pistons rise like lungs, and conveyors carry not just goods but heat, cold, and pressure. Factories hum with excess energy, bleeding steam into the streets the way a kettle whistles. Electrification is everywhere, but it is old electrification: filament bulbs in wire cages, grids patched so many times they resemble lace, power leaping in small, visible arcs where insulators have grown tired.

Light, at 2.3, is ordinary but generous. Sunlight cuts clean across the high plateau in the morning, laying gold across wet stone. By noon, the city reflects itself back in a thousand mirrors of glass and polished alloy. At dusk, the city does not go dark; it dims into amber, then into the blue-white insistence of its own lamps, each one claiming attention like a small, persistent star.

Fauna, at 2.7, is overwhelming. The absence of plants has not emptied the place; it has concentrated life. Colonies of winged things fill the sky in living tides, their wings catching light in clouds of iridescence. On the walkways, something like crabs and something like beetles share the masonry, clattering in choruses that rise with the heat. Between gears, in the warm runoff, eel-shapes flicker—blind, quick, adapted to the metallic soup. Predators are many and middling in size, gliding or skittering, patient as the city itself. The air is thick with leathery beats and small, bright eyes. Every balcony is a roost; every duct, a den.

Weirdness, at 0.8, is a shy guest. It is there in the way water sometimes flows uphill for a few meters before remembering itself, or the way certain arches cast no shadow at noon. Acoustic echoes return a word or two late, as if the city is rehearsing conversations. There are stones that hum when touched, not in any language but in something like relief.

Sound, at 1.7, is loud but textured. It is not noise so much as density of event. The slap of water on metal, the grind of gears easing into alignment, the chitter and boom of fauna, the low thrum of engines—each layer keeps its own rhythm, and together they make a music that the city breathes. At certain hours, the sounds synchronize by accident, and the city thrums like a struck bell.

Dynamic, at 0.8, favors the long view. The city changes in increments that can be measured in seasons rather than days. Water finds new seams only after years of patient dripping. Buildings settle into their foundations with the discretion of glaciers. Fauna populations bloom and recede in cycles that outlast memory. Even the industrial heart of the place beats slowly: machines are built to be maintained, then maintained until they become something else. The city’s mood alters like weather fronts—gradually, with plenty of warning if one knows how to read the runoff patterns and the angle of steam.

In the end, this is a place that has been allowed to become itself for too long to be surprised by its own strangeness. It is wet and warm, high and loud, dense with both built things and living things, and patient enough to let them argue out their differences in slow, mineral time. The city is not a monument to victory over nature, nor to surrender to it. It is a treaty written in iron, water, and wingbeats—a place where the horizon is a promise, the stone is polite, and everything drips with the memory of how to endure.
