The world is a low bowl of mineral skinned in breath.  

From orbit it looks like a thumbprint pressed into warm wax: a shallow depression that never drained, ringed by rims still soft enough to slump. There is no sea, but the basin keeps a memory of wet in the way its solids sweat—thin halos of salt that bloom overnight and vanish by mid-cycle, leaving crusts that ring like struck glass. The elevation is so low that horizon is a close thing, a lip you could step over if you didn’t know how far the fall would carry you. Gravity here seems to hug the ground, as if the land were still deciding how heavy it wants to be.

The terrain is embryonic. Ridges are not worn so much as exhaled: ridges like cooled ropes of dough, lobes of stone that slid while half-set, faults that have not yet learned to be cliffs. Rock is porous, honeycombed with veins of mica and filaments of iron-rust that look like root maps sketched in rust. In places the surface is almost fabric, a felt of compacted dust and shard that gives underfoot and remembers each step. Colors are warm brass, bruised peach, terra-cotta, and the pale green of copper salts. There is no water, yet the minerals glisten as if they had just been licked.

Vegetation is lush but not soft. It crowds in, claiming every pore. Frond-thickets grow in crystalline whorls, their stems ridged like bone, their leaves thin sheets of resin that catch light and hold it like foil. Mosses here are not damp mats but felted films of metallic fluff that squeak when brushed. Root systems are visible, braided across the ground like cables, binding the loose stone into a single, living pavement. There are no hollows between things. The density is absolute: every handspan holds a thicket, a tangle, a tower of growth pressing against its neighbor, trunks ribbed and ridged, canopies that interlock into a continuous roof a story or two above the floor. It is a city of leaves.

And it is a city in every sense. Settlements are grown as much as built. Walls are thickets trained into grids, arches woven from living beams that have been guided while supple. Floors are tapestries of resilient moss and packed grit. Towers rise not from masonry but from braided trunks that have been encouraged upward, their upper branches plaited into platforms and balconies. There is no metal, no glass, no tool-marks—only patient direction. Pathways are canyons cut by passage, their walls glossy with polishing oils from countless bodies. Infrastructure is hydraulic in a biological sense: gutters are grooves lined with sponge-moss that drinks the condensed sweat of night and releases it as a thin, warm vapor at dawn; cisterns are hollow trunks lined with resin that seals like amber. The settlement breathes.

The scale is continental, yet intimate. Valleys run long enough for their own weather to be born: by afternoon the lifted heat and the sheer mass of transpiring life build banks of haze that pile against the rim like slow tides. Within them, convections begin, small circulations that smell of warm sap and stone-dust. Storms do not bring rain but pressure: gust-fronts that collapse through the canopies, shaking loose clouds of pollen and powdered leaf, setting the hollow stems to humming. The sound is a hush punctuated by clicks and creaks: joints of growth shifting, seed pods popping like tiny drums, the soft percussion of fruit falling into padded litter.

Fauna is abundant and woven into the fabric of the place. Creatures move in densities that turn motion into texture. Six-legged grazers with plated bellies crop the metallic moss, leaving trails that shine briefly before reseal. Above them, gliders with paper-thin wings bank along the canopy highways, their membranes catching the light in ripples. There are burrowers that mine not for food but for minerals, carrying away beads of iron and copper to line their nests, strengthening the walls of their tunnels with secretions that harden like enamel. Predators are patient, draped over branches like folded cloth, their colors shifting to match the living masonry. Nothing here is rare; everything is present in numbers that make solitude feel like a kind of crowd.

The atmosphere is warm, not hot, the kind of warmth that settles into the lungs and loosens time. Light is ordinary but filtered, gold-green, dappled into a perpetual late-afternoon. Shadows are sharp where canopies overlap, soft where they part. The air has weight, scented with resin, bruised leaf, and the faint ozone tang of growing stone. There is no water, yet nothing thirsts; fluids are held in balance, exchanged in closed loops of sap and salt.

Rules of the world are patient and cumulative. Growth is the primary force; erosion is still a rumor. What changes does so by accretion: layers added, not stripped away. Time is thick here, viscous. Boundaries blur between city and forest, between geology and organism. To walk is to press into a living system that yields and resists in the same measure. Silence is not emptiness but fullness held in check—the land holding its breath between cycles, waiting for the next slow swell of heat, the next thickening of the canopy, the next soft shift of the ground settling into itself.

In the end, the place is a single, slow pulse: a vast, low basin where life and stone have crowded together, warm and crowded and quietly becoming, with nowhere to go but deeper into itself.
