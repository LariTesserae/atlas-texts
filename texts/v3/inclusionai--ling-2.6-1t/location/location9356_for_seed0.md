The dark does not sit like a lid here; it is a broth. A warm, low-lying basin that never forgets it was recently heaved up from deeper stone keeps exhaling its youth in sharp-edged scarps and raw talus that still glint as if the fracture happened yesterday. The earth smells of bruised loam and petrichor even when no rain is falling, because the moisture—eight parts out of thirty—has learned to haunt: a skin of condensation that gathers on broad leaves at night and weeps slowly through the day, rivulets that braid and unbraid across slopes still too new to know which way is downhill.

Vegetation has answered with a near-syllabic yes. Two and a half parts out of three means green is not a color here but a pressure. Ferns unfurl into ferns into ferns, staircasing into gloom. Mosses pile up like felted wool over every stone, sponging up the damp and the dark and giving it back as soft phosphorescence the color of bruised plum. Canopies knit themselves into a roof so thick that sunlight, when it dares, arrives only in rumors—stray coins of gold dropped through gaps that close behind them as vines remember how to move. The density is a crowd: stems shoulder stems, roots braid into arguments, flowers bloom inside flowers, and the air tastes thick, sweet, fermented with pollen and rot in equal measure.

It is warm, but not the easy warmth of a blanket. This is a fever-warmth, the basin holding heat the way a cupped hand holds water. Elevation is modest, so nothing rises far enough to find relief. What goes up must curl back down, heavy with its own breath, and the air presses back, dense with bodies. Fauna is not abundant; it is overwhelming. Two-point-seven out of three means every shadow is occupied, every leaf is a perch, every stem is a thoroughfare. Winged things shear the humid air in densities that cast secondary shadows. Legged things flow across trunks like spilled ink, while things with too many joints skitter through root mazes, drumming the wood like telegraphy. Predators do not hide; they advertise in clicks, thrums, and wet trills that bounce off the young rock and return annotated.

And the sound—two-point-six out of three, deafening—is not a single noise but a layered roar of small events. Millions of wings stich the dark with turbulence. Rain, when it chooses to fall, does not patter; it detonates on broad leaves already convex with stored water, turning the canopy into a drumhead that shudders down to the soil. The young terrain answers: stones still sharp enough to sing when struck, scree shifting in miniature avalanches, mud slumping with a wet gasp, hollow trunks booming like struck gourds. The result is a cacophony so thick it feels tactile, a texture that coats the tongue.

This is a land in unstable balance, dynamic to the point of delirium—two-point-six out of three. Storms do not pass through; they coil in the basin like sleeping cats and wake without warning. Wind arrives in lungfuls, tumbling the layered green, snapping tender shoots, and in the same gust sowing others. Surges of water pour from higher folds, carve new channels, abandon them by afternoon, and leave behind wet lace of silt that steams in the warm dark. The ground itself seems to remember being magma only a breath ago; every slump and slide is an aftershock of shape. Yet nothing here is ruined by this turbulence. Life answers with equal velocity: a stem cut today knots itself with scar-tissue by night; a den collapsed by flood is, by morning, a new terrace of moss and beetle-work.

Weirdness hums at one-point-three—not extravagant, but insistent. There are flowers that open only when no light touches them, pale cups that collect not rain but the sound of wings, trembling as if full. Certain stones have veins that thrum in counterpoint to the fauna’s drumming, suggesting the mountain is not deaf but listening. Pools that form in the undrained hollows of this low basin do not reflect; they absorb, turning the dark even darker, and things that live there have no need for eyes, only for the language of vibration. The fauna knows this; you can see it when a thousand clicking throats go quiet at once, listening downward, deciding whether to drink.

No built thing interrupts the continuity—not a wall, not a tool left behind. What technology exists is simple and handheld: chipped stones that fit the curve of a palm, cords woven from stem-fiber and sinew, bone hooks that carry the memory of their former shape. These things do not dominate the landscape; they slip into it. A path, where it exists, is a suggestion pressed into fern and mud by repeated passage, closed again by growth when the passage ceases.

And still the place expands. Two-point-zero out of three in scale means there is always another fold, another valley-rim, another throat of stone exhaling damp into the dark. Features multiply: a ridge that looks like a limit reveals itself as a bridge of roots; what seemed a mere thicket opens into a glade where the canopy is a skylight of steam and wing. The darkness is not empty; it is full to bursting with presences that touch, overlap, climb over one another, bloom out of bloom, and sing in a roar so immense it sounds like the basin itself learning how to be a home for all this life, wet, warm, young, and never still.
