The city does not sleep because it cannot: it sweats.

Down here, just below the skin of the world, a compact urbanity has been extruded like a bead of glass cooled too fast. Its footprint is small enough to walk across in a morning, yet it presses inward as much as outward—towers leaning to whisper to bridges, pipes nesting inside pipes, streets doubling back through basement mouths until the place feels several times larger than its walls should allow. Boundaries are sensed rather than seen: a press of mineral lips that close gently when the heat rises.

The material is young, still tasting of itself. Walls bear tool marks fresh enough to glitter in the dim light, their angles too crisp to be polite. Basalt and slag have been fused with something glassier, a dark resin that weeps slow tears and hardens into balconies. Erosion has not yet learned patience; edges nick and flake at the slightest provocation, leaving glitter on fingertips like ground stars. The stone is warm to the touch, not from the sun—there is none here—but from the city’s own metabolism, a fever held politely behind a façade.

Heat sits at the upper limit of bearable, a dry burn that pulls moisture from breath before it can be called vapor. Yet water is present, perversely, in small persistent amounts. Condensation beads on conduits and gathers in gutters that were never meant to carry rain, pooling in cupped maintenance platforms. The liquid is faintly metallic, tasting of copper and old fuel, and it moves with purpose, slipping through sieves to cool bearings, then disappearing into seams that steam softly. You hear it: a faint, rhythmic slapping, the sound of wet cloth on hot metal, a whisper that keeps time with machines.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. In cracks that open only when the city flexes, a few wire-stemmed shrubs survive, their leaves reduced to silver needles that catch the dim light and throw it back. Roots, where they appear, have learned to grip moving parts, wrapping pistons like loyal vines. They do not grow so much as colonize, staking tiny claims in oil slicks and settling for dust as soil.

The atmosphere is dense in a double sense. Air is thick with particulate patience, ash and filings that drift like a second fog, catching on lashes and clothing. But there is also density of presence: things crowd one another without touching, a choreography of nearness. Pipes pass ducts that skim walls that lean against other walls, all sharing breath. You feel watched not by eyes but by geometry, an insistence of angles leaning in to share secrets too hot for words.

Sounds are mostly quiet but far from silent. A low thrum rises from turbines that seem to be turning not to make power but to keep time, their note flattening occasionally when heat swells a bearing. Somewhere, a liquid drips into a tin bowl with a patient plink, answered by a distant clank as a shutter settles. These noises are enough to make the silence feel inhabited, like a room holding its breath.

There is no fauna here, at least none that were born of blood. Instead, maintenance machines drift like slow fish, finned and jointed, mending breaches and polishing surfaces until they shine like wounds. They ignore witnesses, focused on tasks too small to be urgent and too constant to be ignored.

The city runs on industry scaled to its compactness. Factories do not sprawl; they stack. Elevators carry ore upward while slag slides down in mirrored chutes. Electricity is carried by hair-fine wires that glow when the heat peaks, sketching blue veins across the ceiling. Control rooms are embedded in hollows, their dials winking like restrained laughter, recording temperatures that climb and dip as if the place were breathing.

Light is dim but not absent. Most illumination comes from things that are warm: seams, joints, gratings that glow dull orange when the city works hardest. There are lamps, too, hooded and sparing, casting pools that shift when the heat warps the air above them. Shadows do not fall cleanly; they soften at the edges, as if afraid to cut anything.

What makes the place unusual is not its strangest part but its mood. This is a city built to last while knowing it will not. Its youth is evident in every sharp corner, yet it behaves like an old machine conserving energy. The heat is not a byproduct but a participant, shaping decisions, narrowing paths, forcing proximity. The damp is not relief but a partner, carrying heat away drop by reluctant drop.

And the city is active in ways that feel biological. Streets contract slightly as temperature rises, opening vents that hiss like exasperation. Water routes rearrange themselves through soft junctions, choosing new paths overnight. Walls settle into one another with soft knocks, aligning to share load. Even the sparse vegetation adjusts, withdrawing silver leaves when the heat spikes, then cautiously unfurling when the machines sigh.

To walk here is to feel pressure without weight, heat without flame, and a density that is more social than physical. The place is small enough to memorize, yet complex enough to surprise. It is a settlement that has learned to sweat, to leak, to hold its shape by constant adjustment, and in its dim, crowded warmth, it persists like a thought you cannot quite finish but cannot let go of either.
