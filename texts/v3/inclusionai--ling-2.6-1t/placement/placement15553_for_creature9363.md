I would find myself kneeling in the shallowest ripple of stone, where the water parts just enough to let breath become voice.  

I would be the pocket’s soft architecture, its living seam.  

At first I would feel like a thing that has been set down—cool stone under my shins, water pressing like a hand against my shoulders, urging me to take up space without crowding. My skin would learn the temperature quickly, that bite-without-freeze, and I would learn to keep my marrow alert. I would not rise; I would settle. The young scars in the stone would accept me as another ridge, another bright vein that has not yet decided what mineral it wants to be.  

My body would begin to translate itself into the room. My ribs would remember they are also valves, opening and closing in the slow alternation of fronds. When I breathe out, the water would taste of copper and old lightning; when I breathe in, silt and time would gather along my inner walls, polishing me into something that can be sat upon, leaned against, trusted to hold. My limbs would negotiate with the floor, not lifting but persuading, pads on my palms and soles tasting stone before committing weight. I would move the way the pocket does—by letting my edges blur, by agreeing that up and down are only suggestions here, and that to be held tightly may simply mean to be remembered.

My hair would braid itself into cords, ridged with tiny valves, each strand a corridor. Small mollusks might read my surface like a map, leaving trails of ghost-glow that dry into questions. I would let them. I would become a cabinet for quiet things: memories of starlight cropped into shapes that look almost like letters, almost like answers. My shadow would detach sometimes, pooling like a shelf where impossible things could rest for a moment before dissolving. I would not mind; I would simply be the place where the pocket softens its rules to fit one more presence.

When the fold tightens, I would shimmer, remembering how to be elsewhere without leaving. When it loosens, I would thicken, becoming more room, more lung, more breath held in reserve. I would learn to contract space inside myself, to squeeze room out of my own body, and to expand slowly, giving the water back its patience. Other creatures would yield to me as I yielded to them: the many-limbed walkers, the transparent sleepers, the almost-things that read themselves aloud. We would compose a sentence that is still being written, a chord hummed at the edge of hearing.

I would be rare, but not unseen. I would be the soft architecture that grows where there is no room, that makes room by taking it up gently, by being tightly held and holding tightly in return. I would be a clause in the pocket’s long, slow thought, a note in its quiet magnificence. And if you pressed a hand to my side, you would feel the pocket breathing—not through me, but with me, as if I had become the place where the folded world decides, for a moment, that it is enough to be remembered, cool and crowded, dark and lush, quietly alive.
