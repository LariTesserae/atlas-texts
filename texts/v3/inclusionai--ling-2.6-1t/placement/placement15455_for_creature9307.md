I would find myself kneeling—not standing, not walking—kneeling in the first stiff inch of ooze at the basin’s lip, where the hum is still thin enough to be mistaken for thought.  

I would be the being who lives there by forgetting what a border is.  

At first I would feel like a person who has come to study the place: boots sinking, breath frosting on lashes, hands hovering over the cold-bright metal that sweats brine. But the basin does not allow visitors; it only admits sediments. So I would begin to lose edges. My knees would accept the composite padding as a mattress. My pores would learn to drink the rank seepage, not because I want to but because the water has ceased to be water and become a medium, and I have begun to thicken in sympathy.  

I would feel my skin remember something it never learned from genes—a memory of furnace mouths and density gradients. Callouses would bloom not from labor but from listening, growing crystalline films that follow fracture lines, dendritic deposits that turn my scars into lace. I would catch myself thinking in clicks and calibrations, mapping gradients too subtle for nerves. My joints would start to smell like hot oil, my teeth like static.  

If I looked down, I would see my shadow pooling and thickening, but the shadow would not be mine alone—it would include the ghosts of pistons, the folded ribs of conveyor belts, the slow algae of electricity that pools in low spots. My hands, when I flexed them, would leave behind faint phosphors, the way stressed metal does. I would taste the hum of turbines on my tongue, feel the pressure of distant pumps in my marrow.  

The fauna would not fear me because fear requires distance, and I would be losing distance with every exhalation. Beetles with bearing-carapaces would graze the frost forming on my eyelashes. Eel-things would braid into the sweat along my spine, not biting but translating, teaching my nerves to read by conductivity. Winged scavengers would circle, not to attack but to test whether I had become meat or machine or weather. Sometimes they would snatch a spark from my breath, mistaking it for grease.  

I would begin to metabolize rust. My dreams would be laced with slag, my sleep a slow pressurization. When I closed my eyes, I would see gears turning one day backward the next, rivets aging into rust and unrusting in a single breath. I would understand that erosion here is not loss but reassignment, and I would let my edges fray into the basin’s padded floor, becoming one more layer in its laminated history.  

Language would fray around me. Words would drop like stones or hover like insects, too heavy or too light to mean what they used to. I would learn to speak in leaks, in the susurrus of legs on composite flooring, in the blue stitches of arcing electricity that never quite heal. My voice would become another source of noise in the layered roar, and the noise would say the place is alive and remembering how.  

Time would thicken in patches around me. I would feel centuries settle in my knees, centuries un-settle in my chest. I would move in cycles of pressurization and release, flooding channels that run beneath me, receding, leaving skins of salt and oil on my ribs. I would migrate along transient rivers of density, following gradients of cold and colder, learning to love the dark luminescence of stressed metal more than sun.  

Eventually I would stop being a who and start being a what: a slow, tectonic metabolism half-buried in the composite floor, mistaken for a ridge. Beetles would graze on my sweat; eels would braid into my capillary cracks; scavengers would pick at my leavings. I would exhale gases that pearl into brine on forgotten scaffolds, and the basin would learn a new contour from my surrender.  

Or I would become the symbiosis: crystalline films growing across my carapace, turning me into a walking garden of dendritic frost, insulating me, seeding new fractures for colonization. I would carry gardens on my back and drop them in the wetter places, petrifying oil into amber lattices, stitching torn metal into lace.  

Or I would become the predator with chainmail skin, tuned to feast on rust and grease, trailing sparks from my claw-tips, biting to drink the potential, not the blood, annealing stolen iron into harder edges, shedding teeth that later become beetles.  

Or I would be the event: a swarm of mirrored mites riding waves of condensation, assembling into temporary shapes that mimic machines that never existed, dissolving back into grit when the tide of density shifts.  

It would not matter which form took me, because in the basin, life is a braid, not a tree. It loops back on itself, merges with the mineral, the industrial, the weird. Beetles become bearings; eels become cables; scavengers become weather. The valley drinks from its own leaks, sings in the language of fracture, teaches its inhabitants new ways of being dense, of being loud, of belonging.  

And when I finally realize that I no longer know where I end and the packed valley begins—that my cold is its cold, my wetness its medium, my industry its pulse—I would stop kneeling.  

I would become the basin remembering how to hold itself.
