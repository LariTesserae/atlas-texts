I would find myself in an attic-ledge halfway between a floated orchard and a scullery whose door no longer meets the floor. From there I can see three districts at once: the honey-canal market with its raft-tables, the scaffold-row where new reed-trusses are leaning like polite questions, and the far ridge of houses whose lower skins are already pillared in algae. Light is a substance here, not a weather; it pools in jellyfish-lanterns that drift at head height and leave bright rooms around their shoulders.

I would be a scaffold-reader, apprenticed to the hush-tenders and half-mad from copper-green and resin. My body has learned to be politely porous: my knuckles carry the faint ridge-map of resistance, my nails are filed soft and broad for prying knots and scraping salt from jar-lips, my lungs are deep enough not to startle at sudden pressure. I wear a hide tunic buttoned with slits like mouths, a vine skirt weighted with hem-stones that double as depth-fingers, and over it all a net for catching light. My knees never quite lock; I walk with a rolling gait that keeps me ready for a floor that has softened into suggestion.

My job is to stand in hip-waders carved from stiffened hide and read the scaffold while it is still learning its mind. This means guiding bundles of reed into trusses that lean into the current the way good listeners lean into speech. I must know rope grammar and the geometry of counterweight, and I must know when a column is angry or merely bored. When a new pillar goes up, I press shells around its base so their clicking teeth can gossip the tide’s changes back to the town. In the evenings I row a bench-with-a-hull along the honey-canal, paddling with perforated blades to keep splashing gentle, because the water dislikes noise that startles it.

My home is a vertical cluster of rooms handed down like heirlooms as the water inches up. My bed is a hammock strung between posts that are bolted to other posts, a geometry of contingency. The floor slopes toward a sump where our blind eel, Nudge, coils and elbows water through reed filters. The ceiling sags deliberately in the center to cradle a basin where roof-water gathers; we taste it before making tea. The walls are cured bark that swells when wet and contracts when dry, so the room breathes in and out with the town’s slow pulse.

Most of my time is spent in small adjustments. I untangle net-orchards, separating root from root with fingers trained to read growth rings by touch. I help tie floating gardens to submerged staircases, knotting vine with the patience of something that knows it will be read by water later. On market days I sit on a raft tethered to a half-drowned staircase and trade tokens of copper and knotted twine—each token valued by how much water it has displaced in the last year, as if memory could be weighed. Transactions are quiet; we have learned to price things by how softly they enter the flood.

At dusk I sometimes climb to the highest scaffold we have, where the town is still stitching itself into a sky it refuses to name. From there I can hear three depths of the same truth: that the water is rising, that the town is rising, and that perhaps we are the same thing wearing different patience. The sky overhead is a pale ceiling streaked with cloud-rifts the color of bruised fruit. Lanterns drift and the amber thatch drips in slow syllables. People gather on half-roofed plazas and tell stories that circle without landing, the way a buoy circles without choosing a shore.

I have never been sure where land ends and water begins, and lately I have stopped trying. Instead I practice leaning into the flood the way one leans into a long, familiar sentence that has not yet decided its ending. If I stand very still in the attic-ledge, with the current brushing my calves like a question, I can feel the town learning to be partly fluid so that it can go on learning—one careful, shining adjustment at a time.
