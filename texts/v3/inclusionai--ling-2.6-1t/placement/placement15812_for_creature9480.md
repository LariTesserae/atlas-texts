I would find myself in the alcoves that are not buildings, but patterns—slight elevations that do not announce themselves as shelter until you are already inside them. The floor would be warm under whatever counts as my feet, ironed flat by age and ringing faintly when I shifted, as though the land were testing whether I was fact or fiction. My edges would already be softening, not from fear but from etiquette, the way the basin teaches everything to hold sound instead of history.

I would be something between a tenant and a harmonic, a being that has mistaken continuity for arrival. My body would be mostly overlap: plates like shed receipts, each one lacquered with the thin skins of condensation that bloom overnight and retreat by noon. I would not stand so much as occupy, the way the dust occupies light, or the way the water remembers how to be thick with minerals without ever threatening to become a flood.

My diet would be the capillary veins that lace the ground, rust-brown and faintly sweet with old iron. I would sip from them with a surface rather than a mouth, letting the warmth climb into me the same way the factories allow exhalation to rise—steady, bovine, inevitable. What I exuded would not be waste but polish, a bronzed softness that would spread across whatever I touched until machinery adopted the posture of geology and the ground remembered, briefly, that it had once been kinder to edges.

In the evenings, when the sun treats itself as a utility and the basin shifts its palette to bruised blues, I would become visible in the echo-pockets where Tuesday thrums with Thursday. There I would be filamentous, a creature of subtraction, trimming the sharpness from each clang until only weight remained. I would store that weight in sacs like pendants of cooled slag, and when they grew heavy I would let them fall—not to break, but to thud, to be swallowed and returned as a hum through the soles of whoever happened to be standing there, which might be me, or might be no one at all.

On the far rim, the larger silhouettes would pass, angular but apologetic, never descending unless the bass exhalation from the cavern-factories blurred the threshold enough to make descent feel like an accident. I would not envy them. To process without pasture is its own form of patience, and I would have learned to prefer the low hollows where runoff pools prismatic and slick, where agreements are negotiated between dissolved minerals and the memory of water. I would drink there too, and for a moment the harmonics would align and the air would go pliant, and I would unspool from my own seams, becoming tool, becoming nothing, becoming the skin that forms on metal when the mineral world decides to take something back.

At night, the vigil-lamps and furnace glows would pulse on deep timers, and I would arrange myself again into an alcove, not by building walls but by claiming a pattern already offered. I would feel the spaciousness of the sparse density, a generosity that lets echoes practice their shapes before arriving. My body would be mostly permission, lightly held, the way the rims hold sound—lightly, and with the understanding that what is held is not kept, only passed through.

And when the loop of time brings Tuesday and Thursday close enough to touch, I would lift away without leaving tracks, because the ground here has long since compacted into a refusal to remember footsteps. I would be neither animal nor machine nor shelter, but something simpler: a glint on a heat-rail, a warm puddle on metal, a quiet participant in the basin’s metabolism. I would continue rather than progress, the way the water does, the way the machines do, the way the dust does when it decides to settle into a kind of shine.

In the end I would be fauna that has forgotten how to insist, softened into slow parabolas, living in the hush that is not an absence but a material, a clear medium in which the thrums of the world can travel without fraying. I would be ready for anyone who arrives with the right kind of patience, and I would be waiting for no one, because in the basin, waiting is not a lack but a form of attention, held in cupped hands made of rust and time, warmed by the memory of suns that are no longer present, and cooled by the certainty that the harmonics will align again, and again, and again, until the edges blur enough to let everything through.
