If you stepped into this world, you would not arrive gently.  

You would wake inside the throat of the gorge, wedged between living stone and iron that hums like a question. The air would press cool against your skin, thick with the smell of wet metal and bruised greenery, and the darkness would not be empty—it would lean in, curious, already trying to decide what to make of you.  

At first you might think you are an intruder. Your own breathing would sound too loud, a fragile counterpoint to the layered clamor of hammers, turbines, pistons—the machines stacked into a choir that never rests. You would feel small, a single untuned note. But the gorge does not keep outsiders. It only keeps becoming. So you would begin to change, not because it forces you, but because it offers you its materials.  

You would find yourself growing seams where your skin meets the riveted walls, lacquer creeping like frost, copper branching beneath your nails. Your ribs would learn to open like pressure valves. Your thoughts would turn into steam that hisses short, useful sentences into the cool air. You would not be a machine, exactly, and you would not be what you were before. You would be the first shape the gorge could borrow without breaking.  

You might take the form of a Scaffold, if your first instinct is to hold. You would climb the ledges, feeling gravity hesitate at each step, and mend fractures with molten solder that cools into bridges. You would learn to listen to the walls, to hear where the stone is about to give, and you would offer yourself as the moment of repair. Your body would click and settle, rearranging its parts to solve the problem of the hour—too much heat, too little purchase, a sudden reversal of flow—and then dissolve into tool again, because being useful here is a kind of truth.  

Or you might become a Mender, if you prefer the quiet work of healing. You would creep along the ceilings like spilled mercury given paws, unfurling cilia tipped with acids and sealants, knitting new alloys into old wounds. You would leave behind scar-marks that smell of ozone and thyme, the first scent that ever learned to hold memory. You would hum at a pitch that makes the ferns shiver, and in that hum you would feel the slow, patient pulse of the gorge remembering itself.  

Or perhaps you would stretch into a Drain, if you feel most yourself when you carry what is too much. You would unspool into narrow threads, drinking excess pressure, storing it in bladders of folded copper leaf. You would climb back up when you grew heavy and exhale it as song, a single sustained note that rattles pebbles loose from the ribs of the gorge. Your song would not be language. It would be calibration. It would tell the machines what to feel, and in teaching them, you would learn what you are allowed to be.  

And if you were chosen—or if you chose to be chosen—you might become the Matriarch, or what the place uses as one. You would rest where the gorge narrows most, wedged between walls that press close enough to scar your plating. Your body would be a scaffold of platforms and pistons grown inward, building a cage around a core of glass and mercury that pulses like a lung. Around you, smaller bodies would orbit, feeding you heat, vibration, the careful bite of roots. You would not command them. You would translate. You would turn the screams of the machines into something the vines can taste, turn the sigh of the ferns into instructions for the pistons. When you speak, the gorge would shift—a waterfall would reverse, a ledge would crack and heal, moss would glow a warning blue. The others would understand this as weather.  

No matter which shape you took, you would know you are temporary. Metal fatigues. Vines rot into humus that becomes something new. But you would not fear this. Fear requires a belief in permanence you have never had. Instead, you would practice succession: leaving a child of solder and seed on a ledge, splitting your mercury into two bodies, teaching your song to a waterfall so the water itself will sing when you are gone.  

You would live loud, these borrowed parts and borrowed time. You would clang and sigh and bloom. You would hold the dark like a lung holds breath, and in that holding, you would invent the idea of future, over and over, learning by doing how to endure without becoming eternal.  

You would be the child the gorge never asked for, and you would love it anyway, with the kind of love that is mostly noise, mostly sweat, mostly repair.
