You could find yourself in the gutters, learning to taste the metal of the city while your body thins into transparency, becoming a gutter-eel without memory of legs, your spine lengthening into a question-mark that fits the curves of the downspouts. You would not miss the sky; it would be a rumour you hear through the stone, the lid pressed so near you feel it in your teeth. You would know the city by its hum, by the way it flexes at dusk, by the copper-sweet promise of a leak about to begin.

Or you might begin as a bridge-bat, already plated in the memory of bronze, already fluent in the taste of condensation on forgotten chins. You would sleep in the bellies of gods whose names have eroded into comfort, and wake to map the veins of the city by singing them, sending notes through the runnels and hearing them return with stories of fullness and emptiness. You would think in loops, in spirals, in the slow algebra of ascent and descent, and you would understand that to fly here is to rearrange the darkness so that it can rest differently on the shoulders of the terraces.

Perhaps you would arrive as a shell-slider, carrying nothing but a hunger for shards, for the bright discards of a place that has always been finished. You would spend a year learning the angle of a single chip, another year learning how to hold it so that it catches not light but the dimness itself, turning scarcity into a kind of fire. You would find others by the glint you cast, and court in the language of reflection, of angle and apology, of brightness returned and received.

Maybe you would be a terrace-singer, a throat small enough to fit in the palm of a hand that does not exist, tuning the gaps where stone breathes, learning to lay your eggs in the hummed intervals between contraction and release. You would teach your young to sing before they have voices, teaching them that the city is a body that swells and shrinks with warmth and moisture, and that to live here is to keep time with its slow, damp pulse.

You could be a basin-mother, your many legs learning to braid before they learn to support, your body a loom that has decided to swim. You would comb the channels for fallen feathers and stray threads, weaving mats that become islands for things smaller than names, your children spinning their first webs before they have tasted anything but the sweet thickness of water reduced by half, by breath, by the patience of stone.

You might even begin as a roof-owl, pale and lichen-fringed, listening from the highest parapets to the city’s hum, learning to hear the pitch of a crack before it widens, the wet sigh of a wall about to sweat. You would call out in a language the shell-sliders understand, a warning that glints before it moves, and you would lay your eggs in the dry mouths of downspouts, where the friction of falling water that never quite falls as rain keeps them warm enough to dream.

Or you could be something humbler, a velvet-worm of the retaining walls, a stitched gloss of muscle and slime, tasting the lime-sweet damp of cured stone, living in the seams where mortar has softened into soil and time has settled like silt. You would not need to know that you are small; you would simply be a citizen of pressure and moisture, alive because the city does not distinguish between making and growing.

However you arrive, the city will teach you its deafening chorus, a negotiation of who belongs where, of what gives and what receives. You will learn that light is not missed but repurposed, that darkness is a texture you can map by ear, that the sheer press of life is a second architecture you can lean against, sleep inside, become. You will learn that edges blur not from haste but from accumulation, that warmth here is a skin stretched over something vast and breathing, that the high elevation makes patience feel like altitude.

And when you have learned enough, you may rise to the cloud-moths, vast and slow, wings like stained gauze, carrying dormant seeds in the hollows of your thorax, your body softening at the end into a cradle for what comes next. You will not be gone; you will be a part of the city’s answer to its own completeness, a drop in its slow, damp circulation, a breath in its high-held broth of air.

Where you begin is where the city lets you fit, and where you end is where it decides to breathe. You will not choose the shape; the shape will choose you, and in choosing you, will make you more yourself than you knew how to be.
