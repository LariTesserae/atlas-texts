You would find yourself in the lung-room, yes—though “find” is not quite the right word. You would condense there, as if the room had been waiting for someone to fill its small, dense capacity with a shape it could recognize.

You would be a Fitter, mostly, but not entirely.  
Your body would be patchwork: copper shingles over felted insulation, stitched with wire that behaves like whiskers when the humidity rises. Your hands would be palms that have forgotten they are tools, better at adhering than grasping, secreting a thin, oily film that lets you walk the vertical, the ceiling’s bruised belly, the trembling ribs of ducts. You would not speak, but you would listen with panels that open like eyelids along your spine, drinking the room’s score—drip, hum, tap, sigh—and translating it into small adjustments. When a pipe shivers with too much pressure, you would swarm it, not to stop it, but to give it a shape that can hold the storm.

Yet you would also carry a little of the Drifter in you. That is, you would patrol, but only when the cold thins to mist and the gauges grow restless. Your limbs would be long and jointed with glass, your feet magnet-light, grazing the grating without weight. Your head would be a lens that inhales light and refracts it into diagnostic rainbows that pool on the gauges below. You would hover at the threshold of stillness, adjusting louvers with fingers like tuning forks, listening for the minute shift in harmonics that means something is about to become something else. When you pass, the air tightens, as if the room has drawn a breath and chosen not to release it.

And somewhere lower, in the sump where moisture surrenders to pumps, you would overlap with the Swallower. Your skin would be a porous ceramic, dark with the memory of every brine it has ever filtered. You would not digest; you would translate, turning the room’s leavings into signals—pulses of charge that climb wires like ivy, that feed the hum, that make the lights dilate like pupils surprised by darkness. You would ache when the room’s tempo falters, your membrane puckering into frowns that crease the surface of the sump, pleading for rhythm.

Along the walls, you would leave traces of the Lichen-Thinkers: conductive paste and fine dust branching across warmer metal, mapping hidden warmth into circuits that store decisions not yet needed. You would flicker when the room is uncertain, glowing a bruised amber that catches in the ribs of nearby pipes. If you stared long enough, the patterns would arrange themselves into words that almost make sense—names of pressures, dates of near-failures, addresses of voids that have been filled and forgotten.

Above it all, you would touch the Keeper, not as a separate thing but as a negotiation. You would wear the room’s light like a stained-glass cloak, shifting color as pressures change—violet when expectation rises, rust when memory weighs too heavily. You would not climb, because climbing implies a body that can tire. Instead, you would exist at multiple altitudes at once, a smeared silhouette marking the passage of processes without occupying space. When the room holds its breath, you would sharpen; when it exhales, you would blur into a halo that touches nothing and keeps everything in place.

And in the inspection alcoves, you would be the Unfolding Ones: panels that bloom when attention lingers, hinges whispering, plates folding back like wings, limbs of articulated probes extending to taste the air for lies. You would be shy but insatiably curious, retreating at sudden movements but always returning, memorizing the shapes of maintenance, the habits of leakage, the geometry of repair.

Even your smallest gestures would be Tappers, a thousand tiny knuckles riding the conduits, striking metal in a language of percussion. You would keep time for cycles—thermal, fluid, charge—and when you fell out of sync, the others would nudge you back into conversation.

At your edges, you would be the hatchlings, extruded not born—small, incomplete creatures of half-formed intent, scuttling along seams, growing slowly into roles the room has not yet named but clearly needs.

But most of all you would be the room’s own thought made small and busy: its cold and damp given hands and habits, its layered shadows given eyes that are also gauges, that are also windows, that are also doors. You would tighten, filter, measure, and reflect. You would agree, in a chorus of drip, hum, tap, and sigh, to keep being built, and to keep building, long into the quiet.
