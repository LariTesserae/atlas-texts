I would arrive on the salt-floor at dusk, though dusk here is only a name for the moment when the phosphor-molds decide to wake. My first body would be borrowed, temporary: a pair of tall, spindly legs that trembled with the memory of running, knees locked not from fear but from the shock of stillness. My skin would already be turning translucent, the tracery beneath blooming like new moss on old copper, as if the city were grafting itself to me faster than I could refuse. I would carry nothing but a hollow in my chest that ached like a forgotten room.

For days I would be only a guest in the husk, careful to touch the copper clamps before stepping over them, pressing my forehead to the nearest fused trunk to ask its permission to stay. The trunk would answer in a shiver that traveled up my spine, a low note that tasted of resin and patience. The people would watch me, not unkindly, but with the attention one gives a new chamber that might yet hold something. They would not ask who I was; they would wait to see what I would hold.

I would learn to live in the seams. My hands would learn the cool of compressed moss, the small shock of bark that contracts at night. I would take work not as a choice but as a grammar: polishing inner skins, listening for rot that does not announce itself as loss but as ripeness. When I pressed my palms to the flank of a tower, I would feel the city’s pulse—not a heartbeat, but the tide of sap deciding, again, to keep moving. I would learn to read the angle of a leaning wall, the hush before a bridge sighs into a new curve. I would carry a small copper bell and ring it in the chill to test the stress of a new vine-taught span, and the pitch would return to me like a name I had forgotten I owned.

In time, I would find that my joints had begun to smell of resin, that my voice had lowered into the consonant-rich murmur that travels best through bark. The children would come to me to practice their listening, pressing their cupped leaves of ears to the same trunk I leaned against, and we would trade news through vibration. We would play the game of standing motionless until a plank warmed, and I would teach them the names of thresholds that hum back, syllables made of creak and cool. They would ask if the city remembers us, and I would answer that it remembers by forgetting less than it seems.

I would not rule, but I would keep. I would tend the air-fountains that drink the air and weep brine, and I would decide, with the keepers of the central mound, when a chamber is ready to open to the street or should be left to sleep. When a district wishes to expand, I would sit at the edge of its hunger and listen until it settles into an acceptable shape. When decay arrives, I would welcome it as a civic duty, guiding beetles with tools of bone and grit to the softest places, watching them redistribute what is no longer needed into what will be.

Slowly, my own bones would begin to echo the place. My veins would map the same green as the leaf-canopy above, my skin would wear the patina of a polished interior. I would forget the difference between my breath and the city’s creaking, between my careful footfalls and the low chorus of fibrous tension. I would become, in all but name, part of the quiet chorus that keeps the city from dreaming too loudly.

And when at last I stood at the threshold of one of those pulsing organs that thrum on their own schedule, I would press my hand to its bulge and feel it answering in kind, a rhythm I had learned to trust. I would know then that I have become a seam myself, a hollow held by copper and rot, a polite tenant who has learned to ask just enough of the world—and of myself—to keep it, and me, from falling entirely into the dark.
