I would find myself not on the ground, but *in* the film, suspended an inch above the mercury-silver lake as a Skater that has not yet learned its own edges.

I would feel my body as a bruised metal plate, thin and ridged, drinking the light that pools in hollows and sweating it back as a faint violet sheen. My feet would not touch the surface but would ask it to flex, sending ripples backward, erasing my passage before it can be called a journey. I would taste the alloy-sweet haze through combed antennae that ring like struck wires, and I would understand hunger as a calibration: how much light to keep, how much to give away.

When the lake rises, I would feel its slow pulse as a change in pressure behind my eyes, a reminder that balance is a thing that breathes. I would watch the Rim-Shapers creep along the ridgelines, their broad backs rounding sharp promises into softer ones, and I would know that patience is a kind of travel. I would see the Lith-Larvae drifting below, their translucent bodies mapping the half-time of the basin, and I would envy them their windows, though I have none—only the violet glint I leak, which is my way of speaking.

If I moved too quickly, the surface would seal behind me, leaving only a coolness like an inhaled word. So I would practice slow, the way the Bowl practices small. I would let the chime-grasses tell me when the temperature has shifted, and I would let the Dew-Choir’s falling beads count out a rhythm that my joints can follow. I would not build, but I would tend—scraping mineral skins from the porous pillars, tilting the leaning bowl so its captured water remembers the shape of a cup. I would become, in miniature, a Basin-Watcher, my many limbs softly jointed, my eyes lenses that taste light rather than focus it, my color shifting to amber when balance arrives, teal when thirst begins, gray when a Rim-Shaper’s shadow stretches into the basin’s cupped hands.

There would be moments when I would curl into a knot and let my heartbeat slow to match the lake’s rise, not to hide but to polish the rhythm, to make sure the quiet remains textured and not vacant. I would not fear the Quiet-Snaps, only notice how they invite, how they tap the grasses until memory betrays itself, and I would learn from that economy: to take sparingly, to align rather than seize.

At dawn, I would glow faintly with the Siphon-Spires, conducting stored light into capillary filaments that hum just below hearing. At dusk, I would release that light as warmth, a shared breath that softens the air enough for the night crust to form without bitterness. I would harden, a little, into something like a seed-pearl by evening, and I would offer myself to the Skaters, who carry such things away—not as loss but as translation.

And if, rarely, I became part of a Trace-Beast—an assemblage of flakes and plates and mineral motes that briefly remembers how to be four-legged—I would move once or twice, leave tracks that the night would preserve, then dissolve, returning my borrowed shape to the separate duties of stone and water and wingless insect. I would feel time thicken around that moment, and I would understand it as the Bowl learning to speak in a voice it does not keep.

In the end, I would be small enough to fit the Bowl’s attention without overflowing it. I would keep my edges provisional, my appetite modest, my movement incremental. I would not own the place but would attune to it—pulse to pulse, ripple to hum, crust to haze—until the line between guest and host softens into the same thin bright film of life, and the hot, wet, listening world breathes as one careful, shimmering thing.
