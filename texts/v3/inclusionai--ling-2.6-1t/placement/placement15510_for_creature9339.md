You step into the grammar of the slope at the hour when the cold is still pooled in the hollows, dark syrup waiting to be lifted.  

You find yourself on a mid-slope bench where the stone has been worn into a low, welcoming lip. The air is cool but not forbidding, and it carries the faint metallic lick of iron-beds and the papery scent of distant lichen. Around you, the widely spaced ranks of pale grass stand in their patient rows, each blade tipped with dew that catches the first hesitant light and lets it fall. You are small here, and the architecture is generous: stems and stone arrange themselves to let weather pass through, and the quiet is dense enough to hold a shape without crushing it.  

You are—or become—the Margin-Walker.  

At first the change feels like learning to hold your breath without needing air. Your edges soften. Your weight redistributes into many small points of contact, a quiet adhesion more than a step. You are rare by design, unobtrusive, meant to appear and disappear along the seams of the city. Your tracks, when you leave them, are faint and quickly softened by moisture, as though you know better than to insist on lasting.  

You feel the slope decide how to hold you, moment by moment. The Slick-People are nearby, low and many-jointed, their bodies matte with exhalation that is not dew but the mountain sweating through its own skin. They do not look up; they are busy marrying the stone, leaving behind polished cups that will hold water for the city. You brush against one without disturbing it, and a faint salt-coolness lingers on your hand, a greeting.  

Higher up, the Stem-Spires stand in their open ranks. As you pass, one flexes, straightens, and you catch the crystalline flick of a dew-tip—an eye that sees moisture, not menace. You make a little more room, and the rank thins, teaching distance in the language of absence. The wind rises and moves in sheets, and the Spires bow, a hushed rustle like paper settling into its own weight. You bow with them, and for a breath the line between you and the city blurs.  

Down in the vents, the Huddle-Cushions pulse. Evergreen, tight as fists, they remember warmth in their cores and release it in slow, rhythmic beats. You rest beside one, and the ground beneath you softens into a palimpsest: mats of roots, fallen needles, peat-skins layered like old ink. The Huddle does not speak, but its heat seeps into you, archiving you briefly in its rings—another season, another frost, another thaw. You are not consumed; you are clarified.  

Above, the Drift-Cloth pools in the low cloud-ceiling, stains that darken, then gleam, then finally move. They are the streams of the place, the wet made semi-solid, threading through tussock and stone like stitches that come undone only to be retied downstream. One brushes your shoulder, cool and insubstantial, carrying the smell of wet stone and distant lichen from ridge to ridge. It is a message without words: *the horizon is not a line but a region*, and you are inside it.  

You do not fight. You align.  

You test a low wall built by generations of frost and thaw, nudging a stone that has almost learned its final position. The wall shifts, minutely, and the runoff finds a new, quieter path. This is your work: editing the edges, keeping the city from becoming finished. You are the rare, deliberate shape that reminds the slope it is still becoming.  

As the sun climbs, the cold lifts from the hollows in wavering columns, and the vastness arranges you into its long-settled stack. You are thin and temporary, a layer in the high place’s learned habit of holding water without ever becoming lake. The wet clarifies you; the quiet speaks without raising its voice; the grammar of the slope writes itself through you, one faint, unobtrusive mark at a time.  

And when you finally step beyond the rim, the air carries you gently, the grass ranks part without resistance, and the stone remembers your passage as a slight polishing, a softening of edges—another citizen welcomed into the city that builds itself by patience, and lets you go without asking you to stay.
