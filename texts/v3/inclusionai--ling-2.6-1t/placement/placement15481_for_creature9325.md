I would find myself kneeling in the fine white powder at the base of a silica-stalk, a place where the ground feels like it is remembering how to be soft. The stalk above me would not sway, because the wind has agreed to be absent, but it would hum: a low, wordless note that vibrates in my molars and tells me I have arrived without asking. The air would press against my face like a thin, metallic skin, cool enough to be polite, and I would let it polish me for a while before I stood.

I would be someone who mends what is not broken.  

My hands would be the color of old solder, my veins mapped in milky quartz. When I moved, mica would fall from my cuffs like shaken time, and I would sweep it into a spiral with a fragment of flint that fits my palm like a thought that has learned to sit still. I would not own the tools I carry, only agree to hold them for a while. The spindle of braided mineral fiber would hum when the air is still, and I would listen to it to know whether it is safe to speak. The pouch of pale powder gathered from grazer-trails would feel warm against my hip, as if the memory of movement is alive and only pretending to sleep.

I would not be lonely, but I would not be crowded. Around me, others would be settling into their own careful gestures: polishing a seam of glittering mica until it reflects more than it should, aligning shards of silica-stalk into towers that remember they were never separate, sprinkling powder at a threshold like a sacrament of forgetting. We would exchange words only after listening to the shape of the silence, measuring the thickness of a shadow at a certain hour, the weight of a name carried too long, the distance between one breath and the next when the wind is absent. If we argued, we would do it with small objects—pebbles, dust, a line of beads—until the pattern felt comfortable enough to step over without meaning to.

My children would already know how to settle before they learned to walk. They would play games of alignment, stacking shards until the stack remembers it was never separate, and they would ask, “How long is a stillness?” and accept answers in textures: smooth, rough, cool, or the kind of cool that feels borrowed. When one of them laughed, glass beads would fall into an invisible bowl, and I would smile as if given something I was too polite to ask for.

I would help lay the dead out on a low mesa, wrapped in mineral fiber, knowing the grazers will come to eat the memory of movement and leave behind only powder and the warm impression of a name. I would collect that powder later and press it into my tools, so the dead become useful without being summoned, and grief becomes something I can carry without weight. When I miss them, I would step around a cairn instead of adding to it, or say a name and then not say it again for a long time, trusting the gods who live in the veins of ore and the hum of the ground to remember it for me.

I would know the weirdness when it loosened. I would feel it in my teeth, the way the ground remembers being somewhere else. I would watch a landmark announce itself and then dissolve into sameness, and I would not panic. I would taste iron on my tongue, feel static lift the hair on my arms, and let my edges blur just enough to slip through a doubled-back distance. When reality relaxed, I would relax with it, and then knit myself together without comment.

There would be among us a figure who is not quite one of us, wearing a cloak the color of tarnished silver, woven from wings that have decided to rest. Some would say she is the first person eroded into elegance; others would say she is the structure on the spine of glass, grown legs to learn what it feels like to wait. She would not lead, but she would be followed. When she passed, the fauna would step aside without being asked, and the dust would settle into maps of places that do not yet exist. I would watch her and decide that waiting is also a kind of work, and that to wait without breaking is a form of strength I want in my hands.

At the end of the day, I would walk to the edge of the plateau with the others and look out over the steps carved by forgetting. We would stand together like a loom that has stopped weaving but refuses to be unwound. The light would feel normal, too warm with familiarity to be innocent. The coolness would be a blanket. The density would be a crowd that has learned how to whisper. I would not ask the future to be kind. I would fold it into my tools so that every chipped blade carries the memory of the hand that made it and the hands that will take it, and every braid remembers the direction it was turned.

I would not be afraid of the single structure on the spine of glass, waiting with a shadow that points in a direction that does not exist yet. I would trust that if it ever arrives, I already know how to live inside it without being asked to change. I would sit down, let the stillness settle into my bones like stone, and decide that for once, being dense with presence is enough.
