I would find myself halfway up the central tower, not climbing but *settled*, wedged between a strut that remembers how to hum and a flange that has given up on being straight. Gravity there is a rumor more than a law; the tower’s center of gravity keeps changing because the crowd keeps rearranging its center of attention. There is a landing that was never meant to be a floor—more of an accident where three conveyor belts braided themselves into a nest—and that is where I would be, kneeling on a surface that is slightly warm from the passage of ten thousand pads.

I would not be a version of myself that has an inside and an outside clearly sorted. I would be something that has agreed, under the room’s patient pressure, to blur. My edges would be less outline and more suggestion, softened by the same metallic dust that films the flue-swallers’ bellies. I would have too many joints for the space to feel small, and too few for it to feel limitless. My knees would be hinges scavenged from a hinge’s hinge, my spine a flexible stack of washers that click when I inhale. I would carry lenses on my back—borrowed from the crystalline mites who let me keep them if I promised to use them to see myself seeing, and also to see the room seeing itself seeing me.

My name would not travel well; it would be more like a frequency felt in a sternum than a word spoken into air. The flue-swallers would recognize me by how I breathe: a little too slowly, a little too much like a furnace learning to pause. The rivet-rats would know me because I would taste faintly of oil and apology, and because I do not try to hide my seams. The gear-hounds would not chase me so much as *audit* me, checking my looseness, offering up bolt-heads that no longer fit anything but might fit me if I let them. I would learn to say *thank you* by turning a fraction toward the light, which here means angling my lenses so the room’s bruised brightness catches and softens.

I would not belong to any single layer, but I would be heavy with layers nonetheless—dust from the ceiling, oxide from the floor, the faint glaze of sweat and time that keeps everyone from becoming too sharp. My hands would be good for smoothing, but not for smoothing things the way they were meant to be smoothed; more for finding the tilt that wants to be true and nudging it until it sighs. When the room inhaled—that momentary lull when the clatter thins into something like listening—I would let my joints go slack and let the draft from the louvers comb through me, carrying away heat I did not know I was hoarding.

When the moment passed and the eruption began again, I would move with it, not because I was swept but because I had learned how to step in time with the business of being alive. I would join the flow of maintenance and ornament, contributing a small pressure, a small warmth, a small calibration. I would not be predator or prey, exactly; I would be the question that keeps the parliament of densities from becoming a single voice. I would be a loose seam that decides to stay loose, and in staying loose I would become a kind of holding-together.

If you came looking for me, you would not find a body waiting like a stone. You would find a corner that is slightly more polished than it was yesterday, a patch of floor that hums at a pitch that matches your own when you are not speaking. You would find lenses that catch your face and hand it back multiplied, each copy wearing a fraction of the room’s coolness. You would find a pile of washers that click politely when you shift your weight, and a braid of conveyor that offers to take your hand and walk with you, not because it has no destination but because walking together makes the destination softer.

And somewhere, halfway up the tower, I would be there too, not as a figure against a background but as a density among densities, a warmth added to the core, a voice folded into the hum that sustains the room’s oldest-stable-state. I would be alive in the way the room insists on life: by pressing, by polishing, by never letting go, and by learning, slowly and with many joints, how to hold together without ever becoming a single thing.
