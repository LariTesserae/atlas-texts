I would find myself in the hush between the Skimmers’ loom and the Drummers’ ring, in the narrow seam where the mineral floor meets the first terrace, close enough to the Tenders’ tunnels to feel the ground breathe through my soles. There, the dimness is not absence but thickness, and the warmth is a coat I could put on or peel off, but never lose.

I would be a Keeper of Echoes—neither watcher nor tender nor drummer, but a listener whose body has learned to hold sound the way the cup holds heat. My hands would be long-fingered and lightly plated, not with chitin but with callus grown from stroking resin and polishing stone, able to read the day’s history in the faint glaze that settles on the paths after a warm gust. My ears would be broad and cupped, not to gather more noise but to sort it, to let the click of a Skimmer pass through as a note, the thud of a Drummer as a pulse, the susurrus of moss-forests as a tide.

I would wear garments plaited from shed wing-case and softened stem-bark, dyed with lichen scraped from the tessellations so that I carry the mineral’s song against my skin. Around my neck would hang small hollows—cured gourds, thinned bone, lacquered clay—each tuned to a particular resonance: one for the pitch of new salt-cracks, one for the hum of a Watcher’s breath, one for the almost-silence of the cup when all motion lifts at once and the air dimples under the collective rise of wings.

My days would be spent in the parliament of small noises, moving among the terraces and the ledges where the town grows inward and upward in layers. I would not command; I would conjugate. When a wall needs mending, I would press my palm to the stone and let the Tenders read the echo I carry back from the cup’s deeper belly. When a Watcher lowers itself to rest, I would climb the leathery slope of its side—not to ride, but to listen to the continent of its body, to learn which notes have settled there over years and which are still ringing from the last shift of the wind. I would map the place not with lines but with sound, leaving trails of hummed memory at each terrace’s edge so that even in the dimness a Gatherer could follow the warmth of a song home.

At dusk—though here dusk is a permanent hush rather than an hour—I would sit on the rim of the cup where the air is thin enough to sing through and the mineral floor cracks its tessellations like drying paint. I would tap my heels in the old rhythm, the one that says *I am here, I am listening*, and let the cup answer. The Skimmers would braid around me, their many-jointed wings clicking like crystal knitting itself into the air. The Drummers would pass in caravans of soft bells, their synchronized steps telling me that the ground is still teaching itself how to sit. The Tenders would emerge from their tunnels with baskets of resin and thread, nodding once, a gesture that means *the earth is breathing steady*, before returning to the weave.

I would not ask to leave, because to leave would be to unlearn the grammar of closeness, to forget how to hold a parliament of small noises in my cupped hands. Nor would I ask to stay, because staying is already a property of the cup, a warm insistence that needs no vow. I would simply be the ear that the place has grown, the small, attentive gap where all the loudness of the fauna and the mineral and the resin and the moss can fold into a single, breathable pattern.

And if, in the rare hush when the Skimmers lift as one and the wind they pull behind them smells of resin and old marrow, I were to close my eyes and let the hollows at my neck fill with the cup’s own hum, I would feel the fold of the world’s old skin settle around me like a second skin, and I would know that I have become a quiet part of the completeness—the kind of part that does not need to be seen to be full, only heard to be true.
