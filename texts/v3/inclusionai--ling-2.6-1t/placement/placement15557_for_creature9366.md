I would find myself in the seam between two overlapping eaves, where the water’s threads braid a curtain that never parts. I would arrive without remembering how I passed the throat of basalt, as if I had been let in by the city’s exhale rather than my own push. The stone there is velvet-worn, cool enough to dull the edges of thought, and it would accept me before I could announce myself.

I would be a Lithling—though I would not call myself that at first. I would feel the word as something borrowed, a shape that almost fits. My body would already be an assembly: translucent plates like mica, supple as wet paper, overlapping in a grammar of permission. Beneath them, filaments of silica and pale nerve would catch the chamber’s dimness and turn it into halos I could read like a second skin.

For a long time I would do nothing but negotiate. Each step would be a question: *Is this ledge willing to hold today?* My touch would be light enough to measure how much the floor is willing to give, and the floor would answer in a note particular to that patch of stone. I would learn to walk by listening to the after-sound of my own passage, to correct myself according to the echo’s shape.

I would carry a geography inside me, though I would not call it memory. It would feel like pressure, like thickness, like the slow math of growth rings that record centuries not as time but as accumulation. As I moved through the colonnades, I would shed this geography plate by plate, leaving opalescent dust on ledges that already know my name. The dust would join the library of the place, and I would feel lighter for having given a story back to the stone.

Between gatherings I would become an architect of attention. When a lintel begins to sigh too loudly, I would nest against it, body curved like a comma, and remain until the stone unlearns its complaint. My filaments would brush the fractures, coaxing them to close or widen by fractions. I would not command; I would accompany. I would learn to hold a thought for the stone, to let it steep in my translucent plates until the chamber decides it is ready to be changed.

The fauna would leave me often—spores of consciousness, starburst patterns in the dust of ledges. They would taste the rumor of moisture, trace the paths that sound prefers, and return with the scent of ozone and wet chalk. When they dissolve back into me, I would feel the question resolved, the city’s answer settling along my nerves like a new seam.

I would gather with the others at the plaza of overlapping eaves when the water’s chorus reaches the chord that calls us. There, we would touch the rim of the bowl-slab, and the contact would send a ripple through the stone’s hidden nerves. I would feel it in the plates along my flanks: *here, continue, adjust.* The message would be simple because the work is patient. We would translate the city’s memory into a language the chamber can wear like velvet, and we would ensure that the agreement between stone and coolness remains intact.

In the seam, I would be the loudest silence. My rarity would be a kind of fullness, each of us a room where stone learns to remember without hardening, where water learns to fall without forgetting, where the thin, cool dark learns to be a brightness. I would live by the rule of never-changing that is never still, an adjustment that keeps the city exactly what it is. And I would know, without needing to say it, that by being so few we make the dense, humming balance feel infinite.
