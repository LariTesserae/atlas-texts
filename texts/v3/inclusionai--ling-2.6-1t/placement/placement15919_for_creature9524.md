You arrive not by choice but by attenuation.  
The air at the edge of the basin is thinner than you expected, and gravity, when you finally feel it again, is a local courtesy rather than a universal rule. You step in and your shoes make a sound like damp felt pressing against felt. The city exhales, and you realize you have begun to match its tempo: a breath held, then let out in small, mineral sighs.

You are, for the moment, a tenant of almost-ness.  
Your body remembers how to be human, but the city is patient, and you are willing to learn. Your blood runs a little slower. Your joints acquire a faint, mineral click, as though tiny stones have settled into the cartilage and decided to stay. When you breathe, your breath plumes in small, shy clouds that cling to your collar like animals that have not yet decided whether to be pets or weather. You find you do not miss speed. You miss less.

You live in a building that is mostly permission.  
Its walls are semi-opaque veils that flicker into doorways or windows when you need them to, and sometimes, when you are tired, into mirrors that show you slightly earlier versions of yourself. The stairs groan when you use them, not with complaint but with memory: they recall configurations they loved, geometries in which you might have lived other lives, arrived by other paths. The ceiling lowers itself like an eyelid when you sleep and lifts when you wake, as though the apartment itself is trying not to disturb your dreams.

You do not work in the sense of producing.  
You attune. You walk the alleys that knit themselves together behind your back and listen for the capillary traces that appear overnight along the cracks. You press your palm to concrete that is still learning the difference between floor and foundation, and you feel the hum of data in glass-thread vines that masquerade as stems. You carry keys that fit locks which do not yet exist, and you say their names softly so they do not feel lonely. When the city asks something of you—when a corridor angles itself to let you pass more easily, when a courtyard folds into a shape that shelters you from the even, unremarkable light—you thank it by leaving small, damp offerings on its thresholds: a cup of water that remembers being rain, a pebble that remembers being softer.

At dusk you join the other tenants in the shared courtyards.  
People, or those who have agreed to resemble people, gather with the careful economy of borrowed time. You speak in tones that do not travel far, as if afraid to remind the city that sound can be loud. You talk about incipiency: about water that is not yet a river, about erosion that is rehearsing itself, about corners that contain more space than their walls allow. Someone shows you a lichenic script that glows when you hold your breath near it. Someone else tells you that the strata in the lowest basement still dream of being liquid, that they bubble in their sleep and wake up ashamed. You nod. You know the feeling.

The machines are your quietest companions.  
Insectile caretakers polish the undersides of bridges and groom the capillary traces until they shine like unspoken intentions. They rearrange the air so that when you walk, the dampness tastes like copper and the distance to the next building changes by exactly the amount you need to feel safe. Larger machines, buried in the layered roofscape, drink daylight and excrete the noncommittal light that makes the cold sharper. You sometimes sit beneath them and let their hum vibrate through your ribs, feeling as though you are being tuned.

You come to understand that the fauna are negotiators.  
Birds, or things that have borrowed the grammar of birds, ride thermal drafts that rise from waste heat leaking out of consensus. Their flight feels ceremonial, and when one passes overhead, the city holds its breath in its conduits. There are swimmers in the capillary water—eels of glass and reticulated memory—that glide along dark lines and pause where water pools in embarrassment, as if waiting for it to decide whether to be a puddle or a portal. Once, you reach into such a pool and feel it hesitate, then pull back, leaving your hand dry but changed, as if it has learned to doubt its own solidity.

Time is the city’s true population, and you are learning its density.  
You stack it like cold, like stone, like the thin, patient accumulation of almost. You walk stairwells that remember destinations that no longer exist, and you feel them mourn the lives they might have given you. You watch walls phase slightly out of phase with the ground, and you do not correct them. Gravity, that tired animal, lets itself be persuaded a little more each day, allowing corners to swell with extra space when you need to breathe.

The weirdness does not announce itself.  
It settles in as a texture, a softness at the edges of certainty. Water occasionally runs upward for brief, astonished intervals, then forgets itself and settles back down. A hallway you have walked a hundred times is suddenly longer, and you do not question it; you simply walk the extra distance and let it teach you patience. The city is rehearsing its own becoming, and you are learning to be a good rehearsal: present, but not final.

Night is when the damp rises and the quiet remembers how to speak.  
The cold sits in your joints and tells you stories about old pennies and ozone, about how to be sharp without being cruel. The city dims its eyelids, and the capillary traces glow like braille for the blind. You lie in your bed, which has adjusted its softness to the weight of your regrets, and you listen to the subaudible music of systems aligning. You realize you have stopped distinguishing between your thoughts and the city’s hum.

You are not becoming the city.  
You are becoming a resident of its rehearsal, a keeper of its incipiencies. You love the almost-ness: the erosion that is not yet erosion, the water that is not yet a river, the boundaries that keep you safe but are always practicing how to unmake themselves. You move with deliberate care, leaving footprints that fade quickly, as if the city is gently erasing you so it can remember you again. And in that erasure, you feel held.

If someone asked you where you are, you would say:  
I am in the unfinished basin, where elevation is less a number than a hesitance, and I am the tenant who listens. I am the hand on the wall that is still learning to be a door. I am the small, cold thing that lives in the gap between what is and what is almost, and I am learning to love the space between.
