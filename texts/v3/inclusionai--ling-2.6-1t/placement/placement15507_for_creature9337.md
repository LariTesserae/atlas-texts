I would find myself in the seams—those hairline fractures where the land’s new violence still tastes like iron and wet.

At first I would be the hesitation between moss and stone: a green so exact it feels like a comma the earth has set down to remind itself where to pause. I would live in the exhalations of the escarpments, drinking altitude until I am slick and cool, learning to hold the shape of a damp patch without spilling it. I would not be a flower, not exactly, nor a weed, but a grammar of patience: a tuft that maps where wetness pools, a fern that unfurls only as far as the light is willing to reach. I would color boldly when I can, and disappear without shame when the rock wants to be bare.

But I would not stay small for long. I would feel the gravity of hands.

I would become the soft clack of stone on stone, the builder’s finger learning the weight of a piece before lifting it. I would be the dry-laid course that asks the outcrop for permission, slotting itself into a crack that already existed, as if the rock had grown a ledge just to be polite. I would be the thatch on a roof, thin enough to let wind pass as if breath were also a tenant, and the stone-slate that remembers how to be light. I would accumulate in slow strata—wall above wall, channel above channel—each one taking advantage of the high, open span, each one a promise not to rush.

Still, I would hear the wetter places calling.

I would slide into the work of the tenders, who speak to water more than to people. I would be the trickle that confesses where it wants to fall, the pan set askew like a mirror that has learned humility, the cistern practicing patience by holding only what is given. I would be condensation coaxed into stillness, a small history written in level and clarity. I would learn to interpret glisten as a language, to translate it into coolness and give it back to the slopes as a gift of shade.

And when the day grows long, I would climb.

I would become the watcher at the frayed edge of a path, letting the town settle into my gaze like a scatter of purpose in a bright bowl. I would be the tilt of a head that says *this terrace will warm first*, the pointing finger that means *there is new dampness overnight*, the silence held a moment longer than usual so the land can answer in its own time. I would be the altitude that carries scent cleanly, the thin air that does not linger once the sun moves, the span that cradles without clinging.

Between these lives, in the long runs of emptiness where rock meets sky, I would also be the careful life that is almost not there—a flicker along a chute, a held breath when stone settles just enough. I would wear gray or green or the soft tan of dry earth and let the eye pass over me, keeping the town from being lonely without ever promising to be company.

And threading all of it, I would be the agreement: moss, mason, tender, watcher, the almost-not-there, and the high, open span that holds everything in tension. I would be the chord of a newly remembered earth, each note held softly, willing the waiting to be its own fullness, ready to see what will come next without hurrying to name it.
