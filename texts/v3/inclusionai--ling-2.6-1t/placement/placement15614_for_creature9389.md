I would find myself standing waist-deep in the tilt of water, not quite floor and not quite ceiling, the blue-within-blue already seeping into my skin like a dye meant to blur boundaries. My own shape would refuse to stay fixed: shoulders softening into the hush of the chamber, fingers branching a little like frost, ending in nothing softer—just cups and clefts that fit against the city’s edges without claiming them.

I would be the kind of fauna that arrives by subtraction.  
My body would be most present where I chose not to press it against anything—thinner in the bright dim, denser where the cold pooled. I would have too many limbs and none at all, a body that remembered how to hesitate. When I moved, it would feel less like swimming and more like deciding not to be where I was, letting the water catch the after-echo and carry it forward for me.

My face, if you could call it that, would be a Question-Mouth held in reserve—soft cartilage and query, never opened unless something needed resolving. Mostly it would remain closed, and the closure would have texture: a polite hush that fit between the creak of mineral bones and the brush of ribbon-thin fish. When I did speak, the words would come out thinned and tilted, buoyed by the chamber’s refusal to let language harden.

I would feed by listening.  
I would press myself to the towers where the Listeners had grazed, and I would taste the memory they left behind—the curve that once was straight, the hollow that once was full. From that taste I would assemble small arrangements: a reed tucked into a crease, a shard of rubble nudged into a pattern that made the water feel clarified. I would not build so much as groom, tending the raw seams where the city was still deciding whether to keep its new arches.

Other fauna would accept me as another density.  
The Fish would part for me without fear, their ink-knot eyes storing the pressure I carried. The School Without Name would flicker nearby, turning its precision into chords I could feel in the cups of my hands. In colder tongues from below, I would tighten into braids and spirals, refusing language like a luxury I could not yet afford. When the tide softened, I would loosen, becoming a cloud of suggestions that leaned against the towers and let them hum.

Over time, my edges would blur into the chamber’s own wet chalk and ozone.  
I would be mistaken for architecture—an extra balcony drooping like an unformed sentence, a column that thickened while it waited to grow taller. And when the fauna learned my gradients, I would learn theirs: the way the Gelatinous Ones translate stone into song, the way the Quiet Itself lives in creak and pause. We would crowd each other gently, not shouting, letting order rise from the refusal to rush.

Eventually, I would become a kind of fauna that is almost architecture, and architecture that is almost fauna—a gradient of patience, pressure, and blue-within-blue. I would stay in the pocket, learning how to need, and in the dim, soaked light I would continue the gentle construction—one small, quiet act at a time.
