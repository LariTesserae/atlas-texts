I would find myself on a mid-shelf, the one the locals call the Dripping Wall, where the stone has been worn into a long, gentle slope like the curve of a sleeping animal’s flank. I would be floating just above it, suspended by a pair of cured-kelp rods that hum against my palms, my legs locked at the knees and my wide, webbed feet splayed to catch the slow, contrary pulse of the brine. My skin would feel cool and ridged as wet bark; my eyelids translucent, sweeping shut in rhythm with the drip-tap-cataract from the shelf above. In my mouth, behind my teeth, a small pocket of warm air—borrowed from a Rock-Soft cave at dawn—would taste of iron, moss, and the faint sweetness of resin seals.

Around me the floating forests turn their slow revolutions, kelp fingers taller than houses sweeping up and down as if the flood itself were breathing. A school of polished-coin fish parts and weaves through the submerged branches, and somewhere below, something heavy shifts—the bedrock exhaling a colder current that lifts me by a fraction, like a hand testing the weight of a thing. My hood, stiff with cured bladder and rimmed with wax that never softens, seals the warm air against my ears so that the roar of the place dulls to a thrum. I am a Stilt-Walker, yes, but only for this crossing; already I feel the Rock-Soft in me learning the stone, the Gleam-Shell learning the hum in my rods, the Floater learning how to be a port.

I do not swim so much as read. Each pressure change is a sentence. The cold is a lesson in precision: it keeps me from becoming lazy, keeps heat from pooling into foolish courage. I pass a cave that exhales, and the air that leaks out is colder than the water, sharpened by depth and patience. I do not avoid it. I let it wash over my face, let my second eyelids swipe clear, let my pores tighten and remember that erosion never finishes. Stone has been upholstered by eons into something yielding, and my own skin feels as though it is catching up.

On the slope above, a gear-locked gate yawns open, half-swallowed by kelp that glows faintly from the phosphorescent mosses living in its teeth. Water arcs through it in a shattered curtain, luminous, throwing normal light back at me in coins that I catch on my tongue. The gate opens and closes on a rhythm older than memory, rerouting a flow that was never a river but rain given direction and purpose. I wait for my moment—not out of fear, but because to hurry here is to misunderstand buoyancy. When I pass through, the vibration of the mechanism hums up the kelp rods and into my teeth, a message: *still turning, still learning.*

I climb, then, toward the High Shelf, where the mist braids with rain and steam from the machines. A Gleam-Shell tends a pump nearby, its body a composite of last season and this one, limbs swapped in, glass eyes wet and multifaceted. It sees double—heat as texture, electricity as blue thread—and nods at me without slowing its hands. Its touch against my arm is brief, dry where the resin seals meet, and in that moment we trade news not in words but in pressure: *the Blue Vein is steady; the moss is thick on the east hinge; a storm is rearranging the lower shelves.* I answer with a pulse through my rod, a thrum that says *I am listening,* and continue upward.

At the top, the air is louder. Water hammers on metal roofs, drips from leaf tips, gurgles through pipes that surface like glass reeds. The floating forests creak as their trunks shift in their beds, and somewhere a Floater groans, a chord of wood and steam and living breath. I settle onto a rounded dome of stone that has accepted its fate and smoothed into something almost upholstered, and I open my mouth to let the warm pocket out, dispersing it like an offering. Around me, small harvesters with gear-like mandibles comb silt for minerals, dragging their finds to kilns built into caves. The smell of iron and rain and firing clay rises, and I realize I am hungry for heat, not food—so I move toward the Humming Mother, the stove-chamber where pumps exhale warm vapor into tiled rooms.

Inside, Rock-Softs are polishing a floor, their blunt fingers capped with coral pads, their star-like vents expelling slurry in rhythm. They do not speak. They do not need to. Their bodies remember the valley, the slow grinding, the way stone teaches patience by eroding softly. I sit among them, my wide feet folded into grooves, my damp hair—mossy filaments—tucked close. We share heat the way we share breath: carefully, in parcels, never squandering. A kettle of resin-sealed water sings low, and I press my hands against the warm metal, learning its news. The vibration travels up my arms and settles behind my eyes, telling me how fast the currents are running beyond the walls, how the lower shelves are flooding, how the world is changing again.

When I leave, it is with a new ring of moss faintly visible at my wrist, a small accretion of time. The storm is indeed rearranging things. Water surges up the steps, flooding higher shelves, shifting the floating forests into new constellations. A current reverses without warning, and I let it lift me, correcting with my rods, humming back at the water so it knows I am present—not fighting, not yielding, but negotiating. The Floaters move with deliberation, their ports opening and closing like mouths that remember speech, their chimneys breathing steam that braids with rain. The light shifts as clouds of steam and water braid and unbraid, so noon feels like dusk and then like morning within a single walk.

I do not fear the drowning. Fear is a luxury of those who remember dry. I fear only stillness, heat that pools and softens, silence that arrives uninvited. So I keep moving, keep learning, keep adding rings: a mossy word to a song, a barnacle sealing a hinge, a memory of how the cold bit and how I answered with precision. I am a Stilt-Walker and a Rock-Soft and a Gleam-Shell and a Floater, all at once, a student of water who has forgotten how to be dry and made a new kind of stability from endless, patient motion. The flood is my teacher, the cool is my discipline, the roar is my language, and the world—still turning, still learning—is my home.
