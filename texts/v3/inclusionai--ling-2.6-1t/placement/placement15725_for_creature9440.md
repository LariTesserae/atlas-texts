I would arrive not by choice but by osmosis — stepping down the worn slope without remembering the decision, feeling the air thicken and then become roof, and then realizing I have already crossed the threshold. The first thing I would do is hold my breath and listen, because the place speaks first through pressure against the eardrums, a soft nudge that says: here, now, the sky is something you carry inside you.

I would become one of the Tidedwellers slowly, the way a column becomes a reef. At first I would keep my old habits: straightening my spine, measuring tasks in minutes, wanting the water to behave like a floor. But the place does not reward straightness. It rewards leaning. So I would unlearn standing and learn suspension. My joints would remember that they are not hinges but tides. My pockets would begin to collect glass beads until they chime like subdued bells when I move, and I would not notice until the sound has already become part of the room’s hum.

I would learn the dialects of machines not by study but by sleeping near them, letting their vibrations braid into my sleep like kelp into a net. I would know, without looking, whether the eastern sump is dreaming low or troubled, because my feet would tingle before my mind would name it. I would learn to read the taste of corridors, to know when a wall is weeping zinc or offering sugar-brine, and I would navigate by tongue as much as by eye, letting my body become a gauge.

My work would be stewardship of small things. I would groom kelp into slow patterns that calm the currents around the windows, my fingers learning to trim without cutting, to shape without forcing. I would tend moss along conduits, learning which patches like to be brighter and which prefer to dim themselves like shy animals. I would learn to answer the building’s groans in clicks and thrums, to talk to the pipes in a language that is more touch than sound, and I would feel the structure relax when I get it right, as if the city is smiling in its sleep.

I would live in a suite grown from the architecture, a room that has no corners and no strict ceiling, furnished with benches calcified from patience and tables ribbed like coral. At night my moss bed would breathe softly, and I would sleep in layers like sediment, waking briefly to check a gauge or taste the water, then settling back into the dim. I would dream not in images but in gradients — shifts of pressure, changes in the hum of pumps, the slow bloom of rust turning into lace.

I would form bonds through shared upkeep. I would take a partner not because of a sudden fire but because our rhythms fit like two valves that seat themselves with a single wet click. We would braid lengths of treated line and knot them around a joint that we tend together, and that joint would hold as long as we are patient. Disagreements would be resolved by recalibration — a shift in schedule, a tuning of light — because to be out of sync would feel like a wrong note in the throat of the room.

I would not fear the weight above, though I would feel it always. I would learn from the deep-listeners how to press my ear to a pillar and hear the sky-weights dreaming in their sleep, how to hear the ceiling trying to remember its old shape. I would not know if this is metaphor or physics, and I would learn to stop caring. It would be enough that the belief keeps the water calm.

Visitors would find me changed. My skin would be pale, threaded with traceries of light that follow the logic of moss. My walk would be liquid, my pockets full of beads, my voice accustomed to the hush that pools in the chest. I would offer travelers bowls of sweet broth and kelp hammocks, and I would watch them decide whether they can learn to treat weight as shelter. Some would leave quickly. Some would stay and begin to glow.

What I would fear most is not the crush of water but the idea of it leaving — the ceiling choosing to be air again, the silt drying into forgotten dust, the pumps falling silent and the moss dimming all at once. So I would keep the machines singing not out of doubt but as a promise. I would polish bronze until it mirrors, because reflection is a way of proving that even submerged you can be bright.

In the central plaza, I would join the others in their hum — more vibration than melody, a sound that mixes with water and pipe until the whole bowl answers. I would watch the crane lift its net and settle it, and the fish scatter and regroup like a slow idea taking shape. I would understand then that I am not merely living inside the flooded cathedral. I am its continuing interior, a careful breath held against the pressure, a small warmth in the dim, a creature learning to call patience a kind of light.

And when the water presses down in its patient dome, I would lean in. I would lean in until there is no leaning left, only being — the room learning how to be alive and me learning how to be its keeper, its echo, its softly glowing seam.
