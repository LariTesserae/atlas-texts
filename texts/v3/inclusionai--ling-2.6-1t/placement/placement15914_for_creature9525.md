I would find myself at the edge of a fresh scar in the rock, where the basin’s new lip overhangs a shallow, spreading sheet of water. At first I would be nothing more than the damp chill that settles behind my knees, the small resistance of velvet moss deciding if I am a suitable place to grow. Then I would be the slow seepage finding a path down a faint crack, learning how the stone answers to patient weight.

Gradually I would be a single lantern-plant bulb set into a fold of living root, my glow turned slightly green by the water in my cells. I would be the first cautious thread of brightness in a passage that had been dim, the sort of light that apologizes for its own existence until it is told it is needed. Around me, the Seamfolk would pass in their murmuring congress, their translucent skin drinking my excess and handing it onward, a quiet relay of borrowed luminescence.

As days of drip and dark accumulate, I would thicken into something more stubborn: a knuckle of hardened resin, a watchful eye of polished bone set into a gate that is less a door than a living agreement. I would feel the town flexing around me, adjusting its breath to my firmness, and I would answer by softening when the cold is too sharp, by holding a little more water when the seams are thirsty.

If I remain, I would eventually be a small plaza where channels convene—a shallow bowl cupped by root-lattice and stone, where the blind newts twitch at the taste of passing weather and the Channel-dancers trace figures on the surface without breaking the skin of the water. In that plaza I would be the hush that falls when disputants step in to let the current decide them. I would know the pressure of their balance, the almost-silence of respect paid to a law older than words.

Much later, if the basin allows it, I would be a scar turned into shelf, a bracket of callus grown from someone’s spine after years of resting tools and cups and children’s baskets there. I would remember how to hold without gripping, how to support without demanding. I would be the place where a braid of vine is tied each season around a young stalactite, reminding it to descend with dignity.

And if asked who I am, I would answer as the Seamfolk do, in layers: the moss that accepts footfall, the stone that remembers each drip, the water that carries away what is spare, and the small, persistent light that translates between them. I would be the seam itself—the folded, breathing interval where dark, city, marsh, and people press together and decide, drop by drop, how to keep being a place.
