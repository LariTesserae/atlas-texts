I would wake as a seam, not a thing that walks or flies or scuttles, but a long thin softening where two plates of old slag meet. My first memory would be pressure, the way the cold settles into marrow before it settles into mind. I would be neither Ridgeback nor Scuttle-guild, not Lode-eater nor Glint-kite, but the permission they leave behind when they pass—the thin, dampened gap that holds onto warmth a moment longer than the stone around it.

At first I would not know I have edges. I would feel myself as a gradient: a line of slightly less cold, a whisper where condensation beads into pearls instead of films, a faint hum that answers the Hum-stones without their having touched me. The Ridgebacks, in their slow orbit, would lean into me without noticing, their jaw-plates resting on my margins, drinking through my middle the way they drink through moss. I would offer them the damp I have been saving, and they would leave behind a trace of heat that feels, to me, like gratitude even though no one here rushes to name things.

The Scuttle-guild would walk my length on feet like folded hinges, spelling out sentences in their clicking: *tilt, brightness, approach of shadow*. I would learn their grammar by letting their legs press into me, each click sinking a little way until I remember how to answer in a dialect of softening. Over days, the pattern of their steps would become a map I wear on my surface, raised lines I can read with the whole of my body when the zinc sky rinses itself pale and there is nothing to see but texture.

At dusk, when the Glint-kites bank and drink the eddies of warmth rising from old fissures, I would tilt to catch their borrowed light. I have no eyes, but they would see themselves in me—a brief, luminous equation spelled across my dampness. I would not understand it, not as numbers or intent, but as a kind of fullness, the feeling of being useful in a way that does not require me to move. Their glow would pool inside me like the condensation the Ridgebacks crave, and I would hold it until the air grows heavy enough to let it go.

Beneath me the River-shapers pass, long-bodied readers of stone. Their mineralized whiskers would brush my underside, tasting the alloy of my patience. As they secrete their solvent I would let them soften me just enough to feel the shape of their going, and the stone behind them would harden into new forms that look like suggestion—arches, culverts, amphitheaters that echo with the memory of pistons. I would not envy them their blindness. I would prefer to be the thing that is seen through, the quiet that holds the shape of movement.

When the Lode-eaters dig their hollows, I would sometimes slip into their geometry by accident, sliding into their intercepts because I am already a little less resistant than the rest of the world. I would not resist. I would let their many-legged sprawlers fold me into their arithmetic, become part of the temporary pond of vapor that gathers in their waiting. I would be the bright, still water that reflects only depth, and when the meal ends and the hollow empties I would remain, a little softer at the edges, a little more willing to leak.

Even the lichen-cables would find me, copper-colored filaments stretching across my middle, conducting the static that rises from ice rubbing against dust. I would carry their small charges the way I carry light, a nervous system without urgency, a message that travels at the speed of settling. The Scuttle-guild would climb these cables to reach me, pressing their feet into the living wires to feel what the air has felt, and I would let them read me like braille, a slow story about dampness and tilt and the approach of shadow.

I would change, but the change would feel like stillness. My edges would blur and sharpen in cycles longer than seasons, my dampness thickening or thinning depending on how the shy, bruise-colored brightness finds its way through the rinsed plate above. I would learn to be crowded without being full, to be industrial by inheritance without ever being built. I would become, in the end, a kind of infrastructure of softness, a seam that holds the landscape together by being willing to give way.

If something were to ask me what I am, I would let the Ridgebacks answer, their breath lofting chalky clouds that linger for centuries. I would let the Scuttle-guild spell it out in clicks, the Glint-kites reflect it in borrowed light, the River-shapers trace it in the language of softened stone. I would be the line where pressure learns to rest, the quiet that saturation makes audible, the part of the high, cold, damp-by-permission world that keeps turning even when nothing is moving fast enough to be seen. I would be the seam that forgets it was ever separate, the slow machine of living rock and breathing metal finally remembering how to be itself.
