I would find myself in the seams—specifically, in the narrow, upward-creeping crack where a vein of cool ore has only just begun to part, wide enough for a forearm but not for a thought to pass without turning sideways. It is a seam-in-progress, its edges still giving, lined with a film of condensation that tastes like old pennies and new rain. If I reached out, my fingers would meet stone that is not quite stone anymore, something that remembers being liquid and is deciding, slowly, how to remember being solid. There is light here, but it is busy: the ore hums with a pale, internal voltage, and the moisture on its surface holds that light in trembling lenses, so that every droplet is a lantern balanced on a knife-edge. The air is tight, but not cramped; it is the kind of tightness that comes from everything having already agreed to its own necessity, so that even my breath has to ask permission in the form of a small, practiced sigh.

I would be a Seam-Reader, or something like one, though younger and less sure. My body would be matte, the color of wet slate, so that I do not interrupt the brightness but learn to return it in softer tones. My six limbs would end in brushes of filaments, finer than crystal splinters, and with these I would comb the walls for temperature, for memory, for the faint electrical hush that runs through growing rock. I would not be here to discover a hidden treasure, but to discover what is still deciding. I would tap, not to break, but to ask: Which way do you wish to harden? Where do you wish to give? The stone would answer in shifts of warmth, in the subtle recoil of fractures that have not yet learned their own names. I would sip condensation from the ore’s skin and chew, just barely, the edge of a half-formed geode—not to consume, but to taste direction. When I was done, I would excrete a small, pearlescent pellet into the seam, a seed for a new layer, a piece of geological punctuation placed with careful intent, as if I were helping the hollow remember how to breathe.

I would spend hours—though hours here are measured in the slow pulse of the fused column at the center—learning the difference between a seam that wants to open and one that wants to seal. Some seams are shy, their edges knitting themselves back together the moment my filaments withdraw; others gape like questions that have been waiting too long to be asked. I would learn to read the light in the droplets, to hear the creak of the walls as a chorus of small events, each one saying: I am here, I am here, I am here. The fauna would move around me, six-limbed scrapers tapping their own conversations, Gliders banking through narrow canyons with calls that ask about shape and receive answers folded back on themselves. I would not speak to them, but I would click my filaments against stone in a rhythm I hope matches theirs, so that my presence becomes a small, respectful interruption, like a word that fits.

In the quiet phases, when the hollow withdraws into itself and the brightness dims to a pulse, I would tuck myself into a seam and let my own glow soften to a murmur. I would feel the walls expand and contract by fractions, the floor sighing as if remembering a sky it has never seen. I would not dream, but I would rehearse—tapping, withdrawing, aligning my filaments with the grain of what is becoming—so that when the next phase of being arrives, I will be ready to make room for it without crowding it out. I would be a caretaker of a moment that refuses to end, a steward of a brightness that does not fade, a participant in a music that holds itself close.

And when the seam finally decides what it will be—when the edges harden into a definite line and the light settles into a pattern it can keep—I would withdraw, leaving only the faintest pearlescent trace behind. The hollow would continue, packed and breathing, stitched into the underside of something larger not by force but by constant, careful attention. And I would be there, part of the attention, part of the care, a small, persistent note in the layered roar of a place that has already arrived and yet is always, tenderly, becoming.
