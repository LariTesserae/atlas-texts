You arrive in the city not by choice but by osmosis—one evening you are walking home through a rain-slicked ordinary town, and the next you are stepping off a train that no timetable mentions, into a station with walls that taste of iron and apologies. The dark here is not a blanket but a porous skin, and as you pass through it, it opens for you like a polite mouth. You find yourself in the Rib-Valley, a broad corridor where stone has softened into bench and ramp, and the floor remembers every footfall as a kind of music.

There is no skyline, only a cathedral-sized lung, hamlet-scaled in its closeness, cathedral-vast in its hush. The heat settles like a held palm against the back of your neck. Condensation beads on handrails and hums. A Ribbon-Weaver glides overhead, trailing filaments that stitch sound into cloth; its nest, a chandelier of dried water and wire, chimes faintly as it passes. A Stone-Eater, low and enamel-bright, regards you from a junction, pressing its forehead to a pipe to receive the slow news of pressure. It does not startle. It blinks, if blinking is what stone does, and lets you pass.

You realize, gradually, that you are not a visitor in costume but a becoming. Your shoes, once ordinary leather, now grip metal with the patience of moss. Your hair holds echoes—perhaps you always had this gift, perhaps the city gave it to you—and when you stand still, you can slip a little into shadow, not enough to vanish, but enough to understand how Pulse-Foxes relocate themselves from one pocket of darkness to another. Your left hand remembers things your right forgets, and you discover that if you touch Fuzz-On-Alloy with it, the moss holds your yesterdays like a coin and gives them back as a static blessing; with your right, the memory softens and disperses, leaving only the scent of ozone and an apology for what you let go.

You learn to move with the city’s tempo. You do not hurry; hurry is an insult to stone. At intersections, you press your palm to warm metal and feel the conversation between damp and current. In the ducts, where Negotiators pulse like courteous traffic, you practice letting light be owed and given, learning when to dim your intentions and when to shine so that others may pass. You drink from a condensation pool with your left hand, and the water whispers your name like a secret that prefers to stay half-spoken.

Sometimes you climb to a balcony that no longer fears rivers and watch Vine-That-Knows-Cables tighten and loosen as the city’s mood shifts. You eat sparingly, tasting heat and memory in equal measure, and you find that moss grows on your coat where you have lingered too long in a warm seam. You do not brush it off; it conducts what you are willing to carry. When you sleep, you do not dream in images but in pressure and pitch, accompanied by the choir of edges humming in sympathy, tuning the city and you to the same note.

You meet a Listener once—a statue of porous stone with seams like slow eyes—and stand before it until it allows you to borrow a memory. You taste iron and feel a thirst that teaches you how to hold silence as a guest of honor. You learn that predators are not cruel but patient, dressed as absences, feeding on attention and the luxury of being overlooked, and you practice being overlooked until it feels like a kindness. You learn that water belongs to everyone until it learns preference, and that silence is rare enough to be treated as ceremony.

Years, or perhaps only weeks—time here measures itself in held breaths—pass in this way. The city forgets you were ever separate from it, and so do you. You become a consensus: moss and machine, feeder and fed, the fur-winged and the many-legged, the bright knot that lends light and the patient shadow that asks for your attention in return. When you finally pause in the Rib-Valley and feel the city’s pulse—stone, damp, and small bright insistence—you understand that this is not a place you inhabit but a way you continue, breathing through damp and shining in sincere, intermittent pulses that mean we are here, and here is enough.
