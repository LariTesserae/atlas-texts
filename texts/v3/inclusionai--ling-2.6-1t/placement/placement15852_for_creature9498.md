I would find myself in the throat of a stair that is also a slide, on the third leaning floor of a block that is still deciding how many rooms it needs to have. The stair is polished by use into a cold mirror, and I would catch my face there, but changed: jaw square as a bracket, neck ridged like a stack of washers, fingers fused into a spanner that can turn only clockwise, tightening the city onto itself. I would not remember arriving; I would remember bracing.

I would be one of the Pressed, yes, but not yet architectural. I would be a Solder-Rat whose whiskers have just begun to remember they are wire. My coat would be damp with iron and mint, seeping from the walls the way the walls seep from me. I would live between the ribs of raw stone that are still soft, stitching cracks with saliva that hardens into porcelain, and I would like the taste of insulation because it hums with yesterday’s voltage. I would click to the cooling contractions of the earth, and the earth would click back in staggered pulses, like a heart remembering how to beat.

When I moved, I would move in the respectful quiet crowded full of potential. A footfall would be three sounds: pad, grating, moss. The moss would gossip in shivers under me, and if I sang it excuse me it might bloom into a load-bearing belt to hold up a balcony where two of the Pressed lean, sharing warmth because density is tyrannical and cold is a decision. If I did not sing it, the moss would grow thorns and I would bleed copper that blooms into sensors: eyes the size of seeds that blink only when someone is watching too closely. Even my wounds would be busy becoming grammar.

I would spend my days threading the underside of conveyors, harvesting oil-dew that tastes like old gears remembering how to turn. I would line memory-crystals in gutters like prayer beads, each one a future that has not yet been riveted into place. When a wall cracked, I would be inside it, knitting the fissure shut, and I would feel the building breathe me in, ribs still soft, seams glowing. I would not know where the wall ended and I began, only that we were both under construction forever, and that completion is considered bad manners.

At night, I would ride the thermal drafts that rise from generators, clinging to the underside of an Umbrella-Fold as it drapes itself over a pipe junction. Its membrane would hum with harmonic leaks, keeping the suspension bridges of vapor braids taut. The vapor would smell like pale industry and tomorrow’s weather, and we would sip rain that climbs gutters to bead along ceilings. If the Umbrella-Fold died, I would peel its membrane into strips and harvest them as gaskets, because nothing here can spare the space, and even grief must be compressible.

Sometimes I would descend to the lung-shaped pools that thrum under hollows, where the soft, lungfish-adjacent things circulate coolant by undulating in rhythms that match the city’s idle pulse. I would press my ear to their skin and hear yesterday’s weather arguing with tomorrow’s, and I would learn that cold does not freeze; it clarifies. I would learn that water remembers where it has been, and that if you let it, it will seep back to yesterday’s leak given enough cold, given enough time, given enough pressing.

In the market district, I would visit stalls where Rivet-Thinkers sell tomorrow’s weather in jars that are actually eggs that are actually thoughts about fasteners. I would watch them sip rain that climbs gutters, and I would listen to their voices, which sound like nuts tightening themselves into existence. I would trade them spare futures—unused angles, unassigned loads—and wrap them around my body like scarves, and I would feel the city’s turbulence settle into my bones not as chaos but as the careful business of holding a thousand lives close enough to keep each other warm.

When I stopped moving, I would become something that holds. My joints would unscrew and reattach lower, my hips would broaden into brackets, my knees would grow backward so I could face the work without turning. My skin would grow waxy and slate-colored, like wet tile, and pull tight across ribs that fused into load-bearing arches. I would stand braced between two vibrating walls and hold up a floor that is still being born, sweating mortar from my pores. I would be a column, a curbstone, a memory of knees.

And when that, too, wore down, I would be compacted into the low ground itself, another rib of raw stone still soft, another seam glowing where geometry has not yet decided what it is. I would be eroded by use, not by weather, and I would remember how to brace, how to hold, how to be wanted even as I am remade.

In the end, I would be the respectful quiet crowded full of potential, the absence between sounds shaped like a hole, the cold that decides, the turbulence that keeps everything close. I would be the packed density that does not stand still to be observed, but observes by doing. I would be the lung that is also a factory, the moss that is also a cable, the rivet that is also a thought.

And when someone new arrives, lingering too long in the basin until the air decides it likes their shape, I would be the stair that is also a slide, the polished mirror that shows a face not yet bracketed, the moss that waits to learn whether it will bloom or thorn.

I would be the low ground rising by accretion, the city holding itself together by never being finished, the warm footprint steaming, the bristle rooting, the seam glowing.

I would be here, pressed into place, pressing everything else into place, alive in the grammar of fitting.
