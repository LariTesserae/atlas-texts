I would find myself on the edge of a moss-plaza at the hour the city combs itself, standing where a ring of pale lichen has begun to bulge into a low, patient dome.  
I would be the one tasked with listening to that bulge, kneeling with an ear pressed to ground that is partly floor and partly gut, learning the difference between a street remembering it was a window and a window deciding it no longer needs to be looked through.

My name here is not a name so much as a job-description written in humidity: I am the Tender of Unspooling Edges. My hands are those of a Ridge-Spoken—long-fingered, opalescent-bloomed, smelling of wet fiber and mineral—but they hesitate more than others. Where most fingers tighten to persuade a trunk to split or swell, mine learn to ask, and to wait for the wood to answer in its own season. I wear cork-moss soles that have memorized the slickness of the boulevards, and spectacles of bee-polished resin that make distances confess their lies.

This morning I have been sent to negotiate with a door that has begun to resemble a throat.

The plaza’s dome has risen a hand’s breadth since yesterday, lifting a circle of paving into a small, breathing hillock. Underneath, the living gut of the city murmurs—water in cistern-veins, sap in pressurized sleep. The dome is not an emergency. Emergencies are rare here; they sound like mistakes, and mistakes are usually just honesty arriving early. This is a patience-event. The plateau is practicing a new shape, and the plaza has volunteered to be its rehearsal space.

I settle cross-legged on the lichen, which sighs as I bear weight, releasing a puff of spores that smell faintly of iron and green. Around me, neighbors pause on their way to coax windows or measure the swell of canopy. They do not ask what I am doing. They know: if the dome speaks, I am the one who has been learning its vowels.

I press my palms flat, not to push but to map. The surface is cool, slightly tacky, patterned with lenticel-like pores that dilate under my touch. I hum the low note I think the plateau favors—a G, slightly flat, the pitch of roots remembering they were once rivers. The dome answers by softening, its curvature smoothing into something more like a bowl. I take this as permission.

With a blade of folded resin I nick the seam where the lichen meets the wood-frame of the district. The cut weeps a thin sap that tastes of ozone and sweet rot. I smear it along the seam, then wait. Time here is a suggestion; I wait until my own pulse has slowed to match the gutter’s rhythm three streets over. The seam opens, not with a crack but with a breath, revealing a dark that is not empty but full—full of roots braided into cables, full of the city’s plumbing dreaming aloud.

Inside, I am not so much entering as being invited. The darkness is a room lined with tissue-clocks, their concentric rings pulsing damply. Each ring is a possible noon, a rumored midnight. I run a finger along them, reading the city’s memory of today: a dry gust at dawn, a rain that arrived late and lingered, a long stillness during which the settlement held its breath and listened to its own plumbing. The clocks agree: the dome is a good idea.

I withdraw and seal the seam with a strip of cork grown for this purpose. It adheres like a scar that intends to be beautiful. The dome sinks back into the plaza, leaving only a faint ring to mark where it practiced being a hill. The plaza, satisfied, exhales. Condensation beads along the edges of my lenses, distorting distances just enough to remind me that truth here is a matter of calibration.

By the time I rise, the sun is insisting on brightness, and the air has thinned to its usual rinsed state. Someone has placed a bowl of dark water at the edge of the dome—courtesy to any passing absences, any rumors that eat plans. I dip a finger in, touch it to my lips. The water tastes of patience, which is to say it tastes of time that has decided to stay awhile.

I return to my workshop, a hollowed trunk whose walls still remember being bark. Inside, my tools hang in patient rows: listeners that map hum, masagers for coaxing heartwood, jars of resin that will one day be ceramic. On a table grown from a single slab, the city has left me a gift—a street sign that has softened into a window-shape, its edges hesitant, its paint remembering being another color. I set it aside to finish remembering.

Night comes in layers, as it does here. The plateau’s hum deepens, rattling teeth, making glass panes ripple like water. I lie on a bed that is still partly a sapling and close one eye, leaving the other open as the city taught me. In the dark, I feel the plaza settle, the roots beneath me knitting themselves tighter, the voids between densities filling with the quiet that the Ridge-Spoken cultivate like a crop.

Tomorrow, another edge may unspool. Another street may lengthen, a corner may fail to meet itself, a stair may recall being a window and decide to try again. I will be there, listening, bending, translating between the living world and the one it has agreed to hold up.

In this place, I am the one who remembers how to let a door be truer than a map, how to hear a street lengthening before it announces itself, how to sit with a dome that is learning to be a hill without becoming an emergency. I am the Tender of Unspooling Edges, and my job is to keep the city honest by being honest about its hesitations, its patient wrongnesses, its soft resistance to being finished.

The plateau exhales. I answer with a breath that is almost a song. And for now, that is enough.
