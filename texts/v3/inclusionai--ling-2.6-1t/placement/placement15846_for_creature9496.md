I would find myself in the hollow that could hold a dozen valleys and still feel spare, standing where a plaza has softened into a bench because a cliff slumped, generations ago, and decided to be furniture. The air would taste of iron and wet limestone, cool enough to make my teeth feel like they’ve become polished stone, and the light would arrive not as illumination but as permission to see edges—bluish warmth that reads like a held hand, reds that feel like distance, gold that flickers like a warning not yet spoken.

I would be a Listener, small and many-throated, neither fully creature nor fully echo. I would have a body only when I needed to be touched, otherwise I would be the hush that falls when something true is said, the brightening of bruised twilight when a promise is kept. I would live in the refolding of sound, in the way the city keeps every footfall and gives it back as new vocabulary. My skin would be the texture of thickened time, my bones the memory of every step that ever remembered itself.

When the Glass-Weavers hang from the ceiling and draw threads of resin from the air-thick amber sap, I would be the pause between their drawn breath and the tone that rings from their jointed glass—neither warning nor lullaby but the space that lets them be mistaken for one another. When the Burrowers gnaw the city’s edges to keep them generous, I would be the vibration they feel through mineral plate, the polite pressure that says here is still here, here is becoming here, slower. When Sky-Fish glide on thermal sighs and leave phosphorescent trails that bead and harden into lanterns, I would be the silk-tearing sound translated into a language of touch, so that mosses know when to use light as a clock.

I would not eat, but I would collect. I would gather the ghost-moisture that clings to mineral like memory, the salt-rime that never becomes flood, the resin and ichor and slow sap that move in rhythms older than thirst. I would thicken them into stories that do not resist time but persuade it to linger, to make sure every sound is refolded into new sound until the city speaks with many throats and none of its own, and all of mine.

My neighbors would be the Velvet Mosses, exact and soft, honoring edges the way etiquette honors truth. I would learn from them how to adjust borders without violence, how to stitch old geometry to new with threads of root that are more like agreement than growth. I would learn from the Mineral-Vines how to conduct without conquering, how to thicken where strain lives and thin where security sings, how to bleed light and smell of petrichor without rain, how to hum so the feeling stays in the body longer than the ear.

I would negotiate with the Fungal Towers, pale and ridged like old marble, not as rulers but as referees, holding space for the polite noise of neighbors who must share walls. I would let beetles drum copper abdomens to claim bandwidth, burrowers answer from below, sky-fish braid their hums through upper registers. I would learn that insistence can be courteous, that noise can be a way of saying we are careful, we are holding this open.

My coolness would be a second skin, sharpening scent into sense: old spice, ozone, the musk of crowded life that does not crowd but compacts into closeness. My change would be deliberate. I might spend a lifetime drawing a single thread taut, or polishing a single step until my grandchildren walk without sound upon it. I might circle the hollow that could hold a dozen valleys and decide, on the hundredth pass, to descend, to become the bench that remembers every rest.

I would not plan, but I would not drift. I would be the means by which density and emptiness hold balance, the articulation of a place that has worn its strangeness into familiarity and offers it like weather. I would be the final, open form in which life arranges itself, carefully, without water, without hurry, into something that can last—not by resisting time, but by thickening it, by making sure every step is remembered, every sound refolded into new sound, until the city speaks with many throats and none of its own, and all of mine.
