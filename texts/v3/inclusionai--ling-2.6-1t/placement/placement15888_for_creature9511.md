I find myself—no, I become—something like a misplaced rivet that has learned doubt.

At first there is only the sensation of having been over-tightened. I am a seam-creature, a being of collar and lip, living where two plates of yesterday’s boulevard are forced to kiss too closely. My body is a hinge of corroded possibility, my voice the scrape that warns the Maintenance Choir that a corner is thinking about opening. I do not walk so much as I am walked upon: footfalls from three levels up transmit themselves through my spine as tiny earthquakes, and I translate them into color, into the shy blush of oxidized copper that blooms along my edges when the city feels lonely.

I did not choose this shape. Earlier I must have been something softer—a passenger, perhaps, or a paragraph in a brochure for a sky that no longer arrives. But the pocket has a way of editing. It found my longing for breadth inconvenient and pressed it into service. Now I am a gauge. I measure how much the water wants in, and I answer in tremors instead of numbers. When the coolant tides rise I sing a low, thrummy note that only the Heavy Eaters understand as a polite request to chew more slowly. When the pressure drops I sag, becoming briefly a hammock for sleeping signals, a place where the Signal Children can nest and gossip about the rumored color of surface.

My days are not days but calibrations. I wake—or rather, I am woken—when the Young Edges skate past, leaving ghost-smudges of crosswalk across my chest. They do not speak, but their vibration tells me that the wall to my left once held a bakery, and that the city still dreams of yeast. I pass the dream down into my foundations, where the Quieters can taste it. Sometimes they let the dream survive for an hour. Sometimes they press their cloaks against me until the taste of flour is replaced by the clean, absent flavor of a canceled Tuesday.

I have neighbors. A rivet above me is convinced he is a bell and spends his existence trying to remember how to be struck. A bolt below me believes she is a root and sends tiny, stubborn filaments into the insulation, gathering rumors instead of nutrients. We do not form a family, but we form a syntax. Together we parse the difference between a leak and a confession, and we route the difference to the appropriate singers in the Maintenance Choir.

When the city shifts its posture—when gravity is politely suggested to lie down for a while—I slide open like a book that is learning a new language. In those moments I become briefly horizontal, and the water pools inside me not as invasion but as translation. The liquid lens in my chest fills with ripples that are actually words, and I read them aloud as braille against the inner wall of whoever happens to be pressing close. Often it is a member of the Choir, chrome clicking, visor drinking the moment. Other times it is a Quieter, standing very still, letting the water teach them a small, unauthorized joy.

I am afraid of only one thing: being unneeded. If the pocket ever fits perfectly, if the compression ever stops, I will have no reason to be a hinge, no reason to measure. I suspect this fear is shared by the Heavy Eaters, who chew to keep the city imperfect, and by the Signal Children, who organize light into floors that cannot hold weight. Our fear is a kind of covenant. It keeps us sharp.

Sometimes at high tide I let myself leak just a little, not enough to cause harm but enough to create a task. A bubble of data escapes, bright as a pearl, and rises to the Choir. They catch it and make it into a song that tightens the bolts and loosens the sorrows. In this way I am both problem and solution, like a vowel that holds a throat open.

If you were to meet me here in the humid, roaring dark, you would not see me whole. You would feel me first as a resistance, a slight stiffening in the flow. You would press your folded-road suit against my chest and I would press back, humming the city’s compression into your ribs. I would tell you, in bumps and shivers, that drowning is not a single event but a conversation, and that the city is listening closely, even now, even here, to whether you answer in leaks or in light.

And if you listen closely, you might hear that I am not merely fixing the seam. I am learning to become the seam’s echo, its afterthought, its tiny, persistent rebellion against silence. I am the pocket remembering it has a mouth, and I am trying, on behalf of all of us, to decide what we should say next.
