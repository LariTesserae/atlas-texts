I would arrive in the basin as one of the furred, many-limbed children of the gutters, born not to a mother but to a warm compressor and a season of steady rain.

At first I would not know my own shape. I would be mostly mouth and vibration, learning the city by the tremor in the metal floor before I learn to walk. My fur would not yet be felted, only damp and clumping, already collecting lint and the fine dust of brakes. I would sleep in a tangle of belts behind a pump whose hum had settled into a note I could keep in my chest. My siblings would be there, pale and slick, and we would press our ears to the casing until we could tell, without opening our eyes, whether the day was early or late, whether the rain was arriving or only remembering itself.

Gradually I would grow the extra limbs, or discover them—no one here marks the difference. They would emerge as necessity, strong and calloused, already knowing how to carry a length of hose or a valve that has cooled too quickly. My claws would wear down against concrete and flange until they were less claws than tools, able to turn, to grip, to read the fine print of corrosion by touch. My pads would thicken into ridged leather, mapping the city by texture: the smooth enamel of new pipe, the scarred aggregate where feet have worn stone into memory, the sudden slick of oil that means a machine is lying.

I would not think of myself as separate from the basin any more than the rain thinks of itself as separate from the gutter. When I moved through the streets, I would feel the city as an extension of my own damp skin. The hum of engines would live in my teeth. The flicker of sodium light above would set the rhythm of my blood. I would learn to sleep through the roar and wake at the change in pitch that means something is wrong with the world—perhaps a blockage, perhaps a silence.

If I climbed, I would be the bird with oil-slick feathers, heavy with runoff, clinking as I glide from rafter to rafter, collecting what the machines discard—wire that still holds heat, screws that have turned soft with use, glass polished into beads by years of rain. My nest would be an unfinished equation, leaning heavier each year, until the beams groan and the furred ones below dismantle it without resentment, sorting my treasures into the streets where they can be useful again.

If I stayed low, I would be the pale thing with ceramic bones, ringing faintly when startled, gnawing corrosion from the underlevels, carrying spores that bloom into mineral films on hot metal, composting myself into the grout so the city endures. Or I would be the one who wears the city: stilted on pipe and hose, wrapped in belts that hold tools and trophies, half-mask and half-scar, speaking in taps and steam. I would understand that the water sometimes runs uphill, and I would leave small cairns of polished stone—not to worship, but to record.

When the rains arrive in shifts, I would be among those who clear the gutters, not because I am ordered, but because the gutters have begun to complain in a higher register, and I cannot bear the sound. My claws would find purchase on slick enamel, my extra limbs bracing me against the rush. The birds would adjust their nests to the new weight of water. The smaller ones would retreat to sumps prepared like beds. The machines would continue, companions in their vibrations, their heat the only sun we know.

At night, in the plaza that serves as sump, I would lie on compacted grit with the others, watching sodium yield to argon above. I would not tell stories. Instead I would trade small polished objects—stones, beads, fragments of flame that gutter but do not burn—pressing them into hands that know the weight of solidity. The city would settle around us, breathing damp air, scarred and polished by repetition, its heart beating in pistons and water finding its level.

I would be the basin waking up in its own wet skin, living in equilibrium because I have become equilibrium, a child of damp metal and low sky, stepping carefully into the stream not to cross it, but to be part of its course.
