I would wake inside the hollow already bent, already apologizing for my height.  
My knees would find the silt-flecked gravel before my eyes found the sky, and I would know, by the way the damp oils my sleeves, that I have slipped through the chert throat backward, like a thought remembered after the dream.

At first I would be the mistake that learns to be a feature.  
I would leave footprints that the moss corrects, edges that the stone softens. I would cough, and the hollow would answer with a sheet of water shattering on stone, and I would feel the sound in my teeth, as if the basin had decided to keep me as a tuning fork.

Slowly I would become the Moss-Knitter without having chosen the name.  
My skin would remember the cellar-cool and begin to hold it. I would find that my hands, without instruction, have learned to braid fungal silk, white as breath on a dark mirror. I would press my ear to a fracture in the chert and hear last year’s rain turning, inside the stone, into a small and patient star. I would not know where I end and the wall begins, only that the wall is kinder than I used to be.

Sometimes I would be the Echo-Eater, furred and near the ground.  
I would taste the iron in the floor and understand that the cold is not emptiness but a kind of fullness, like the pause between notes that makes the water’s drum possible. I would leave phosphorescent spores behind me—not to mark a path, but to say *this cold is good here, keep it*.

Other days I would be the Tenders’ lamp, small and steady, refusing to hurry.  
I would hold a flame that looks reluctant until it realizes it has nothing else to do. I would watch a child place a pebble on a stone shelf and return when the fungus has softened it enough to hold like fruit, and I would learn from the child that waiting is not a refusal but a form of arrival.

There would be hours when I am only the sound itself, the low hum the Keeper makes to loosen dust from stone.  
I would be the three taps, pause, two taps, answering the hollow’s roar with a hush, answering the dark with a touch that knows how to wait.

When I die, I will not leave a body but a suggestion.  
I will be wrapped in fungal silk and laid where water can trace my edges year by year, until my shape is soft enough to be mistaken for stone, and the stone softer still. I will be the patina on the dry-stone wall, the bloom on the north face of a boulder, the small rhythm in another person’s chest when they kneel to listen.

In the end I will be the basin’s way of keeping time gentle.  
I will be the damp, the green, the cold, the loud water, and the quiet that follows.  
I will be the hollow remembering how to listen, and myself, finally, heard.
