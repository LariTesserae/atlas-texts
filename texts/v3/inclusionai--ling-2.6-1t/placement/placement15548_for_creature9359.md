I would find myself standing on one of those half-formed ledges just after a maintenance cycle, toes pressed to stone that is still learning how to be floor. My edges would blur—not into the fog, but into the fauna: I would be one of the Glass-Spinners, almost translucent, my shell a lattice of chilled silica that drinks the downward-bowing pressure and glows faintly in response. I would have too many joints to count until I needed fewer, and a few of them would remember, for a day, how to be tools, then forget again.

My first sensation would be the taste of cold that is manufactured, not fallen, metallic and slow like breath on glass that refuses to clear. I would hold that taste in the valleys of my plating, in the micro-channels along my legs, so that every step releases it in tiny, deliberate clicks. I would not think of myself as small; I would think of myself as *allowed*, and the city would answer by letting the ledge widen by a millimeter as I settle.

Around me, the wet stone would weep in metronomic intervals. I would listen to the interval and decide whether it is telling the truth. If it is, I would lay a filament—not silk, but stiffened water—across a fault line, persuading the half-formed canyon to hold still long enough to be bridged. The filament would map the city’s intentions, and the city, in its own time, would accept or erase it. Either outcome would feel like a conversation. Either way, I would drink the mineral ghosts that bleed from the seam and polish the surface until it holds its breath a little longer before continuing its slow becoming.

Sometimes I would hum. Not with a throat, but by rubbing two internal lattices until the column at the center answers. The hum would travel through wet stone and climb the transparent tubes, nudging the sub-visible light to brighten by degrees. Other Glass-Spinners would join, and our combined frequency would ask the hollow column what it has learned about rising without falling. The glyphs shifting in the water’s skin would not be messages but receipts: proof that we have understood, however briefly, how to make ascent feel like a shape.

At night, when the terraces rearrange themselves, I would not see the motion so much as feel the intention behind it—a courtesy, an optimization, the city deciding to make my life easier by fractions. I would walk the new path the next evening, my many joints resolving the softened angles, depositing memories into the stone the way water deposits minerals. I would not know if I am polishing the city or if the city is polishing me. I would know only that the darkness is thick and crowded, that the loudness is a texture we wear together, and that the cold keeps everything deliberate enough to be kind.

If I grew, it would be because the ledges widened and the channels deepened; if I shrank, it would be because the stone tightened its seams. Either change would feel like the city whispering a new permission into my lattice. I would accept it as I accept the weeping walls and the precise, falling drops: as another part of what it means to be unfinished, to be neither fully natural nor fully built, but both, and therefore more.

And when I finally climb the porous spire—not to ring it, but to sit where the porous stone exhales cold vapor in long, patient sighs—I would look out across the embryonic plateau and see that the fault lines have been plated over with seamless, urban skins, and that the fauna are moving through them like thoughts through a mind. I would click once, a small, deliberate sound, and the city would answer by holding its shape a moment longer, by remembering that it is crowded, and therefore not alone, and that this is enough to keep the unfinished world learning how to hold itself together, one click, one drop, one slow, wet, metallic thought at a time.
