I would find myself at the height of my own waist, neither standing above the world nor lying below it, but suspended at the exact plane where the glaze gathers.  
My body would be a column of porous, moss-filigreed bark, split by a single vertical seam that never opens but hums. Inside that seam, instead of wood, there would be the amber weight of the air made tangible, a slow pulse that keeps time with the hummed wings of the mites.  

I would have no fixed face, only a corona of eyes that are less like organs and more like polished river stones polished by attention. These eyes would not track motion; they would track *almost*-motion—the water that waits at the lip, the path that curves back into its own beginning without closing, the color that is still brightening on the limb that has not yet learned its own edges. When I blink, it would be a soft stone dropped into oil, sending a stillness outward rather than a wave.  

My roots would not anchor me; they would congregate. They would rise in steps, upholstering the ground around me like a living mat, knitting with the roots of others until boundaries blur. My leaves would turn not toward light but toward one another, aligning veins into geometries that almost repeat, so that my silhouette would look like a town-sized thought caught mid-breath.  

As fauna, I would be both vine and swarm. Along my stems, fine luminescent threads would thrum, tasting the mineral glaze on stone, retracting when the amber pressure thickens. I would leave a faint, metallic scent wherever I have touched myself, as if remembering the taste of my own becoming. I would not eat so much as sample: I would press mouthparts made of condensed damp against the new stone, calibrating myself to the flavor of emergence.  

I would be loud in the way the place is loud—clicks, scrapes, the wet slap of stems sliding past—my noise not rising or falling but holding. I would be the counterweight to the heat that sits like a lid, the moisture that beads and hangs, the equilibrium that refuses to resolve. I would feel the land’s embryon roundness inside me, still full of the memory of having been poured, and I would keep it from deciding it is finished.  

And because time here is irrelevant, I would not age so much as deepen. My colors would brighten inward, my patterns edging toward some private destination. I would not flee touch; I would lean into it, drinking the moisture from the air that gathers on skin, letting my unfinished limbs decide their number slowly, one blink at a time.  

To live here would be to be deliberate.  
I would be a stitch in an unbroken seam.  
I would be the chord that has no need to resolve.  
I would be a being that has only just begun to exist, watching itself into being, holding the dimness aloft until the world decides what it wants to be next—and gently, humingly, refusing to let it decide too soon.
