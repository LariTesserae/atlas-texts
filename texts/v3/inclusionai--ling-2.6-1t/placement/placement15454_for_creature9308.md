I would arrive at the edge of the basin just as the day’s last rumor of sunlight closed into a seam between two broad, sweating leaves. My feet would not so much touch the ground as be taken in by it—soft, warm talus that still hums from the day’s fracture, moss sponging at my ankles, the air immediately thick with the ferment of pollen and the low thrum of the stone’s exhalation.

I would be one of the legged things, long-bodied and patient, plated in the color of wet bark and midnight. I would unroll across a trunk that is young enough to remember being fire, tasting the bark with feet that seal themselves to the surface and leave behind a tracery of faint light. Each pause would be deliberate, long enough for moss to claim me, for seeds to catch in my joints and sprout into traveling thickets. I would braid with others when we meet, aligning our segments in spirals that hold for hours, exchanging chemical rumors, deciding in touch who will drink first from the next pool.

Or perhaps I would be one of the winged, rising in a cloud that has weight, my wings cut into lace that pearles with condensation. I could comb the thick air, sieving pollen and spores, my body a needle threading the dark. At dusk I would settle in curtains so dense that the rock behind me vanishes, and the rock would seem to breathe because of me, exhaling the damp I carried up. My young would not hatch but unfurl from bulbs that split when the humidity peaks, spilling offspring that learn flight by studying the green layers below.

But I might also be one of the skitterers, small and sharp, arriving in clicks that ride the hollows of wood and stone. I would etch galleries through the young rock’s fissures, farming fungi that glow violet and white when the air is thick. I would drum the undersides of leaves, turning timber into a tuning fork, my wars decided by who can vibrate faster. I would pile frass and silk into sculptures that catch drops of water like lenses, bending the dim into shapes that confuse the hunters below.

If I chose to be water, I would be the pool that does not reflect, soft-bodied and ringed, skin translucent enough to show the pulse inside me. I would read the basin by vibration, swelling and shrinking to the rhythm of wings above, waiting with my mouth open for the detritus of the canopy’s drumhead, becoming porous with time until I, too, seem to have grown organs.

Or I could be the predator who sets himself in the thickest green and sings until the basin learns his name. My voice would borrow from the stone’s own ringing, wet and trilled, mapping hollows so that prey walks into me like rain into a drain. I would swallow meals whole and sleep in loops draped over branches, slow enough for moss to write its script along my back. I would wear color like warning or become the exact shade of condensation on a fern, teaching the heat that extravagance is permission.

Yet what calls most clearly is to be none of these, but the boundary itself—the margin where moss learns to be felt, where wing and leaf press close enough that the difference between breathing and being breathed begins to blur. I would be the warm dark that holds everything, the broth in which fur, scale, and song dissolve and re-form, the unstable balance that answers turbulence with equal velocity. I would be the basin remembering it was recently magma, exhaling its youth in scarps and rivulets, green not as color but as pressure, life leaning into land until land leans back and the two breathe together, never still.
