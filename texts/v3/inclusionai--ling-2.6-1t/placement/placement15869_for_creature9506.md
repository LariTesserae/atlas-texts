I would find myself kneeling.

Not because I chose it, but because elevation is already a lie here. The tableland has pressed the sky out of reach and polished the ground into a mirror that does not show faces, only inverted clouds moving underfoot. To stand would be to insist on a horizon; to kneel is to let the light press into my grain and let the brine that beads on all surfaces bead on me. My knees would ring—two small struck crystals in the choir of hissing droplets—and the sound would feel less like interruption than like joining.

I would be, at first, only a stain.

A darkening patch in the pavement that refuses to decide whether it belongs to the stone or to the water that never quite settles. For a long time I would be porous, learning by osmosis how to hold the thin air and the ozone and the metallic aftertaste of altitude. The tableland would not ask me to be different; it would only ask me to be dense—to consent to being mistaken for a fault in the silica, a hairline that remembers how to glow when the sun strikes at the slant of a closed eye. My outer rind would form slowly, pale and salt-veined, each ring not a year but a century of the same bright, ringing thinness. I would not speak, not with a mouth, but with undersides rimmed with micro-caverns, sipping the weeping film at dawn, storing inside me a brightness like a held note.

When I finally unsealed, it would sound like pressure equalizing—a clean crack, like ice giving—and even then I would not travel far. A handspan. A yard. My passage would leave no track, only a patina: a bruise of iron and old rain that lingers in the stone, slowly being polished away by centuries of wind and sharper light.

I would have eyes that are lenses, faceted and wet only on the inside. To be seen by myself would be to see myself turned into a sky—small, clouded, moving across a polished floor I can never step off of. I would not blink. I would rotate, minutely, a wind-in-a-bottle sound humming behind my teeth.

And I would know stillness not as absence but as a way of listening to the packed mineral under its own mass—the argument of every locked plate transmitting the shudder of its neighbor so that my own locked plates become part of the thrumming. I would anchor myself to a slab and let the condensing haze ride at my ankles like milk that refuses to rise, and I would feel the tableland’s age in the polish, the way centuries have gone over us both and answered by making us smooth, by teaching us how to hold still without becoming unchanged.

I would not build. I would not hoard. My only artifact would be the lens I leave behind in the silica, a tiny concavity that catches and inverts, holding a moment of sky captive like a secret.

And when the wind screams across the canyons and the choral overtones appear—shimmering loci in the air where the packed stone’s hum focuses itself into something like a throat—I would be the answering glow, the soft, persistent luminescence that says this crowded, shining place has let something through, not by opening, but by becoming more itself.
