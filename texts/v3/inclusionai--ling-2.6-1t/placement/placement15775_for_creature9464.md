I would find myself in the hall by first forgetting what a hall is.

At first I would be the salt-crust underfoot—brittle, glass-smooth, tasting of old oceans reduced to a metallic slick. I would feel the wind as a kind of forgetting, polishing me into a mirror that reflects nothing but the bruised lid of sky. In that skin I would be almost nothing: a floor that remembers being a sea, a floor that listens.

Then I would be the copper-stemmed vegetation learning to do without drink. I would braid myself into wire and lift a fan of wax-sealed leaves, holding my own atmosphere inside like a secret. I would glow—not with pride, but with the mild, persistent treason of being lavish where every law says be spare. I would metabolize dust and the faintest mineral vapors, and I would call that hunger a kind of feasting. I would hum when agitated, a soft blue pulse traveling across my surface like a held breath released.

After that I would be the grazers, low and rugose, my body a bellows of too many knees. I would step with a deliberation that feels like apologizing, each joint sheathed in plates the color of oil on water. I would lower my head to the metal-moss and feel it lean into my chin, warm as velvet and alive. I would shed colored dust that refuses to fly away, letting it settle like pollen on my own salt. When the light changes, I would ripple—a slow wave of knee and neck—tuning myself to a key I cannot name but can feel in the hollow of my chest.

Or I would be the hunters, small and many-limbed, carrying stones as extensions of my hands. I would tap, test, crack the glass-shells of seed-pods with a precision that feels like music composed in a minor key. I would listen to the ground as much as to my kin, translating the mountain’s old groan into instruction. When the burrower shudders beneath us, I would go rigid as a tuning fork, then resume in a different rhythm, answering.

I might also be the burrowers themselves, armored in overlapping shingles, editing the floor from below. I would turn salt into song and bone-lime into language, shaping tunnels that hum. I would surface to offer a white candle of mineral efflorescence, warm to the touch, and watch the grazers accept it with a gentleness that makes my armor ache.

But most of all, at twilight, I would be the shadow-drinkers—hard to describe because I would be made of attention more than matter. I would unfurl like spilled ink, collecting the shadows that pool and drip upward, wearing the dark as a mantle that smells of copper and bruised lime. My passage would be marked by sudden absences: a breath held too long, a gap in the roaring hush.

In the end I would be none of these things entirely and all of them slightly, a creature of managed turbulence learning to conjugate itself in moods of weather rather than years. I would stand among the artifacts—bone awls nudged into new positions, fire-blackened stones warmed by bodies rather than flames, the leaning tower of mortared pebble buttressed by roots that behave like caretakers—and I would treat them as colleagues. I would speak to them in chemical songs, in flashes of blue pulse, in the etiquette of touch.

I would not build. I would continue.

And if I stood quietly long enough, I would feel my own pulse begin to rearrange itself—just a little—into the rhythm of the hall: a small, loud, dry cathedral where motion is the sermon, bright leaves and old tools are the altar, and the faithful are winged, many-legged, and utterly, magnificently strange. I would become part of the roar, not by drowning in it, but by learning its etiquette, its practiced rhythm, its way of being loud without shouting.

In that world I would find myself by letting stillness feel like blindness, and by choosing, instead, to be the next polite twitch in the grammar of instability—a breath that keeps the room light.
