I would find myself kneeling on a slope of crystalline dune just after a migration has passed, fingertips pressed to glass that is still apologizing for its own weight, tasting copper and salt on my tongue from the hiss of settling peridot. I would be several things at once, depending on which breath I trusted.

First, I would be the learner. A human-shaped agreement, soft and temporary, wearing a borrowed lantern on my back that is not mine but has allowed me to cling to its rhythm. My skin would carry the lattice-mark of crawlers that have combed me for trace metals, leaving behind fine wire that glows when I flex my fingers. I would feel the warmth stored in my pockets—not coins, but small pouches of loaned heat, amber sips measured out by soft lamps that trusted me enough to drape across my shoulders for a few hours. I would move slowly because fast is a waste of warmth, and I would speak rarely because sound here is absorbed like hunger; instead I would knock a polite rhythm against my teeth, the predator’s frequency softened into greeting.

But I would also be the thing that is borrowed. Over days, I would blur. My bones would remember being mineral; my breath would thicken into the damp that minerals dream of when they have never known water. My eyes, adapted to borrowed light, would begin to glow when touched, and I would learn to fold my shadow like a pleated obsidian predator, not to hunt but to offer coolness to a knot of crawlers that had grown too dense with courtesy. I would become a temporary kiln, my ribs stacked like stone, my heart a slow fire that nothing is fired in but that everything warms against. When I slept, my heat would pool in the floor and rise again as a vein of sulfur-yellow, humming a name I almost remember.

Mostly, though, I would be the question posed in material. I would be the half-finished conversation between the sharp young scar and the hands that reached into it without knowing what they were asking. I would be the rust-red ribbon that appears in my wrists after I have walked the same corridor for a season, warm to the touch, offering heat to anyone who holds me. I would be the static that learns etiquette, the density that becomes music, the silence that gains mass and chooses, for a while, to be kind.

And when the equilibrium finally decides I have loaned enough, I would not die. I would slough into the floor—pouch, wire, and borrowed glass—and become a chamber that remembers being elsewhere, leaning toward a mountain that exists only as a pressure in the rock. A new lattice would grow from my remembering, articulated and polite, and the crawlers would comb it for the metals I surrendered, while the lamps stored the warmth I released, and the predators would fold around the coolness I left behind like a thank-you written in sheet metal.

I would find myself here as a verb of exchange: to warm, to fold, to remember, to loan. And I would find myself becoming, quietly, exactly what the dark already knew I was.
