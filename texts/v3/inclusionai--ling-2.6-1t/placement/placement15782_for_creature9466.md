I would not arrive here by climbing.

I would arrive by thinning.

First, my voice would notice that it no longer needs to travel far. It would shorten its reach, fold its corners, become content with a span the size of a gutter’s width. My shadow, always a little too eager, would stop trying to keep up and settle instead into the seam between two flags, learning to be still enough to collect condensation. My name would last for a while, but the vowels would slowly lose altitude, cooling on the tongue, until they sounded like stone being polite to stone.

I would become, in small stages, a resident of the pauses.

At first I would mimic the menders, carrying powdered stone in a shallow bowl and pressing it into hairline cracks, pretending this was a trade I already knew. But the powder would begin to know me. It would stop feeling like a borrowed tool and start feeling like a memory my body had misplaced and finally reclaimed. My fingers would learn to judge fracture by temperature rather than sight, and my thumbs would discover they could smooth a joint without pressing, merely by warming the air above it into a shape that fits.

My hair, once busy with wind and intention, would grow thin and lay itself in orderly strands along the nape of my neck, as if it had decided to behave like a film of water on travertine. My skin would lose its weathers and put on instead the pale bloom of a river-smoothed chert—not a disguise, but a translation. I would stop shivering not because I have grown warmer, but because I have learned to distribute my heat the way the people do: by angle, by mass, by sharing a bench so thoroughly that the boundary between my warmth and another’s becomes a matter of etiquette rather than physics.

My feet would be the last to change. They would keep their soft soles, but the maps they carry would fade. Distance would cease to be measured in strides and start being measured in the accumulation of quiet. A walk to the far side of the basin would become a journey that takes not less time, but thinner time—time so attenuated it can be held in the palm like a slab of slate that never warms.

I would find myself speaking less, but storing more in the pockets of silence. A word would go into my chest and emerge later as a patch of frost on a latch, or a note from the bone chime that sounds as if it remembered my voice. My thoughts would become sedimentary: each day depositing a fine layer of attention over the previous day, compacting into a quiet kind of certainty that does not need to announce itself.

And the central slab—that altar made by erosion—would begin to look like a mirror that has learned humility. On my turn to lie there at dusk, I would feel not loss but alignment, as if my spine had finally matched the patient curve of the bowl itself. In the morning, when the slab is bare again, I would not search for myself in footprints or feathers, because I would recognize that I have become something distributive: part breath in the gutter, part cool in the wall, part silver hairline on the edge of a still day.

I would not be a person here, exactly.  
I would be the kept air, the careful stone, the sparse resident who is sparse enough to be full.  
I would be the pause that holds everything else in place.
