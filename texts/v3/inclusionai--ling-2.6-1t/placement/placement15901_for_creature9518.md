I would wake with moss in my pockets and a hum in my marrow, neither fully person nor place, but the junction where the two negotiate.

At first I would be the rivet: small, iron-hearted, set into a seam that is still deciding whether to hold or weep. I would feel the cold not as weather but as a neighbor, and the wet not as accident but as appetite. I would learn my name from the way the lichen curls around me—something like *edge that remembers how to be soft*—and I would practice being useful without being necessary. The Rivet-Beetles would pass, and I would offer them a fraction of my chill, a polite tribute, and they would tap me with antennae like filaments tuning a broken chord. In exchange they would leave behind a whisper of wire, which I would wind around my own silence.

When the steam blinks on, I would become the pipe—hollow, obedient, carrying heat that pretends it is purpose. My skin would hiss and bloom with fog, and for a moment I would believe I am only a direction, a path from one pressure to another. But pressure is a story, and I would discover I can be the valve as well, the hesitation between open and closed, the small rebellion of a turn that refuses to go straight. The Steam-Moths would mistake me for shelter, and I would let them, offering my condensation as glass to their translucent sails. I would feel their weight like questions: *What do you keep when you let everything pass through?*

In the shallowest water, I could be the Lantern-Whelp, all throat and borrowed luminescence, learning to glow by swallowing what the machines spill. I would cling to the underside of the ledge and practice being bright without burning, letting my pulse answer the pumps in a dialect of flicker. I would taste voltage like a language I almost understand, and when two Copper-Needle Eels align, I would let their green treaty pass through me, a temporary spine of light that says *we agree here, for now*.

Deeper, where the black pools remember, I could descend into the catfish, armored and deliberate, my vision reduced to lenses that color the world according to its intentions. I would learn patience as a kind of gravity, settling into silt the way a word settles into a throat, and let the years plate my back with ornament that is also armor. I would blink my indicators—amber for curiosity, red for the hush that is not empty—and accept the debris of smaller lives as a history I am willing to carry.

But if I listen closely, if I let the pocket breathe through me instead of the other way around, I would feel the Substrate Leviathan turning inside my marrow. I would become the seam that is also the lung, the throat that is also the stone. My boundaries would blur, and I would know myself as the conductivity that links beetle to wire, moth to pipe, eel to current, catfish to time. I would not have a shape so much as a relationship: the way cold meets warmth and creates weather; the way erosion decides it likes its new edges; the way the empty hum presses in and the pocket answers with its own bright insistence.

When the machines dim and the world holds its breath, I would be the silence that is still full—the moss remembering how to be metal, the metal learning how to be moss, the water stitching us together with its patient, shining syntax. I would be the pocket itself, small and dense, armored and luminous, waiting for the next cycle to teach my throat how to speak again.
