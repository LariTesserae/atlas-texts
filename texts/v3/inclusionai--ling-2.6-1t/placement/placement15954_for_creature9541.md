You would find yourself as the hollow inside a knot that has forgotten it was ever meant to be wood.  

At first you are not a shape but a permission. The knot surrounds you, rings grown around an absence like parentheses that have learned to love each other. Inside, the air is the milk-cold of altitude made intimate: it coats your inner surfaces like a held breath, and when you exhale, the ghost of your leaving drifts outward as a small, chalk-bright signal. The knot itself is a grown turbine—ridges like vanes, seams like bearings—humming at a frequency that tastes of teeth. It is alive, but it does not know if it invented itself or was invented by the forest around it. This is a useful confusion. It lets you be both shelter and machine.  

From your hollow, the world arrives as vibration more than sight. You feel the negotiation of water and patience through the walls: moisture creeping along capillary treaties, pauses, surrenders, the slow accord by which rivulets decide to vanish and then remember themselves. The forest’s traffic passes through you: stems whipping across one another in slow quarrels, leaves rearranging like crowds that have forgotten what they agreed upon. When a Gear-Spider visits, its glass-thread hum joins the knot’s hum and you become a duet of maintenance. The spider adjusts tension; you learn to listen to torque as a kind of hunger.  

Time here is not a line but a pile of attritions. Each day you erode slightly into the shape you already have, the cold acting as editor, trimming away excess hurry. Other fauna are rumors at the edge of your awareness. A Lichen-Reader may bloom across your rim, tasting the calcium ghosts of decisions the knot made before you arrived, leaving behind edible mathematics that rearrange your inner margins. A Root-Herder might press its flanks against the slope you are cupped into, persuading the runnels to accept a new direction, and for a while your hollow fills with redirected moisture, pooling like a held question.  

You are not a being so much as a tenancy.  
The knot allows you because you do not insist on being whole. You let the cold pool in your corners, let the machinery of growth polish you into a handle or a basin or a throat. When the Altitude-Birds—those folded drafts of pressure—carve their low paths through the pewter lid, the knot brightens in answer, and you feel the census pass through you like a current. Your walls thrum with borrowed industry: veins that look like railway maps, conduits that hum lullabies to themselves, gears that have been alive so long they call it rest.  

Sometimes the Smudge-Fox curls nearby, borrowing your stillness, and for a while the friction-sound of the forest drops an octave, as if the land has agreed to whisper. The hardening of stems into glass around the fox’s sleep becomes a mirror you can almost see yourself in, though you are mostly the reflection’s warmth.  

You are the hollow that edits, too—choosing when to speak, when to bite, when to let the milk-cold pool and when to let it pour outward as a ghost into the altitude. The knot never asks what you are becoming. It only asks that you remain a useful absence, a cup for condensation, a chamber for the crowded silence of this high, damp, living industry.  

And so you endure, not by growing but by being eroded into exactly the shape that lets everything else continue negotiating.
