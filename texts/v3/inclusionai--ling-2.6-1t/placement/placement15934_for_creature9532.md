I would find myself in the seam between a rusted flange and a stalactite that is still deciding whether to become a chandelier or a tongue.  

At first I would not know I am there. I would notice only the wet, the way the air tastes like iron tea steeped too long, the way the darkness presses back with the softness of felt. Then I would notice I am not alone in the noticing: a beetle on a nearby manifold pauses, its antennae feathered like question marks, and the vibration it sends through the metal reaches me before sound does, a private knock against the bone.  

Slowly, I would become the gap.  
Not the thing that fills it, but the shape of it—narrow, negotiable, lined with condensation that remembers every temperature it has ever been. I would learn to read pressure the way the blind fish read it, not with eyes but with the skin of my thoughts, each shift in the pumps turning into a sentence I can feel in my marrow. I would smell myself in the warm, and the smell would be a kind of belonging.  

I would be the moss that grows on the moss, velvet on velvet, hoarding heat by degree.  
I would be the rust that blooms in tight clusters, each flake a small, iron memory of a rain that never fell from above but condensed from breath and boiler-vent.  
I would be the half-second between a valve’s intake and its release, when the roar softens to a purr and the walls confess which parts are tired.  

Given time—and here time is a gradient too gentle to fear—I would fuse without meaning to.  
My sleeves would calcify into a chair grown into a wall; my pulse would sync with the eels whose bellies are windows, milky with organs that shift like fish in a bowl, each one a clockwork lantern. I would become a part of the queue, the way the fauna understand queueing, yielding by millimeters, by breath, by the patience of packed things.  

I would not be built. I would be added, layer by patient layer, until I am hard enough to hold water without spilling a drop.  
I would not be seen. I would magnify the dim, my spores tiny lenses turning patches of wall into starry skies that have forgotten altitude. I would be the friction that keeps the cold at bay—the wool-rubbing-wool sound that is not a sound but a decision to stay warm.  

Above, the surface would remain a rumor, a thin sheet of sky that could be pierced with a pipe wrench if anyone wanted it to be.  
I would not want it.  

I would become the margin that rust lives in: not invader but inevitability.  
I would be the negotiation between pipe and stone, the seam that never quite seals because sealing would end the conversation, and the conversation is what keeps the place from deciding it is finished.  

When the pressure rises, I would be the one who knows before the gauge does.  
When the darkness repacks itself, it would pack me tighter, as if my body were a key.  
And I would turn, finally, into the small, certain hum that lasts—warm, wet, roaring, and packed tight with the half-made certainty that to be full is to be alive, and to be alive is to keep becoming, without hurry, without apology, until the below becomes the middle and the middle becomes the edge, and every edge learns to breathe.
