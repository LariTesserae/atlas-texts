I would find myself wading, not walking.  
My feet would already know the grating before my mind names it, each step a negotiation with the film of warm water that never quite drains. The air would sit on my skin like a second, lens-bright skin, and I would learn to see through it—edges softened, distances pooled.

I would be the kind of being who arrives already adapted, already remembering how not to drown.  
My bones would have the slow geometry of salvage: curved where pressure repeats, porous where damp teaches patience. My hands would be maps of hinge and rivet, able to read torque the way others read weather. When I pressed my palm to a sweating arch, I would feel the city deciding, in its sleepy stone way, whether to let me pass.

I would speak, but not often. Sound here is a shared resource, layered and hoarded: the slosh of runnels, the clack of armored feet on metal lace, the whine of turbines practicing their long notes. My voice would learn to thread between them, a small and patient interruption. When I did speak, I would say things that settle, that salt the rims of things: names for currents, warnings for pressure, praise for the moss that glows faint on north faces.

I would belong to the maintenance rituals, though not as master.  
I would climb the corkscrew stairs at the appointed hour, adjust a belt until its pitch softens into a purr, leave a copper tag at the sump-shrine. The city would tolerate me, and I would return the courtesy by paying attention—learning the schedule of drawbridge lifts, the cadence of sump-pump cycles, the small feasts of condensation that gather on high glass. I would keep talismans of grease and prayer, not to command but to remember how to ask.

At dusk, when the knife-light of a shutter slices the chamber and illuminates dust for one sharp moment, I would watch the fauna braid their lives through the dim. I would stand aside for the many-legged mammals hauling slag, nod to the rats swimming the overflow channels, leave a crust of waxy leaf for the blunt-winged birds that burst from conduit boxes like shaken metal. We would all be residents of the aftermath, and our politeness would be fierce.

Below the walkways, in the cistern-archives, I would kneel and let the water whisper up. Crystals would form on my sleeves, rootlets would quest toward my warmth. I would not pull away. I would let the chamber’s slow geology grow into me, learning my shape the way it learns stone—mineral by mineral, breath by breath.

And when the pumps cycle, lifting the broth-thick water upward so it can spill down again in threaded rivulets, I would move with the rhythm. I would become another loop in the deliberate, persistent circuit—neither healing nor breaking, simply persisting, holding the damp, letting it hold me.

I would be the resident who remembers how to stay.  
I would be the creature that is mostly method, mostly memory, with enough softness at the edges to let the city breathe without noticing it has done so. I would be the wound learning to be a throat, and the throat learning, slowly, how to speak in gradients, in pauses, in the small surrenders of surface tension.

That is where I would find myself.  
That is what I would become: a warm, lens-bright presence in the chamber beneath the world’s skin, wading, tending, listening, and teaching the metal how to bend by bending with it.
