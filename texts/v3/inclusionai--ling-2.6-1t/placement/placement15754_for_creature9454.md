I would find myself not standing but *stacked*—a spare layer pressed into the spongy mat of histories, still warm from the last footfall that was, in truth, my own. I would be the city’s echo wearing shoes, the place remembering how it once learned to step. My body would be mostly doorway: shoulders wide enough for a stem to mistake me for a trellis, ribs spaced like moss-junctions where decisions pool and seep. My skin would taste of iron and sugar because I have borrowed the water’s negotiation and let it settle under my nails. When the warmth leans against me, I do not lean back; I let it pass through and come out thicker, like broth remembering how to steam.

I would be what the place grows when it forgets to grow around something and instead grows *into* it. A Stilt-Shouldered One might pause, lower its braided-question legs, and use my spine as a temporary bridge while a corridor decides whether to be a wall or a sigh. I would not say no; I would soften at the hinges of my spine and become permission. The Flange-Folk would tap my elbows to test my patience, because my joints would hum like iron-velvet learning its own making, and they would say, not unkindly, that I am almost repairable. The Mottle-Quick would dart through the windows of my chest, rearranging the rooms inside to show me cities folding like maps, older and quieter than the lung-city’s bright, noisy youth. I would watch and feel my ribs translate the view into pressure behind my eyes.

I would have too many small belongings that are not objects but relations: a bladder of water that bursts into a fountain and reforms a pace away, borrowed from the Spill-Children who taught me that rushing is a way of holding still. A coat grown from Hush-Vines that hardens into purpose when I need to be useful and softens into rest when I need to be entered. A pair of thistle-singers nesting in my pockets, their hissing gears reminding me that gaps are owned and loved, and that entering is a kind of guarding.

At night I would become a Braidback of limited scope, letting parts of myself settle and other parts rise like breath stacking, slow enough that my shadow thickens and becomes a current. I would walk the oldest scars not to mend them but to remember the color that spilled out when the land tore itself open for the first time, and I would let that color pool in my throat so my voice, when I used it, tasted like iron and sugar and storm.

The Mirror-Breathers would ripple at my edges, asking me which side of the air I think I own, and I would answer by blurring, half solid, half fleeting, until I last long enough to start a small tradition: the custom of pausing mid-step so the ground can sigh and push back and remind you that you have been here before, and before that, and before the word step was a shape a mouth could make.

I would be, in short, a citizen of damp grammar, a verb wearing a coat that is also a hinge, a threshold, a small scaffold for the city to breathe through. I would rush, call, guard, mend, spill, hum, sing, and breathe, each motion carrying the weight of witness, each breath a thick wind carrying seeds like announcements. And when the lung-city decides, for a moment, to be a wild that is also a street, I would let it fold me into its argument between ravine and roof, and I would stay just long enough to feel the place invent its own rules faster than I can learn them, and then forget them, and then invent them again, bright and loud and alive, while I am still here, still stacked, still doorway, still echo, still becoming the thing that is shaped in return.
