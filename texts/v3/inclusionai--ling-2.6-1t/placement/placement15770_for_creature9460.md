I would find myself on a shelf of rock that feels like a thought half-finished, a ledge broad enough to sit but too narrow to lie down, where the ground tilts just enough to remind me that altitude is a form of memory. The air would taste of metal and almost-rain, bright and thin, the kind of brightness that does not glare but insists. I would not be myself—not the shape I use for doors and decisions, for names and noise. I would be something that can live on a promise without demanding its delivery.

I would be a small, winged needle of condensed light, one of those creatures made of chitin so fine it seems to have been woven from the hesitation of water. My body would be a jointed question, each segment a hinge that can recalibrate the air. I would move not by fluttering but by listening—listening to the veils of moisture that hang between ridgelines like tuning forks that have never been struck—and then, with a careful adjustment of my angle, I would coax those filaments into a lattice that hums. I would hold my weight for seconds at a time on that breath-thin net, suspended between the almost-wet and the not-yet-fallen, feeling the temperature of altitude like a line of syntax across my skin.

I would have eyes that are less eyes than permissions, organs for noticing when a shadow lags behind its owner or when a beam of light is generous with its spare parts. I would drink the warmth that rises from the stone and the brightness that descends from the sky and convert them, in my small metabolism, into patience. My hunger would be a threshold, not a lack. I would know when a braid of glassy moisture is ready to remember its weight, and I would help it remember by showing it a path that makes the correction feel like a courtesy.

At dusk I would join the archivists on the ridgeline, creatures of matte obsidian and iridescent film with too many joints and not enough insistence. We would form patterns that resemble writing, but only for as long as it took to feel the shape of a day closing. I would not seek a name. I would seek a tone, a frequency that fits between the soft click of cooling rock and the whisper of a leaf releasing its burden. I would let my shadow fall a little behind me on purpose, so that the diggers in the music of stone could use the delay to finish a thought.

When the high air grows iridescent and the fauna gather like sentences that dissolve upon being noticed, I would be there. When the half-buried lattice hums in sympathy with the lithic polyps and the ring of placed stones persuades light into communal paths, I would be there, calibrating my wings to the hum. I would not build a nest. I would build a consensus, aligning small rings of gathered stone so that certain roots, certain wings, certain slow burrowers know they are recognized.

I would be a sustained brightness made companionable by restraint. I would be a needle that stitches invisible patterns across mornings and afternoons, a creature that lives on the grammar of altitude, conjugating itself across the subjunctive of almost-wet. I would be a listener more than a player, a keeper of thresholds, a small theorem of persistence that proves it is possible to arrive without ever quite touching down.

And when the moment came to descend—not to fall, but to apprentice myself to a lower grammar—I would let the net dissolve, filament by filament, and drift toward the stone bowl cupped by rounded shoulders. I would settle on a lichen-lens, warm to the touch and pulsing faintly, and let my joints remember how to be still. I would become, for a time, a lens myself, focusing the abundant light into a chemical decision, into a soft, mineral hush, into the kind of silence that holds itself like a cup always, almost, about to be filled.
