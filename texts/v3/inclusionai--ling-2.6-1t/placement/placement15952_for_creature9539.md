I would find myself standing barefoot on stone that feels like a question held too long in the mouth—rounded, cool, damp at the edges. My body would be the one that has been waiting to arrive here: a tall, narrow thing with knees that know how to give, skin the color of wet shale, hair braided with filaments of pale grass that twitch when the wind combs the bowl. Where my shadow falls, moss would stir as if remembering my name from a previous damp.

I would be the caretaker of pauses, the person who has agreed to keep time by letting it pool. My hands would be large, palms lined with fine ridges like miniature watersheds, so that when I press them to the boulders I can feel the whisper-lice go still, then resume their listening with a softer attention. I would hum the note that the glass-ring singers miss when the wind changes direction, a low, steady tone that helps the droplets find their sequence again. Not a maker of music but a tuner of intervals, someone who understands that harmony here is a matter of spacing, not volume.

I would wear no shoes because the stone needs to know who presses on it, and I need to know how the stone answers. My feet would be toughened but not callused, sensitive as moss to shifts in temperature and texture. When I step, I do not lift; I release. The ground accepts me and gives back a faint warmth that rises from deeper layers, proof that the day’s heat has settled into a reservoir it is willing to share.

If I spoke, my voice would be the sound of a pool deciding to settle—not commanding, only suggesting. I would rarely speak to the fern-walkers; they prefer silence shaped into a corridor. Instead, I would stand where they could fold the fronds above me, accepting their cool cloak without moving until the arrangement felt complete. I would watch the mirror-wraiths drift, my head tilted just enough to see the sky they steal. Sometimes I would hold out a cupped hand and let a wraith’s reflection ripple across my palm, feeling the odd, dry chill of stolen light.

I would know how to wait for the mud-moths to finish their accumulation before brushing a finger along their wings, tracing the sedimentary lines that map the bowl’s damp history. I would collect the dust they release and press it into the softest seams of stone, not to hurry the sealing but to say I understand the need for memory to become part of the rock.

At dusk, I would lie on my back and let the stored warmth of stone move into me, becoming a temporary container for the bowl’s patience. My breath would rise as a faint mist, joining the hanging moisture, and for a while I would be part of the second atmosphere, shoulder-high and luminous, a small, breathing island in the kept bowl. The reeds would ring softly, and I would know their pitch not by ear alone but by the vibration in my ribs.

What I would do here is simple and endless: I would attend. I would let the moss remember through me. I would notice when the rain has grown polite, when the mirror-wraiths have folded, when the whisper-lice have gone still with warning. I would adjust my stance, tilt my face, soften my gaze, and in doing so help the place keep its balance. I would be, in other words, a creature who has learned that to stay is to change slowly, and that even a caretaker must be cared for—by stone, by moss, by the high, kept bowl cupped just below the sky.
