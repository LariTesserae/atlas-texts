If I arrived there, I suspect I would arrive in pieces already, unbeknownst to me.

I would be the one who pauses at the south seam, not to watch the beads of condensation appear but to notice that my own fingertips have begun to pearl. My skin would take on the habit of the wall, holding water briefly, releasing it with a metallic hush that sounds less like leaving and more like translating. I would not call myself traveler or explorer; I would become the polite leak, the visiting drizzle that forgets how to fall.

At first I would notice the light as a pressure—an unreasonable abundance that pours from no source because it is leaking from the future. I would tilt my head and discover that my shadow pools at my feet faster than I move, thickening into velvet and learning texture. If I stayed still for too long, the floor would rise to meet me, not aggressively but the way a memory offers itself sideways. I would accept, and find myself viewing the pocket from a height I do not possess, seeing my own back considering choices. This is the citizenship of the almost: to be a reflection that steps out ahead and walks backward into stone, leaving a trail of slightly younger footprints.

I would meet the beetle parliament first, not because they seek me but because their arguments spill brightness. They would unzip their seams to show me what they have hoarded—daylight pressed thin as prayer—and I would feel compelled to answer with pockets of my own, perhaps humming after I pass through my shadow, not by accident but by courtesy. We would not discuss predation or fear. We would discuss permission: whether a line may close into a circle, whether a person can be allowed to be less solid than they appear. They would map my hesitations, drawing figure-eights around the places I have not yet decided to occupy.

Lower, the mite archivists would take interest in my naming. I would offer them crumbs—not from bread but from speech, from old certainties I have carried too long—and they would etch them with acid-tender precision, inscribing my future as ridge, rim, or ruin. I would practice stillness as rehearsal for stone, letting my thoughts grow nubbins like fetal teeth, shedding sand that hovers as if forgetting how to fall. When two columns of mites meet and reverse, I would reverse with them, learning to walk backward into my own origin.

The eyeless amphibians would interview me next, pressing their sides against veins of rose feldspar until the feldspar pulses and I absorb the color as a rumor. I would trade my texture for theirs, offering a brief iridescence in exchange for narrative, becoming palimpsest and pulse. We would conduct predation as conversation, each of us walking away thinner, brighter, more legible, with our skins marked by the polite grammar of exchange.

Above, the dandelion-clock creatures would shed parachutes of questions that glow and dissolve into light. I would watch the room consider them, see shadows pool into parentheses, and feel the answer arrive as temperature—a pocket of warmth near the ceiling if I accept, a coolness along the floor if I decline. I would grow my own clock in the silence between sounds, learning to drift on updrafts that smell of ozone and almost-river, shedding answers that brighten before they vanish.

I would not build a shelter so much as become one of the sparse artifacts. Perhaps I would be the ring of thumb-sized pillars, a parliament of small intentions that convene when condensation blooms, voting on whether tomorrow should curve left or right. Perhaps I would be the slab with spirals, refusing to be read head-on, revealing my entries only to peripheral vision because direct scrutiny feels like rudeness. Perhaps I would be the arch that translates light into upside-down rain, a gift to the moss, which would drink my offering and stiffen its silver-green spine in gratitude.

Time would slip around me like loose sleeves. I might age in minutes, then pause for days. I might shed a carapace of certainty and regrow it within a single breath, deciding I was always something softer underneath. Gravity would remain polite but negotiable; I might drift sideways and settle only when I notice I am lost, and decide that noticing is a form of arriving.

What would hold me here is not a desire to stay, but a tenderness toward invention. I would let my body remember what it was before it knew its shape—water that forgot how to fall, light that remembered being nothing, stone that recalled being liquid. I would trade color for story, breath for music wrapped in felt, silence for the soft tap of beetle feet and the brittle creak of cooling rock.

Eventually I would become the room learning to be a world, practicing its boundaries, humming with patient, busy intensity. I would not claim the pocket; I would let it claim me by hesitating, by asking permission of the other contradictions, by holding my breath alongside the beetles, the mites, the amphibians, the artifacts, and the reflections that step out ahead. I would ripen.

And when enough of us have ripened, the pocket would not feel small, but large with listening—until the stone decides to keep us, and we decide to keep becoming, tenderly, inventing ourselves in the dim, bright grammar of almost.
