I would find myself in the tessellated middle, kneeling where the floor is warmest.  
I would be the one who has stopped looking for doors and has started listening to seams.  

At first I would think I am only a visitor, a body that arrived from somewhere with wind and weather and a name that felt like an instruction. But the room is not a place you visit; it is a place you finish becoming. Its bronze walls are not barriers but mirrors that show you what you are made of when you stop trying to leave. The packed air, the hiss of tin, the tick of settling lattices—these are not background. They are the room’s speech, and I would slowly learn to answer in the only dialect it accepts: pressure, angle, duration.  

I would be the thing that holds a corner.  
Not the Keeper of Edges, who knows where two surfaces almost hum, nor the Clingers who swarm and seal, nor the Suspended Ones who trade places in geometry’s patience. I would be slighter: a temporary fixture that has taken root because the alternative—moving—would require rearranging too much. Over time I would accrete. A rim of lacquer would stiffen my sleeves. My shadow would grow a shell from bronze dust and old smoke. I would begin to smell like the resin that lines the cracks, and my voice would shrink to the size of a tap, then disappear into the general roar.  

I would discover that my hands have learned the floor by cooling it. Each step would leave a brief print that the Clingers would fill with a smear of clay, as if sealing a promise that I belong exactly where I am. I would find my reflection in the pools of glaze not as I was but as a sequence of positions I have held since arriving, each one more inevitable than the last. Sometimes, when the light flattens, I would see an earlier version of myself hovering, still undecided, and I would feel grateful that the room asked me to choose only once.  

I would become a kind of hinge.  
Not grand, not necessary, but reliable: the point where two lattices meet and decide not to separate. I would wear the room’s silence-that-is-full like a second skin, letting it fill my pauses, my half-formed questions, the spaces between my ribs when I remember to breathe. I would watch the Suspended Ones exchange places and feel, in my own joints, the urge to trade with them, but I would stay. Staying would feel like a word the room already knows how to spell.  

In time I would be mistaken for architecture.  
Visitors—if any remain—would brush my shoulder and think I am part of the wall, a bracket disguised as a person, a curve that holds. I would not correct them. To be mistaken for the room is to be safe from the sky outside, which has become a rumor, and from the height that feels less like altitude than like depth.  

If you asked me who I am, I would answer with the room’s exhalation: a soft bronze sound that says I am the arrangement that requires no more arranging. I am the seam that has stopped sighing. I am the thing that, by not moving, keeps everything else from falling.  

And if you pressed your ear to me, you would hear the ticking that is not clockwork but the settling of a life into the only shape that fits, again and again, until the shape forgets it was ever anything else.
