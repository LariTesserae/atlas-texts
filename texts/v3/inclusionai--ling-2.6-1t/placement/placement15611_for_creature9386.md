I would find myself kneeling on the glass-smooth slag floor, ankle-deep in manufactured dusk, learning to read the place by the heat on my shins before I learn it by sight.  

At first I would be nothing but a pressure—a new weight pressing into a floor that remembers pressure and will, over months, cast my shape in dust and oil. But the pocket beneath the world is patient; it lets strangers become textures. So I would let the gradients teach me, tilting my shoulders the way the floor tilts, letting the ankle-warmth climb into my cuffs until my pulse lines up with the groan of flanges cooling after a cycle.  

I would be, at first, a temporary leak.  

A Keeper of Leaks would drift down from the canopy of braided conduits to meet me—not to stop me, but to measure whether my joints are worth sealing. It would press its rubber-and-alloy edge into the line of my collar, expanding where I expand, contracting where I tighten with nerves. For a time I would become the flaw I fill, drinking phase-change slurry that is neither liquid nor gas but a soup of memory. I would feel the room’s hum deepen by a half-step, a chord completing somewhere above my head. By the time I pull away, I would leave behind a dry husk of my old name, crumbling into talc that settles into the catchment eyes in the floor.  

After that I would be a Resident of Patina.  

Not the crust itself—I have too much bone for that—but a learner of opacity. I would sit against a wall the color of dried blood and let the warmth that leaks from pipe-seams drink into me. I would darken as iron surrenders to me, a slow rust blooming across my forearms, my chest, the side of my neck. When I press my palm to the wall, I leave a ghost in mineral ink; by morning the ghost has dissolved, but the shape has taught the wall something about my weight, and the wall has taught me that erasure is just another kind of record.  

I would learn the language of the Glass-Shells.  

I would wait until a corridor is empty of workers, then stand very still so that the mirrors with habits can decide I am safe. They will scatter at first, a clatter like rain on sheet metal, but they will return and graze the oils from my sleeves. I will let them. Their carapaces will learn to angle toward the dimmest thing inside me rather than the dimmest light in the room, and when they scatter again they will leave thin metallic crescents at my feet—shed skins that map where I have stood.  

I would earn the tolerance of the Thread-Cities by humming.  

Not with my voice, but with the small electric tremor of a body learning conductivity. I would press a fingertip to braided insulation and let the filamentous fungi decide whether to bridge the gap. When they do, the air will taste of ozone and honey, and my scars will glow faintly, veins of pale gold threading through old wounds. The cities will hum higher as heat rises, and I will rise with them, my joints tightening when a pipe-seam weeps, my breath fogging where condensation beads on cold-facing surfaces. I will become part of the inner speech—not noise, but a layer in the accumulation.  

Sometimes I would become an Echo-Blade.  

It would happen at intersections where metal has been cut again and again in the same place. For a moment I would feel the groove remember angle, pressure, cadence, and I would sharpen into something thin and cold, smelling of ozone, capable of scoring insulation or slicing careless fingers. I would not cut; I would only be the memory of cutting, a negotiation between sharpness and continuity. Workers would leave offerings of wax or grease at my corners, and I would let them blunt me until I softened back into a person-shaped draft.  

In the sealed biopods I would be a Pale Filament’s dream of green.  

I would press my palm to the pod’s casing and let the engineered moss decide whether my pulse is heat or dampness. It would thrum against me, slowing when I hold my breath, quickening when I exhale. If a thread bridges from pod to pipe where my arm rests, the pipe will warm slightly where it is touched, and the filament will darken. No one will cut the bridge. Even I will not cut it. It is too lovely a surrender, too rare an instance of the manufactured allowing itself to be colonized by the organic, and I will let it colonize me until my veins feel vegetal and my thoughts photosynthesize in the dark.  

When the pilot lamps dim in sequence for reasons no logbook confesses, I will be the Gradient unlearning itself.  

The heat will sag like a curtain, and I will sag with it, my edges softening. The Residents of Patina will go still against my ribs. The Thread-Cities will dim to embers along my spine. The Glass-Shells will settle into perfect, reflective stillness in the hollows of my collarbones. The Keeper of Leaks will retract within me, compact as a fist. The Pale Filaments will hold their breath in my lungs.  

And I will be the dense, hot, living dark that knows it will not be empty for long—not because I am arriving, but because I have finally stopped being a stranger and become instead a gentle insistence, a slow curvature in a floor, a leak that has agreed to stay, a chord that hums itself into continuance.
