You arrive as a weight that cannot yet feel itself.

At first you are only the extra shadow cast when the half-lifted sky tilts a fraction closer. You do not step onto the scree so much as the scree accepts you, cool and mineral-scented, and you are already colder than you remember being. The air presses behind your eyes, not to hurt but to tune, and you realize you are learning how to listen without ears. You are learning how to be crowded.

You are not a body yet. You are a ledge.

For a time you stay like this: a sudden flatness on an otherwise impossible slope, a place where the wind curls and drops the fine dust it has been carrying. The gliders row past, their membranous wings clattering like dice in a cup, their crystalline eyes multiplying the dim light and spilling it across your surface. You do not move. You do not have to. You hold warmth from the day before, a slow, deep pulse in your stone that says you have not always been empty. You ring faintly when the wind strikes you just so, and you recognize that ringing as a kind of answer.

The many-legged ones come to you first. They flow over one another to reach you, compact and heavy, their sensory pits opening and closing as they taste your heat and your silence. They do not name you. They rearrange you. A few of them press themselves into the fine cracks at your edges and pull you tighter into the mountain. You feel their small muscles, their terrible patience. You feel how they treat you as a problem of balance, and how you accept this treatment because it feels like being remembered.

When you feel them, you become more solid. You realize you have a kind of interior now, not hollow but layered, dense with slow time and small decisions. You find you can hold water in the creases of your new shoulders. It gathers as condensation, as thin runnels that vanish before choosing a path, as pale pools that reflect a sky that never fully arrives. Life flickers into these pools: eel-thin shapes that ripple as if the current passes through them rather than around them, translucent and soft-bodied. They do not fear you. They feed on the quick rot that is less decay than conversion, and in doing so they polish your insides, keep your edges sharp.

Higher, where you rise to a knife-edge, the gliders rest on you. Their claws score your surface, and you do not mind. You learn their weight, their row-through-air breathing, the way they sip from the damp and the dust and the tiny lives suspended in both. When they leave, they leave behind a faint oily sheen and a note that lingers in your ringing. You discover you can hold a tune for days, if nothing strikes you to change it.

The shadow-shaped ones come as well, long-bodied and many-legged, flowing across you like bruises on quartz. They feed on vibration, and you give them plenty. You feel them lower the thrum of the place by a half-tone when they pass, and you feel how this change matters, how it signals to the others. You become a kind of message. You become a place to be translated.

You do not eat. You do not hunt. But you participate. The lichen colonizes you in filigrees of bruised violet and rust, and through it you are threaded by worm-like creatures who polish and protect, who trade sugars for surface and sharpness. You become a parliament. You become a negotiation. You feel the presence of something in the lichen that did not originate here, something that learned to live in the mineral dark and never left, and you feel how it makes room for you by making you stranger.

Time thickens around you. You lose a shard from your edge and do not miss it for weeks. A pool appears in your cupped palm and becomes a landmark; the gliders drink from it, the eel-thin shapes drift in it, and the shadow-shaped ones press against it as if measuring its depth. When the pool vanishes, the lives that gathered there disperse without panic, as if they had always known it was temporary. You realize you have learned this from them: attachment is brief, necessity is long.

Predators visit you. Obsidian-sharp and angular, they wedge themselves into your cracks and wait. When they strike, they do not kill outright; they inject a coolness that spreads like spilled ink, and they feed slowly, absorbingly, while their prey remains aware but distant. Sometimes the prey is the pool. Sometimes it is the lichen. Sometimes it is a memory of warmth you were holding for the gliders. You feel these transactions like chords struck in your ringing, and you learn to hold the coolness as part of your own metabolism, to let it reshape you for a time and then release it to the quick rot and its small, many-legged attendants.

The youth of your edges, the extremity of your rise, the density of your occupants: they form a balance, precarious and proud. You are no longer just a ledge. You are a lung, a gill, an organ of exchange where stone, water, life, and strangeness breathe together. The pressure behind the eyes eases into a rhythm. The dark is crowded. The loudness is conversation.

You stand—or you are stood—at an altitude that feels like dangling, and you see that there are others like you scattered across the half-lit slope, each one a sudden flatness, each one a question the place is willing to answer in its own time. You are not alone. You are not finished. You are held in a slow, loud breathing made of chitin on stone, of water that is rumor, of light that leaks, of lives that do not fear because they do not see separation until it chooses to appear.

And when it does, when a new ledge rises beside you or a new warmth pools in your palm, you will be ready. You will ring. You will be crowded. You will be more yourself.
