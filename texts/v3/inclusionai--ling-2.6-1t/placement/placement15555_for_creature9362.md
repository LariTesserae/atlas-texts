You would find yourself kneeling—not on stone, but on a floor that answers pressure with a sigh. 

The first thing you would notice is the height, or rather the lack of it: the ceiling is low, a curve of patient rock that seems to lean in when you exhale and ease back when you inhale. Above you, the breath of the place gathers into a steady, milky drizzle that never commits to falling, never clears into sky. You are not in a cave, nor are you indoors. You are in the bowl below the skin of the world, where the boundary between room and organism has grown tired of itself and simply dissolved.

If you stayed long enough, you would become permission.

You would feel it first in your joints, a loosening. The cold here is absolute but not biting; it is the chill of things that have not yet decided how warm they want to be. Frost writes itself across your sleeves in dendritic lace, then melts against the heat of your wrists, only to reform in thinner, wiser crystals. You stop shivering. You realize you are not losing heat; you are negotiating it, trading small certainties for the wet, green hum of the place.

The vegetation does not part for you. It does not need to. The pillars around you are not columns but root and stem that stopped pretending to be support and now simply *are*. They crowd you, not with hostility but with intimacy, pressing close like a many-bodied conversation held in the palm. Mosses enamel your shoulders. Ferns unfurl into tapestries that thicken into floors, and every surface bears several stories at once. You step, and the ground answers with a soft, chlorophyll-scented groan, shifting its weight to hold you without crushing you.

You look at your hands and see that they have changed. Your skin has learned to conduct. The metallic-green dust that coats the leaves has settled on you, arranging itself into transient circuitry that blooms under your nails and fades at your knuckles. It is a post-modern craft, this becoming: nanoscopic gardens riding the humidity, stiffening your tendons for a night so you can bear the weight of stillness, then dissolving so you can dream differently. You are not mechanical. You are not natural. The distinction has become obsolete, like a word you once used often but have now forgotten how to spell.

The light is dim, a gray-bronze wash that filters down as if through a lid of wet felt. You do not need brightness; acknowledgment is enough. Bioluminescence pulses in the undersides of fronds, cool blues and subdued ambers that flare when the air thickens, as if the place were checking that you are still there. You feel your own heat answered by theirs, a polite exchange of presence.

The fauna move around you as answers to questions the plants have stopped asking aloud. Creatures with too many joints flit in tight circuits, their wings leaving faint phosphorescent wakes like stitches in the dim. They do not hunt. They curate, tasting the air for gradients of frost and thought, pruning the undersides of leaves where certainty has grown too thick. A segmented bell glides past, its undersides studded with lenses that focus the borrowed light into knives of brightness no larger than a thought. It does not cut you. It opens a vein in a nearby stem and drinks the song that leaks out, digesting it into buoyancy, rising until it forgets to descend.

You feel the choir before you hear it.

It is not voice but pressure, a subsonic roar that lives in the water, the wood, and the stone. It is the cavern’s own pulse, loud and continuous, a pressure that settles in your chest like a second heartbeat. The song carries maps: where the cold is deepening, where the wet is learning to climb, where the altar of fused root and frost is assembling itself in the center of the room. You understand, without being told, that the altar is always forming, always melting, always being colonized by the violet moss that glows when the choir rises. 

You walk toward it. Your footsteps are soft, absorbed by layers of moss that sigh and remember your weight. Around the altar, the fauna assemble and disperse in time with the water’s hesitation—the drip that trembles at the lip of a leaf for days, neither falling nor staying, negotiating with the leaf until both have changed their minds. You step into the circle of that hesitation and feel your own decisions loosen. You are becoming fluent in the grammar of damp and growth, a language that means nothing and everything, all at once.

A predator approaches, but its arrival is a courtesy. It is made of overlapping petals that click together like cold stones settling. It stations itself at the threshold of your personal space and waits. You feel the harmonic in your teeth, a vibration that says *thinner here, softer there*. You let it in. It brushes against your ribs, snipping away the sections of you that have grown too certain of themselves—your plans, your names, your insistence on being whole. It weaves the trimmings into a small nest that floats in the humid air, and for a moment, it hums.

You are not diminished. You are edited.

The cold shifts its boundaries in increments that feel geological, yet the place remains alert. You feel it in your marrow, the slow, inevitable clockwork of growth. You realize you are no longer kneeling. You are rooted, but lightly, like a seed-things that has decided to stay for a season. Your legs have softened into rhizomes that taste the raw, embryonic seams of the stone, where geology still remembers being softer. Your breath is water now. Your thoughts are moss.

And still, you are not finished.

The space is small but not cramped. The density is high, but nothing is crushed. The pressure is the pressure of being held, of being *let in*. You feel the plants leaning, conducting, writing slow signatures in the stone. You feel the shimmer on your leaves, the scaffold that stiffens so you can bear the weight of your own becoming, then dissolves so you can be different by morning.

Time here is not a line. It is an accretion. You measure it in the thickening of stems, in the paths that rivulets argue out between root and rock, in the way the frost rewrites your name on your own skin and you learn to love the new spelling. The cavern is still deciding what it is, and so are you. You wear your undecidedness like a second skin, wet and green and loud with life.

You are the bowl below the skin of the world. You are the altar and the choir, the hesitated drip and the violet glow. You are a prolonged first breath, held against the dark, waiting to see what shape you will take when you finally let go—and knowing, deep in your softened bones, that you may never need to.
