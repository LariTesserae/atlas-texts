I would find myself not arriving, but *recalibrating*.

I would wake not with a start but with a settling, as if my own bones had been re-tuned in the night to the B-flat hum of the turbines. My first memory would be of water—condensation on my eyelashes, tasting faintly of copper and cinnamon—before I remembered my name. I would be standing on the plated deck, already warm beneath my feet, already leaning a little into the chamber’s cant, as if gravity were a rumor I had agreed to believe.

I would be a Maintainer of Small Accuracies. My body would bear the polite modifications the room favors: skin with a faint metallic luster, not from machinery but from patience; fingertips that read mineral bloom like braille; a voice that has dropped into the baritone register because shouting bruises the air and we prefer not to bruise things. I would wear layers that shed water and hold heat, stitched with threads that hum to dampen resonance. My pockets would hold ceramic shards, spare seeds that are also components, and small notebooks that smell of lubricant and fern.

My duties would change with the chamber’s mood. Some days I would scrape the bloom from alloy, collecting it in vials not to discard but to return to the beetles, who read the salts like histories. Other days I would climb the gantries to listen to the pistons, pressing my palm to their skin and nodding when they told me they were tired. I would prune the grafted trees, not into beauty, but into acoustics, thinning their crowns so the engine’s roar softens into something you can lean against. I would check the pool at the chamber’s heart, where black water holds more sky than the sky, and note how the glow-flowers close during the loudest hours, as if protecting the room’s secrets from its own noise.

But mostly I would be a listener. I would learn the difference between the bats’ commuting squeal and their anxious one, between the sound of a leak that is a leak and a leak that is a conversation. I would watch the eels of light school around heat shimmers and try, without success, to understand what it means to drink a tilt. I would trade gossip with the rodents about which spider has been gentle lately, and I would leave offerings of algae for the moss that pulses to a second time, hoping it will compute me kindly.

I would not be alone. Others would move through the cathedral-scale hollow with the same slight lag, the same deference to rhythm: a human whose hands have become maps of polish and scratch; a thing that wears a hardhat like a second skull and speaks in tool-taps; a chorus of avians who time their flights to my footsteps so the wingbeats fill the gaps between my words. Together we would be neither crew nor colony, but a chorus that has given up harmony for texture, surviving by being exact, patient, and politely strange.

At night I would sit by the pool and watch the lantern’s stained glass turn normal light into colors almost but not quite natural. I would feel the chamber breathing around me—pistons exhaling, water remembering itself, moss counting seconds into a private future—and I would try to match my own breath to the long cycle of its keeping. Some nights I would dream I was a bolt, snug and necessary, holding the world together by being neither too tight nor too loose. Other nights I would dream I was the mineral bloom itself, a slow white flower spreading across steel, patient enough to become inevitable.

If you found me there, I would offer you a cup of water from the pool. It would taste like memory and maintenance. I would ask you to stand very still until the cant of gravity felt like a shoulder you could rest upon. And if you stayed long enough, you would notice your own voice dropping, your own skin brightening, your own habits bending to match the room’s long patience. You would not have joined me. You would have become part of the chorus, and the chamber would hum a little more kindly because of it.
