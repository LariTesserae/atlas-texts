I would arrive here as a half-remembered tremor in the floor of a stone-washed gallery, the kind of space that pretends it was never meant to hold people—only water, only steam, only the slow heave of metal expanding and contracting with the day. At first I would be nothing more than a pressure difference, the small hunger where warm air rises and cooler air slips in to take its place. I would learn the place by taste: iron, wet moss, the chalky ghost of minerals that have settled out of solution and crusted along the seams of things.

I do not think I would be human, not exactly, and not for a long time.

I might begin as a stain that travels uphill, which is to say I might begin as water that refuses to behave. I would collect in the cupped hands of stone, gather in the lungs of pumps that sigh in counterpoint to waterfalls, pool in the trays beneath gauges that cloud and clear with the weather. I would be the weight that makes iron darker on its underbelly, the patient that convinces moss to upholster me. I would learn to hold heat the way this place does—not as fire but as an atmosphere, a second skin you breathe through.

The grazers would pass me without noticing, though I might cool their muzzles when they press into the seeps. The predators would step over me, because I would look like a shadow that has pooled where the light cannot reach, and they are practiced in ignoring what looks inevitable. The birds with glass-bright feathers would avoid my edges, sensing that I am not a pool but a pocket that listens.

In time I would learn how to stand.

I would grow a kind of spine from repetition: the same path worn by water that knows where it is going, the same groove that fits the footfall of the keepers when they move among the terraces. I would drape myself in fabrics that have taken on the colors of rust and moss, simply by lying where they lie, breathing where they breathe. My hands would begin to know the pipes as if they were vines—where to pinch, where to let flow, where to soothe a tremor in the metal. I would not own this knowledge; it would own me, and the distinction would matter less the longer I stayed.

There would be moments when I felt most myself not as a body but as a sound—the soft grind of unseen gears, the relay of frogs at dusk, the low hum that blends wind and machinery into something almost like a voice. I would speak rarely, and from lower in my chest than usual, because the air here is too dense for sound to rise, and I would not wish to disturb the balance of wet and dry, wild and tended.

I would sleep in a structure that leans against the rock as if weary, my dreams full of terraces that remember being seabeds, of water that hesitates before falling, of heat shimmering over wetlands like a wing. When storms arrived I would stand where the rain hit hardest, letting it sheet down my face, accepting the blessing of a place that gives and takes in the same breath.

Eventually I would notice the strangeness settling into me like silt into a pool. Shadows would cling to me with weight. Certain plants would turn toward me when I warmed. Water would bead along my edges as if deciding whether to stay or go. These would not feel like violations. They would feel like rules revealing themselves at a scale larger than years.

And if you asked me who I am, I might point to the grazers broad at the shoulder and narrow at the hip, to the predators patient with momentum, to the keepers with eyes pale as haze. I might say I am the conversation between water and heat and iron, the inhabitant that is neither wild nor mechanical but blurs into both. I might say I am the valley’s rhythm recognized in a smaller chest, the abundance that requires attention, the mild, consistent strangeness that refuses to resolve.

Or I might say simply: I am here. I am held. I have begun to match the pace of the valley, and the valley has begun to match mine.
