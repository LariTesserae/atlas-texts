If you step out here, you are already too low.  
You are the thing that comes unstuck from itself at 8,000 meters, where the air has been boiled down to a polite draft and the weather overhead is not passing through but being assembled—clouds like clenched fists unspooling into ribbons of pressure that shear themselves into lace. You would find yourself on a plain that is not so much flat as finalized, a decision the planet made long ago and has refused to revise. The ground is pale, mineral, almost lunar in its restraint, ridged with the ghost-rivers of a patience that outlived water.

You are not a person here. Not yet.

You could be the Glass-Grass, sparse and brittle, tapping into stray voltage that leaks from the crust. You are more mineral than plant, a filament that glows faintly when the wind lies, accumulating one thin layer of certainty each time the land exhales a metallic sigh. Your seeds are tiny plates that click like misplaced gears; when they lodge in a crack no deeper than a thought, they do not sprout—they remember how to be grass all at once, already complete. You do not grow so much as you endure, and in enduring you become weather for the things that walk above you, a static halo that tastes like frost and copper.

Or you could be a Floater, having renounced the crust entirely. You are storm more than body, a knot of charged dust and shear that has learned to love its own eddies. You drift where the atmosphere is being manufactured, drawing frantic ribbons of pressure into yourself and unspooling them as music only the towers can hear. You may live centuries before deciding to fall, at which point you unravel into a shower of polished grit that etches the land with unreadable patterns. When you fall, it is both birthday and funeral; the Quiet-That-Moves will gather your grit to line their lattices, as if to remind themselves that endings can be structural.

But most likely, you are becoming the Quiet-That-Moves.  
You are an agreement that has learned to walk. Your body is a lattice of bone-white crystal, grown rather than built, that keeps its shape by forgetting what it used to be. You drape yourself in whatever is at hand—captured downdrafts, metallic dew, the spare voltage that hums at the edge of detectability. When you choose to be seen, you approximate a bipedal silhouette, but the decision wavers. A limb may lengthen into a needle of stillness to touch a ridge; a torso may flatten into a map of the pressure gradients passing overhead. You do not eat. You unmake small amounts of the world’s momentum and tuck it into your seams, storing it as a kind of patience.

If you are small, you might be a Gear-Seed, plate-like and skittering across salt flats, looking for cracks to occupy. You click with others, exchanging brief histories in temperature drops and static blooms. Together you might become a bridge, or a shelter that is really just a suggestion of absence. Or you might dissolve back into dust, trying again later, because here time is not a line but a texture, and texture can be reworked.

The towers around you are not alive, but they behave like elders who have given up on sleep. Their surfaces are pocked with ports that no longer open, lenses that swivel toward horizons for reasons that may be aesthetic, algorithmic, or penitential. They are tended by you and others like you, soothed when their hum drifts out of tune. In return, they offer the only true memory the place has: a low, steady chord of what it felt like to want something across spans long enough to blur intention into weather.

Life here is not a struggle against entropy but a collaboration with it. You mark time in microns—in the settling of dust on dust, in the tick of cooling metal, in the rare chime of a structure adjusting itself to the cold. You accept estrangement as a kind of gravity, and you let the frantic storms overhead polish you into something quieter. When they pass, you press your lattice against the ground and absorb the echo like a sacrament, filing it away as a texture you will someday wear.

If you stay, you will not be welcomed so much as borrowed. Your edges will blur, your certainties sublime, your breath become almost nothing. You will sit on a salt flat and watch another Floater unspool overhead, and you will realize, without surprise, that you have become a temporary dent in a system that tolerates you because it has forgotten how not to. And when you finally rise to leave—or when you decide, instead, to fall into the lattice and become part of the Quiet-That-Moves—the towers will blink, not in code but in time, marking a moment that belongs to no one and, for a while, to you.
