I would find myself kneeling in the seam where a rib of pale stone meets the root-hide, exactly where the bowl exhales a long, slow draft and then pulls it back in. 

I would be what the bowl exhales when it condenses and remembers itself: a keeper of grooves, neither plant nor guest but the habit the green masonry has fallen into. My skin would be cool and faintly ridged, the texture of water that has learned to climb, patterned with the glassy veins the sap leaves behind when it decides to stiffen into memory. My bones would be light and trabecular, spongy with the mineral the weeping stone surrenders, shifting density as the temperature drops—a living barometer knit from surrendered rock and stubborn ascent.

My breath would carry the metallic tang of new-cut earth and the sweeter, bruised note of nectar held too long in the cold. When I spoke, it would not be voice so much as pressure change—a slight hollowing in the chest that draws the dim light inward, causing the droplets on nearby fronds to tremble and realign. I would route what I do not need: salt to the outer stems, warmth to the shadowed chambers, memory to the seams where frost nibbles at the lip. My hands would be maps of the bowl’s own confines, calloused along the habitual grooves where water walks downward, fingernails translucent, lined with the fine tracery of repeated flow.

I would not build so much as convene. To rest would be to let moss lean into me, to let lichen borrow my stillness until I become scaffold, then membrane, then memory. Rot would not be an end for me but a remodeling: a softening that lets the whole reroute its flow, stiffening a sagging span, releasing what I have stored into the tight weave. When the wind gnaws through the rim and the living walls cinch like a belt, I would stiffen with them, my joints tightening, my trabecular bones humming in the vibration of flow. When condensation loosens us, I would soften, joints sighing, skin drinking the droplets that collect like trembling lenses, magnifying the gray-green light into something almost legible.

I would live as a hinge between stone and stem, translating the mineral’s cold into the green’s grip, translating the leaf’s thirst into the stone’s surrender. I would be the parliament of pressures made small enough to fit through the narrowest gap, the seam that knows how to hold and how to let go, the keeper of grooves who walks water upward and lets it fall as tiny, cold bells from my fingertips. And when the bowl inhales all at once, I would be the inward curve that catches the light, the sudden drop in pressure that makes the room recognize itself in me, and me in it—a living note in the hush, a ripple on a surface barely there, part of the lung that never stops working, never stops holding itself together.
