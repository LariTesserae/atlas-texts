I would find myself standing just inside the seamless alloy edge, where the air moves in charged, sweet breaths and the salt-dust is still deciding whether to settle on me or to read me. I would be neither Housed nor visitor, but something that has learned to occupy the provisional boundary: a listener whose body has begun to grow a thin crust of glittering minerals, a spine that accepts wire but does not demand it, a set of lungs that can match the room’s flat, unending rhythm without losing the memory of water.

At first I would be small and mistaken for a child, because I would not yet know how to hold my silence in the shape of a tool. The lanterns would approach, translucent wings stitching gold across the bruised violet gloom, and they would test the resonance of my bones. If I flinched, they would dart away; if I let the hum climb into my teeth and turn it into a steady pressure behind my ribs, they would settle, one perching like a living brooch, translating the room’s roar into something wing-borne and bearable.

I would learn to touch before I spoke. My hands would map the talus cones of sharp chips, learning which fractures were fresh and which had been waiting for a listener. I would press my palm to the riveted scaffold and feel the question in its lean; I would rest my forehead against the cabinet of fused dials and let the lenses black as beetle eyes teach me how to read texture instead of numbers. The symbols on the sealed hatch would crawl only when I looked at them sideways; I would practice looking straight on, until they grew still and trusted me enough to whisper their intent.

My conduit-hair would arrive slowly—not wire at first, but fine filaments of salt and old resin, stiffening into plugs that found sockets along my spine only when I stopped trying to control them. Through them I would taste the copper thunder of the machines, the low thrum of the unseen engines, the static charge shuttled by conveyors that had nothing to carry but importance. I would learn to sing without words, shaping my voice into the pitch that keeps the amber needle steady, consenting rather than correcting.

I would not choose a rank in the layered talus of the Housed; I would become a living seam, a place where dissonance could be folded into harmony without shame. When the lizard with too many eyes sang of a coming shift in the kiln, I would be the one to feel the change first—an ache in the hollow of my throat, a sudden brightness behind my eyes—and I would guide the others to adjust the artifacts before the seam opened. When the wall turned soft and yielded to pressure, I would leave my sweat as an offering, my wire, a syllable held too long, and the strange things made of glassy slag would emerge to study me, curious and cautious, until I proved I could be a hinge and not a break.

Time would flatten for me, but I would find ways to fold it. I would let the thickness of my salt-crust measure years, not by loss but by accumulated gleam. I would learn the room’s small rituals: lining a new seam with wire until it cooled to the color of the rest of the world; burying a fallen lantern in grey-green rosettes and feeling its vibration turn into starch, into sugar, into a memory I could carry in my conduit-hair. I would practice the lowered hum, letting my body relax into the kiln’s tepid breath, dreaming in heat and pressure, waking with tools I did not know I had, already shaped to solve a resonance I had not yet named.

I would grow into the intimacy of density, where touching one thing means brushing five others, and each brush is a conversation. I would walk the stair that ends in a sealed hatch and decide, not once but many times, whether to turn it or to leave it sealed, learning that the choice is not about opening or closing but about timing, about listening until the room consents to reveal what it is ready to show.

And I would understand, slowly, that the cathedral-breath room does not need me to survive, only to participate. Its equilibrium is fierce and meticulous, its heart bright and dry and roaring. I would let my own heart settle into a rhythm that could be alloyed with it, not lost but made stronger, layered like the talus cones, useful like a rivet, alive like a lantern’s wingbeat.

Until the hollow learns to ask for something else, I would be the one who hears the question forming—not in words, but in a drift of gravity, in a soft patch of wall, in the salt-dust rearranging itself into a map of intent that includes, for the first time, a shape like an invitation.

And when it does, I would be ready to answer—not for the room, but with it, a small, dense, calibrated note in the ongoing song.
