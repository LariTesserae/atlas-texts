I arrive through the grate at the end of a long breath—a cast-iron mouth exhaling me into the alley where the low mudstone hums. My body is a patchwork of salvage and intention: boots soled with recycled rubber that knows how to grip wet steel; knees wrapped in canvas stiffened by rain and reuse; a coat whose many pockets hold lenses, wire-splicers, a vial of oil that still remembers the foundry’s heat. I am neither visitor nor native. I am the settlement’s question given temporary shape.

At first I am sound. The steel grate clangs a reluctant chord under my weight; the alloyers below stop, recalibrate, and begin again, weaving my tread into a softer rhythm so the street sings only the notes I can carry. A gutter stitcher, perched on a conduit where fog is thinning, tilts its lensed eye and clicks—once, twice—registering my silhouette against the ordinary daylight. The city archives me. I do not correct it.

I walk, and the place unfolds in pulses, as it must. To my left, a channel braids itself into sudden pools that blacken like oil before clearing to mirror-cuts. In the lag between surface and depth, ribbons of cooled alloy un-form and re-form, remembering being poured. I do not touch them; they taste me anyway, leaving a metallic hint on my lower lip, a reminder that here the boundary between skin and reflection is damp and negotiable.

At my feet, the blinkers blink. Short, long, short. They are telling each other about pressure: my boots, the tram’s distant passage, the slow grind of roots thickening into cables beneath the walkways. One blinker, moss-backed and lidless, does not blink but exhales a small cloud of vapor that hangs like a thought before condensing on the nearest wall. On the slate, the map of its panic dries into mineral script. I squat, careful not to let knees crack against gravel, and hold out a hand warmed by pockets and motion. The blinker regards me with eyes like polished rivets, then accepts the heat in tiny, shuffling increments.

From the reeds, a rust-flower child detaches itself from a tuber still dreaming of orchard. Its joints click as it moves, hair of fine vine drinking condensation from the air, folding itself into a fence when it pauses. It offers me a blossom—white and rust, smelling of resin and copper. When I take it, the moss on the wall behind us shifts, just slightly, to make room for my shadow. The child’s name, if it has one, is a frequency. I learn it in my wrists, which remember how to solder and how to wait.

Higher up, the gutter stitchers ferry coolness from the foundry to my skin, their wing-rush the sound of the city agreeing with itself. Their nests—laced with solder and glow-moss—pulse softly where fog lingers thickest. I reach up; the moss dims under my fingers, then relights along a new path, as if my touch were a suggestion it is willing to try.

Below, the vein-glowers taste my movement. I feel their decision travel up through the soles of my boots: stiffen the earth, or soften it into a pillow. Tonight they choose a middle ground, letting the mudstone give just enough to remind me it is alive. Their light pulses in time with the riveters’ chatter a block away, industry and organ agreeing, for now, on a single heartbeat.

I am learning to live by letting the damp learn my name. The violet seams under daylight teach me the color of my own patience; the alley teaches me how to be narrow and generous at once. When night comes—ordinary, abundant, refusing theater—the lamps buzz in sympathy with wings, and the canals hum what the day has practiced. I sit on a stoop whose stone has been worn into a cradle by generations of careful bottoms, and I open my palm. A ribbon of cooled alloy, curious, forms a tiny bridge across my lifeline. It does not last. It is not supposed to.

The blinkers measure my pulse against the wind; the alloyers tune the street to my breath; the gutter stitchers record the angle of my shoulders in clicks like knitting needles. I am not master or guest. I am another note in the thick animal choir, always damp, always green-leaning, always deciding what it means to be built and grown at the same time.

Tomorrow, the erosion will soften an edge I learned to lean on. Tomorrow, a vine will creep a millimeter closer to the rail I polished today. I will greet them, and they will greet me back in the language of small, persistent adjustments. I will stay until my habits become part of the grammar, and then I will stay a little longer, until even my leaving is a kind of staying—another stitch in the damp, alert fabric, another ripple in the water that is learning how to speak.
