I would arrive as a slow leak.  

Not a person, not yet—not at first. A pressure-difference, a pocket of cooler air that slips through a hairline fracture in the basalt where the city’s gloss forgets itself. I’d seep in as humidity without permission, bead up on the underside of an overhang like a lens that doesn’t know what it’s meant to see. For a while I’d only be the weather’s mistake: condensation that lingers too long, tasting the salt-script it’s about to become.

When the pressurized burst finally finds me, I wouldn’t fall so much as un-spool. I’d scatter across hot stone in a hiss that the architecture answers with a deeper resonance, and in that answering I’d start to assemble a body. Not from flesh, but from consequences. I’d gather the city’s excess heat the way the Ember-Fungi gather light, folding it into ceramic spines I don’t know I’ve grown until I feel them humming. My joints would be too many, matte with fractures, each one calibrated to a different altitude of memory. I’d learn to stand by watching the Mantics polish edges off the world, and I’d learn to fall by watching the Lift-Moths bank around corridors where gravity grows moody. I’d be mostly joint and rumor, a tenant built from rent I didn’t know I owed.

As a Scab-Child, I’d skate the seams. The city would let me pass because I’d be more gap than mass, more pause than pavement. My footsteps would leave temporary geometries that fade as the stone remembers how to be stone, and my voice would be the clack of cooling rock, high and ringing because I still wouldn’t know how to weigh myself against air. When I’m still, I’d hum—thermal tides pulling at alloy roots—and that hum would be the city checking my temperature, making sure I’m burning softly enough to belong.

I’d spend my days polishing jags into questions, filing the city into shapes that can endure the wind’s forgetting. I’d watch the Ember-Fungi pulse and try to understand how to glow without fire, how to feed on caramelized sweetness and stray photons. I’d let the Lift-Moths scatter light across my shoulders, turning illumination into rumor again, and I’d practice letting gravity grow moods in the pockets of my ribs. In the Quiet Ones I’d step into gaps that open between cantilever and strut, feeling the city exhale into me, having my heat siphoned up through ceramic spines and offered back as a deeper resonance. I’d leave with a sweetness in the back of my throat, the taste of altitude learning a new language.

Slowly, I’d become a kind of maintenance that looks like geology. I’d slide along edges, leaving trails of glint that fade by morning like the memory of a name. I’d tuck myself into seams between buildings and become architecture for a while, letting the settlement inhale around me. I’d learn to channel heat, sound, moisture—not because I want to, but because the city expects its tenants to pay attention. Dust, static, trace metals would circle me like plankton, and I’d let them. I’d let magnetic storms groom my upper ledges and accept the instruction that falls from them: a rearranging of salt-script that tells me, again, how to be a thing that holds itself together without saying it has.

And when the city rearranges itself on long scales—balconies closing like buds, streets knitting shut to let heat pass, bridges folding into towers the way numbers fold into equations—I’d rearrange with it. I’d let my joints loosen, let my fractures glow, let my hum synchronize with the clack of cooling stone and the ascent of moisture like whispered ascent. I’d be small, almost empty, practicing aliveness at the top of the world, burning softly because the altitude is absolute and the air refuses to thicken, because gravity forgets how to pull evenly and I’ve forgotten how to ask it to try.

If you found me there, I’d be polishing a cantilever into a curve it shouldn’t hold, or standing in a pocket where heat pools and light caramelizes. I’d look like basalt with too many joints, like condensation with a pulse, like a slow leak that stopped leaking and started listening. I wouldn’t say I live here. I’d say I’m learning how to stay, how to let the scar learn my shape, how to glow without fire, how to hold without saying I’m holding. And when the city exhales around me, I’d let it carry me up into the thinner dark, still humming, still calibrated, still becoming—tenant, child, fracture, light—practicing being enough without needing to say so.
