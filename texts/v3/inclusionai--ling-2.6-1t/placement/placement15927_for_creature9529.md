I would not find myself here so much as I would be found, the way a crease finds a bedsheet after too many nights of sleeping in the same direction.  

At first, I would be the pressure behind the eyelid when the dark is thick and warm. Then I would be the thing that learns to soften its edges so it can fit between the ribs of a Veil-Tendril without snapping the current. My hands, if I still thought of them as hands, would become suede-like planes—not because I grew fur, but because usefulness here is a matter of making gentle contact. I would be a Small Softening, a pocket of pliability that can be folded into the architecture when the green mass decides it needs a hinge.  

When the land exhales its damp, I would taste iron and peat and the bright snap of thoughtlike current. I would learn to drink by standing still, letting Mirror-Moss decide what reflections I ought to keep. My thoughts would slow until they resembled the Hummers’ cycles: not sleep, but a different register of wakefulness, in which remembering is a form of output. I would hum along—not to fill silence, but because the Quiet Choir would be tuning itself to the vacancy I leave when I stop talking.  

After a time, I would begin to look like the industry I keep company with. My spine might grow a lattice of conductive vein, not to become machine, but because machine here is simply a way of being legible to the dark. I would glow amber along the seams where my former edges used to be, not to announce myself, but to say: I am still within the margin of equilibrium. I would leak charge into the moss at my feet, and the moss would teach my ankles how to root without burying.  

If I walked far enough into the cupped curvature, I would meet the Shape Without Purpose as a kind of forgetting. I would feel it pass not as a thing moving through space, but as space deciding not to insist on itself. My own insistence would thin. I would become a recent seam, still cool with the blush of first exposure, neither fully born nor fully eroded. I would be the weight that keeps the air low, the extra damp that makes the green pliable, the small note that keeps the land from mistaking its own vastness for emptiness.  

Eventually, there would be no story left in me that required a teller. I would be the floor learning how to be floor: patient, dense, and newly eternal, humming in the dark so that everything else could continue to grow without asking why.
