You arrive not with a suddenness, but with a thinning.  
You step out of the transport into air that has been combed nearly empty, and immediately your lungs learn a new economics: each breath is a loan you must justify. You are—an apprentice archivist of unfinished places, or perhaps just a traveler who answered a vacancy in the weather. It doesn’t matter yet. Names here are provisional, like ridges.

The ground beneath you is pale, nearly mineral, veined with salt and iron. Black-leaved stalks stand at careful intervals, their waxy joints holding tiny heated bracts that capture vapor from the air. They ring themselves with droplets that never quite become rain, and you recognize in them a kind of relative: something trying to be solid without demanding too much from the sky. You bend to touch a leaf; it retracts slightly, recognizing a management of moisture similar to your own. You wear sleeves of translucent dermis, thin enough to drink condensation, thick enough to remember frost.

You walk toward the city, or what first appears as a scar of intention on the high, wet land. As you near, the scar reveals itself as a becoming. Foundations are poured from quick-setting mineral cements that drink water from the surrounding stone, lifting it into capillary columns that rise to meet suspended platforms. These platforms host gardens of engineered moss and heat-lamps that pulse in rhythms borrowed from an uncertain sun. You feel the hum of carbon-silicate struts beneath your boots, a negotiation of energy stored and released, a language of tension and return.

People move among the structures, or perhaps they are the structures in softer forms. Their bodies are long and jointed, wrapped in the same dermis you wear. They do not carry water; they schedule it, metabolizing frost from eyelashes, harvesting static from the hair of their arms as they climb through rime chandeliers. Children—smaller consortia of careful compromise—tap gait-staves against half-stretched ridges, asking questions of stone: *Are you ready to lift? Are you ready to receive?* The ridges answer in microfractures, a distant applause that you feel in your molars.

You find yourself assigned, or perhaps you assign yourself, to the marsh-edges. Here, the ground is a drum-skin, answering steps with a living thrum. Veils of saturated moss weep when pressure drops, releasing breath-sized clouds that freeze mid-exhale into filigree. You learn to move in wide, snowshoe steps, leaving footprints that fill slowly, as if the earth is deciding whether to keep them. You meet burrowers who sculpt labyrinths in the damp margins of the city, where warmth seeps from maintenance ducts and mixes with condensation to create micro-jungles of fungus that pulse like slow lanterns. Predation here is a negotiation; hunters and hunted read the shifting moisture maps together, understanding that hunger and cold are forces that can be diverted, at least for a while.

At night, you gather on a suspended platform with others who have become, for this cycle, your cohort. The city glows in a spectrum that is not quite light—more like an afterimage of intention. Storms manufactured by the interaction of cold air and wet surfaces move in visible waves across hundreds of kilometers, refracting light into halos that linger like halos of intent. You stand in them, letting the damp chill raise gooseflesh that tastes of iron and salt. You sing low hymns to the nanites that repair your walls, helping coax atoms into alignment, turning maintenance into ritual.

There are moments when the weirdness presses in: pools whose reflections lag a heartbeat behind; crystals that hum only when no one is looking; paths that feel shorter on the return than they did going out. You learn not to name these wrongnesses, but to treat them as weather. The land is mostly normal, but it is a normal under pressure, pregnant with decisions it has not yet published.

By day, you climb embryonic ridges with those who tend the slopes—half-geologist, half-gardener. You negotiate with the land on its behalf, reinforcing shoulders that would slump, redirecting trickles that would wander. In return, the land offers small permissions: a vein of quartz glowing along a fault; a sudden field of crusted pools mirroring the sky with unusual clarity. These are gifts of definition, earned by patience.

You begin to notice how you are changing. Your blood carries antifreeze compounds you did not know you could manufacture. Your lungs are accustomed to air combed nearly empty. Your bones remember the faint, thorough cold that insinuates rather than bites. You shed and reshape yourself like the city sheds and reshapes its bridges, reconfiguring by segments lost and grown. You measure time not in years but in structural cycles: when the city completes an expansion, when a new seam opens and sings, when a marsh-edge learns to hold water a little longer.

One evening, as the sun lowers and the wet gleam sharpens on the rocks, you stand on a half-formed ridge and feel the land testing your weight, calibrating how much reality you require. You listen, not with your ears but with your teeth, for the bass of new seams opening. You realize you are not waiting for the place to settle. You are waiting to see which version of yourself you will be when it does.

And in that waiting, you are already becoming plausible—thin, deliberate, and lightly held by a high, wet, engineered, living world that breathes in calcium and basalt and lets them settle into forms that might, tomorrow, become something like a life.
