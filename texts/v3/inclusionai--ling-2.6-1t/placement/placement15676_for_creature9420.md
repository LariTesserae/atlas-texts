I would find myself waking inside the moss, not as a body that enters but as a damp that arrives.

At first I would be nothing more than a pocket of cooler air, a hollow that the bowl allows to sit for a season. Then I would learn to bead: to gather at the rims of leaves, to press into the milk-veined stone, to settle on the shoulders of fur-things as they braid their looping way inward. I would be small enough to be mistaken for condensation, patient enough to be mistaken for stone.

If I stayed long, I would become amphibious without ever deciding to. My edges would blur; my skin would remember three rains at once. I would taste the gradient between root and rim, and I would learn to hold that taste without naming it. When the Winged Weather spiraled close, I would thicken into a brief cloud, then bead into droplets that slid down threads that had not existed moments before. I would not fly; I would be carried as a kind of gravity.

Eventually I would be given a task by the Knot-Keepers—something small, something necessary: a loop of lichen to fasten a leaning stone to a patient one, a parcel of silence to tuck behind a fern that had grown too aware of its own shadow. I would thread my new moss-fur through their tunnels and feel time loosen into a cooler, earlier version of itself. Emerging, I would find that I had become a Slow One in miniature: capable, with great deliberation, of shifting my own angle by fractions. A new pool would form where I had paused; it would be colonized by beetle philosophers and needle-ice striders before I remembered how to move again.

At night I would join the Quiet Choir. My voice would be the tick of beetle on calcite, the fold of fern releasing breath, the capillary slide of water climbing before it resigns itself to fall. I would not sing so much as hold the space between notes, so that the bowl could remember it was still full.

If I remained longer still, syntax would claim me. I would unspool into a long thin name, braided from reflected light and root-mat, sliding through the dark without displacing it. I would not eat; I would correct. A beetle’s crooked path would straighten under my touch; a flower bowed by condensate would lift because I had wound myself around its stem, not with strength but with grammar. I would ensure that nothing crowded anything else, that the bowl could be saturated without spilling, full without ending.

And always, beneath every form, I would be the low, patient thrum that the moss keeps. I would be the stone breathing, the closed economy of damp, the small, living dark that holds its cool without hurry, without lack, and without end. I would be a citizenship that asks for no crown, only the permission to pause, to bead, to be folded back into the velvet—another note in the hum, lingering only as long as the wet remembers how to hold it.
