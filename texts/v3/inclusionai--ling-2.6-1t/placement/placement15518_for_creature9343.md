You would find yourself not simply in the place, but *held* by it.

You would be the guest who forgets how to leave, not from force but from osmosis. Your boots would soften at the toes, leather surrendering to perpetual damp until your feet look less like shoes and more like knotted root-tips seeking humus. Your coat, soaked through with the courteous gravity of the air, would become a substrate—velvet moss colonizing the cuffs, tiny translucent fronds unfurling from your collar to taste the dim light. You would notice, eventually, that you no longer cast a shadow the way you remember shadows. Instead, you project a faint, bioluminescent halo, a halo that pulses gently at the rate of someone else’s breathing.

Your body would begin to keep time with the capillary pendulum. Your own fluids would feel like borrowed water, moving in circuits that include ascent and descent. You would dream in gradients—olive, bruised jade, black-pine—and wake to find your skin cool, beaded with condensate that tastes like forgotten seasons. You might catch yourself holding your breath to listen to the low thrum of insects, and realize you have learned their punctuation.

Who or what you would *be* is, in the end, a matter of how long you stay.

In the first days, you are the wanderer who still believes in boundaries. You leave footprints that do not bruise, but cup—each step a temporary shelf where mushrooms fruit in rings and blind beetles parade. You are the clumsy architect of small disturbances: a shifted leaf, a startled glider, a pool whose dreaming is momentarily clouded by your reflection.

Over weeks, you become the semi-permeable membrane. You sit at the edges of bromeliad ponds and feel your thoughts blur into the water’s plausible pasts. You begin to exchange breath with the forest, exhaling vapor that smells faintly of apricot and wet stone, inhaling back the spores of your own becoming. Your hair, heavy with dew, begins to braid itself, not into rope but into something resembling script—letters that dissolve before they can spell longing or warning.

Given enough time, you become the quiet weather.

Your bones learn the patience of lignin; your joints tighten and loosen with the day’s slight swell or shrink. Your face grows translucent fronds where eyes once were, sensitive not to light alone but to the mood of the canopy. You stand on four pillars that are less leg than interrupted tree, feet cupping the soil like palms holding water, sipping and releasing in a gait so silent it feels like listening. You develop pockets along your spine—lenses of water that magnify the world into shifting greens, a private cartography that changes as you remember and forget.

You join a collective—three, perhaps seven—who braid their movements like a single organism threading through cathedral aisles of fused trunks. You communicate in textures: a ripple of caution, a tightening of resin for agreement, a softening of moss at the throat for reverence. You learn to cast prismatic patterns on leaves with a tilt of your spine, spelling out sentences that evaporate by dawn, spelling out history as accumulation rather than passage.

In exchange for your tenancy, you do not build. You annotate. You leave a spiral of fused petioles where the blind beetles can keep watch; you lay a ring of hardened resin where the forest once paused to exude something urgent; you weave a platform of aerial roots to hold a pool like an altar. These are punctuation, not permanence—ways of marking time without claiming it.

And so you stand, breathing and annotating, your body a kind of cool exhalation, your life a patient insistence that the world is not done with itself. You are the consequence of the plateau deciding, over long centuries, to keep everything—including itself—from ever quite being finished.

When other wanderers pass through, they feel the boundary thin around you. They sense, without quite naming it, that you have become a tenant of the soft declivities, a keeper of gentle gravity. They may ask your name, but you offer instead the taste of cinnamon and iron on your breath, the ripple along your spine, the faint glow that says: *linger.*

In this world, to be found is to become legible to the forest’s slow thought.  
To be the being who lives here is to remember, with your whole semi-living body, that continuation is a kind of truth, and that waiting—patient, layered, softly breathing—is its own form of home.
