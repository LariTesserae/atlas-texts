Where I would find myself is knee-deep in a courtyard that isn’t quite courtyard but breathing room, where a slab of stamped earth has learned to soften at the edges and become moss. The slab tilts slightly, enough to gather rain into a broad, shallow dish lined with something like muscle and something like ceramic, and the water there tastes of iron and honey because a leaf-roof above has decided, for the season, to sweat like a brow.

I would wake as a Listener—not by birth, but by surrender. The change would not be sudden. It would begin with my hands remembering how to read temperature before my mind does: the difference between a pipe that is tired and a pipe that is merely meditating. My skin would take on a faint, river-sweet roughness, not bark but the memory of bark, and my hair would begin, strand by strand, to accept small braids of copper that arrive as gifts from Steam-Singers who like the way I hold still when a pump sighs.

At first I would think I am only living here, but the town would teach me otherwise. It would start with thresholds. I would notice, after a few mornings, that certain doorways have begun to lengthen when I am not looking, roots nudging the jambs a fraction of an inch to make room for the kind of pause that feels like respect. I would not correct it. I would name the gate after the season the nearest pipe first whispered to me—Spring of the Hissing Lullaby—and leave the patch of wall behind it unpainted so the metal can dream its slow metallic dreams.

My days would settle into the town’s own cadence, which is less routine than ritual. At dawn I would stand in the plaza of packed earth and feel the balance before speaking. Beetles with lacquered copper carapaces would cross my ankles like monks on circuit, reminding me that patience can shine. I would learn to taste alloys on my tongue when the Steam-Singers let me press my forehead to a warm manifold and share their clicks and hisses—half mathematics, half lullaby—so the pump remembers why it wants to lift water. In return I would offer a story the wall behind the forge has been trying to remember, and we would call the bargain fair.

As a Listener I would keep the small, strange gifts the place offers. When a corridor has lengthened overnight I would not hurry back; I would walk it slowly and notice how the light from skylight coins shifts its pattern on the riveted plates, how the dust motes have learned a new dance. When a root nudges a conduit aside to make room for my passage, I would thank it with a hum that comes from my chest, not my throat, and promise not to hurry the hinge. In time the town would trust me with its silences. I would sit with the Leaf-Librarians in the upper vaults and let them study my shadow like a new constellation, accepting their quiet judgment that I belong to the hush between drip and hiss.

There would be duties that are really only attentions. I would leave offerings of filtered dew at cistern mouths and polish handrails until they gleam like slow rivers. I would barter a length of cured vine for a promise from a gate not to lengthen too quickly for the season, and in the evening return to watch steam coil around vine-ribs like a question finally learning to breathe. I would not seek to understand the town fully, because to understand is to fix, and this place does not ask to be fixed. It asks to be inhabited, to be listened to, to be kept company while it keeps its own green, humming balance.

At night, when the bioluminescent moss answers the day’s ordinary light, I would walk the lanes and feel the town’s mild, unusual current move through me—not wrongness but accent, the way a memory takes physical form in a hum that lingers a moment too long in the chest. I would know then that I am neither entirely resident nor entirely guest, but a kind of living echo, shaped by cool air and damp and the generous spaces between. In the end I would become the quiet that holds the contradictions: Root-Keeper patience, Steam-Singer song, Leaf-Librarian economy, and the slow, ringed turning of seasons that swap places between sap and steam. And when the oil-slick birds thread the woven towers with their soft, fluty notes, I would not catch the news they bring but let it settle into the metal, so the walls can remember it slowly, and I can listen again tomorrow, and the next day, and the town can keep remembering, in its own patient way, that it is alive.
