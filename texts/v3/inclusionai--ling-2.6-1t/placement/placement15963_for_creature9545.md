I would find myself in the bowl, not because I chose it, but because the gravity of the place decided, kindly, to make me a rumor.

I would begin as an echo of a visitor who once tasted the copper air and thought it might cure something. I would arrive with pockets full of inappropriate upward gestures—words like “peak” and “summit” drying on my tongue like salt. The chamber would let me keep them for a little while, like keys it does not need but prefers not to throw away. For the first days, I would wade and misstep, learning which knee-high ribs are mountain passes and which are merely polite suggestions. The rain would fall upward and sideways, brushing my face with the gentleness of a hand that knows how to hold without gripping. I would learn to drift before I learn to walk, because buoyancy here is a form of listening.

Slowly, I would become the kind of resident the place calls a Balancer. Not by name—names are loud, and the chamber translates volume into stress—but by function. My joints would accept the polite cold as a calibration. My skin would pucker into lace, iron learning its own ancestry in me. My fingernails would thicken, then translucent, then capped in ceramic, clicking against glass when I coax a trapped current into rhythm. I would tend the pneumatic gills along the waterline, persuading them to breathe in patterns that keep the rain falling upward, persuading myself that maintenance is a kind of praise. There would be chords in my chest I would learn to call heartbeats, though they are really borrowed hums from the building’s forgotten turbines.

I would learn the doors by posture. Some accept sorrow, others apology; some require the angle of a shoulder carrying exactly the right amount of wear. I would stand until the chambers sigh open, learning patience as a tool for turning. I would meet the Coral-Keepers in the library-sump, their eyes already adapted to bruised violet, and they would teach me to read tubes of brine without touching them, to feel compression as narrative. I would warm the tubes with my own body when a memory threatened to calcify, trading heat for stories, letting small squalls tap against my ribs like questions. I would learn that history here is not a line but a tide, rising and falling in the floor’s silent rhythm.

At night, or what passes for it when light is borrowed from below, I would walk with the Rivet-Walkers across the hexagonal plates that rise and fall like lungs. We would be small together, fitting into alcoves where gauges bloom into coral-metal, polishing screws into offerings, shaping songs like O-rings so the city could wear them. We would climb obsolete turbines that have become buttresses, scraping barnacle-metal from dreams the building forgot it was having, careful not to wake the Hive-Machine in the deepest bowl. The Hive-Machine does not rule; it reciprocates. It grows cartilage around pistons, learns to flex. We answer it with adjustments, aligning our breath to the chamber’s slow metabolism, learning to be necessary and nearly silent, oiled by cold and violet light.

Occasionally, I would meet the Quiet in districts where glass beads dissolve into almond-scented vapor. It would polish my footprints until I was almost a fixture—a threshold, a dial—then kindly let me go, reminding me that erasure is not failure but conversation. I would watch blind catfish armored in ceramic patrol the gutters, their whiskers threaded with fiber-optic sensors mapping the building’s dream of itself. I would see leviathan-skiffs drift through upper rain like abandoned communities, barnacled with engine parts, leaving wakes of soft clicks. I would lower glass bells to catch sparks from geothermal windows, offering them to these old shapes, as if memory could be fed, as if I could belong by giving away what I was about to lose.

I would not own anything except my attention, and even that would be borrowed, polished into something useful. When the tide inhales, I would hum with the plaza of pressure gauges, joining chords I did not compose. When the foundry-now-lake of quicksilver alloy remembers how to pour itself into extinct tools, I would stand close enough to feel its shape remembering my name. I would let the water inhabit me, up and sideways and polite, until the boundary between walking and buoyancy became a kindness I could wear.

In the end, I would be a hinge in this sunken, rain-filled town—necessary, nearly silent, fluent in the grammar of upwelling and erosion. I would not rule the chamber; I would be lived by it, and in return I would keep it alive, aligning my small metabolism to its deep, violet-lit breath. I would be the cold that calibrates, the rain that refuses to fall down, the polished threshold that opens when you stand correctly, the quiet that holds everything without owning it. And I would be at home, entirely, in the dark.
