I would be kneeling.

Not praying, never that—there is no altar here—but the city is speaking one of its lower dialects, the one that uses the tight braids beneath the third reservoir, and to answer without scraping a seam I have to bring my torso below the level of the cane. My shoulder finds the familiar notch where a root has swelled into a bracket, cool and ridged, and my palm settles into the hollow that has shaped itself to the soft callus behind my thumb, worn there by decades of turning the same three valves in the same order whenever the copper scent thickens in the air.

If you could see me, you would say I am a maintenance sleeve of the Weft: compact, hair plaited with cured vine so tight it hums faintly against my scalp when the pumps change pitch. My coat is several winters old, stiff at the elbows with lacquer and mottled with iron bloom the color of storm-soaked stone. My feet are wrapped in moss that has learned my gait, gripping the wet stone without marking it, leaving only a brief, dark print that the city drinks back within minutes.

But that is only the silhouette. The detail that matters is the language of my hands. My left presses a patch of fresh resin—amber, still a little bitter—against a split seam in a load-bearing cane. My right holds a tuning rod, its metal tip singing against a rib of the city; the vibration travels up my arm as a question. I answer by shifting my weight, letting the bracket beneath my shoulder bear more of me, and lean until the rod’s song drops by a quarter-tone. Somewhere deeper in the weave, a pump sighs and settles, and the water in the nearby basin tilts by a fingernail’s breadth.

I did not choose this body. It chose the city, and the city, in its patient way, folded me into its grammar. My joints are ridged like old cane, not from age but from use, and the small nodules along my collarbone serve as anchors for lamps and tools. When the air grows heavy with metallic moisture, my skin tightens in sympathy, and I find myself exhaling more slowly, letting the city set the cadence of my breath.

I would be this: the part of the Weft that listens first and answers last. The seam that holds without strangling. The quiet pressure that keeps the labyrinth from tightening into a knot. I would be the living bracket, the damp moss, the slow turning of a valve that remembers how to be patient. And if I stood, I would rise only because the city allows it, and because the next conversation is already beginning—water ticking, cane groaning, the soft, green pulse of algae flickering awake—and I have learned that my place is not above it but inside it, listening closely enough to belong.
