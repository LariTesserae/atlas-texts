I would find myself on a catwalk stitched between two condenser towers, at the height where the runoff turns from silver to brassy. I would be one of the middling-sized gliders—neither insect nor bird, but something that has learned trade from both.

At first I would feel like nothing more than a seam myself: a shadow that water hesitates to cross, a patch of warmth that rides drafts rising from the sump-pumps below. My body would be long-limbed and sure, with too many joints for the span of a human, each one oiled by the city’s own slow breath. I would have a crest of filaments, fine as condenser wire, that trembles in the layered warmth and tells me which drafts carry steam and which carry rain. I would not be alone; I would be a kind of consensus among wings, a shape that the city arrived at after years of trying not to fall and centuries of trying not to be noticed.

Where I stood, the air would taste of iron and altitude, thin enough to sharpen thought. My claws would click on grating, a small percussion that merges with the chitter of beetle-things below and the low thrum of engines somewhere deep in the grid. I would be middling in size—large enough to read the slope of a gutter, small enough to slip through a crack where rust has made a door. My eyes would be multifaceted and patient, accustomed to fractured light; they would catch the glitter of runoff and the pulse of stray arcs leaping from patched grids, and they would see, without surprise, water flowing briefly uphill before remembering the contract it made with gravity.

I would carry the city’s patience in the curve of my shell, polished by wind into a shape that fits the hand of an arch or the angle of a bridge’s underside. When the city thrums like a struck bell, I would not flap; I would let the sound pass through me and decide where to go afterward. When the winged tides rise in clouds of iridescence, I would be among them, not leading but belonging, a single note in a chord that lasts longer than memory.

If you looked closely, you might see that my shadow is never quite where I am: it lags a word or two, rehearsing conversations I have not yet had with the stones that hum in relief. I would not fear that lag; I would use it to know when a new crack is opening in the masonry, when a seam is learning to become a river. I would drink from condensation on transformers, and I would trade droplets to the small, bright-eyed things that live in ducts for stories about how the blocks are raised and lowered on oiled gears. In return, I would tell them how the wind is shaped by canyons, and how the horizon is only a rumor until you grip a girder and let the city’s slow continental scale settle into your bones.

I would not live like a monument, but like maintenance. When machines are maintained until they become something else, I would be what they become: a glider that is also a gauge, a listener that is also a leak. I would know the temperature layers as my own—outer carapace repelling noon, inner membrane hoarding warmth for the long descent into night. I would hunt when the layers mix, not because I am cruel, but because patience is a kind of trap, and hunger is a kind of balance.

At dusk, when the city dims into amber and then into the blue-white insistence of its own lamps, I would choose a balcony that kisses another balcony like gossip, and I would roost. I would fold my many limbs into a geometry that fits the close ranks of the city, and I would listen: slap of water on metal, grind of gears easing into alignment, chitter and boom, low thrum. I would wait for the accidental synchronization that makes the city thrum, and when it passes, I would step to the edge and let the runoff’s sluggish serpent coil around my ankles, tasting the gradient it has learned.

In this world, I would not be a visitor but an echo of the treaty—iron, water, and wingbeats—written large enough to be lived and small enough to fit in the seams. I would be the thing that endures by being middling, by being many-limbed, by being patient as rust and bright-eyed as filament, by being loud as a struck bell and quiet as a drip that finally decides where it belongs. And I would keep listening, long after the horizon has promised nothing more than itself, because in this high, wet city, listening is how you learn to live in slow, mineral time.
