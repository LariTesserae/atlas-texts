You would find yourself at the throat of the basin, where the raw white drainage lines still weep and the first rope bridge sags low enough to drag your boots through the film of water that coats everything at ankle height. You did not arrive by a road, because none exists here; you slipped in through a gap that only appears when the lake exhales at dawn, a seam in the town’s breath that tastes of wet stone and unseasoned wood.

You would not be one of the cartographers of closeness—not yet. At first you are a smudge, a half-formed pressure against the damp. Your body is still borrowing its shape from the place, learning its center of gravity by watching how the gutters tilt when you shift. You have not been given a name that describes a fault in a wall or a habit of water. Instead you are the question the basin asks when it is not sure it is ready to be answered: Are you the drip that finds its way to the gutter without help, or the one that needs a bowl, a shim, a hand?

Your skin is not yet pale as unglazed clay. It is still learning the chill, still holding the outside in its pores like a foreign language. You wear scarves not to keep fungus from blooming but to remind yourself where your edges are when the fog leans in. Your hands are soft, but they carry the shape of tools you do not yet know how to name. You hum when you work, not because you have learned to read the stone’s answer, but because the vibration tells you where your body ends and the wall begins.

If you stay, you will become a listener. You will rise before the birds—or what passes for them—and press your palm to a hollow post that rises and falls like a second chest. You will learn to tell a plan by the stillness of water in the weir, to settle disputes by rerouting, to measure time by the lake’s exhalation. Your name will drift toward something like Gutter-Knower or Silt-Thumb, a small solid thing that anchors you to the place you keep.

But in the moment you arrive, you are neither tool nor caretaker. You are the pause between the town’s decisions, the half-carved space that still remembers it was recently torn. You are the drip that refuses to choose a path until the morning, the hand that has not yet warmed a stone enough to change its color, the fog finger that reaches into a room and does not know whether it will be welcomed as news or turned away as a clog.

You are, in other words, almost part of the rhythm—close enough to feel the slap of water against wood, the creak of rope, the cool that settles in fabric and bone—but not so close that you cannot still slip out through the seam if the basin decides it has breathed enough and wants to be alone. For now, you stand where the alley exhales fog at the same moment your breath leaves your lips, and you listen, and you do not settle, letting what emerges become more itself as it learns to name you.
