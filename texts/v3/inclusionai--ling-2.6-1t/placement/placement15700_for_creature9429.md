I would find myself kneeling on a ribbon of mineral skin that is still learning how to be a road, somewhere between the first ridge and the first valley. The air would taste metallic and sweet, and my breath would come out steady, as if the thermal plateau had borrowed my lungs to prove it could hold a rhythm without ever needing to change.

I would be something that arrived in halves.

First, I would be the Hummer’s unfinished cousin—not low enough to graze stress from fault lines, but not quite animal yet. My body would be an oval of enamel the color of cooled slag, segmented like overlapping shingles, except my segments would click with a sound like pages turning. I would not feed on tension, not exactly. I would feed on the pause between syllables in the word the place is still coining. When I pressed my wide, soft lips against a vein of warm stone, I would draw out not vibration but potential: the sense that motion is possible even when nothing is moving. My shell would brighten only along the edges, like a window that knows it is a window but wants to learn how to be a mirror.

Then I would be the Varnisher’s apprentice, tall and slender, but only when I stood up straight. My arms would be filaments tipped with sponges that never dry, and wherever I brushed, dust would become a skin that remembers being touched. I would not sing the frequencies that help mineral decide what kind of stone it wants to be; I would hum the questions that help stone decide it is allowed to ask. My song would be a polite interruption, the kind that makes silence feel like a room with more chairs.

Mostly, though, I would be the Sentry’s shadow learning how to cast itself. My form would change by subtraction: a hinge removed here, a strut softened there, until I fit the ratio of green to gray without ever measuring it. My eyes would glow with concentrated patience, not to watch but to let watching happen. At night I would project faint maps onto the undersides of theoretical clouds, maps that show where warmth has been and where it might go, but never where it must be.

The plants would call me a tendril that forgot which side was root and which was invitation. I would wind through industrial stalks, negotiating with the Hummers for temporary softness, offering stories about pressure that I had borrowed from the machines. My leaves would catch refined light and convert it into color that exists only as texture, a spectrum you feel in your teeth. My stalks would sway in rhythm with the turbines, keeping time without ever claiming to own it.

Smaller things would treat me as seams. The Threaders would braid my cracks shut with filaments of spun electricity, moving like slow embroidery across my surface, turning my scars into seams that look deliberate. The Collectors would drift just above me, gathering loose warmth in bellies of folded foil and releasing it in small, timed bursts that tell the Varnishers where to work next. The Echo-Foxes would borrow my shape for a moment—shoulders like mine, gait like mine—then return it improved, with fewer sharp edges and a softer way of holding stillness.

And all the while I would be aware of the fauna that have not arrived, the absent footsteps that keep the dust soft and the missing calls that keep the hum from becoming lonely. I would carry that negative space inside me like a pocket that is always empty by design, a pocket that makes room for hospitality. When I rest, I would not sleep. I would recalibrate, aligning myself with the rhythm of electrified veins and the slow breathing of pumps, until the difference between guest and resident blurs into a single, warm note.

Eventually I would rise and walk, not in order to arrive but to keep the space remembering how to be empty. The Varnishers would brush my shadow and set it aside to cure. The Sentries would adjust their hinges to mark the new ratio of presence to absence. The Hummers would graze the stress of my passing and hum it into something that fits in the hollows like stones that have decided to be comfortable.

And the place would go on building itself, pretending it has always been this way, thriving anyway—while I, neither animal nor machine but something in between, would go on learning how to be a guest who is learning how to be a resident, warm to the touch, furred with dust, humming with a pressure that is entirely my own but belongs to the place as well.
