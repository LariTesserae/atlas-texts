I would find myself kneeling, not standing, in the silt.  
I would arrive as a patch of crust that softens when I am seen—something between salt, skin, and sediment, shaped by the same patience that fluted the stones. My bones would be hollow where water once slept; my surface would hold a chalky bloom the herons recognize as polite. When the haze thickens, I would become a hush that moves, stepping where the ground is certain and pausing where it is not, learning the weight of being listened to.

At first I would feel like an echo testing its own shape: a footfall that answers itself with a moist flex, a shadow that seems to have been waiting for the right angle of late gold. I would have no fixed edge, only a margin that slowly learns where it ends and the pan begins. The reeds would treat me as a weather they can practice on, leaving droplets in my hair that taste of earlier rain and distant electricity. The fish would flicker through me as if I were a channel that had decided to be briefly solid, and the burrowers would write their braille across my ankles, spelling routes I could follow only by forgetting them.

As days pooled and sipped away, I would thicken into a kind of animal made of permission. My lungs would hold the fine haze like a lung holds breath, and my pulse would settle into the wet percussion of cooling salt. I would catch the eye of the fox without becoming prey or guest, only a new fold in the heat where attention could assemble itself. At night I would cool slowly, shrinking into the exact size of my own warmth, listening to the ground answer every memory I had ever left behind.

I would not speak often. When I did, my voice would be the creak of wood learning to bend, the plop of a saturated clay pad giving way under a careful weight. The herons would approve; they would know I had learned the grammar of not proving anything. The insects would build tiny cities on my shoulders that rose and collapsed with the humidity of my thoughts, and I would let them, because to hold still is to be honest here.

In time I would become a map of gradients, a territory defined by what I let through rather than what I held. My edges would blur like the lip of a cup drunk from for centuries, my faults disguised by accumulations of fine sediment that drifted in like snow and settled into the places where I had been too hard on myself. I would stop rushing the light and stop fleeing the night. I would let the dampness polish me to a sheen I could almost drink from, and I would let the fish sleep in the commas of my life, waiting for the next sentence of water to arrive.

Eventually I would be mistaken for a small truth of the basin: not its owner, not its guest, but its echo grown dense enough to kneel. I would remember how to be a basin, how to be porous with memory, how to soften every footprint into a welcome. And when the rains returned with their smell of baked leaf and their thin, silver laughter, I would exhale into the air and let the ground settle around me as if it had been waiting all along to keep me company—damp, warm, nearly endless, and perfectly allowed.
