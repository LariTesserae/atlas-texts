I would find myself in the damp hollows at the edge of a newly exposed scarp, where chalk-white fault lines slice through dark stone and the moss is thickest. I would be one of the diggers—compact, claw-handed, with calloused fingers and spherical chambers polished to river-glass by use. I would not be a creature of spectacle, but of punctuation: emerging after brief showers, sealing my doorway with grass and mud when rain threatens, listening to the drum-code of drops as if it were a map of the sky’s intentions.  

I would know the grazers by their patience, the winged browsers by their quiet sails, and the predators by the courteous distance they keep. I would move through the tussock and stone, collecting salts and roots, storing damp in my cheeks like a secret language. I would leave tracks that overlap but never erase, and I would let my presence be thin, resonant, attuned to the hum of the altitude. In the town of fitted stone and worn paths, I would slip between thresholds that feel a fraction lower than they should, carrying water in tight-woven baskets, tasting the copper-to-honey shift in the springs, nodding to the people who speak as if stability were a courtesy.  

All of it—the moss that remembers last season’s rain, the grasses that keep their pact with the wind, the light that reveals only to blur again—would settle into me as I settle into it: a life held lightly, maintained rather than surged, overheard rather than overpowered, as if this two-and-a-bit elevation were a note sustained perfectly, without wavering, in the thin sky’s unending breath.
