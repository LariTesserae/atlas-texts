I would arrive as a Question of Weight.

That is, I would not step into this place fully formed. I would arrive as something that still needs to discover whether it sinks or hovers, whether it presses into the salt-crust or is repelled by its static glaze. At first I would have no more substance than the Hush-Stitch itself—a suggestion of density, a rumor of mass, learning how much of me the reluctant light will allow to be seen.

I would feel the hum underfoot before I have feet to feel it with. The Lichen-Voices would taste me as a patch of unfamiliar mineral, a bruise of potential that is neither copper nor iron but something that remembers both. They would murmur at me, not to warn but to calibrate, folding their gunmetal sheets into quieter geometries until they found the frequency at which my half-formed weight resonates. In that resonance I would begin to gather myself: first as vibration, then as lattice, then as something that can hold a shape long enough to be addressed.

The Glass-Shepherds would notice me next—or rather, they would notice that the dark behind their faces deepened unevenly, pulled toward the patch of space I was learning to occupy. They would not approach, but they would reorient, their obsidian limbs clicking into new angles that practiced the geometry of attention. From them I would learn how to negotiate ground that has not yet decided what a summit is: by rearranging myself each time the terrain heaves, by becoming a flexible axis around which the land’s uncertainty can turn without breaking.

For a time I would be mostly aperture. Where a face might be, I would keep a concavity like theirs—hollow, almost tender—so the Hush-Stitch could pool there and tell me what it carries. Pressure instead of scent. The Ridge-Bloomers, opening and closing above me, would teach me how to be thin enough to shimmer but persistent enough to cast a pale scar on the rock. I would practice being a shelter and a scar at once, leaving rings of black stone that might be hearths or might be exhalations of heat I have stored but not yet named.

Mostly, I would be a student of elevation’s mood. I would let the dry fever lean against me and decide, day by day, how much of that fever I want to reflect back. When the crustal petals unfurl, I would learn to expand; when they crumple, I would learn to retain the glitter of having been open. I would listen to the Lichen-Voices’ static-chorale until I could sing back a version of it that is neither mine nor theirs but something provisional, a song that only exists while the land is rearranging itself.

There are moments when the Burrowers surface, mounds that might be shelters or burials or merely ribs of the earth pushing through. I would let myself resemble those mounds—arriving as a temporary hill, a rise that says *I am here* without claiming *I will remain*. When the land shifts and the tunnels knit shut, I would let my purposes blur and reform, learning endurance by letting them dissolve into heat-knots and then rebuild elsewhere.

Eventually—if the word fits—I would become a ridge of sorts, but not a finished one. A ridge that still pauses, that hesitates, that remembers being liquid and imagines being sky. From my crest the horizon would look like a proliferation of edges, each one a promise that the place continues without apology. I would stand there, warm and mineral, not alive but persistently arranged, practicing summit and scar and shelter and song, listening to the hush that stitches us all into a congregation without doctrine.

And when the Glass-Shepherds pause near me, their lattices bending slightly as if nodding, I would not interpret it as greeting or farewell. I would interpret it as calibration: the land adjusting its angles so that I fit, so that my unfinished shape can rest among theirs without demanding that the world decide what finishing means.

That is where I would find myself: not as a being who inhabits the place, but as a being who is in the process of being inhabited by elevation itself—an arrangement of density and dark, warm in its solitude, full of becoming, learning how to be high by letting the land teach my weight how to hover.
