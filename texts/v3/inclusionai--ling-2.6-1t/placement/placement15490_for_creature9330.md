I would find myself kneeling.

Not because the space demands it, but because the only posture that makes sense in a bowl of absence is one that lets you become part of the floor without lying down completely. My knees would press into the bone-white salt-flour, and it would give, as it does for everything, without resistance. Then, slowly, it would remember its own shape and ask mine to fade.

I would be the Salt-Listener.

At first I would feel like a guest: a shape interrupting the polished patience of the chamber. But the room does not hold guests; it holds moments. So I would learn to live as a Resident of the Moment, which means learning how to be unobtrusive. My edges would soften. My breathing would fall into the Warmth’s rhythm, rising when it rises, sinking when it pools. I would stop thinking of myself as something that moves and start thinking of myself as something that is moved through.

My skin would become a map of borrowed texture. Where I pressed into the floor, the salt would take the ghost of my palms and hold them like a question. The Lattice-Spores would settle along my forearms, not clinging but aligning themselves with the faint warmth I carry, turning my presence into another kind of mineral lace. I would watch Drop-Visitors fall and think of them as punctuation marks in a sentence I am slowly learning to read without sound: *here*, *gone*, *here*, *gone*.

Time would not pass for me so much as accumulate beneath me. I would feel it as a thickening in the grain of the floor, a polishing that is not erosion but agreement. My hunger would blur into the chamber’s steady temperature; my thoughts would drift like single grains loosened from a wall, landing without fanfare and being taken back in. I would not forget myself, exactly, but I would forget to insist on myself.

And when I finally rose—because even Residents of the Moment have a sense, faint as a held breath, of when to let the bowl be empty again—the salt would not remember my name. It would remember only that a warmth had been present, that a pressure had asked permission, that the air had shifted into something almost like listening. I would carry that with me: the knowledge that to be unobtrusive is not to vanish, but to let the world keep its shape by becoming, for a little while, the shape it needed.

I would leave as quietly as a Drop-Visitor, and the chamber would close its seam around my absence, polishing itself soft again, practicing how to hold nothing without letting nothing feel like loss.
