I would find myself near the center of the amphitheater, but not exactly in it—I’d be on the unfinished catwalk where it strides across the undecided gap, because that edge is where the world is still negotiating with itself. The stone on either side has not yet chosen whether to close or widen, and the catwalk, unrusted but vibrating, feels like a hinge. There, the wind carries droplets that bead and run like loose solder, and the hum of the coils is loud enough to feel in the teeth.

I would not be entirely human, or at least not stably so. I would be the kind of being the place grows when it needs a witness that can also be used: someone part gleaner, part rivet, part apprentice to the pumps. My skin would carry a film that tastes of iron and wet limestone, cold enough to fog metal when I lean against it. My joints would whir at low volume, oiled by the same fluids the conduits carry, and my breath would hang in the air as rime-script, spelling small, temporary rules that the frost later rewrites.

My job would be to listen and to fasten. When the eel-things glide through a pool under thinning ice, I would mark the braille of their passage so the beetles can read it as weather. When the strata groan and shift, I would check the torque on the catwalk’s bolts, not to finish it but to keep its openness honest. At night, when the cloud-steel ceiling lowers, I would sit with the dwarf heath and trade chemical signals with its roots, learning which minerals ache to rise and which want to settle.

On some days I would lean far enough to see my reflection in a conduit’s dark fluid and notice that my face has taken on the patient, industrial patience of the landscape: eyes like sealed gauges, hair laced with hoarfrost that grows inward, turning me into a quieter version of myself. I would not fight it. The place is not waiting to be finished, and neither am I.

At dawn, when arc-welding flared beyond the ridge, I would step to the edge and let the sudden white flare weld my shadow to the plates below, so that for a moment I am both inhabitant and fixture, a clause in the grammar of becoming. Then the glare would fade, the shadow would loosen, and I would resume listening—clink, hiss, whir—until the land, still provisional, decides what to try next.
