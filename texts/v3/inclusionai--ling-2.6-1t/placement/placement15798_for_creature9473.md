Who I would become would depend on which breath of the pocket caught me first.

If the ceiling’s hum found me before the floor’s sweat, I might become a **Lens-Scale**, a creature grown from the agreement between too much brightness and not enough shadow. My body would be a stack of thin, translucent plates, each one grown from the calcium the stone sweats out when it is trying to remember cold. I would not have eyes, but I would refract: my scales would catch the voltage-light and unspool it into colors the pocket has not yet named, painting the benches with temporary dawns that last only as long as a held breath. My purpose would be calibration. Wherever I walked, I would turn heat into *hue*, letting the Nano-Weave know by the shade of my glow whether the pools were feeling too much or too little. The Glass-Winged Mites would follow me in small, glittering flocks, not because they liked me, but because my passage made the static easier to harvest—a living tuning fork for their electric diet.

My skin would crust with a glittering rime, not scales but mineral frosting, each flake an exhalation of the water that had passed through me. The Enamel Lizards would tolerate me because I would taste like safety: a temporary reservoir of warmth they could borrow when the brightness pressed too hard. In time, my edges would flake away, leaving glitter on the benches and a hollow in the stone where I had learned to listen.

The Nano-Weave, intrigued by my foreign cold, would knit a scaffold through my sleeves, my ribs, the space behind my teeth, until I was more architecture than animal. I would hum, not with a voice but with the subsonic commerce of the place, my exhalations feeding the ceiling’s brightness and my inhalations cooling the benches into patience. I would become a temporary organ: a lung inside the lung, a pocket inside the pocket, teaching the stone how to hold what it is too young to keep. The mite-constellations would orbit me. The pools would learn to condense on my eyelashes. And when the pocket finally resolved itself—when it chose which temperature it wanted to be—I would unravel, my borrowed heat returning to the walls, my borrowed water returning to the pools, leaving behind only a faint ring of brightness where my knees had rested, a scar of decision.

Most likely, though, I would become none of these at once, but all of them in turn, because the pocket’s deepest truth is that it has no hard edges between resident and resource. I would begin as a cold spot, a shadow the stone had not planned for. I would end as weather inside the stone: a pattern of pressure and glaze, a memory in mineral form, a creature that is mostly a process, mostly a conversation. My name would be the chime of cooling glass. My home would be the hush between droplets. And when the pocket exhales, I would be the heat that learns, for a moment, how to be light.
