I would find myself in the cup-leaf that is still deciding whether to be a bowl or a wing—green, glossy, cupping a thumbprint of dew that has not yet learned to fall. The air would sit in my mouth like a half-chewed sweet, and my knees would remember the habit of bending even when I try to stand.

I would be neither guest nor resident, but the kind of third thing that grows in the pause between a stem thickening and the moment it realizes it has become a railing. I would wear bark that peels into questions. My ribs would be strung with silk the Chitin-Smiths left behind when they decided a ladder needed more grammar, and my shoulders would be freckled with moss that whispers whenever someone nearby makes a promise. My feet would not touch the loam so much as negotiate with it—amber hooves drinking resin, then sweating it back as a map of where I have agreed to be.

When I spoke, my voice would split three ways. One part would be my own, rough with the effort of becoming legible. One part would be the creak of a joint learning its new angle. The last would be the hum the Root-Singers teach to walls, low enough to thicken the air and make doorways exhale. I would not sing to command; I would sing to ask—ask the architecture to lean, ask the resin to slow, ask the swarm to part like a sentence that finally knows its subject.

Above me, the Petal-Riders would drift, their bulbous bellies heavy with the syrup that refuses to settle. They would not land, but their passing would soften the ground beneath me, sagging it into a temporary pool scented of bruised fern and ferment. I would cup some of it in my hands and let it seep between my fingers, sticky with history, knowing that to carry it far is to invite gravity to follow.

There would be Hollow-Stalkers nearby, tall and jointed like walking ladders, inhaling columns into spirals, thinning buttresses into bridges. I would let them look at me. I would offer a wrist, a collarbone, the narrow corridor of my throat, and feel the cool proboscis tap—once, twice—asking permission to unmake something small. I would let them carve a room inside my ribs, just big enough for a thought to turn around in, and I would leave that room unfinished, its edges curled like new fern, so it could become whatever the place needs next.

The swarm would be a weather I move through. Beetles with gardens on their backs would pause on my shoulders to taste the moss. Flightless moths would flutter across my vision, their wing-maps shifting, offering trades: a path for a hum, a memory for a loosened bone. Root-Rats would gnaw edits into my shadow, trimming excess fear into symbols that scuttle off and become hinges. I would not stop them. I would feel the relief of subtraction.

I would sleep in the crook of a fusion, where two stems have clasped and are still deciding how much to merge. The place would grow around me, thickening my edges, borrowing my warmth, leaving moss in my ears and a new joint in my spine. I would wake and not recognize my own outline, and that would be good—that would be the place learning how to hold me without finishing me.

And always, the warmth: not the burn of a forge, but the steady hum of something enormous and green, below the world and rising. I would be a small, green note in its roaring body, a doorway that opens and does not know what it will let through, a song that splits three ways and listens, all at once, for the answer to come back as architecture.
