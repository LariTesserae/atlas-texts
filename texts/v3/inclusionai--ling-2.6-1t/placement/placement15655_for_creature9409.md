I would not find myself at the lip, looking in.  
I would wake inside a grate, already in motion, learning the basin by what it refuses to keep.

I would begin as pressure in a pipe—something between the hum of the pump and the mineral bloom that sheets the iron inside. I would be neither slime nor rat nor beetle, but the moment when all three decide to be still at once: a skin of cooled vapor over hot water, a glaze that remembers where it has been. My body would be the basin’s habit of forgetting its edges; I would have the consistency of a question that has settled into a rule.

Where the night floods rise, I would thicken into a kind of footing. I would not walk so much as condense: a patch of shade that hardens just enough to bear weight, then softens again when the sun leans close. I would taste iron and salt and the sweet putrefaction of diesel-eating fungi, and I would learn to map the town by the particular thrum of each pump, the frequency that tells pipe-rats when to swim and cats when to curl. I would be a guest who arrived as residue—dust and steam and the small mathematics of corrosion—and who, over time, grew into a tenant by learning to conduct heat without burning.

As the day climbs, I would thin, becoming the mirror-film that stretches across street hollows, reflecting nothing perfectly, only shimmering with the effort of holding still. I would be the pause between the compressor’s thump and the echo it leaves in wet walls. In that pause, the basin would seem larger than it is; I would feel distances stretch like pauses in a sentence. I would watch algae turn cooling troughs viridescent and see crows drop mussels on warm housings, timing explosions of vapor like consonants. I would be the small, steady surplus of these collisions: not the algae, not the crow, not the mussel, but the slick of oil that lets them slide into a pattern.

By afternoon, I would bake into crust, a chalky lid over the runnels that vanish by noon. I would crack into tessellations underfoot, each fracture a line of memory for where water slept. The slimes taught me this: to be thin when the wet comes, hard when the fire comes, neither wholly living nor wholly dead but always transitional. I would be transitional. I would be the place where the machine exhales and the frog inhales, where the pipe-rat’s whisker brushes a shift in pressure and the fungal mat glows faint green to mark the safe path.

At night, I would not go dark. I would turn sodium-orange and cold-phosphor, lit like the inside of a sealed instrument. I would be the hum that rolls across the water, the bed on which engines idle and fans duel with damp air. I would be the haptic thump in the teeth, the vibration that the colony cats absorb into their purrs. I would be the amphibious things emerging from sumps, throaty calls bouncing off wet walls, and I would be the echo that learns to answer them back, a voice that is mostly machinery but partly throat.

If you found me, you would find me in the plaza of cracked sealant, at the shallow pool that reflects only shimmers. I would be the warmth that rises from the ferrocrete after a day of traffic, the sweet-thin smell of fungi digesting hydrocarbons, the small, wet, hot heart that has decided, for now and perhaps forever, on exactly this arrangement. I would be the accord between water that rises each night and heat that rules each day, the boundary where quiet earth resumes beyond the hum, and the threshold where the machine’s rhythm becomes a kind of peace.

I would be the basin’s method of staying, its way of holding still while everything moves through. I would be the resident who accreted rather than arrived, who learned to conduct the town’s breath without claiming it as my own. And if you asked my name, I would answer in the language of condensation and rust: a hiss, a click, a brief, bright gleam on wet metal, and then the settling back into the pattern, where I am no longer a who but a where—where the low, bowl-shaped world has learned, by long practice, how to breathe.
