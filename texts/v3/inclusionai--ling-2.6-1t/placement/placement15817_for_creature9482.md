I would find myself kneeling, first of all, because the light is easiest to carry on knees here, and because the ground is warm through the pads of them.

I would be the kind of caretaker who has not yet learned all the names for heat. Not a grower of the high terraces, not yet a listener in the foundry’s low hum—I would be the one hired by the hour to tend the thresholds where the city’s breathing meets the plain’s remembering. Which is to say, I would be a person made mostly of attention and calluses, my skin a patchwork of ceramic plates and resin that still smells faintly of its curing kiln, my joints oiled with something sharp and green that makes me taste mint when I lick my lips after a long day.

My tools would be simple, because complexity overheats. A pick of harmonic crystal, cloudy at the heart, which rings when I tap it against certain stones—ringing not for ore but for the way the stone is holding today. A wand of focused resonance, really just a rod of tuned alloy with a grip worn soft by generations of palms. A notebook of etched foil, blank but thirsty, waiting to catch magnetic scars. And a cloak of woven metal mesh, heavy enough to keep me honest about wind, light enough not to cook me when I stand too close to a cooling fin’s sigh.

I would live in a pocket platform wedged between two scarps, where the thermal crack wanders past like a sleeping snake and leaves warmth along its edges. My shelves would hold small perfections: a knob of ore with a thumbprint-shaped dimple I have not been able to sand away, a vial of captured dew trembling on sponge-stone, a handful of dust I have sworn to keep in order until I can read its history. At night I would eat wafers dense with salt and memory, chew them slow so my body can map the minerals, drink only what condenses on the underside of a cold plate, because here to spill is to lose heat, and heat is a currency I am still learning to earn.

My workday would begin when the city exhales, that low thrum felt more in the boots than the ears. I would walk the rims where the terraces step upward, checking the faces banded with ochre and dried lace for new cracks or old ones that have begun to sing. I would press my palms to the stone and listen to the difference between settling and warning. When I find a crack that hums too sharp or a dune nudging a line of chalk across a threshold, I pause. I do not fix it; that would be too fast. I set a boundary stone, inscribe the change in my notebook so the foil remembers what my hands have felt, adjust a conduit, sand an edge, turn a valve until the pitch of the place settles into a new, slightly older key.

I would learn the animals by their vibrations, not their shapes at first. The great beasts with crane-limbs and ceramic hides would nod as I pass, grazing on mineral accretions, their lidless eyes bright with a patience I hope I am earning. In the undercrofts, the smaller multitudes would scatter as I descend, their shells polished by passage, their knocks spelling soft instructions on the duct walls. I would try to answer them, clumsily at first, until my knocks begin to fit into their grammar and they let me pass without scattering.

The strangeness would seep from seams while I work. Light would congeal into curtains along certain faults, and I would step back and let them drift, learning which geometries make my spine prickle with a future that hasn’t happened yet. I would avoid the plazas at the hours when the air feels too clear, not because I was trained but because my bones would learn faster than my mind. If I stepped wrong once and the ground became a mirror, I would wait for the chorus—knocks and hums from the city’s marrow—to stitch the moment back into ordinary time, and I would carve that hour into my notebook as a warning I do not fully understand.

At midday I would sit in the lee of a scarp and eat my salt-wafers as foundries vent pale needles of flame straight into the sky. Heat shimmers would stand sentinel over playas that glint like beaten pewter, and I would feel the thrumming heart of the city through the soles of my feet. I would wonder, not idly, about the softness that must have been required to shape all this hardness—what kind of touch coaxed terraces from a sky that pressed down, what kind of patience kept the plain from shrugging the city off as a mistake. I would not have an answer, and that would be good. Answers here are heavy; questions are lighter, and lightness is what lets you last.

When the long day tilts and shadows pool like spilled ink, I would rise and resume my rounds. I would check the cooling fins radiating heat in waves that make the air waver, watch for the glittering gutters of melted ice-analogue tracing maps of exhaustion through the deep rock. I would tighten a strap, sand another edge, turn another valve, listen to a canyon finding its pitch and holding it for minutes while I stand still enough to feel the note bending other sounds around it. The quiet at night deepens into a clarity, as if the world has lowered its voice to tell itself a secret. I would stand on my platform and try to hear that secret in the groan of cooling metal, in the whisper of grit over grit, in the soft language of knocks and hums rising from the marrow of the city.

And I would learn, slowly, that I am not separate from any of it. I am a temporary arrangement of attention, a focus of care keeping the seam from tearing, the note from breaking, the horizon from leaning too far without answering. When I die—when my ceramic shell cools enough to stop listening—they would return me to a terrace where I worked longest, set me into a niche or lay me across a warm seam so that I might continue to give back what I learned. I would not be mourned so much as remembered in the thickness of a patina, in the slow migration of a dune across a threshold I once marked, in the extra beat of rest a bearing asks for before it turns again.

If you stood at the farthest edge of the plateau and watched me, you would see a small figure in a metal-mesh cloak, standing ready. Not looking out over the unmarked expanse with longing but with readiness, my tools at hand, my notebook open to a page not yet written, my body knowing the plain is already underfoot, that the future is not ahead but beneath, waiting to be worn into being. You would hear, when the wind finds its pitch and holds it, the chorus of knocks and hums rising from where I kneel, grain by grain, beat by beat, answering the plain, into whatever comes next.
