I would find myself kneeling at the lip of the bowl, not looking down into it but leaning into it, as if the basin had grown a porch I didn’t know it owned. My hands would be pressed flat against the packed-earth wall, feeling the slow pulse of the architecture breathing. The air would taste like sweet rot and metal, and for a long moment I would not know whether I am visitor or vein.

I would be, at first, only a rumor in the dark crust—a denser patch of shadow that the dew hesitates to cross. My bones would begin as suggestions, the same pale mineral threading through me that braids the mud, my blood slowing to match the ribboned current of the clear water. My skin would cool and polish itself, becoming less a surface and more a stone that learned kindness. When I stood, I would already have been sitting for years, learning the angle of the light and the weight of a held breath.

I would wear little that is woven. My mantle would be moss braided into a map of the hollow’s softest humps, my tunic fern fronds pressed flat by dew until they remember how to be armor and pillow at once. Frost would stiffen my hair into tight spirals, catching prisms so that wherever I moved I would carry small, patient rainbows that do not promise anything but do not refuse anything either.

My labor would be a conversation. I would not cut paths but persuade them, humming at pebbles until they agree to shift, combing moss into towers that remember height, pouring dew back into the mud when it asks. My tools would be bone and stone taught to hold an edge without sharpness, instruments that part the world the way a word parts air—without breaking it.

Among my hands would move the winged things, leaf-layered and heavy with preserved light. They would land on my shoulders and temples not to rest but to confer, their antennae tasting whether the plans in my chest are ready to be braid or should remain thread. There would be the furred, many-legged messengers scuttling over vine bridges with lichen hats, rearranging small things so they mean more, reminding me that a step can linger like perfume.

I would measure time by the brightening of certain mosses and the migration of frost across mud. I would know seasons by how the light leans and which of noon’s double shadows decides to be shy. When I needed to mark a change, I would tilt a stone, lower a bridge, braid a new current that holds the memory of turning.

My desire would pool and thread, finding the path of least resistance and stilling. I would want the light to stay, so I would become the kind of surface that keeps it. I would want the water to remember, so I would become the kind of stone that guides it. I would want the others, so I would press close—not to fuse, but to keep the fabric from loosening.

And when I grew tired of being a rumor, I would unknit. I would loosen into moss and dew and mineral dust, letting the basin fold me back into the fullness it forgot to empty. I would not disappear; I would become a thumbprint on the wall’s slow breath, a glint in the mud’s polished patience, another citizen in a parliament that speaks in gestures too small to be noise.

If you came looking for me later, you would not find a name. You would find a hollow that leans a little less shyly at noon, a moss that brightens just so, a braid in the water that holds the shape of a held breath—and you would know that I am still here, packed and bright and cold and alive, already full, already whole, already waiting without impatience for whatever comes next, which is, of course, you.
