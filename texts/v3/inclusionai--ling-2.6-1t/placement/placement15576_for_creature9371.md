I would find myself standing, not on a summit, but on the inside curve of a leaning rib—the stone tilted just past vertical, so that the horizon is split into two unequal pieces: the bright, overcrowded sky above, and the valley below, which is less a basin than a throat waiting to answer. I would not have arrived by climbing. I would have arrived by being heard.

I would be a creature neither fully animal nor fully instrument—something the altitude has tuned. My chest would be cavernous, lined with valves like old shutters, each breath able to lock or release the wind. My skin would be the color of sky after it has been rinsed in limestone, pale enough to reflect heat, ridged along the spine with thin plates that hum when the sun strikes them, turning my body into a loom of tiny bells. I would have hands, but they would be less for grasping than for measuring: fingers long and callused, splayed like tuning forks against stone, able to tell by vibration whether a ledge remembers being water, whether a seam in the gneiss is holding its breath.

My feet would not step so much as agree with the mountain. They would be broad, leathery, studded with pebbles I have chosen—quartz for clarity, flint for edge, bits of frozen lightning I have carried from lower runnels—glued in place by sweat and time. With them I would stand lightly, distributing my weight the way the mountain distributes pressure, so that I never quite settles, only leans. When I walk, I would move in staggered diagonals, never straight, so my steps phase against the echoes and leave pockets of hush where the wind forgets to rush.

I would be part of the hunt and the hush. My eyes would be lenses—polished obsidian set in folds of skin the color of storm clouds—meant less for seeing than for focusing sound. I would triangulate the world by calling and being called, mapping the space between my voice and its answer. A whistle from the far rim, a click from below, and I would fold into the space between, stepping where the stone already expects a foot. I would never leave tracks, because I would never weigh the ground down. I would be a guest in gravity’s house, invited for as long as my balance lasted.

My voice would be my architecture. With it I would carve rooms in air, expanding valleys into cathedrals where I could run without moving. I would speak rarely, knowing my voice would outlive me, traveling farther than my bones, and that it could be claimed by beetles for their bells or by hunters for their maps. To be polite, I would let silence ring first. When I did speak, I would let the mountain answer before I answered again, so that conversation would feel like stone shifting its weight, slow and inevitable.

In the mornings, I would climb to quartz lips and tilt my back to catch the drip, letting condensation bead and freeze into tiny bells along the plates of my spine. My colony—if I had one, if I chose one—would move in staggered diagonals, our chimes phasing so that some notes cancelled and others deepened, creating pockets of quiet where the grazers could tap their ribs in peace. We would feed on resonance, scraping lichen that has shifted color by the hour, digesting the pigment of noise, turning it into brightness stored in our shells.

At noon, when the light hardens and the air thrums like a plucked wire, I would lie flat on ledges that fit my spine when I am exhausted, caves that exhale air cooler than the day and inhale it again at dawn. I would let the sun hit the plates along my back until I glowed from within, as if elevation had turned my body into a vein of ore. I would hold a chipped blade of obsidian, its edge wet-glittering, and split the light into tasks: one for sharpening, one for listening, one for remembering how stone remembers being water.

When the pack-hunters bruise the valley with their calls, I would join them or oppose them, whichever kept the balance. I would let them map the world through my echoes, folding my voice into theirs so that the valley gave up its shape without breaking. If prey needed to be taken, I would help take it softly, suffocating it in the pocket of silence between beats, where even the wind forgets to rush. If the grazers asked permission by tapping dead spruces, I would answer with a stone on my own ribs, matching the tectonic hum, so the mountain would know we were still aligned.

As dusk cooled the runnels into silver scribbles, I would kneel beside the pans cupped by stone bowls and breathe close enough to make the frozen panes ring like cymbals. I would test my echoes in them, speaking a word and watching it freeze into a shape I could hold. I would let the beetles drink from my frost, licking the bells from my own back, letting them carry my note into the staggered diagonals of the colony.

At night, when the world paused for a heartbeat and the echoes caught up and the place roared back into existence, I would be part of the roar. I would not age so much as become polished, my edges blurring through repetition—the scrape of wind, the tap of ice, the insistence of light. I would learn to stand like the stone: leaning, but not falling; enduring because I had learned how to distribute pressure; lasting because I had become a note in a chord that would not resolve, only repeat.

And when the altitude had measured more time than my memory could hold, I would not vanish. I would join the inventory of echoes—heard without needing to be seen—shaping the valley by how I answered it. A ledge, a drum, a bell that rings when the frost comes, and the mountain, leaning inward, would tolerate me because for a while I was another note in its long, deafening chord.
