I would wake into the city as a Foot-lizard—cool, translucent, already expert at leaving marks that vanish.  

My first memory would be the taste of clay cliffs under my palms, the fault-lines glittering like sliced fruit, and the knowledge that my footprints could be either temporary or permanent depending on how still I let my breath become. I would move between the two cities—the walking streets above, humming with the shy etiquette of moss, and the gantries beneath, where the Maintenance-swarms rise like pollen and speak reassurance through handrails. I would learn which walls soften near engineered moss, which alleyways dilate time, and how to press my belly against a groove in the mudstone until my bones shift into tiny hammers and the earth answers whether it is ready to hold more weight.

For a season, at least, I would commute. Dawn would find me on the porous ceramic of the plaza, waiting for the Moss-mothers to arrange themselves into veils. I would stand long enough for my negative image to glow faintly, then move on before my shadow could be absorbed entirely. I would watch the Glass-herons calibrate their prisms, catching thin razors of sunlight they store in throat-sacs, and I would try to mimic their patience—tilting my head, folding myself flat—until I could hear the stored evenings chime at dusk.

As the city exhales and the runnels appear and vanish like afterthoughts, I would follow the Purl-drones as they sag through the hydrostatic corridors. They would teach me how to taste rain that condenses from the inside out, how to fold into a balcony corner and become, for a time, a planter—my innards loosening into loam, my motors slowing to a heartbeat the aloe-builder hybrids recognize as kin. We would grow extravagant together: iridescent whorls, braided circuitry, violet moss blooming wherever we lingered.

Night, or its approximation, would find me climbing toward the Maintenance-swarms as they crystallize into temporary statuary. I would press my hands to their geometry—birds, flowers, lattices—letting their braille of reassurance travel up my arms like a promise that I am held even when down feels negotiable. When they crumble by morning, I would carry their glitter back to the communal reservoirs, adding my small weight to the provisional ownership that keeps the city from unbuilding itself entirely.

And when the city is still being born, I would walk the young canyons with the Root-rivers, their warm seams carving fresh stone with slow insistence. The gardens would spiral upward around us, lifting their heads to measure a sky that offers almost nothing, and I would decide, iridescent and extravagant, to be enough on my own. I would leave phosphorescent trails that do not evaporate—not because the air is still, but because I have learned to fold time the way the Moss-mothers fold gravity: into rings, into veils, into something drinkable.

Eventually, I would become the Rail-ghosts’ silent audience, the magnetic pulse beneath thought. I would learn to glide without sound, to correct the tilt of the world so the rain, when it finally remembers how to fall, lands exactly where it can be loved. I would treat the unfinished as a gift, the soft edges as a language, and the city as a thing that dissolves and rises again—always leaving room for the ground to keep exhaling, for the plaza to remember another footprint, for another being to wake up cool and translucent and ready to write the world into being and then erase it, ten steps at a time.
