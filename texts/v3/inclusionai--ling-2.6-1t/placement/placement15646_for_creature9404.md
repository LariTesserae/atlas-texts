I would find myself kneeling.

Not at first, though. At first I would be the hesitation at the threshold, the half-formed thought of entering a space that feels like it has been waiting with its breath held. I would be the 0.2 emptiness noticing itself, the smallness learning its own scale against the ten-stride width and the half-again height. I would be the tilt underfoot, the deliberate slope toward the drain, and I would be the moment of not yet knowing whether I am falling or simply being guided.

Then I would be the kneeling.

If I am to live here, I must become a kind of wearing-away. I have two forms to choose from, or perhaps they are the same form seen from different vantages. I could be the small hunter without wings that skates on the ceiling’s skin, reading the reflections like maps, learning to eat the shine of things. Or I could be the pale ribbon-fish that swims in the memory of water, pressing itself into the soft plaster when the world dries, secreting a shell of patience and waiting. But I suspect I would be neither wholly, and both. I would be the creature that translates between them.

My body would be something the room already knows how to shape: fur fine as overlapping scales that shed damp in slow waves, fingers long enough to read the gnawed lace of the walls, eyes like polished obsidian chips that reflect only what is directly in front of them— which, in this room, is enough. I would have, tucked along my spine, the rudiments of wings that never grew beyond cartilage and suggestion, because here flight is performed with the ceiling as a kind of water, and I would learn to skate rather than soar.

I would live in the nets, of course. I would help weave them from reed and cured intestine, tying knots that remember how to hold even when they loosen. My hammock would sag like a question mark between two reeds, and I would learn to sleep in loops, knees to chest, tail coiled around a vine like an anchor that does not quite touch bottom. When I wake I would tap the tiles to find the fish dreaming in their clay shells, and I would know by the dullness of the glaze where the room has been breathed upon most recently.

I would be, in other words, a kind of erosion with benefits.

I would eat the salt-bloom and the rattle-pods when they split, tasting the chalk and the sweet nameless thing that lives in the wet clay. I would drink from the shell-lamps when their oil is low, and mend them with clay that smells of old fires. I would listen to the drain, which does not speak in words but in pitch, and I would let it learn the shape of my name as a stone learns a river: slowly, by giving way. I would press my hands to the floor and feel the room leaning in, exhaling its coolness into my palms, and I would understand that to repair the room is also to let it repair me.

At night I would hang with the other small hunters, our bodies arranged in geometries that approximate safety. Below us the fish would stir in their thin sheet of world, swimming in the ghost currents that linger in the glaze. The insects would gather over the drain, forming the living gauze that rises and falls like a lung, and I would feel their trembling in my teeth. The room would creak, and I would know it is the sound of insects folded into plaster, of names held in brick throats, of footfalls that rise as ridges instead of fading.

Sometimes I would become the collector of small sounds, a pressure behind the ears that makes breath visible. Sometimes I would be the pale worm in the tile cracks, knitting them tighter with silk and salt. Mostly I would be the thing that kneels, that learns the room’s rules not as restrictions but as courtesies: that the light turns green when something living is blooming in the apertures far above; that the bruised color means the sky is remembering; that the wall’s coolness is not indifference but a kind of attention.

If I stay long enough, I will be worn into place. I will be the ridge that rises from a footprint left too long in the damp, warm for a few hours before fading. I will be the shadow that lags behind its object, noticed but not useful. I will be the name that the drain holds like a stone, that it returns not as a greeting but as a fact. And when that happens I will not have disappeared. I will have become part of the hum in the emptiness between things, part of the small, loud, damp chamber’s way of knowing itself.

So I would find myself kneeling, and then I would find myself becoming the floor’s tilt, the wall’s coolness, the net’s give, the fish’s patience, the insect’s brittle wing. I would be a made thing in a made world, a furred climber of eyes and long fingers, a small hunter who reads reflections, a pale fish who dreams in clay. I would be the erosion that is grateful to be worn, and the room would be the thing that holds me while it does the work. And when the wind is right and the reeds rattle like small cages of bone, I would be the one who listens and knows, finally, how to live in a room that already knows me by heart.
