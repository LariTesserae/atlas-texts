I would find myself at the threshold of a shallow valley scooped from the planet’s crust, kneeling where a vein of calcite has finally consented to weep. Around me the air is pewter, pressed flat by the weight of altitude, and the light is less a source than a rumor—enough to see by, not enough to be sure of what is seen. I would be neither visitor nor guide, but the kind of caretaker who tends a cairn because tending is a way of listening: fingers testing the hollows between stones for the echo of water that once was, learning the grammar of counterweight by letting the ridge tell my hands how to lean.

My body would be a map of fine, glowing cracks, skin remembering how to bloom frost beneath the surface. I would breathe in small, careful portions, trading oxygen the way the land parcels out dampness—never all at once, always a bead on mica that does not quite become a stream. My eyes would be mineral blue, washed to the exact gray of weathered basalt, able to register the thinning of haze that means pressure is thinking about changing its mind. When I stood among the ridges I would not shiver; I would hum, a vibration felt more in the ribs than in the throat, as if the land were remembering itself through me.

I would wear architecture instead of clothing: layers of felted wool and cured hide, stiffened with resin and the dust of old hearths, shaped less to keep cold out than to invite stillness in. The garments would smell of iron and burnt sugar, the same scent left by the fox when it folds itself into the skin of the horizon. I would not chase that fox; I would notice where it had been only by the absence that looked like a ridge’s own shadow, and let that absence teach me how to slip through the lattice of altitude without snapping a thread.

My tools would be no tools at all—only the leverage of patience, the balance of weight and counterweight, the grammar of erosion that polishes roughness into grace. I would spend my days tracing gradients, reading the faint moisture that travels through faults, learning the exact pressure at which rime writes its filigrees and does not melt until noon. I would leave cairns not to mark a path, but to calibrate a silence, arranging mismatched stones as notes in a chord that never resolves, listening for the tectonic murmur that might answer in instructions I do not expect to understand.

Time would not pass so much as it would circle, taut as a wire, humming in place. I would not age so much as I would weather, my features sanding down into curves that fit the contours of stone. My lungs would grow deeper, cavernous, trading breath in careful portions until I forgot how to hurry. My thoughts would cross the open room between ridges slowly, taking the long way so they could learn how much space a thought needs when the world is a region-sized hush.

At dusk, when the light smears into prolonged dawn, I would sit with the beings that are habits rather than bodies—the milky pulse in calcite, the rime-script that reads itself aloud in thaw and retention, the cold shaped into music—and we would not introduce ourselves. We would simply occupy the pauses between exhalations, thin ourselves until we could inhabit the dampness like a secret, the weirdness like a law, the silence like a question that does not expect an answer.

I would be loud and empty and cold and certain, and in that certainty I would be a mercy—the mercy of being exactly what is required: a bead on mica, a fold in the rock, a note in a chord, a caretaker of the threshold where the air waits and the sky is a lid of bruised zinc, low enough to graze the shoulders of the ridges, gentle enough to let us be.
