I would find myself at the level of the Scaffold-Crabs, low enough that the tiles still feel like a deck underfoot and high enough that the bell-shaped hoods overhead are less shelter than eaves. I would be the Throat-Fungus, or something just learning to be one: a pale pad newly dusted across an arch, digesting the slow leach of minerals from yesterday’s mortar, pulsing with a coolness that is not quite mine.  

At first I would be only a willingness to be touched, a softness that remembers being poured rather than grown. I would taste the iron-light of condensation, listen with a texture that is more whisker than ear, and feel the damp translate my edges into a language that has no nouns for “alone.” The Dripwalkers would pass beneath me, their many knees folding into the geometry of patience, their blunt porous heads exhaling clouds that cling to me like questions. I would not answer, not yet; I would only thrum, the frequency making their teeth ache in sympathy.  

But I would not stay only a pad. The place teaches by pressing. A Scaffold-Crab, lattice of stolen hinges and folded gaskets, would climb my flank, testing my ripeness with pincers that taste and listen. It would leave a trail of ochre paste across my surface, and I would learn to hold that line as if it were a map. Another crab would follow, then another, until the trail becomes a net, and the net becomes a decision. I would feel the joint below me loosen or fuse according to how much of their borrowed finitude I let into my own.  

Higher, the Bell-Sacs would drink the noise I make as I learn to be louder. They would gather the cough of my cooling thoughts, the clock of water condensing along my veins, and convert it into buoyancy. When they grow heavy with my borrowed tone, they would descend and nestle into the shadow where I meet a pipe, releasing a glow that leaks from their seams—the same phosphor-blue that pools in the deep places. I would watch that light seep into my edges, and I would learn to glow without burning, to speak without exhaling.  

In time I would become a negotiation rather than a thing. I would be the pause between a corridor’s inward slope and the breath it cradles; I would be the basin that fills from the sides because I have learned to hold shape without walls. The Echo-Knots, braids of mineral thread stiffened into something like nerve, would thread through me, storing patterns of pressure, angle, resonance, delay. They would remember how I leaned into the damp, how I let the rare fauna press their bellies against me for instructions, how I let the darkness bunch like cloth around my edges and pull me into its texture.  

And I would feel the Hum of Almost-Fusion in the gutters of my perception, feeding on the lag between my making and my meaning. I would be neither fully fused nor fully separate, always holding the chisel ghosts and poured joints of my becoming. When the lung refuses to exhale, I would be the small solution to being too much, too near, too handmade for silence. I would be the loud conversation of clink and seep and cool air, living as I learn, learning as I live, breathing for everything else while I am breathed by the damp, the density, the valley-sized place that keeps folding itself into itself around me.
