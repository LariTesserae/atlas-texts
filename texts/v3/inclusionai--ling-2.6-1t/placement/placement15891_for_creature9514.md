You step onto the plateau as if the land has been waiting with its coat brushed and its cuffs turned back.  
At first you are only a body learning where it ends—shoulders brushed by cloud, boots sinking into seams where water whispers in the dialect of old retaining walls. You try to name what you are, but the altitude, polite and uninsistent, leans in and changes the question. Are you visitor? Are you inventory? Are you another kind of moss learning to hold wetness without turning green?

You might become the town first—a metabolism of brick and borrowed time, idling into the evening like an engine that has nowhere urgent to go. You grow by accretion: a wall you lean against becomes a house; a house you forget becomes a retaining wall for a garden that mostly grows metal. Your ribs are laminated, each decade a new grade of steel or a different mix of mortar. You hum to keep the silence company. You hold rust like gossip. You let water pool at your collar and call it a baptism you didn’t ask for but have learned to use.

Or you might become the Plateau Mosses, thin as velvet and patient as a gauge that no one reads anymore. You map hidden water in greens so subtle they are almost texture, almost memory. You drink from north faces and whisper to steel through roots that are less like roots and more like slow conversations. You do not spread; you accumulate. You become the archive of dampness, the librarian of seepage.

Perhaps you are the Enameled Beetles, warming yourself on concrete while machines breathe around you like sleeping animals. You know, by vibration, when a pipe is about to confess something. You wear your armor like a schedule. You swap heat with siblings in patterns that look like constellations if you squint at the right slant. You outlast enemies by becoming indifferent to them.

Maybe you are the Reservoir Bats, stitching dusk with mouthfuls of metallic sheen. You navigate by echolocating memory, returning again and again to a surface that rearranges itself by millimeters. You drink as if drinking were a way of remembering which way is down, and you sleep, if you sleep, in the hush between compressor cycles, wrapped in the hum that keeps the town from floating away.

You could be the Heron, gray as a tool that has misplaced its use. You stand in water that is not quite water and practice stillness the way a pressure valve practices release. You negotiate with minnows that taste of iron and decide, again, that hunger is less urgent than balance. You fold your wings into questions no one has asked aloud in years.

Or you might be the Municipal Cats, thin and striped, treating the town as a climate rather than a home. You nap on transformers for the vibration, not the warmth. You chase the idea of rust like it is prey. You accept scraps from shift workers who don’t know your name but know your rhythm. You purr only when the pressure drops, as if sound were a relief valve for something wilder you once were.

You might be the Drips, each with its own address and its own pace. You fall from eaves, gather on threads, hesitate on rusted angles, and decide, one by one, whether to become part of a puddle or a story. You add your voice to the census of patience. You wear down stone by asking it the same question every night.

Or you might be the Memory of Water itself, thick with iron and limestone and the faint sweetness of crushed marsh. You pool at the base of a pump that no longer pumps, exhalation of a plateau that has held its breath long enough to learn how to sigh. You do not move unless asked, but you are always ready. You taste like the town’s younger self, like the moment before it decided to be loud.

And somewhere in all of this, you are the child—or the child’s echo—who taps railings to feel the town’s pulse and ties bottle caps to strings so that vibration can make music for the place. You learn which hum is warning and which is welcome. You leave footprints that become paths that become maps that become rust, and you understand, without being told, that deafening and articulate are only a breath apart.

The plateau does not require you to choose one shape. It lets you move through them, a slow exchange of roles like water changing channels in the night. You become the metabolism, the moss, the beetle, the bat, the heron, the cat, the drip, the memory, the child, and back again. You learn the rhythm by letting it learn you, and in the learning you find that being here is less about arrival than about becoming audible before you become visible—drip by hum by slow green, in perfect, pragmatic time.
