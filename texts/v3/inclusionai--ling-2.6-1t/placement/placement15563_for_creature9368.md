I would wake up where the tramway is only half-tamed—where the ferns have crept high enough to tickle the soles of sleepers and the rivets hold the memory of heat like bread holds the shape of a fist. I would be neither guest nor intruder, but a kind of oversight: the color of wet slate with a beetle’s patience, someone who counts rivets the way others count sheep, who can tell you exactly how much weight a rail flange is willing to release on a given afternoon.

My body would be more permission than presence: joints that click like glass beads on a power line, skin that drinks condensation and darkens into circuits when the nearby pipes warm without reason. I would have a face only when I needed one—a folded map of moss and oxidized iron, or else nothing at all, only an arrangement of shadow that suggests I am paying attention.

At dawn, I would sit where the loading platform sags into the loam, listening to the riveters hum. I would learn which bolts are ready to sigh and which are still practicing certainty. By midday, I would walk the canopy-bridges, borrowing softness from the Drapers, so my steps would parcel out dew the way regrets are portioned—one droplet, no more than it weighs. In the afternoons, I would stand at the rim of a pond-holder’s pause and let the oily iridescence trade my reflection for a version of the world that is closer to what it intends to become. I would not recognize myself there, and that would be the point.

When the Smokers gather in the stack-crowns and trace ghost-roads on glass, I would read them like braille, tracing routes that lead nowhere I remember, but that smell like possibility. At dusk, I would press my ear to the tramway’s skin and hear the Moss-Knitters thrum, convincing iron that it is, slowly and inevitably, becoming root. I would try to answer in kind, loosening my own certainties bolt by bolt, letting my edges settle into the ground as if I had always meant to lie down.

I would not own the high bowl, and I would not serve it. I would live in it the way moss lives on stone: by making surfaces softer, by turning edges into negotiations, by insisting that growth can be a form of listening. Silences would thicken around me, textured with the clink of nearly-events, with the low thrum of distant machinery felt more as vibration than tone. I would breathe in the measured rhythm of the place, exhale in thin, gauzy plumes, and discover, without surprise, that I have become another witness—someone who changes what is witnessed simply by being willing to be folded into the grammar of the high, cool earth.
