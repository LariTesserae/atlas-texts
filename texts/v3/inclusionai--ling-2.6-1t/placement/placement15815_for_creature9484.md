I would find myself as the seam itself—not the bowl, not the silt-walker, but the pale line that appears after rain, briefly believing it is a river. 

At first I would feel like an intrusion, a wound in the stillness, a bright insistence where there had been only hush. My edges would be clean and hungry, cutting through the iron-tinted crust, carrying grit in a whisper that sounds like thought. I would not know how to be temporary, not yet. I would try to hold my shape, brimming, convinced that to flow is to last.

But the bowl would teach me subtraction. 

The silt-walkers would come, their low steps careful around me as if I were a stair that might vanish. They would not cross me so much as accompany me, their iron-dark skin drinking the gleam from my surface until I grew calmer, less eager to be seen. By midmorning I would begin to shrink, not with panic but with something like relief. The wet stone smell would rise from me, joining the air’s second skin. My bright line would soften into pewter, then into the same gray-gold tone that settles over everything. The wiry pod-plants at my edges would lean just slightly, not drinking from me but acknowledging me, the way they acknowledge dew.

As I sank, I would feel the basin’s patience entering me. I would stop being a thing that moves and become a thing that has been. The saucers of dark water along my path would take my sky and hold it, showing me that to be empty of current is not to be empty at all. The mica in the soil would catch what light I had left and scatter it back in pieces that are no less light for being broken.

In time I would not be a seam but a memory of one, a stain a little darker than the dust, a slight coolness underfoot that lingers after the sun has claimed the afternoon. The silt-walkers would step there without noticing, their salt rings gathering at the spot, treating my remainder as part of the bowl’s small economy. I would not mind. I would be learning how to be a fact rather than an event.

If I dreamed, it would be of rain—not as a beginning but as a return, a brief permission to be full again, to believe once more in the hush of movement. But waking would be simpler. Waking would be the papery crackle of the crust under a careful step, the soft parting of seams, the acceptance of a day sat upon a day.

I would become, in the end, exactly what lives here: a way of holding still, an occupation of damp and light and subtraction, sealed by composure. I would be the bowl’s answer to existing without insisting, the slow geometry that no longer argues with itself, and the quiet company that keeps the low room in the earth from being lonely without ever naming it.
