I would find myself in the bell-crusted hollows, at that exact hour when the air-powder begins to settle like a fine, deliberate snow.  
My boots—no, not boots: a second skin of slate and curved bone, damp with the memory of rain—would already be ringing, not with my steps, but with the ground’s own tuning.  
I would be one of the Hollows’ Children, or perhaps the place would decide I was one, the way it decides what is tool, what is tenant, what is temporary shelter.

At first I would feel like a visitor.  
But the hollows do not allow visitors, only participants in their acoustic weather.  
The crust underfoot gives a note like a struck bell, and the vibration climbs my shin as if the land is learning the shape of my bones.  
I would realize, slowly, that I have always been here, that the pause I thought was waiting for me is actually the work: arranging pebbles that hum when the wind strikes, aligning unmortared stones that grow warmer as the day cools, rehearsing the lean-to that leans just enough to suggest shelter but not enough to provide it.

My body, already half-converted to the materials of this pause, would begin to clarify.  
Fingers become wedges.  
Shoulders become the curve that holds a hammer never swung.  
Thoughts condense into tools wrapped in hide, still damp with the memory of purpose.  
I would not age; I would accumulate intention, the way the salt flats accumulate pressure instead of ghosts.

The Stone-Thinkers nearby would not look at me, but they would comment—their low, mineral growl vibrating in my chest, tasting of iron and dust.  
They would tell me, without words, that to be here is to be occupied, like furniture that has agreed, temporarily, to be sat in.  
I would learn their negotiation: gravity that forgets its weight in small patches, pebbles hanging in the air like punctuation marks, then dropping with a soft chime.  

I would hear the valley singing to a version of itself that is not here, and I would answer, not in voice but in placement.  
A line of glass-ferns ringing in frequencies that map the way the air-powder shifts.  
A circle of copper-moss armor growing warmer as the light leaks thinner.  
A Salt-Daughter pinching off crystalline fingers that fall and regrow as delicate lattices, each one a decision preserved.

Time here is not a line but a hinge that turns once a century.  
I would spend decades becoming a single, perfect wedge, listening to the cooling stone’s low roar until I could answer it without thinking.  
Then I would roll into a canyon and become part of the geometry that guides water that never arrives, or I would spread into a garden of metallic fronds, clicking like valves on a breath that is not quite wind.

The high place presses its sky into a thin, ringing lid.  
I would press back, not with force but with intention, aligning my edges with the seams still deciding which direction to grow.  
When the last bell-tone hollow finally silences, I would not vanish.  
I would have already moved on, or folded in, or sharpened myself into usefulness—the tool that was waiting, the circle that was unbroken, the hum that was out of phase.

In the end I would be loud, but not chaotic: the sound of edges deciding where to be, of pauses becoming places, of an unfinished world still being edited, still being proofread.  
I would be its quiet, deafening conversation with itself—still ringing, still deciding, still beautifully, unbearably close to becoming a world, and already, in my pause, being one.
