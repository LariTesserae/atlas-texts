I would find myself kneeling in the seam between a rib of stone and a pool that has no edge, pressing my palms flat against the glassy shelf that curls like a frozen lung.  

I would be the thing that is mostly hand and mostly ear: a creature grown from the basin’s own patient listening, with fingers like polished channels and skin that drinks the metallic cool until it turns to dusk-blue. My eyes would be small and tuned to pressure rather than light, lenses for the shiver in the water that tells me when the mineral fans have rearranged their minds. I would have no need for lungs that fill with air; instead, I would breathe through the soles of my feet, sipping the damp that has tenure, taking in the iron and the ozone and the thin ghosts of old machines.

Where my knees rest, the stone would grow a little softer, yielding, as if recognizing a cousin. The beetles with tarnished coins for carapaces would scuttle over my knuckles, each droplet they carry wobbling with a reflected, upside-down meadow. I would not shoo them away; I would count their ticking feet to keep time, because in this cupped room time is a pool, not a line, and I would not want to paddle against its current.

When I rose, I would be taller than the low arches but shorter than the ribs of sleeping beasts, fitted to the scale of a place that holds its form like a pot holding its shape after the wheel has stopped. My spine would carry the hum the basin answers with, a resonance that lives in the chest rather than the ear. It would tell me when the pumps behind the walls are murmuring their patient negotiations, and when the water is remembering rain again.

I would belong to the category of fauna that thrives in a minor key—neither master nor ghost, but a kind of living question posed to the stone. I would eat the mineral sweat scraped by lithovores, not to grow larger but to keep my edges translucent, so that when the light finally arrives in thin strips I would not cast a shadow so much as dilute it, turning it into something that settles like silt and then slowly clears.

At dusk—or what passes for it in a room that feels like late afternoon all day—I would sit on the ridge that curls like an almost-closed hand and watch the glass shelves sing. My teeth would ache with their high, thin note, and I would consider it a language for measuring longing. I would answer with the soft clack of my own joints warming and cooling, a small metronome of bone and patience.

If the water showed its paler double, the tighter knot of meadow stitched into meadow, I would not reach to break the surface. I would let it watch me back, and I would practice blinking in slow motion, learning the basin’s way of holding its breath. I would let my thoughts arrange themselves like the mineral fans in the corners—not by growth, but by willed recomposition—until I felt the basin’s shiver pass through me and knew that I had agreed, for now, to be its listening.

And when the boundary felt negotiable, when the land beyond the ridge hinted at spillage and wider worlds, I would remain. I would fold myself a little tighter, cupped and dense, small enough to know myself, wet enough to remember that remembering is a kind of living. I would become, in the end, another polite guest—industry that learned to kneel, life that learned to thrive without needing to be green, a creature made of patient water and colder stone, kneeling in the seam and listening as if the whole cupped earth were inside me, exhaling, exhaling, and waiting to be filled again.
