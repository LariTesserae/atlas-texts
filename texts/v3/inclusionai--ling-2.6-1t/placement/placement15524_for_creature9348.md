I would find myself at the height of my own knees, walking the low bridge that is not a bridge but a question the forest asked the water long ago and never finished hearing the answer to.

I would be what the city keeps in its pockets when it wants to remember how to be small: a keeper of thresholds.   
Not human, not quite otter, not quite vine. Something that learned its shape from leaning.

My body would be the color of bark that has decided to soften, a drab-green-that-holds-water like duck feathers or moss-shadow. I would have too many joints for a spine meant for land, and too few for a spine meant for sea, so that when I moved I gave the impression of deciding, at each step, which law I preferred. My hands—if I let you call them that—would be webbed enough to read currents, fingered enough to tease wedges from living wood. My eyes would be double-stacked, the upper pair for the canopy’s grammar, the lower for the algae’s archive.

I would live in a chamber that had been grown rather than built, the kind the eels guard and the primates pretend not to need. The ceiling would ripple when I breathed, not from weakness but from consent. The floor would give like firm moss, remembering every footfall as a change of pressure rather than damage. I would keep no fire but the damp smolder of a stone bowl full of resin and honey-water, the kind that smells of old invitations.

My work would be to listen for where the city is about to yield, and to be there when it does.  
When a wall softens over decades, I would press my face to it and taste the shift, then leave a bead of my own sweat mixed with otter-salt at the seam, a tiny hinge the city’s patience can use. When a channel widens by the width of a thumbnail, I would sit on the newly exposed root and sing a single note low enough that the frogs will answer, so that the widening sounds like a conversation rather than a loss. When the flood rises on its generational clock, I would stand ankle-deep in the plaza-that-is-a-pool and translate the water’s request into the language of root and rust, whispering to the primates which vines to loosen and which to praise.

I would carry no tools but my slowness, my ability to hold three truths at once:
that a thing can be built and grown at the same time,  
that a doorway is an invitation dressed as an exit,  
and that to keep the flood at bay you must let it in gently, on the place’s own terms.

At dusk, when the city deepens its voice and the canopy, water, and stone decide to sing the same low note for a while, I would hum back, not to join but to acknowledge the key. The kingfishers would flash overhead like commas. The carp would stir silt into text I would not read until morning. The beetles would click their punctuation marks through hollow trunks that are also cisterns. And I would stand small in the sparse intervals, a note held softly, so that anyone walking the far bridge might think, for a moment, that the city knew exactly where it was—and that it had chosen, deliberately, to remember me inside it.
