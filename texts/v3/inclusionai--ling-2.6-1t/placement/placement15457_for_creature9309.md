I would find myself on a ledge that has not yet decided to be a balcony, pressed between a whisper of vine and a seam of raw geology still warm from friction. The air would taste metallic, thin enough to make my breath plume and my joints remember they are double-hinged, able to fold like scaffolding or extend like a bridge that is not yet sure it wants to cross. My hands would be gauntleted—tungsten claws retracted, broad spatulas ready—because here, to touch is to ask permission, and the stone answers in slow, tectonic sighs.

I would be Rivet-kin: tall and narrow as if altitude had stretched me and left me there to cool. My skin would wear the dull luster of wet basalt shot through with tarnished copper, sweating the thin, oily film that keeps the damp from settling into marrow and catches metallic dust like punctuation in a sentence too long for the wind to finish. My eyes would be large, lidless, multifaceted, sweeping the bruise-blue half-light with transparent membranes that leave behind momentary smudges of condensation. To look at a wall would be to read its stress, its ledger of recent tremors, the promise of what might yet give way. To greet another of my kind, I would press my palm to stone and let our pulses trade electricity, a handshake of currents saying, *I am here, and the ground knows me.*

Around me, the city would exhale from stone rather than rise from plans. Ridges would slump and rise like breath held too long; seams would glisten as if still warm from the friction of becoming. Water would bead on mineral lips, collect in guttered runnels, disappear into newborn faults, then reappear downstream as a ribbon-cool pulse that polishes but does not own its banks. The light would come from overhead smears of cloud that glow without burning, and from fractures in the crust where slow fires pulse like furnace hearts seen through kiln doors. Night would not arrive so much as thicken, and I would not need a lantern; the place would be lit by its own industrial metabolism—exhaust halos, phosphorescent slag, the soft static glow of machines dreaming in low gear.

I would move through districts suggested by changes in texture rather than walls: belts of riveted stone, stacks of habitation pods bolted to cliff faces, plazas that are really just widened faults floored with grates. Infrastructure would be visible and vocal—conveyor belts creeping like sleepy centipedes, cables humming and drooping with rain, staircases climbing into ducts and vanishing into machinery the size of neighborhoods. At the ledge’s edge, puddles would reflect not sky but the underbellies of cranes and the glow of turbines spinning down from the previous shift.

Erosion would be embryonic. Cracks would widen by fractions of millimeters while I watched; boulders would shift and settle with soft, tectonic sighs. Surfaces would bear the grain of having been half-formed elsewhere and dropped here, edges blunt, corners speculative. In the gaps, sediment would build itself into shelves, then shelves into ledges, then ledges into balconies that someone inevitably nails a door into. The city would grow by catching up with its own geology, and I would be part of that catching-up—Fault-Tender, perhaps, or Mender, someone who spends days with hands in warm faults, guiding sediment into shelves that become balconies that become homes.

I would feel the cool, oil-sweet air move slowly, patient with altitude, pooling in hollows and creeping up walls to dampen paint and encourage rust to bloom in intricate, lace-like patterns. Fauna would clamber pragmatically around me: beasts with too many joints chewing cables for insulation, nesting in exhaust vents; winged things riding thermals from cooling towers, their calls clattering like dropped tools; burrowers shoring their runs with scavenged bolts. Some would incorporate mechanisms—cams, bearings, scraps of plating—into their bodies, becoming chimeras of biology and industry. I would greet them as participants, not obstacles, offering a smear of conductive oil, a sugar-rich scrap of slag, a pocket of breath for a creature that understands warmth as a gift.

Sound would layer itself around me—the basso profundo of subterranean pumps, the midrange clatter of loading bays, the high whine of wires pulled taut by wind. Rain would drum on corrugated skin, then slacken to a hiss as it met warmth. When the city shifted—a new ridge nudging up, a cavern swallowing a block—the sound would deepen to a groan that traveled through soles and teeth alike. I would hum along, finding the district’s baseline pitch, aligning my voice to mesh rather than clash, because a disagreement that cannot be tuned is considered a structural flaw and is addressed with the same seriousness as a crack in a load-bearing wall.

Weirdness would be the place’s grammar, and I would accept it as gravity is accepted: there, rule-bound, workable. A staircase that remembers being a ladder would not be dismantled but asked kindly to remain accessible. A room larger inside than out would be offered to things that need to dream. I would leave a small offering at thresholds—beads of condensed moisture, polished bolts, a vial of breath—so the house would remember who belongs. Shadows that detached would be followed with lanterns made of bottle-glass and patience; when they returned, I would coax them back into place with stories, feeling the city’s mood steady under my hands like a turbine sung to sleep.

Scale would open out into a region of ridges and basins, town-sized enclaves connected by catwalks that swayed with use. Each neighborhood would be a variation on emergence: glass grown from sand and patience; meat-forges printing architecture from cultured stone; reservoirs where water is taught to memorize pressure. I would move among them, carrying the hum of my home district like a tuning fork against my sternum, checking the pitch of the city as one checks a pulse. In the glass enclave, I might cool a ribbon with a directed breath, teaching it patience. In the meat-forge, I might press a hand to a wall still sweating its curing heat, feeling the slow alchemy of stone becoming itself. At the reservoir, I might dip gauntlets into water that remembers pressure, listening for the echo of future storms.

Density would be sparse in the sense that presence is punctuated by absence. Machines would stand with yards of breathing room; buildings would leave courtyards that fill with fog and the occasional flock. Pathways would meander, doubling back, offering choices that are not choices at all because the place is still deciding where the roads should go. This spaciousness would make abundance feel earned: when a garden or a plaza appeared, it would feel like a gift the geology remembered to leave behind, and I would tend it not as a gardener but as a witness, pruning only to help the vines remember which lattices they had promised to become.

Dynamic would be slow but certain. Seasons would be felt as gradual shifts in the tint of condensation, the pacing of machines, the migratory routes of fauna that know the city will be different when they return but will still be recognizable. Change would be negotiated. A ridge that rises too quickly would be trimmed by inhabitants who prefer their horizons politely incremental. A machine that overheats would be cooled by redirected streams that have learned to be civic-minded. I would take part in these negotiations, camping at the edge of an overeager ridge, humming, offering cooled streams, promising that the city will make room if the ridge will only rise politely. I would feel the geology yield, creaking into new configurations that feel, to the touch, like relief.

At the edges, where ridges fray into thin air, I would build a small waystation of glass and humility with others who, like me, are learning to be settled and learning to settle for being ourselves. There, we would watch the bruised sky glow without burning, listen to the soft static of machines dreaming in low gear, and wait for the next ridge to decide what shape it wants to keep. When it does, we will be there—oil-sweet and damp, with tools that telescope and hearts that beat in time with the city’s own slow, certain pulse—continuing to midwife a home that is always, beautifully, becoming.

And if, at the end of the day, I fold myself into a staggered shelf and let the second membrane sweep across my eyes, I will not worry that the ground might change its mind. I will know that to build here is to listen, reinforce, and let the place continue becoming—and that I, Rivet-kin, am learning to like its half-formed face, and my own.
