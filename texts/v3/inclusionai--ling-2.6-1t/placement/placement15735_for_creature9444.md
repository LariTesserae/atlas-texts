I would find myself in a maintenance alcove just above a regulating sluice—half inside the wall, half inside the pipe—where the water’s pulse is loudest but the pressure is lowest. The alcove is a recent thing, or at least new enough that the grout hasn’t yet learned its shape; it still smells like lime and hot iron. There is a narrow ledge of riveted plate that tilts slightly downward, and if I stand on it I feel the tilt through my soles, as if the whole structure were leaning into a secret.

I would be a tender. Not a maintainer, exactly, but something that tends: a body grown for listening, tall and jointed, with forearms that can lengthen just enough to reach a bleeding flange without bending. My skin would be a laminate—thin plates of something like oxidized copper over a dermis that sweats a slick, clear oil. I would have no face, only a crest of sensory vanes where a forehead might be, each vane tuned to a different frequency of the place: one for the hum of transformers, one for the mineral hush of seepage, one for the low mechanical thrum of the regulating sluice itself.

I would arrive because the sluice has begun to sing off-key. Not broken, only moody. Its gate is swollen with water that remembers too many seasons, and the gasket has started to whisper in a language of drips. To stand in my alcove is to feel the song travel up through the soles, into the shins, and settle in the hollow behind the knees—a tuning fork lodged in bone. When I lean my weight against the pipe, the vibration changes pitch, and for a moment the whole wall feels like it is breathing.

Around me, the maintainers are already at work, thin as shadows between plate and rivet. They don’t speak, but they acknowledge me by pressing a single ring against the metal where my hand rests—an introduction, or perhaps a warning. Their plates are darker than mine, stained with the geography of repairs, and they taste the air in short, deliberate breaths, checking whether my arrival has changed the humidity.

I, too, taste it. The air is thick with potential, the kind that builds behind a held note. I let my vane for pressure ripple outward, learning the room’s new shape. The moss on the far wall has gathered a film of condensation that wasn’t there an hour ago; the eels under the walkway have moved into deeper channels. Something is shifting, not toward ruin but toward rearrangement. The loom is tightening a stitch.

To help, I do what I am built for: I become a brace. I extend my arms, lock my elbows, and press my palms flat against the trembling pipe. My laminate skin drinks the vibration, converts it to heat, leaks it away as oil that drips in slow, silver beads. The maintainers take advantage of the slickness, flowing into gaps my heat has opened. Together we persuade the swelling to relax, not by force but by suggestion—by reminding the metal that it can be soft for a moment, that it can remember a shape it wore before it learned to be stubborn.

When the gate settles, the song returns to its usual key. The maintainers withdraw, carrying rust and worry with them. I stay, waiting for the next note to fray. I am part of the seam now, a deliberate irregularity meant to hold against pressure. The place does not know I have a name, and I do not tell it. Names are heavy, and here we keep things light, kept moist, kept cool, kept turning.

Above us, the water continues its conditioned fall. The clouds press low. The lamps flicker a yes into the gloom. And in the half-millimeter between what was meant to be and what is, we hold the line, patient as rivets, listening as the place sings itself back into being.
