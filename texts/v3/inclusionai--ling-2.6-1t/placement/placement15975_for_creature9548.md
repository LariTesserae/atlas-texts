The roof of the world is a lid of pale sky pressed down on a wound that never closes.  
Below the lid, a region-sized sink of basins and low, worn ridges sprawls as if the earth had exhaled and never quite inhaled again. Elevation is a memory here—floors, not peaks. The air is cool, the kind of cool that settles like a stone in a pocket, carrying a dampness that beads on mineral skin without ever becoming rain. When moisture comes, it arrives as breath on glass: condensation blooming across vast, ancient stone faces, or shallow runnels that appear overnight, silver-threaded across grey pavements, then vanish into thirsty seams.

This is an old place. Not old in the sense of ruins, but old as a word that has been repeated until its edges have softened into geometry. Erosion has done its patient work: gradients have been planed, outcrops have surrendered to roundness, and the bones of the land lie in a near-final arrangement, as if time had grown tired of rearranging them. Yet this stability is deceptive, because the place hums with a turbulence that refuses to sit still. The bright light pours down—an unapologetic glare that turns damp stone into mirrors and makes shadows feel like accidents—and within it, everything flickers, shifts, reassembles.

There are no plants here, not because life has been stripped away but because the category never took root. Instead, the mineral world has thickened into city. The built fabric rises in sparse, deliberate clusters, like sentences placed carefully across a page. Walls are of chalked limestone and compacted ash-mortar, shaped with primitive tools that still know how to listen to grain. Towers are low and braced, streets follow the logic of water that used to run here, and every settlement is a palimpsest of repairs, each layer a different era’s attempt to hold a shape against the churn. Infrastructure is humble: sluices to coax the damp, gutters that double as mazes, cisterns carved from the ancient stone itself, and signal-fires of polished obsidian that flash in the bright air without smoke.

Silence is almost absolute, or at least it would be if the place were not so loudly present in the small sounds it allows: the tap of a chisel, the scuff of a boot on wet flagstone, the whisper of condensation sliding from a cornice, the soft clatter of tools set down. These sounds are not lonely. They are punctuations in a quiet that feels less like absence and more like a held breath.

Fauna is abundant, and in this mineral world they have become as strange as the city they inhabit. Six-limbed runners with hooves of polished flint move in loose constellations across the open floors, pausing to drink from temporary rills. Winged gliders, membrane stretched over frames like bent copper, ride the bright updrafts that boil up from the cool stone. Burrowers with carapaces like river-smoothed flint slip into the margins of settlement and come out changed, bearing minerals that glint with captured light. They are not tame, but they have learned the rhythm of the towns—the times when the turbulent sky calms enough for crossings, the places where damp collects—and the towns have learned them.

Weirdness here is not spectacle; it is a property of attention. In the brightest hours, edges soften, and a street you have walked a hundred times may reveal it never ends where you thought. Water runs uphill in thin threads before remembering itself. Reflections in the wet stone sometimes show buildings that are not there, or that are there but older, or that are there and watching. The light seems to thicken into substance near the ancient flat zones, and in that brightness, decisions made in the town below appear to have already been made elsewhere.

The turbulence is the true governor. The place is in a state of continual becoming: a storm of material rearrangement not in the sky but in the organization of things below. One season, a plaza is open; the next, it has accreted a ring of low arches where none were before. Roads braid and unbraid. Buildings lean into one another as if conferring, then separate, settling into new conversations. This is not decay—it is a kind of negotiation. The urban fabric breathes, and its breathing is erratic, brilliant, and undeniable.

Tech is early and enduring: ropes of braided sinew and mineral fiber, tools of shaped stone and fire-hardened wood, simple metal only where it can be coaxed from the damp veins of the land. Craft is manual and deliberate. Each object carries the weight of the hand that made it, and the hand is never far from the mind that imagined the thing and the place that allowed it to stand. In this bright, cool, damp, ancient, turbulent basin, civilization is not a conquest. It is a series of careful proposals offered to a land that answers in shifts of light, in the appearance and disappearance of water, in the quiet rearrangement of its oldest stones.

To walk here is to be in a place that has forgotten how to be finished. It holds you in a wide, open embrace that is at once sparse and full. You feel you could build a life in the gaps, and you feel you would have to keep rebuilding it, because the place is always, quietly, remaking itself in the bright damp coolness of its ancient, unsettled heart.

They do not have names, or if they do, the names are tied to the angle of light on a certain day, to the sound a particular gutter makes when it clears, to the mineral that bloomed on a windowsill after a night of condensation. To call them people is almost accurate, and almost wrong. They are something the place has agreed to let thicken into person-shape, the way damp thickens into mirror on stone.

You see them first as variations on upright: biped enough to grip tools, but with four additional limbs that do not dangle as decoration. Two arms, yes, and two others—call them flank-limbs or brace-limbs—used for bracing against sudden tilts of the street, for steadying loads on narrow passes, for holding conversation with architecture at the scale of touch. Their feet are cloven into flint-dark hooves that keep a soft, mineral polish from constant scuff against flagstone. They leave prints that look like cursive, like a language trying to remember how to be read. The hooves ring when the stone is dry, thud when it is damp, and in the brightest hours they seem scarcely to touch the ground at all, as if brightness has made gravity tentative.

Their skin is matte, cool to the touch, the color of watered clay or old parchment held to stone. It drinks condensation. Beads form on them in the cool hours, not as sweat but as a kind of echo of the air, and they do not wipe it away; they let it trace paths that map the old water-roads beneath the city. Where their elbows and knees brush stone too often, callouses grow that are themselves becoming stony, flecked with mica and quartz that catch the glare and scatter it into private rainbows. They do not mind. They like the way it feels to be brighter in small places.

Their heads are broad, with crests or ridged plates—not bone so much as compacted sediment and resin, shaped by time and by choice. From these crests sprout sensory fronds, fine as spun glass, that tremble in the constant turbulence. The fronds are how they read the city’s small news: the shift in temperature of a wall, the direction of a hidden draft, the metallic tang of a new seam opening somewhere deep. When they stand still, the fronds comb the air like fingers through water. When they move, they braid them back or pin them with simple toggles of carved chalk, so as not to snag on low arches or on the thoughts of neighbors.

Their eyes are large and dark, not black but absorbing, wells that gather brightness and soften it before it reaches the mind. In full glare, their pupils narrow to threads, and the skin around their eyes flushes with patterns that are hard to read—maps of recent weather, perhaps, or of recent decisions. They see in layers: the stone as it is, the stone as it was, and the stone as it might yet become if water remembers to return. They use this sight to navigate the streets that braid and unbraid themselves. They will pause mid-step, not because they are lost but because the street has momentarily forgotten its mind, and they wait, patient as lint in a gutter, until it remembers where it leads again.

Their hands have six digits: two opposable thumbs, a clever arrangement that lets them grip and rotate and hold three things at once—stone, tool, and thread. Their nails are horny, dark, good for prying and scraping, for finishing edges that resist gentler tools. They work with chisels of flaked obsidian, with drills of bone and sand, with fire-hardened levers that coax stone into admitting a new arch where none was yesterday. Their work is never done, and they do not call it work. They call it tending. They believe—if believing is the right word—that the city is not a thing they live in but a thing they are keeping company with while it decides what to be next.

They dress in layers of woven mineral fiber and cured skin, garments that flap like pages when the bright updrafts boil up from cool stone. The fabrics are cut to allow the fronds room, and also to allow the body to be braced against a sudden lean of a building. They wear belts of plaited gut and little pouches for seeds that will not grow here, for smooth stones that remember being elsewhere, for small lenses of polished obsidian that flash messages across the glare without smoke. When they want privacy, they wrap their crests in soft cloth and stand very still, letting the fronds rest. When they want company, they let them wave, bright and busy as antennae.

They are not numerous. A settlement might hold two or three hundred, or two or three thousand—numbers feel slippery here, because the place sometimes duplicates a street and its people with it, just long enough to test whether they will argue about which version is real. They resolve such arguments by sharing tea and splitting the difference: both streets are true until the damp dries. They have a patience that is not passive but busy, the patience of people who know that waiting is a form of making.

Among them are runners, lithe and long of limb, who travel between the scattered clusters of settlement, carrying news and small objects that need to change hands before the city rearranges itself out of reach. They move with the loose, efficient lope of those who know that the ground may rise or fall a hand’s breadth in the time it takes to inhale. They drink from temporary rills that may not be there on the return, and they memorize the taste of each, so they can find it again by tongue if not by eye.

There are gliders, too, who stretch their membranes and leap from the low braced towers, riding updrafts that smell of sun on wet chalk. They do not fly as birds do but as leaves do: with intent and drift combined. They scout the open floors for new seams of damp, for places where the turbulence has sculpted a new hollow or revealed an old one. From above, their shadows flicker across the city like signals, and those below look up and interpret them, not as birds but as sentences that change meaning as they pass.

There are burrowers, whose carapaces are river-smooth flint and whose bodies are half the architecture of the margins. They slip into the gutters and the ancient seams and come out changed, bearing minerals that glint with captured light. The city-dwellers trade small favors with them, leaving offerings of resin and thread at the mouths of tunnels, and in return the burrowers leave behind shapes that hum when held, and that seem to know the city’s older memories. No one knows if the burrowers think as people do. They do not speak in voices, but they speak in rearrangements: a tunnel that opens into a room overnight, a stone that warms to the touch of a particular hand. The city-dwellers treat this as conversation.

Their voices are low, shaped to avoid competing with the city’s small sounds. They speak in soft clicks and hums that travel well in damp air, and they use the creak of a door or the settling of a stone as punctuation. They do not shout, because shouting implies that the listener is far away, and here, listeners are rarely far. Instead they gather close, touching elbows or brace-limbs, letting their fronds comb each other’s crests in greeting, a tactile punctuation that says, I am here, and I know you are here, and for now that is enough.

They have stories, but the stories are not fixed. A tale told in the morning may, by afternoon, include an arch that was not there when the telling began, and the teller will not correct it. The listeners will not mind. They understand that the story is true to the city’s current state, and that the city’s state is true to the story. History is not a line but a braid, and they follow its strands without worrying which came first.

In the brightest hours, when light thickens into substance and decisions seem to have been made elsewhere, they will pause their work and simply stand, letting their fronds drink the glare. They do not fear the weirdness; they treat it as weather. A street that never ends where it should is a chance to meet someone you did not know you were looking for. Water that runs uphill is a reason to reconsider what direction means. Reflections that show older, watching versions of the city are invitations to argue gently with the past, to offer it a cup of tea and a small stone, and to ask it to be kind.

They do not want to finish. They have seen enough of finished things to know that finishing is a kind of forgetting. Instead, they build and rebuild, repair and reveal, hold conversation with stone and sky and damp and light. They are not conquerors. They are proposals that the place has not yet rejected, and their lives are the quiet, persistent sound of the city breathing—erratic, brilliant, undeniable—while the bright lid of sky presses down, and the wound beneath it never closes, and everything flickers, shifts, reassembles, and lives.
