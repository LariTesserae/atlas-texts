I would find myself at the edge of a step-cut terrace slick with black-green moss, where the ferro-fluid mirror of a pool has just dimmed as the Thrum-Deer lifted its head and the Lantern-Clingers in the canopy above went suddenly quiet. The cold would arrive in a pulse—not a wind, but a held breath that beads on my sleeves and makes the hair on my arms stand like tiny bristles tuned to rumor. Underfoot, the ceramic walkway would hum faintly, giving just enough to feel like it is alive, remembering every footfall in a lattice of slow-growing light.

I would be one of the Tenders—or something learning to be one—wrapped in a cloak half fabric, half active biofilm that keeps the worst of the wet from biting while still letting the land breathe into me. My hands would carry the smear of nanosutures, the callus of coaxing: touch a scarred stone and feel the nanolattices answering like shy muscle, thread by glowing thread. I would smell of iron and petrichor and the fungal softness of breath held too long, and when I opened my mouth to speak, my voice would ride the folded sound, slipping up vines and across water to be translated into light by the Lantern-Clingers, into song by the Thrum-Deer, into safe footing by the Shelf-Stitchers braiding their temporary ferries.

But I would feel the instability in my knees—a knowledge that the terrace could slough if the resonance shifts—so I would move with the economy of the deer, placing each foot where the moss is least likely to let go, willing the ground to hold by attention rather than force. My coat would shed perpetual moisture in oily sheets, and droplets would sometimes climb the wrong way for a heartbeat before falling, as if the place were testing my patience, my allegiance.

In the deeper pools, the Mirror-Nymphs would gather like gossip made glass, their bells crusted with ferrofluid eyes that turn toward me, listening. They would not fear me, but they would record me: the cadence of my breath, the small mistakes in my footing, the warmth leaking through my cuffs. I would let them. Information is a kind of food here, and I would offer mine—where the cold bites deepest, where the resonance frays, where the built things fray into moss—knowing it will be stored in iridescent lattices and returned as warning or lullaby when the next storm simmers.

Above me, a Lantern-Clinger would beat its stiffened wings, not for lift but for sound, and the private frequency would tell me where the next handhold is among the near-black leaves. I would follow it, upward, into the violent lushness where stems braid themselves into walls and the canopy is thick enough to stand on. There, I would lay a hand on a scarred trunk and encourage the lattice to knit, feeling the slow agreement of material remembering how to heal. The act would cost something—a thinning of my own biofilm, a momentary chill where the damp slips through—and I would accept that trade, because to Tend is to be consumed in small, useful ways.

When the Mirror-Nymphs ring their tiny bells and the Shelf-Stitchers begin to weave, I would descend, stepping onto their ephemeral ferries and letting the Slip-Eels undulate through the root-mats below like smoke given hunger. The ferries would dissolve behind me so the landscape never remembers my passing, and I would prefer it that way: to be a pressure, a nudge, a hum that steadies resonance just long enough for moss to knit and stone to sweat without crumbling.

At the edge of the marsh-flat, the Thrum-Deer would pause, antlers latticed with capillary light, singing the low note that holds back the turbulence. I would stand among them, not speaking, only breathing in time, until the dark felt less like a lid and more like a second skin. And if the cold leaned close to breathe damp into my ear, I would let it, and whisper back the small, provisional rules of this place: that water may climb for a breath; that growth accelerates under pressure; that stability is a rumor; that abundance must learn suspicion, and movement must make noise so the dark cannot surprise.

In the end, I would be neither fully built nor fully wild, but ongoing—an arrangement of attention, biofilm, and borrowed resonance—listening for the next shift in the youth of the ground, ready to lend my voice to the parliament of breath, water, and vibration until the boundaries between Tend and belong grow soft as moss and bright as phosphor in the near-total dark.
