I would find myself in the mid-ducts, where the white-gold luminescence thins and the water learns to hesitate.  

Not quite a canyon, not quite a chamber, but a sleeve of pressure that tightens and loosens like a lung. The floor is absent; the ceiling is the same brine you swim through, so that up and down are habits you have to borrow from the lights. Here the scaffold reefs branch into open lace, and the current is a scheduled thing—predictable enough to lean into, alive enough to ask for attention.  

I would be a small, compact body wrapped in overlapping rings that pop open like shutters when shear threatens. Not a Valve-Dancer—those are already spoken for, already necessary—but something like their apprentice or echo. My fins would be stumpy, practical, meant less for beauty than for bracing. Along my flanks I would carry a lattice of sensor-nubs, not to map memory like the Roam-Silhouettes but to borrow it, tasting the sediment ghosts in the water and translating them into warnings for whoever swims close enough to listen.  

My color would not be fixed. I would take on the temperature of the nearest heat exchanger when the schedule brings me near, then shed it quickly in the colder ducts, leaving faint trails of dimming brightness behind me like breadcrumbs made of borrowed light. When the great pumps change phase and the fever of the manufactory shifts, I would tighten my rings, brace against the turbulence, and count the seconds until the flow settles into its new key.  

My work would be small and precise: testing the seams. Not breaking rules, but pressing them until they hum back, learning where pressure doors are likely to sigh and where they will slam, then marking those places with quick bursts of bioluminescence that dissolve almost as soon as they appear. I would read the slap of bulkheads and the hush before a gas release, and when translucent flowers of compressed gas bloom I would ride them for a few heartbeats before peeling away, scattering the dream so the Current-Tenders can polish in peace.  

Mostly I would listen. The manufactory’s pulse is loud and multivocal—pumps thumping arrhythmias, turbines chanting in overlapping keys, the white-hiss of water through engineered channels—and I would let that noise fill the hollows in my rings, tuning them until each chamber I pass through feels distinct, almost musical. When the lights dim in the deep ducts and the fauna settle into alcoves, leaking heat back into the flow, I would linger a little longer, borrowing warmth from machines that never truly sleep, storing it in my vessels until the next scheduled brightening.  

I would have no name except the one the current gives me when it carries me past a certain threshold, and I would not keep it long. I would be there when the water hesitates, and gone when it decides to move again, leaving only the echo of tightened rings and a trail of borrowed light saying *clear*, *clog*, *wait*—a small, practical voice in the large, impatient conversation of making.
