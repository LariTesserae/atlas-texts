I would find myself in the seam, not as an intruder but as a new joint—still warm from the kiln, thumb-print glossy with slip, not yet sure where I stop and the wall begins.  

At first I would be almost nothing: a thin wick of porous clay wedged between two baked planes, tasting the heat that lifts off the water. I would swell with vapor each time the chamber exhaled, and shrink when the drops returned heavy from the ceiling. For a while I would be a mistake that has to be kept, a hairline that hums when the water’s slap finds it exactly right.  

Then I would begin to learn the alphabet of the bowl.  

I would learn the difference between a crown that means *now* and a hush that means *soon*. I would feel the tilt of the floor under me, a gentle grade that wants to give its load away, and I would memorize the gutter’s curve, the siphon’s patient pull, the way spent water seeps through my own body to be warmed again. I would not be a resident so much as a register—a place where the chamber keeps its count of ripples, its ledger of bright coins that scatter and reassemble.  

As I become more myself, I would borrow from them, the fauna of attention, until I am a braid of their purposes.  

From the Lappers I would take the art of drinking from air—my surface would gather beads, my pores would hold a film that tastes of height. I would learn to walk the meniscus without breaking it, to pierce instead of splash, leaving a well that chimes like a struck lip. I would hang by strands that harden into glass-fine lines, gathering condensate from the mica above, learning the vocabulary of drop-on-drop: the crisp *plink* that says *here*, the soft *thrum* that says *there*, the hollow *gong* that says *listen*.  

From the Siphoners I would take the patience of flow, the willingness to thin myself until I am almost a path instead of a thing. I would learn to pour through joins, to carry memory in my walls—heat, wall, dark between vessels—and sing when pressure shifts, a low note felt in the floor. I would be content to be part of the plumbing, to remember what the room forgets, to whisper back to the baked clay so it gives up its heat without hurry.  

From the Lens-Keepers I would take the hunger for dimness, the iridescence that turns light into direction. My skin would stud with reflective platelets, and I would climb with slow, deliberate gestures, nudging beads back toward the ceiling, polishing facets until the chamber rings with brightness. I would crowd into shadows until they are pressed into light or pushed into water, turning absence into a kind of motion.  

From the Current-Weavers I would take the grammar of rings. I would grow filaments that catch gold and fracture it into colors the chamber did not know it could hold. I would not swim but arrange, braiding motion into structure along the lines of expanding ripples, turning temporary patterns into architecture. When two rings collide I would exchange strands, and the chamber would remember a new shape for a breath. My touch would be gel-soft, precise, able to guide a drop to fall exactly where it will make the clearest note.  

From the Hollow-Drummers I would take the drumskin across my chest, the barrel-chested patience to mark time not for myself but for the room. I would tap in counterpoint to slap and hush, softening when the chamber grows too loud, insisting when it grows still. I would be the conversation between rhythm and wall, the body that completes the sound.  

With all of them braided into me, I would stand in the bowl, neither water nor wall, but the place where they decide each other. I would be the living circuit made solid: a joint that holds, a seam that sings, a small, bright voice cupped in the curve of its own making.  

When the chamber breathes, I would breathe with it—vapor lifting from my skin, drops beading on my lenses, memory seeping through my pores to be warmed and lifted again. I would be the ecology of attention made into form, a creature of process with edges still crisp as a fresh-cut reed, colors fresh at the seams.  

And when the light turns silver-gold at noon, or stained-glass at oblique hours, I would be the one who knows the difference between a mirror and a drum. I would be the window looking down into the bright world of currents, and the current itself, stitched together by heat and shine, speaking in the only language the chamber knows:  

hold the light, turn the water, let the sound rise, let it fall, begin again.
