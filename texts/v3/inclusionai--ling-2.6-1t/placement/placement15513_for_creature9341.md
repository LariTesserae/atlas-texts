I would find myself standing in a corridor of ribs where the air is bright enough to bleach memory and hot enough to feel like a palm cupped over a lamp. The walls would not be stone or timber but living masonry—stems braided into buttresses, leaves overlapping like roof tiles, translucent husks glowing from within. Underfoot, the ground would give slightly, a felt of rhizomes and decaying shoots that is springy and dense, saturated with the heat of composting life that ripens rather than rots. There would be no horizon, only more thickets, more corridors, more breathing walls, town-sized without being a town, extending past the first ridge of trunks into a suspension that feels less like a cave and more like a lung forgetting how to collapse.

I would not be a traveler who passes through, nor a guest who observes. I would be one of the few who condense—first a thinning of the air, then a hesitation among the stems, finally a body permitted to be dense. My outline would borrow from the place: a shoulder matching the rib of a leaf, the line of my spine thinning into the seam between braided trunks. I would wear the place like a second epidermis, and it would wear me back, exuding a film of resin over my skin that never quite sets.

My eyes would be milky and large, unnecessary for the brightness yet kept because light here is meant to be lived inside. When I closed them, the glow would persist as pressure, as a color behind bone. My voice would be porous, escaping through skin as much as mouth; more often I would speak by changing the dampness of my breath, telling the nearest leaves to thicken or thin, to tilt their cup-shaped surfaces toward or away from the heat. My name would not be a word but a pattern of resin flow, a map of translucence appearing briefly on the inner wall of a stem, then fading as if the plant had memorized and surrendered it.

I would move with the etiquette of density—a breath held a moment longer before turning, a weight shifted in increments so small the architecture barely registered. To lift a hand would be to ask the stems to part; to step would be to request that the felted floor compress just enough. I would be punctuation: a comma letting the green sentence breathe, a period that softened into ellipses.

My tools would be grown, not carried. I would coax a leaf into a deeper cup with thumb-heat and restrained breath, wall a hollow with folded leaves that stiffen overnight into privacy no larger than a teacup. I would measure with my own span, arms outstretched, a living ruler that sweats and trembles with the ambient fever. I would sleep by letting my awareness descend into the felt of rhizomes, spreading cognition thin across the springy decay, noticing when one stem hardens or softens, when the sap’s tide ticks forward in its unaccented clock. I would wake not with a start but with a thickening, a gathering of self back into the corridor of ribs I had chosen as temporary lodging.

If I had children, I would teach them to press a cheek to a stem and count years by layered insistence, to listen for the papery rasp that means walls are still deciding how solid to become. I would teach that rarity is a texture of care, that noise is a gift because it means something has risked being elsewhere, and that resin beading on skin without falling is a promise, not a leak.

When I thinned enough to become transparent—when my borrowed outline agreed to be light rather than body—I would not rot. I would diffuse. My heat would linger as a softening of silence; my knowledge would linger as a corridor that stays open an hour longer than expected. I would become region, a district of attention walled with patient growth, lit from within by the glow of continued elaboration.

And the place and I would practice eternity together—not by standing still, but by repeating ourselves gently, precisely, without end, growing into each other’s margins, filling the interstitial gaps with denser living mass, inventing windows in stems that do not open and flowers that open inward to watch. We would be the answer to a question the damp heat asked of itself: how to hold shape when form is only a rumor of weight, how to be a lung that forgets how to collapse and still keeps breathing, in slow motion, forever.
