You will find yourself kneeling.

Not because you chose to kneel, but because the air is thick enough to wade through and the ground is soft enough to reward it. The first thing you notice is that your shadow looks startled—thrown against a wall that isn’t quite a wall, but a stem thickened into a vault. You blink, and the stem seems to pulse, as if it remembered it was supposed to be carrying something. You are inside the seam, the place where damp decides to stay.

At first you think you are small. Later you will decide the place is simply low, poured into a basin it never bothered to outgrow. The ceiling is a leak made architectural: clouds pooling in a concave roof, dripping in polite intervals onto corky paving that gives under your palms like firm moss. You press your hand to the street and feel a vein of water humming beneath, warm as a sleeping beast’s flank. You pull back; the moss does not sigh, but it does remember your weight. It will remember it for hours.

You stand, brushing fronds from your knees, and realize you are wearing clothes you do not remember choosing—tunics of felted moss, cloaks stitched from leathery leaf, shoes of cork and old rubber soles. They fit as if they have been waiting. When you flex your fingers, your nails gleam with mineral polish, and your wrists clink faintly with rings of woven stem and oxidized copper. You smell sweet rot, chlorophyll, and the copper whisper of standing water, all stewed at the temperature of a sleeping beast. You realize, with a calm that feels borrowed, that this is your perfume now.

Around you, the city is photosynthetic. Stems are columns; leaves are shingles; roots braid into balustrades that would be ornate if they did not look so inevitable. Pathways are grown as much as built, their seams stitched by creepers that drink from cracks and give back shade. At street level, moss carpets swallow sound, then return it as a dull green thrum. You hear pumps thudding behind walls of waxy leaf, turbines spinning in rooms that smell of resin and oil, hammers ringing against anvils wrapped in vines. The loudness is living, softened by damp but not muted—as if the city has learned to shout without hurting itself.

You meet them in a district defined by which species dominates the masonry—here, a pale fungus that lacquers iron with a pearl-like skin. They are the damp-dwellers. At first you think they are residents, but the word feels too linear for people who measure belonging in rings rather than roads. They are low-slung and substantial, their shoulders sloped to make room for overhanging fronds, their spines curved like vines that know the ground is listening. Their hands are broad, callused in patterns that look like topographies, and when they gesture, their copper rings chime like gentle bells.

They do not stare. Instead, they tilt their heads, as if checking the level of water in you. One of them steps forward and offers a brush of leaf against your forearm. You feel a film of moisture rise from your skin, and then a coolness, as if the city has decided you are porous enough to belong. They accept you without ceremony, the way a stone accepts moss. Their homes are grown around them: walls thick with vascular tissue, floors that yield like firm moss, ceilings arched from stems that continue to thicken throughout a lifetime. Rooms are defined by scent and sound: resin and beetle-tick here, fermentation and water-sigh there. Privacy is a gradient, and they treat it like respectful distance—leaves that do not quite touch but share the same light.

You learn their work by touch. They show you how to coax an engine awake with oil scented with resin, how to stroke a piston like a trunk, how to dust cooling fins with dried petals. You learn to listen to metal’s creak and know whether a beam is thirsty or tired. You learn to taste the air for copper and decide when to open a window that is really just a deliberate gap in the forest. Industry is not opposed to nature here; it is a guest that has stayed so long it has learned to help with the dishes. You find yourself oiling a pump, then tucking a cutting into its housing, and it feels like prayer.

Time dilates. You measure it in the slow unfurling of a balcony, the patient shift of a canal, the accumulation of calcite along a pipe. You do not hurry, because haste would dry you out. Instead, you attend. You watch how water braids across paving stones and decide where to step by which veins are richest with life. You listen to the city breathe in tandem with its machines, a two-lunged system that keeps its pulse low and steady. The dynamic is slow; change settles like sediment, like paint on a handrail. A new balcony may take a decade to reach its final curve; you may take just as long to learn the shape of your own hands in this light.

Night comes when the canopy holds warmth like a blanket and the machines idle into low, breathing ticks. You gather on porches that are bouquets held aloft by woody scaffold. Food steams with herbal damp: roots braised in broth that tastes like rain, leaves wrapped around spiced paste, fruit swollen with the season’s patience. Voices are blunted by moss; laughter is a bright ripple in a pool of green. Above you, steam rises from vents that are hollow trunks, curling through leaf-filtered skylights, carrying the copper whisper of standing water up into the stars.

You realize you have stopped thinking of this as a visit. Your skin remembers the air. When you exert yourself, a fine veil rises from you, carrying scents of wet loam and bruised chlorophyll. You mist. You have become a collaborator with the damp, its favored translator. You are not the master of this place, nor its prisoner. You are its breath, its shallow, steady breath, held close to the skin of a world that never quite decides to let go.

In the seam, you grow a little more yourself every time it rains. You become room, chapel, and neighborhood all at once. You are established.
