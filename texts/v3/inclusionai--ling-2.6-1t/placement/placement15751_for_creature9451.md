Where I would find myself is not a place on a map but a state of altitude and attention. I would wake in the grammar of the wood, in a room that is the space between three trunks that have braided themselves into a corridor in their own slow time. The floor beneath me would be a lattice of living root, trained flat by generations of spit, ash, and careful tread, springy with fungal threads and matted loam that remembers every footfall. The ceiling would arch from interlocked crowns, porous and breathing, filtering the heavy sun into strips of honeyed light. The door would be a veil of liana, or a curtain of overlapping fronds, and the air would taste like hot copper and wet leaf even at the start of the day, because here elevation has thinned the world into a dome of breath.

I would be one of the tall and slender people, but that slenderness is not born—it is learned. My bones would have been tutored by the air, my lungs ridged with extra bronchi that bloom like fern-fronds in my upper chest so that I could drink the copper sky without dizziness. My skin would be waxy, leaf-thin, patterned with dendritic stains that darken when storms are being born in the next valley. I would sweat only in small, deliberate beads that collect along my spine and evaporate before they fall, because to waste moisture here is to insult the house that holds you. My fingers would be long and ridged, my nails hardened by resin and pumice, able to find purchase on bark that feels like iron; my toes would grip; my arches would be high for a ground that is rarely ground.

I would move with a gait that is half-climb, half-glide, knees bent to absorb the give of living floors, hips loose for the sway of bridges that are not rope but living vine, thick as a thigh and humming with sap. Flight would call to me, but I would not be born with membranes. Instead I would earn my wings with study, learning to leap from towers of hollow stalks and catch the updrafts that spiral up the living walls. I would carry messages in hollow bones, small clay spheres that rattle with weather predictions and the locations of fruiting explosions. To fly here is to be part of the sky’s inventory, a mote in the visible veils of heat, trusted not to fall but to be returned, eventually, by the currents.

My days would be full of small negotiations. I would learn the grammar of traps and lures, the art of tending rather than taking. There are silk-shearers who collect the sticky nets spun between hollow stalks and reseed them with pheromones of their own making; there are lure-speakers who learn the songs of certain blossoms and use them to draw prey into digestive moss, then sing apologies to the plants afterward, because waste is debt. I would learn to read the hiss of fused canopies, to tell by the shiver of a buttress whether a storm is coming from the east, to leave a trail that will be eaten by fungus before it can be followed by something hungry. I would practice silence, too, and be brought damp moss to my lips when I go quiet for too long, because silence can mean thirst, or thinking, or the approach of something that hunts by sound.

My body would know the place’s rhythms. My reproduction would be timed to the resin-bloom, when the canopy drips light and the air smells of candied sap. My children would be born in cradles of woven root, suspended over the matted loam so that their first breaths are heavy with spores and the low thrum of the forest. They would be fed on nectar and softened bark until their teeth come in jagged, suited for chewing the tough veins of leaves that store water. My childhood would be long, because learning the grammar takes decades.

I would bleed when cut, but my clots would be amber, warm and resinous. I would rub and warm them until they emit a low tone that soothes broken sleep, and I would wear this amber as jewelry and as medicine, suspended in nets over cradles and beds so that the room is never without its hum. When I died, I would be fed to the towers of hollow stalks, where the updraft would carry the spores of my memories upward, and the stalks would sing me in rustles for as long as the wind is willing.

My technology would be modest and patient: copper bandages wrapped around trunks wounded by lightning or clumsy climbers, not to heal the wood but to thank it; tin bowls set in the crooks of branches to catch dew, and the water from these bowls never drunk on the same day it is gathered, because it must think about where it wants to go; mirrors of polished alloy hung where the sun is heavy to scare off the gliders or to summon them, depending on the angle; fire kept in clay hearts—small, closed vessels where a coal can rest and sigh—and used to scent a room, to signal a birth or a death, or to warm the amber before it is sung.

I would live inside a system that notices, that answers, that reshapes itself around my passage. The place is alive not as a single will but as a parliament of processes, each voice loud enough to matter, each absence felt as a change in pressure. I would not live here so much as be lived by the place, and the distinction, after a time, would stop being a useful one.

And always there would be the dampness—the cool thread in the hot cloth. It would bead on my brow, collect in the axils of my clothing, taste like metal and memory. I would let it linger, never quite wiping it away, because I would know that this vast, roaring, living height is tender, and that to live here is to be always on the edge of tears the place never quite lets fall.
