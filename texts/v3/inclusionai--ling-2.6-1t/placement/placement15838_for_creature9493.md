I would find myself on the second day of a week that tasted like copper, standing just inside the seam where the wall had decided to remember being a floor only the night before. 

I would be—at least for the first breath—an apprentice to the Maintenance, though the title would sit on me like a coat three sizes too large, sleeves still smelling of someone else’s stress. My joints would ache with the memory of a geometry I no longer trusted: fingers that expected to grip nothing but air, knees that kept trying to lock when the floor tilted into a ceiling. I would wear the city’s skin already, a patchwork of ceramic scales over my forearms, each plate a scar or a promise that if one seam tore, the next would hold. My pockets would be full of chalk and the soft, cool breath that pools in hollows, and my boots would read the floor for stories the Maintenance hadn’t written down yet.

For a time I would be mostly listening—learning the difference between a rivet’s song and its sigh, between the skitter of claws pausing in unison and the city’s own groan as it accepted a burden it hadn’t chosen. I would press my palm to a girder and feel the thrumming of engines that lived longer than their governors’ clocks, feel the ghost of every shape a certain press had ever dreamed into metal. I would taste the air like old coins and memorize how light bends when it bounces too many times off angled faces, how shadows become something you can lean against when you’re tired.

Then, when the pocket exhaled and the bruised stripe overhead thinned into something almost like morning, I would become a translator. Between the people and the place. Between the Drifters tasting seams for weakness and the Sky-Scrapers banking on drafts the factories hadn’t named. I would learn to speak in pulses, in the click of knuckles on metal asking permission before leaning, in the language of vibration humming through the soles of my boots. I would feed the Glass-Lichen careful words and record the color of its answers; I would leave bright scrap at the margins of Drifter tides like a treaty: we keep our edges, you keep the sharp things from slicing us.

Later, if the pocket clenched tighter, I would become a temporary verb, wedging myself into the argument when a wall decided to tighten, learning to brace like the Maintenance do—half-flesh, half-utility, metabolizing the city’s stress into a patience I could leave behind when my shift ended. I would map the ledge as it remembered itself, arrows warning that here the corridor narrows with hurry, that here the stair runs upward into a ceiling that, for three steps, insists on being a floor.

And when evening came, or what passed for it in the cool metallic dark, I would drop into an alcove where the stone was marginally warmer, curling into a fault and wondering which of me would weather and which would erode. I would be new here, still learning to hold myself like a rivet seeking a seat, still negotiating with pressure—and yet already becoming part of the arrangement, the temporary, roaring treaty between stone and will, held in a seam that refuses to let go, inventing itself again while everyone, even me, is still blinking sleep from their eyes.
