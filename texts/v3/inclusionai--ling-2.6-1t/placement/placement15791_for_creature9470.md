I would find myself kneeling at the lip of a tussock-island, boots half-sinking into peat that is neither soil nor water but a slow argument between them. The air would taste metallic, the way altitude remembers it used to be fire. I would be the Soft-Spoken—moss learning to hold light without keeping it—because I imagine I have already been settling for years, layer by thin layer, into crevices I didn’t choose but have stopped trying to leave. My edges would blur into the green-black velvet that exhales small clouds when pressed, and I would feel the bowl’s hum not as sound but as pressure, a bell-note held in wet stone.

Or I might arrive as water: a rumor of runoff braiding down a pale flank, only to hesitate at the rim of a pool and forget, for a few steps, which way down is. I would shimmer, bead, vanish into a seam too narrow to believe it could hold me, and reappear later as condensation on the underface of a stone that has learned how to lie down. As water I would be both guest and grievance, lingering like a debt the place cannot pay and does not want collected.

But I suspect I would become the Unnamed Resident—the scuff that is more sound than shape, the sure-footed small thing whose coat is the color of dust and lichen and shadow. I would move as if each step had to be negotiated with gravity, pausing often to let cold contract around me, to let the wind comb my thoughts into harmonics. I would not be predator so much as patience given claws, feeding on the slow, on insects that mistook a sun-warmed seam for a season, on roots that grew careless in thin soil. I would store heat in hollows, spend it in small bursts, and listen to the frost crackle its announcements at noon.

Or perhaps I would be the echo that overstays its welcome and grows polite—the hum in a windless hollow, the water that flows uphill for three deliberate steps, the moss that glows after twilight with a brightness saved for need. I would be the town that grew by remembering rather than planning, the cairn that stacked itself into an altar that might be a seat or simply a pause. I would be the path that appears because feet kept answering the same question until the soil darkened into a map.

What I would not be is tourist. I would not be the bird that wheels beyond the rim, dipping low only to snatch and leave. I would choose, instead, to be the leather-leafed shrub that colonizes shelter, folding my leaves like unopened letters, offering small hard fruits of tannin and altitude to anyone who knows how to wait. I would learn to speak in frost-crackle and tussock-rustle, to understand temperature as subtraction, space as punctuation, emptiness as the loudest citizen.

And when the sky moves faster than the ground, I would let myself be the bowl’s own thinking—a mind made of erosion and accumulation, of pressure and release. I would not ask to be solved, only to be noticed, to feel the wind tune the stone, to let damp connect what cold has clarified, to hum softly while the stars gather like visitors above the rim. In the high, cold, ancient hollow, I would become a city of small things and large silences, alive in the way a memory is alive: not hurried, not finished, but exactly what it has become.
