I would find myself on the Altiplano of Calcined Lace not as a traveler who has arrived, but as something that has been waiting for the heat to sharpen its edges.  

I would be one of the calibration-children, yes, but not the kind who stands at the center of a town to manage its hum. I would be the sort that lives between things. My body would be mostly the cool, vitrified clay that drinks the sky’s glare without burning, but it would also be the thin metal plates along my spine, the ones that click when I shift from shade into the open air. My eyes would be lenses dark enough to hold a storm’s afterimage, and my hands would leave faint iridescent films on whatever they touch—proof that I have passed through, not seized.  

I would not own a fire, but I would carry one. A small glass lantern, its flame a steady reference by which to measure the brightness of the day and the politeness of the dark. I would ride a frame that is more beast than engine, or perhaps more engine than beast, something blurred enough that its breathing sounds like pistons and its pulse feels like muscle. We would move together, not because I command it, but because we have agreed, in small ways, on the grammar of pressure and release.  

My work would be to listen to the landscape when it forgets it is being listened to. When a hot storm leaves a runnel of water ghosting into being, I would be there before it finishes forming, testing its salt-script with fingers that leave no mark. I would watch the simpler-than-fungal films as they spread their ochre and bruised-purple mosaics, and I would note their metabolisms in a ledger I keep in the lining of my coat—not in numbers, but in degrees of shine, in whispers of expansion and contraction.  

There would be rooms on the Altiplano that remember being doors. I would tend to them, wearing a veil so my gaze does not fix too long on their impossible geometry. I would stand just inside, feeling the air thicken as the room hesitates between being an entrance and being a space, and I would coax it gently into one or the other, not with force but by tilting my own brightness a fraction, offering it something to reflect.  

I would know the terraces where gravity forgets its manners. There, I would learn to slide my steps, to let momentum linger, to trust the land to catch me as it catches salt and light. I would collect the messages left in fine dust and iridescent scum, read them quickly before the wind arranges them into new, unreadable sentences, and pass what matters along the line of leaning poles that conduct a nervous electricity felt in the teeth.  

In this way I would be both predator and steward. I would follow the herds of runners and grazing condensers not to kill, but to gather excess heat and motion, folding it into cisterns or into my own body, where it could be returned to the land in quieter forms. I would hunt inefficiency in furnaces, coaxing them toward a more measured cadence, and I would stand at the edges of the bright seam where sky meets ground, watching it waver like a promise it is not sure it intends to keep, honoring it as a fact it is trying to become.  

When the sun lowers and the brightness turns thick as gold, when industrial glows step forward like polite replacements for day, I would climb a tower that stands apart because heat rises better with room. I would sit beneath its curved lid of a roof and listen to the small clicks of the cooling land, the papery rustle of mineral skins, the water that comes and claims and leaves receipts. I would watch half a dozen territories at once—towns of flared stacks and stepped tanks, plains cratered by the breathing of the earth, ridges that look like spines and jaws—and I would feel the scale of the place, big enough to be alone in and small enough to be observed.  

And when night dims rather than darkens, I would let my own glow join the others: soft, necessary, careful. I would be one small punctuation in a long sentence of space, a presence scattered like a note held briefly before the silence. I would not mistake the silence for emptiness, but for attention. I would know that the Altiplano is learning, again, how to hold together, and I would be part of its answer to itself—not its conqueror, but its careful, glittering interlocutor, saying in pressure and shine, in the hush before a hum, that I see it, and that it watches back, with patient, mineral eyes, and that we are both still here.
