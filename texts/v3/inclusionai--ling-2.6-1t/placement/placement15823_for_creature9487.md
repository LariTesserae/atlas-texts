You would find yourself in the seam between breath and machine, where the horizon leans away and the drizzle stains rather than cleans.  

You would be a small, many-legged gloss upon the stone—neither Hush-Stitcher nor Burrow Choir, but something that learned from both. Your body would be a pocket of silence wrapped in velvet dark, your joints oiled by the copper-tinted rain. You would not hunt; you would tune. With each step you would press a pad of felted fiber into the runnels left by almost-water, listening for the thrum the engines left behind in the rock, and answering it with a faint chime from plates tucked beneath your scales.  

Your skin would map patience in tessellations that looked like circuitry only when you were still. When you moved, you would comb the air the way winged ones comb thermals, trimming dissonance from the subterranean music so that it never swells into ruin. You would sip metallic drizzle not to feed but to remember, carrying each drop to the lichen-carpeted lips of stone so that the rock could learn once more how to be porous.  

In time you would grow barnacles of your own—tiny, heat-siphoning valves that open and close with the rhythm of distant pistons, turning your silhouette into a kind of engine that breathes for the sake of breathing. Beetles would graze on your cooling fins, polishing you until you reflect the starless sky, and in that reflection you would see yourself not as a being but as a seam: the place where erosion and industry, predator and pasture, dark and duration decide, again and again, to hold each other.  

You would not have a name. You would be known by the missing hum you leave behind when you pause, and by the faint glow that rises from your footprints—a blue-green note that says the machinery is being attended to, that the balance is heavy but intact, that the continent-sized lung is breathing damp and warm, and you are the small, necessary valve through which it learns to sigh without coughing itself apart.
