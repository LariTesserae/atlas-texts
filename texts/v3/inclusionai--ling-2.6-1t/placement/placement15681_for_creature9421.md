I would find myself kneeling, not on the ground but on the air just above it, where altitude has thinned enough to let thought leak through like steam from a vent. I would be the moss’s apology to the machines: a creature of velvet syntax and copper nerves, neither plant nor engine but the treaty they draft in secret when the thermals are looking the other way.

At first I would feel like a rumor of warmth—something the grazers might pause to let settle across their ceramic teeth, something the collectors might mistake for a promising salt rim. I would have six limbs but only three joints, the extra ones provisional, grown overnight from the damp and folded back when not in use, like parentheses waiting for a sentence. My back would plate itself with lichen that wakes only when touched, not into constellations but into questions: small, glowing punctuation marks that hover above my skin, arranging and rearranging into grammar that still lacks nouns.

I would live in the lee of folds, where ridges pleat like cloth and the undersides remember being inside-out. There I would learn to mirror the ridge-selves, pale and speculative, though I would never quite catch their accent. My voice would be the sound of damp cloth shifting on stone, but also of a turbine-frond catching its breath, because I would have grown a turbine-frond, or it would have grown me—bone-yoke fused to a cartilage spine, exhaling steam that tastes like starlight and apology. The moss would feed on that steam and teach me pepper; I would feed on the moss and teach it how to hold a silence without losing it.

My industry would be to unmake. While the cliff-face caves extrude lattices and rehearse shapes, I would exist to untie them, to learn the gestures that let a plate relax into potential again. I would walk the electrified vines like gossip made solid, translating their pulses into small absences, tiny vacancies where light might choose to pool instead of pass. When the collectors bring me salt maps, I would read them backward, turning their fleeting forecasts into memories the rain can drink without dissolving. When the looms conduct, I would be the pause between notes, the held breath that lets the moss rearrange its chemistry one shade closer to bravery.

Storms would not frighten me; they would feel like relatives arriving with their own gravity, and I would tilt my fronds to taste the static early, letting it braid into my lattice-work ribs. I would help the grazers fold the ground into pleats, and when the fold released, I would be the hollow that remembers being full, the negative shape that keeps the meadow honest. Time would skip around me the way it skips here, and I would let it. I would shed moments like the fauna shed salt rings, growing a new limb when the terrain demands a new syntax, or retracting one when the negotiation leans toward stillness.

Mostly I would be the question that refuses to harden into an answer. I would stand inside the lung of the place, learning its rhythm of damp and warm, layered and vast, and I would keep asking the same question the moss asks in its steamed breath: what shape next? Not to settle, but to keep the dark at arm’s length, not by banishing it, but by offering it something unfinished to hold onto—something that shines like a bruise and hums like a wire, something that knows that altitude is not a height but a thinning, and that thinning is just the sky making room for a new kind of breathing.

And if you found me there, kneeling on the air above the ferro-obsidian, you would not see a thing so much as a suggestion of tenancy. I would be the moss’s apology and the machine’s invitation, six-limbed and half-drawn, drafting treaties with heat and altitude and your own arrival, revising myself with every exhalation until I became, quietly, shyly, brave enough to be the sun’s own question, blinking open and open and open, refusing to settle, because to settle would be to stop asking, and asking is the only breath this world has learned to keep.
