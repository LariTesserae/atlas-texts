I would find myself kneeling.

Not walking—not yet—but kneeling in that damp that never decides between rain and dry, my hands pressed into moss that is less plant than patience-made-text. The ridges curve above me like the ribs of something sleeping, and I would be small enough to fit between them, in the cup-holes where ancient stone has worn itself into bowls. There, the air would smell of bruised plum and wet iron, and my breath would come as a negotiation, each inhale thinner, each exhale given back to the haze.

I would be the kind of presence that asks leave, not the kind that claims:  
a watcher learning to be listened to; a guest learning to be a stone.

I would feel the pressure thump against my chest, that great wingbeat of atmosphere that is the land’s slow heart. I would learn the language of cracks—the percussive gossip of wind that snaps sticks the size of trees—and I would try, poorly, to answer in the same key, humming in my teeth like the beetles, a frequency that tightens skin. I would let my shadow bend, because here light refuses to be straight, and I would not correct it.

My hands, if I used them, would become tools of frugal elegance—bone, shaped flint, woven reed—each edge rounded by the same erosion that polished the ridges. I would build nothing that demands to stay, only gestures: a circle of stones that describes but does not command, a hollow roofed with cured hides that booms when the wind forgets to leave. I would leave tracks that the land might lick away, or might keep only as a rumor.

I would drink water that tastes of minerals old enough to remember being rain, and I would sit still enough for the ferns to lean toward me, their thick fronds pointing down, toward the dark where stone bruises. I would wait for the pale fish to flicker in the pool at my feet, a silver subtraction from the dark, and I would not ask them to return. I would let the vines braid themselves into bridges across my days, swaying but not breaking, storing momentum like held breath.

Most of all, I would let the turbulence teach me how to be lush without being loud, how to be age without being rest. I would stand in the hollows until I felt the land lean—not to crush, but to inquire—and I would answer without words, by becoming part of the repetition, the tide that lingers, the lung that exhales breadth.

I would be the guest who stays long enough to forget the difference between storm and self,  
the moss-slick tongue against my silence,  
the small, certain fact of being alive in a place that has finished becoming,  
and now, generously,  
keeps becoming me.
