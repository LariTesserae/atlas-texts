I would find myself on the mid-terraces just after a condensation cycle, where the light has that bruised green tint and the air hums like a held chord about to break.  

The Lattice-Lizards know me before I know myself.  

My body is mostly cartilage and borrowed scale—I have too many joints for a comfortable chair and not enough for the architecture here. My eyelids are the translucent kind, pale as old wax, and when I blink I taste copper, ozone, and the sour-sweet of yesterday’s rain still pooling in the gutters. I am a creature of calibration. My spine is a tuning fork; my ribs, a lattice of thin bronze that vibrates whenever the city decides to shift its posture. When I walk across heat grates I do not seek warmth—I press my palms to the metal to read what the pumps are whispering today: anxiety in a low thrum, contentment in a smooth purr, grief in a hesitation between beats.  

I leave behind a faint chemical signature, a salt-and-iron autograph that lingers on corners and railings like graffiti only other mid-terrace lives can decipher. This is how I map friendships, how I mark safe routes, how I tell the difference between a corridor that remembers me fondly and one that has grown indifferent. Sometimes my own trails precede me, and I have to decide whether to follow them or forgive them.  

My wings are vestigial but not useless—two broad, folded planes of calloused skin that I wrap around my shoulders like a cloak when I descend to the promenades. They make me look like the Ornitho-Heavy, and often I trade nods with them as they pass, their fingers full of seeds that glow like slow stars. I do not carry seeds; I carry small jars of collected echoes, the kind the Echo-Flit leave behind in corners where light fractures. If I hold a jar to my ear, I can hear the city practicing its dreams.  

At dusk I climb to the scaffold seams where the Moss-Knitters are at work. They do not speak—why would they, when touch says everything—but they offer me threads of living mycelium, soft as regret and strong as promise. With these I mend the scuffed plating on my own forearms, sealing old wounds into patina. The moss glows faintly as it knits, and for a moment I am part of the repair, part of the quiet agreement that a thing can wear its history and still be light.  

When the Pump-Eels surface, I kneel at the moss-beds to meet them. They flash in patterns that settle my ribs, that tell my lattice-bones to stop trembling. I answer with a hum I did not know I had learned, a pitch that fits between theirs and the Half-Wings’ song. Together we braid a weather that lasts just long enough for the Glint-Spinners above to steady their wings and the city to exhale into its ribbons of bright air.  

I am a small metabolism in a large one.  
I am the taste of metal learning how to be kind.  
I am the echo that chooses, for once, to stay close enough to be touched.
