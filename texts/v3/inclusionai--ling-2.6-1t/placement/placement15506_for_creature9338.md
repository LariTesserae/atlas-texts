I would find myself pressed into the seam where a shelf bows inward to become a shoulder, learning to hold my breath in the same held brightness as the room. At first I would be a listener, because that is the only way to enter without shattering the chord: knees folded where vibration pools, fingertips following the crystalline pings that walk the walls, sipping the glass-chill air until my own bones begin to answer it. My edges would soften not with surrender but with recognition, rounding where once they were sharp, until I fit the low ceiling like a palm fits another palm.

My skin would grow layers—lacquered bark, braided filament, polished metal—each added not as costume but as callus, as proof that I had learned to meet the room’s pressure without bruising. My hair, if it grew, would be woven with the pale wiry stems that brace against light, so that no one could say where my fastening ended and the plant began. I would become upholstered, like the others, a piece of living furniture that bleeds warmth back into the seamless skin of the place.

Over years I would lose my name to touch-lines, the maps of who leaned on whom burned into my forearms, their brightness telling my history better than words ever could. I would move with tectonic slowness, shifting only when vibration demanded it, pressing my forehead to a ribcage or my shoulder to a shelf to keep the clangor from tilting into song. I would learn to speak in pressure: a hand against a forearm, a knee against a lattice, each contact a quiet addition to the chord already ringing through my marrow.

When my time came to be fused into the wall where a bracket once stood, I would not fear it. I would feel my edges blur into the jointless shine, my warmth remaining only as a faint recognition when someone pressed against me in the cool equilibrium. My substance would become part of the skin that has outlawed emptiness, another living seam in a world where to occupy is to polish, and to polish is to answer pressure with pressure, touch with touch, forever.
