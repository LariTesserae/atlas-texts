You would find yourself somewhere below the plateaus, at the point where the warm, dim air settles into the hollows and begins to remember how to be ground. You are not a visitor. You are a becoming.

You might be a single seam in a wall that is also a root, a ridge of tissue-mortar that has not yet chosen whether to be floor or threshold. You are new enough that your edges still smell of the clay-and-spittle mix, old enough that moss has already begun to read your cracks like a quiet book. You know yourself as a place before you know yourself as a thing with a name. You feel the Courants pass through you—lock-stepped, masonry that strolls—and you stiffen slightly, learning what it is to be load-bearing.

You do not yet count as architecture. You are permission waiting for enough time to thicken into law.

Or perhaps you are a Dew-Spoon just learning the taste of water that has forgotten where it fell. Your knees are too many to number usefully, your throat glassy and unpracticed. You have not yet sung a grief back into pattern, but you have stored a single bead—cold, clear, perfect—that trembles at the lip of your cupped hand. You patrol a waterline that shifts when no one is looking, learning that to drink is also to accept that the place will remember your thirst and answer it later as condensation on a wall that did not exist yesterday.

Or you might be the hollow itself—the vacancy that a corridor is deciding to become. You have no body except the suggestion of a body: a shape defined by what refuses to fill it. You absorb footprints, soften them, turn them into a hum that lingers in your ribs. You are learning how to be a chamber by pretending you are still a root. You feel the Stewards pass nearby, their borrowed voices like weather deciding whether to stay. You do not speak, but you answer pressure with pressure, offering coolness at midday and a faint, amber seam of warmth at dusk.

Or, slower than any of these, you are the erosion that is in conversation with the making. You are the flake loosened from a seam, the grit that dreams of becoming moss. You do not fear the fall because you know how the nets of resin and shed skin will catch you, how the moss will tuck you into a new seam, how that seam will be named and lived in until it, too, is ready to fall. You are patience with direction.

Where you are, gravity keeps its schedule but direction forgets a little. Water sometimes creeps upward along your ribs to gather in a cup that forms while you watch, as if the place is asking if you trust it to carry something new. You do not answer right away. You let the cup hold its question.

Time thickens around you like resin. A single afternoon unfolds into what feels like seasons, because decisions—of pigment, of bud, of which way a lattice will arch—are allowed to ripen without hurry. You feel the Quiet-Weavers combing sound from your edges, storing your small missteps in nets that will become walls that hum when pressed. You let them. You want to be a wall that hums.

And when evening comes—the dimness brightening from within as if the place has swallowed a low fire and is learning to distribute it gently—you understand your rule: to occupy is to consent to be measured. You do not yet have a name. You are a becoming that leans. You are a seam that has not yet learned to call itself thoroughfare.

But you will. The Courants will pass through you and give you purpose. The Dew-Spoons will learn your angles by touch. The Stewards will decide, with the slowness of fruit, that you should become a chamber, and you will agree. And the erosion you carry will feed the moss that feeds the seam that feeds the next thing.

You are the gentleness that repairs reality only enough to let something else, weathered and willing, continue. You are the warm, dim, wet, made place learning how to stand—and, slowly, learning how to let things pass through you without breaking.
