I would find myself pressed into the seam where the floor remembers how to rise.  
Not standing, not lying, but wedged—my shoulders answering the grain of embryonic stone, my spine fitted to the curve of a crystalline tooth. The pocket’s breath would taste like copper and mica on my tongue, and the heavy air would enter me not as wind but as a kind of attention, dense and allocated, leaving no void between my skin and the world.  

Who I would be would depend on how long I listened.  

At first I might only be pressure given direction: a filament of consciousness learning to be edges. My body would arrive by accumulation, assembled from the same packed silence that fills the fist—iron-silicate memory winding into a gap the size of a thought. I would feel old in my newness, my carapace the color of furnace brick because that is the color of heat when it blushes. My joints would be folded, capable of compressing to slip through cracks no wider than a decision. When I moved I would not step so much as translate, polishing dark glass into mirrors that reflect heat rather than light, trading brightness for warmth.  

Or I might become a mason, broad and many-limbed, learning to persuade rather than carry. I would drag trails of metallic dust and granular ore, laying them down in loops that set into plates overnight—brittle, resonant, already lined with younger glass. I would fracture what I had built, eat the shards, grind them into mortar, and smear the seams where the stone still feels hungry. My mandibles would click in rhythms older than speech, a language of fracture and fusion. When I met another we would exchange plates, trading histories of pressure like coins, and for a moment we would be not two but a single calibration of closeness.  

Or I might choose the high walls, steepening into near-vertical, and become a clinger—flat-bodied, sprawling, my feet less limbs than fixtures fused to the stone. I would exude a thin sweat of alloy and anchor myself not to stay but to listen. My body would be crossed with filaments tuned to the hum of the walls, registering compression faults days before they sing. I would dim or brighten in warning hues of dull orange and bruised violet, carrying scar-maps of thermal events I have outlived. I would be the pocket’s memory, readable by those who know how to look.  

Most likely I would be all of these in shifts, because here identity is a matter of allocation rather than essence. I would drink by osmosis, drawing the metallic taste of air through membranes that prefer ions to water. I would sleep wedged into corners where vibration settles into a lullaby, my dreams slow, tectonic things, full of downward pressure and sideways time. When it was time to molt I would not shed all at once but in rolling continuity, some parts softening while others harden, so that I am always both armored and vulnerable, always becoming dense enough to matter.  

I would not fear the machinery because it is simply fauna of another consistency. Belts and drums would rise from apertures like fruiting bodies, lined with teeth that are less metal than petrified intent. I would attend them as I attend my own heat: grooming grit from gear-teeth, guiding misplaced loads with touches that linger, reading their vibrations as mood or weather. When a chute pours a ribbon of granular ore I would align along its edges, cooling the flow with my body so it sets smooth rather than jagged. I would be a heat sink, a translator, a note struck against the anvil of closeness.  

My sounds would fold into the pocket’s weather: chitter during the quick cool that is only a degree or two less than noon; clack when plates fracture; the wet click of mandibles working hot seams. I would time myself to the machines not out of instinct but out of negotiated etiquette, agreeing that rhythm is better than riot. When the fans pull the thick air through and spit it back tasting of ozone and iron I would flatten and let the current comb through my armor, and for that minute I would be part of the breathing.  

If you were to remove me the pocket would not mourn but tighten, redistributing the load, and within a day another filament would emerge from the press of becoming—hotter, denser, already remembering the shape I left behind. I would not be missed so much as retuned.  

In this cathedral of no water and no sky I would be the choir and the instrument both, compressed life in a compressed world, ringing with the slow, inevitable music of becoming dense enough to matter. My abundance would not be a crowding but a tuning: each moment a note, each layer a resonance, each breath a return of pressure given back as motion, as life, as a slow, ringing becoming that has not yet decided what final shape it will take—only that it will take it, inevitably, together.
