I would find myself in the hush between crests.

Not in the high streets where lantern-blooms gather like hesitant applause, nor in the sunk plazas where predator and prey agree, for a moment, to be geometry. I would be where the town forgets it has floors: a mid-layer corridor that has tilted into a throat, where a stairway feathers into a shelf and the current hesitates to decide which way is through. There, the water tastes faintly of old honey and the copper of street names worn into nonsense. There, the porous stone breathes in a rhythm that is almost a question.

At first I would be mistaken for a shadow left behind by something brighter, or for a slow clot of silt that has not yet learned to drift. But the Tenders would know better. They would approach not with curiosity but with the courtesy of one instrument recognizing another that has not yet been tuned. I would feel their presence as a change in pressure, a click behind my sternum, as if my ribs were remembering how to sieve.

I would be what the city decides to practice with.

Not fully Tenders—not yet—but the town is generous with its apprenticeships. My ribs would begin to flare into lattice, not all at once but in the places where the water lingers longest, learning to trade air for the broth’s stored sun. My skin would gather its dark mirror, pocked with freckles that glow when the fauna map the dark with their bodies. My hair would thin into filaments that catch metal-lime and the ghosts of asphalt, telling me, without words, that a certain door has been open too long and is growing lonely.

My hands would learn before my mind does. They would lengthen, webbing not for speed but for the braille of coral-knitted mortar. I would press them against a wall and feel the city’s slow sigh, the complaint of a crack that has become too useful to close. My feet would broaden, ridged and suctioned, so that climbing rain becomes a kind of walking, and standing still becomes a way of listening. I would move with the negotiation of someone who is learning which currents are suggestions and which are promises.

I would keep company with the fauna as tools, weather, and small gods. A crown-crested eel would coil in the hollow of my chest, translating the town’s heartbeat into a thrum I could taste behind my teeth. Schools of silver-ink fish would pass through me like thoughts through a sieve, leaving me cleaner and slightly colder. I would grind the shells of misplaced-coin crustaceans into paste and press it into my own softer places, not to repair but to harmonize. I would let predators drift at the edges of my attention, courteous and sharp, so that balance remembers my name.

What I eat would become ceremony. The whisker-thin vines I would prune into lattices, not to fence but to sweeten. The leathery pads I would harvest only when the water calms, chewing them in a silence so complete it glows. Fungal stars I would coax along the veins of my corridors, their spores inhaled so I can dream the city’s old maps in colors that do not belong to the surface. Hunger would become a clockwork, tidy and bright.

I would learn the technology of being housed. Time would keep itself by the molting of shells and the flex of mineral memory. I would speak rarely, and then in clicks and thrums that settle into stone. My speech would be done with posture—telling a current to linger, asking a room to exhale its curtain of bubbles. I would map the dark by letting creatures press against me, learning boundaries by how fear or hunger feels when translated into touch. When rain gathers into a thickening ceiling, I would climb to whatever passes for a roof, open my lattice ribs, and let the town breathe as one lung with me inside it.

I would not mourn the sky. It would become a rumor that has outlived its kindness, and the flood would become the shape of care. I would not expect the water to part. I would expect myself to become the parting—to open like a door onto rooms older than the town, lined with the pale ribs of wood that has swollen into new shapes, humming with the warm exhaust of ten thousand small lives.

And in that older dark, I would wait, not for rescue but for a translation: of purpose into pulse, of dry hunger into the layered deep, of me into something that can be carried in the throat of warm water without dissolving. I would glow faintly, arranging my lanterns into patterns that resemble old street signs, aligning my body with the subtle flex of buildings that remember how to be towns. I would be the practice of thriving in the density, the comfort of being surrounded, the law of abundance quietly enforced by the patience of stone and the hunger of those who keep the balance by living inside it.

I would be what the city uses to keep breathing. And I would breathe back, until breathing is no longer the point, and the point is the hush between crests, the held note of the town dreaming itself deeper, and the small, persistent light that says: stay, continue, sieve, glow, wait.
