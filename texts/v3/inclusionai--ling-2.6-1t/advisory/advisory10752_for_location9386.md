How to prepare for the Room of Altitude

I. First, unlearn the word “ground.”
The Room is not under you; it is happening around you. Treat altitude as a permission slip, not a measurement. Before you cross the threshold, practice “light consent”: spend an hour with a helium balloon tied to your wrist, letting it tug you into micro-rebalances. Notice how your ankles learn to negotiate weight that isn’t yours. In the Room, gravity will ask your opinion. Answer with soft knees and a willingness to be wrong about which way is down.

II. Dress for damp metaphysics.
1.6 wetness is not rain; it is atmosphere remembering it was ocean. Wear a membrane suit that can inhale and exhale without deciding between waterproof and absorbent—a second skin that puckers like a raisin and plumps like a grape at the same pace as the Room’s coin-sized tarns. Carry gaiters lined with phase-change gel that turns from silver to mirror as droplets bead and lift; it will teach you when water is choosing to fall.

III. Armor against low-contrast light.
The sky’s dimness (1.3) compresses shadows into bruises. Bring lenses that do not brighten but translate: lenses that map luminosity into texture, so you can read the cloud-metal lid’s ribs like braille. A wide-brimmed hat whose underside is matte-black velvet will carve a personal horizon; without it, you may forget where sky ends and thought begins.

IV. Train your hearing to handle generosity.
Fauna loudness (1.9) is a density, not a volume. Before you go, spend time in rooms filled with tuned glass and thin metal, learning to separate signal from architecture. Memorize a simple rhythm you can whistle backward—the Room’s creatures respect visitors who can answer in counterpoint. Buy or borrow a “thrum-catcher,” a palm-sized disc of resonant ceramic that you can hold like a seashell; it will show you which frequencies have weight and which can be spent to buy safe footing.

V. Map the half-made.
Erosion is 0.1, geography embryonic. Bring a notebook bound in something that stains easily—tea or beet ink, something that will bloom when it meets the Room’s mineral sweat. Sketch what is suggested, not what is: rims that might become walls, bowls that have not yet decided to be basins. Carry a soft hammer of basalt wrapped in velvet; use it only to tap, never to break. The echoes you coax will tell you where the ground is still practicing its name.

VI. Negotiate with engineered grace.
Tech (2.8) here is spider-silk and decision. Do not touch the alloy ribbons unless they have invited you by humming in a key that sits comfortably behind your sternum. Learn to read spherical nodes: when they glow warm amethyst, step lightly; when they flicker mercury, they are rearranging local consent and you should freeze mid-breath. Respect built things (1.4). The spire with no door is not a puzzle; it is a question. Bow to it anyway. Platforms that phase in and out of tangibility prefer witnesses who do not stare too hard; look at them from the corner of your eyes, like stars.

VII. Learn the etiquette of fauna.
Creatures are abundant and many choose to be more verb than noun. The winged seed-pods with crystal wings do not fly; they sing the air into holding them. Carry a small pouch of glass beads etched with your name in a simple tongue. If a pod asks for tribute, offer one bead; it will add your name to its song for a time, and you will glide farther with less effort.

The liquid fauna that climbs glass-grass in spirals is older than it looks. Do not step in its pools; they are libraries of future motion. If you must cross, remove your shoes and let your feet memorize the temporary choreography. You may find you can walk on walls for minutes afterward, and you should use that gift gently.

For the hunters of folded light and the grazers of mist, remember this contract: movement is currency. If you freeze, you are invisible; if you move, you are part of the conversation. Practice “still walking”—advancing without committing to travel.

VIII. Prepare for weather as diplomacy.
Storms here last minutes and arrive as luminous beads. Carry a lattice umbrella open on one side only, so you can let selected weather in to taste. The warm drafts rising from the weeps will try to braid themselves into your hair. Braid them back; it is polite. When gravity lightens, exhale slowly; when it deepens, let your exhale pool at your feet like a loyal dog.

IX. Pack lightly but meaningfully.
Essentials:
- A vial of your own tears or sea-water, sealed in flexible glass. The Room respects water that remembers.
- A small bell whose pitch matches your resting heart rate. Ring it when you feel lost; the local echoes will guide you toward yourself.
- A map made of soluble paper. Draw your route in honey; the Room’s fauna and streams will redraw it as needed, and you will learn more from their edits than from your intentions.
- A jar with a loose lid to catch silence. It is rare and expensive; you can trade it later for kindness or clarity.

X. Mind and body rehearsals.
Meditate on states of matter that refuse to choose. Practice being a question instead of an answer. When you speak, favor incomplete sentences; the Room’s weirdness (2.0) favors listeners who allow themselves to be clarified by surprise.

Stretch your ankles and wrists daily; the Room’s dynamic (2.4) favors those who can hinge and pivot. Learn to fall without choosing a direction—fall like a leaf practicing its spiral, and trust that the Room will catch you with its sparse vegetation or its engineered fields or the shoulder of a grazing mist-beast.

XI. Arrival protocol.
Step across the threshold with your weight distributed 60/40, favoring the lighter foot. Announce yourself not by name but by change: “I am becoming lighter” or “I am learning to hold wetness.” The Room will answer in the hush of droplets falling all at once, in the silver veins looping underfoot, in the cloud-metal lid shifting its ribs.

XII. Departure and aftermath.
When you leave, the Room will give you three gifts, though you may not recognize them until later:
1. A recalibration of mass—you will find you can carry more longing with less strain.
2. A quiet pocket of high, dim light behind your eyes, useful for difficult decisions.
3. A single bead of water that refuses to fall, which will, in time, teach you how to rise.

Leave something small and earnest in return—a note pressed into glass-grass, a melody hummed into a node, a promise to return not as a traveler but as a neighbor.

Final intuition:
Visiting the Room of Altitude is like standing inside a breath that is still deciding its shape. It is loud with becoming, patient with its own edges, and courteous in its strangeness. Prepare to be changed not by extremity but by intimacy—by a small, high place that teaches you how to be light, how to be wet, how to be listened to by the geometry of almost-things. If you go gently, the Room will not let you fall. It will let you practice flying in small, careful increments, and you will return to other worlds with a new vocabulary of elevation, written in water that remembers and light that hesitates.
