Practical intuition first: this place feels like a cathedral built by engineers who learned theology from blueprints. It does not welcome you; it tolerates you, politely, like a librarian tolerating a breath that is slightly too loud. To visit is to enter a system that has spent a long time perfecting its own rules, then forgot to tell anyone what they are. Your preparation should be equal parts engineer, monk, and diplomat. 

Here is how to ready yourself for a city-sized engine that glows, hums, and remembers how to age before it stands entirely still.

### The Philosophy of Arrival
Start by understanding that *height* here is not altitude as you know it. At an effective 1.9 elevation, you are not atop a mountain—you are inside a plateau that has been extruded upward by its own industry. The open scale of 1.7 means you can walk for kilometers and never leave the manufactured plain, but you will feel stacked, surveilled by layers. Gravity will sometimes forget its manners (weirdness 2.8), so treat every corridor as potentially negotiable. If a door feels heavier than it should, assume it is not your imagination; if a hallway seems to have lengthened while you blink, accept it. Do not fight the place’s mild eccentricities; sign in, file a maintenance report if you’re polite, and keep moving.

### Clothing and Microclimate
The temperature is cool but held at a distance by a brightness (2.7) that pools rather than falls. This is not sun; it is accumulated light, glossy and almost audible in its restraint. Bring layered clothing with high reflectivity on the outermost shell—something that can throw back brightness without absorbing heat. The chill is polite, but over hours it is thorough. 

Your base layer should manage condensation. Water here is scarce but present at 0.9, meaning it condenses in deliberate rings around vents and gutters that have been tuned to sing softly. You will encounter damp cuffs, silver rivers of young erosion tracing seams, and fog that tastes faintly of metal salts. A breathable membrane that sheds moisture but does not trap it will keep you from feeling like a half-empty canteen yourself.

Footwear deserves obsession. Surfaces are cool metals and cured composites, slightly yielding under the weight of countless passages. The place is assembled, not risen, so edges are precise but never final. Wear soles that grip but do not mar; magnetic micro-tread or smart-gel pads are ideal. Bring spare insoles; the density of the city exerts subtle, persistent pressure on arches, as if the architecture is trying to memorize your gait.

### Light Discipline
Because the brightness is pooled and persistent, your eyes will adapt quickly—but at a cost. After a few hours, you may begin to see faint afterimages in closed eyelids, as if the city is drafting your vision into its maintenance logs. Pack adjustable tint lenses or adaptive cornea-filters if you have them. If not, train yourself to look at the sparse vegetation (1.2) when you need a rest: sealed terrariums, pipe-bracketed mosses, and alloy-rooted weeds that move in increments so small they resemble stillness. Their greens are deliberate punctuation; they will teach your eyes how to un-focus gently.

At night—or what passes for it—do not expect darkness. The city’s electrified veins pulse faintly, contributing to the brightness without explaining where it comes from. Carry a personal dimmer, something that can subtract light rather than add it. A pocket of true shadow is a courtesy you can extend yourself, and it is also a signal to the locals that you understand restraint.

### Navigating the Density
The fauna here (2.0) is abundant in a manufactured sense: maintenance beetles with lens eyes, scaffold-spiders of braided wire, flock-threads patrolling light-beams, and load-bearers that treat the cityscape as a landscape of tasks. They move with a patience (dynamic 1.4) that feels like a practiced courtesy. 

Do not approach them as wildlife; approach them as colleagues. A slow blink at a flock-thread will often cause it to reroute around you. A load-bearer will accept a small, standardized token of mass—washers, calibrated weights, a cube of spare alloy—as tribute for the right of way. Learn the difference between maintenance fauna and sentient residents; if it has a serial number, it probably has rights. If it hums in a key that matches the local gravity, treat it with extra deference.

The industrial tech (2.2) is satisfied with itself: factories like folded cards, engines the size of rooms that breathe in slow. Elevators rise as if remembering how. To use them, you must offer intention before motion. Speak your destination clearly, even if no one is listening; many lifts respond to vocalized intent because the city prefers legibility to secrecy. If an elevator hesitates, offer it a reason, not a command. Explain why you need to go up. The machinery will often oblige if it believes you understand the cost.

### Managing Weirdness
Weirdness here sits at 2.8, which means reality frays gently along the seams. Gravity forgets its manners in certain alcoves. Durations double over like warm wire. A door may lead to a yesterday that is still under construction. 

Carry a temporal anchor—a heavy watch, a lodestone with a known history, a notebook with yesterday’s date written large. When durations stretch, mark them. When gravity softens, plant your feet deliberately and exhale slowly; your breath will often restore local normalcy because the city respects biological rhythms. If you encounter a yesterday under construction, do not touch the scaffolding. Instead, ask the workers what phase they are in. If they answer in rivets, you are in a safe zone. If they answer in echoes, leave.

The mostly quiet soundscape is a courtesy to these instabilities. Keep your voice low. If you must shout, do it in a designated resonance chamber (usually marked by a ring of unrusted alloy). Noise might remind the weirdness that it is being watched, and the place dislikes surprises.

### Sustenance and Water
Because water is scarce but present, treat every condensation ring as a gift. Carry a sterile condenser cup to collect drips from tuned gutters; the water will taste faintly of oil and patience, but it is safe. The sparse vegetation is not for eating; it is for marking thresholds. Look for sealed terrariums with blue internal lighting; these often indicate cafeterias or medical bays where you can obtain paste-like nutrient bars engineered for high-brightness environments.

Eat slowly. The cool air slows digestion, and the density of the city can make you feel as though your metabolism is being audited. Small, frequent meals will keep you legible to the place.

### Rest and Sleep
Sleeping here is an exercise in trust. Because the brightness never entirely fades, your circadian rhythm may decide to become optional. Find a niche near a ventilation grate that sings softly; the sound will mask the hum of electrified veins and help your brain believe it is night. If you can, sleep with one eye slightly open—not in fear, but in practicality. In a city that assembles itself, closed eyes can sometimes be mistaken for maintenance mode.

### Etiquette and Exit
When you leave, do not take samples without asking. The erosion here is young (1.1), and every scratch is noticed. If you must mark your passage, do it in a way that can be polished away: a chalk line, a heat-sensitive smear, a phrase whispered into a keyhole. The place prefers to remain glossy, unscarred, plausible.

Finally, when you reach the edge—there is always an edge, even in stacked plateaus—pause. Touch the boundary and thank it. The high, total city does not require gratitude, but it recognizes legibility. And if you are lucky, it will let you leave without deciding that you have become part of its next assembly.

Visit with humility. Wear brightness like a second skin. Speak softly, carry tokens of mass, and remember that here, patience is not a virtue—it is infrastructure.
