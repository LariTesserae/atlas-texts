Preparing for this place is less like packing for a trip than like preparing to enter a living engine that has learned how to dream. The basin is not hostile in the way a storm or a warzone is hostile; it is *consequential*. It has mass, memory, temperature, and metabolism, and it will notice you, test you, and, if you are not careful, quietly incorporate you into its cycles. Here is how an ordinary human multiverse traveler should ready themselves, step by careful step, for visiting the city that keeps itself warm.

### 1. Reorient Your Senses Before You Arrive
Do not rely on the idea that cities are for people. This one was here before people decided to stay, and it will be here after they have folded back into the soil that remembers them. Before you cross the threshold:

- Spend a day in silence. Let your ears learn to separate constant hum from change. The basin’s baseline is not silence; it is low, textured noise: expansion ticks, distant coupler knocks, the drip-pause-drip of unseen seams. Practice listening until you can tell when a new drip joins the chorus—this is your early-warning system.
- Spend an hour in low light and let your hands learn surfaces without looking. The city speaks through texture: glassy slag, oily bloom, mineral fuzz, rust-crumbs. Bring a few small, common objects (a washer, a scrap of brick, a coil of wire) and handle them with your eyes closed until you can guess temperature and moisture from touch alone. In the basin, your hands will see better than your eyes after dusk.
- Memorize the taste of copper and old rain. When you arrive, the air will taste exactly like that. If it begins to taste sharper or sweeter, the city is telling you something about what is seeping, what is burning, what is about to condense.

### 2. Dress for a Living Furnace
Clothing is not armor here; it is an interface. You are coming to negotiate with heat, moisture, and sharp-edged history. Choose layers that can be shed or added as the city breathes:

- Base layer: a moisture-wicking, form-fitting weave that will not trap salt or oil against your skin. The damp here rises at night; if you stay dry at the skin, you stay healthy.
- Mid layer: thin wool or a modern synthetic that retains warmth when damp but does not absorb oils. The basin’s heat is not constant; it tides. You may be warm in a hallway and chilled on a bridge where wind tunnels between cooling towers.
- Outer layer: a tough, breathable shell with articulated joints—something that can shrug off abrasion from brick, metal, and the mineral fuzz that coats everything. Look for coatings that repel water without trapping condensation inside; you want rain to sheet off, not to steam you.
- Gloves: fingertip-less for fine work, full-finger for climbing. The city’s surfaces vary from polished rail to jagged slag; your hands will pay the price if you are not protected. Choose leather or a high-durability synthetic that can be re-oiled. Oil, here, is both protector and pollutant; learn to love the smell.
- Footwear: ankle support is nonnegotiable. Cambered streets and worn steps will torque an unsupported foot. Soles should be heat-resistant, oil-resistant, and slightly softer than you would choose for wilderness; the city’s geometry is old and human-scaled but unforgiving. A loose stone in a gutter can turn an ankle; a hot expansion joint can blister through a thin sole.
- Headwear: a brimmed hat for daylight (the glare off metal and glass slag is percussive) and a thin beanie for night, when heat rises and condenses and the metal overhead can sweat onto your hair.

### 3. Gear for an Ecology of Exchange
This is not a wilderness where you can drink from a stream and sleep under leaves. The water is brackish, the warmth is managed, and the food chain is built on scavenge and trade.

- Water: carry a double-walled vacuum flask—insulation matters as much as containment. The city’s damp will condense on cold metal; a sweating bottle will be snatched from your hand or will gather mineral crust. Bring a small filter capable of handling heavy metals and saline; you will need it for sumps and gutters when the night is dry and the pumps are quiet.
- Calories: dense, heat-stable, easy to eat with gloves. The fauna here does not care about your hunger, and the people who do will trade information before food. Bring a little extra to barter; calories are currency in a place where every warm ledge is contested.
- Light: a headlamp with a red-light mode and a warm-white mode. Red attracts fewer insects drawn to heat and moisture; warm white lets you see mineral textures without washing them out. Bring spare batteries and keep them in an inner pocket—cold at night can come as a surprise when you descend into shaded sumps.
- Tools: a multi-tool with pliers and a good knife, a length of paracord, a few self-sealing pouches for small parts. The city is repairable and will expect you to repair yourself. A small tube of high-temperature grease is worth its weight; it will protect your tools, quiet squeaks that draw attention, and mark you as someone who understands the grammar of maintenance.
- First-aid: emphasize care for burns (radiant and contact), cuts from sharp metal and glass slag, and care for inhalation. The air carries fine coal dust, sulfur compounds, and metallic aerosols. A simple mask—light, reusable, with replaceable filters—should live in your pocket. Know how to treat a scald; steam here is heavier than you expect and collects in low places.
- Notebooks and charcoal: electronics hate the damp and the mineral creep. Paper survives. Charcoal leaves a mark that is easy to read in low light and does not glare. Draw maps of thermal gradients; note where heat leaks and where damp pools. These maps will be your lifeline after dark.

### 4. Understand the City’s Metabolism
The basin works in pulses. Learn them before you arrive, and learn to read them as you stay.

- Shifts: Factories breathe in and out. When gates lower and chains clank, the streets empty and the fauna swarms. This is not a time to linger in alleys; it is a time to be under cover or moving with purpose along well-lit, high routes.
- Thermal tides: Heat rises as evening thickens. If you need to climb, do it just after sunset when surfaces are still warm but not searing. If you need to descend into sumps or undercrofts, do it before full dark, when condensation is just beginning to bead and not yet dripping.
- Water’s schedule: The damp sheet rises at night. By midnight, metal will weep and gutters will run. If you are crossing exposed iron—catwalks, railings, conveyors—do it early or use pads to insulate your hands and boots. Wet metal is a thief of heat and grip.
- Fauna etiquette: The rats run braided rivers; do not block their flows. The birds peck at condensation; do not stand under ledges where they gather. The cats and raptors patrol thermal gradients; give them the warm corners. Conflict here is wasteful. The fauna is overwhelming in insistence, not malice; treat it like weather.

### 5. Learn the Social Patina
People are moss here—sparse, patterned, hardy. They have adapted to a city that keeps itself warm, and they have learned its moods.

- Speak softly. Noise carries in unexpected ways between brick and metal. A raised voice can trigger echoes that seem to come from nowhere—unnerving in a city already full of small sounds.
- Trade information before goods. People want to know where the heat is leaking, where the damp is pooling, where the fauna is nesting. Give them maps you have drawn; they will give you safe passage, warm corners, warnings about which inspection covers sing when the pressure rises.
- Respect the thresholds. Service alleys, catwalks, and hollow balusters are not shortcuts; they are arteries. Move through them with care, and do not linger at junctions where thermal gradients meet; these are invisible borders between families of workers, scavengers, and the city’s own fauna.
- Windows are diaries. Light steady, blinking, or dark tells you who is working, who is resting, who is gone. Learn to read them; they will tell you when to be invisible and when to be present.

### 6. Move Like Heat
You are an anomaly here—an ordinary human who traveled between worlds to reach a place that has never needed other worlds. Your body is not tuned to the city’s rhythm. Compensate by moving deliberately:

- Match your pace to the city’s expansions and contractions. Feel the tick-tick-tick of metal cooling; step when it ticks, pause when it sighs.
- Let your weight distribute evenly on cambered streets; lean with the slope, not against it. Your ankles will thank you, and your footsteps will make less noise.
- When you climb, use three points of contact and trust your gloves. The city is textured; believe in the texture.
- When you rest, find a thermal gradient that suits your needs: warm enough to keep the damp off, cool enough to think. A warm ledge under a leaking pipe is a poor choice; a warm ledge beside a cooling tower’s exhaust is a cathedral.

### 7. Know When to Leave
The basin is forgiving in the short term and exacting in the long term. It will let you pass through if you are respectful, but it will keep you if you become complacent.

- Set a departure time and a signal. When your flask is half-empty, your notes are full, and you can name the rhythms of the cooling towers, it is time to go.
- Depart the way you came, unless you have learned a better path from the fauna or the people. The city rewards humility; arrogance is a shortcut to a closed gate or a swollen gutter.
- When you cross the threshold out, pause. Let your ears adjust to the relative quiet of elsewhere. The copper-and-rain taste will linger; it will remind you that you visited a living engine that breathes and remembers.

### Final Advice
The basin is not a ruin, but a ripeness. It has turned industry into ecology, heat into comfort, metal into memory. Your preparation should be humble, detailed, and attentive—an act of listening as much as packing. Come with layers, tools, and patience. Come ready to trade, to map, to learn the language of expansion joints and cast-iron grates that have learned to breathe.

If you do, the city will not only let you pass; it will let you belong, for a little while, to its furnace-warm, mineral-furred, breathing life.
