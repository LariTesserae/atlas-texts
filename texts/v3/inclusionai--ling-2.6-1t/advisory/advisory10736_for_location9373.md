How to Visit a Place That Has Forgotten How to Be a Place  
(A Traveler’s Guide to the Glass-Plateau at the Roof of Forgetting)

1. Decide How You Want to Arrive (Because the Place Will Decide Anyway)

The first mistake most multiverse travelers make is assuming they choose the terms of entry. At the Glass-Plateau, that courtesy is revoked. The air is so cool it has settled into a thin metallic skin, and the elevation is high enough that the horizon acts less like a line and more like a verdict. If you step out of a gate without preparing your body for thin, still air, your lungs will remember that they used to expect weather, and they will panic.

Prepare for entry the way you would prepare to step into a photograph that is still developing: gently, without assuming the image will hold your shape. Bring supplemental oxygen not because you will necessarily need it, but because the *rhythm* of breathing it imposes will remind your autonomic nervous system that you are still a guest. Calibrate your implants to ignore barometric nostalgia—altitude sickness here is less about oxygen and more about the land’s indifference to your verticality.

Arrive at dusk if you can manage it. The Plateau’s light is normal, but it feels like a gift left too long in a pocket, and at dusk that familiarity turns tender. If you arrive at noon, the white glare will feel loud; if you arrive at night, the dark here is dense, almost heavy, like wool. Dusk lets you see the mica seams catch the last light and whisper. It is the politest time to arrive.

2. Dress for a Museum That Breathes

Do not wear anything synthetic that expects to stay clean. The dust is not dirt; it is history powdered, crystalline lattices grown in overlapping generations. It will seep into zippers, into the pores of synthetic fabrics, into the microscopic gaps between armor plates. It will not ruin you, but it will remember you.

Wear layers of natural or adaptive cloth—silk, treated wool, smart-weave that can stiffen on command. You want to be able to brush against silica-stalks and not shred your sleeve. Wear gloves that are thin enough to feel but thick enough to repel the fine glitter-dust that clings like guilt. Your boots should have soles that do not chatter; the ground here hums without sound, a density of potential held in check by a dynamic that refuses to change. Vibram soles are fine, but cork or felt will teach you more.

Carry a cloak or shawl the color of bruised violet or slate. Not for camouflage—nothing here hunts by sight so much as by pattern—but for architecture. When you sit, when you kneel, when you want to become part of the landscape rather than an interruption, the cloak will help you fold into the palette. It will also catch the mica-dust and turn you into a temporary landmark, which can be useful if you lose your way and need to spot yourself from a distance.

3. Pack for a Place That Collects

Water is practically nonexistent here, and what little exists is remembered only as a tension in salts that have hardened into glassy skins over the ground. Bring water, obviously, but also bring something to trade it for memory. Small glass vials, ideally ones you have etched with your own breath or sealed with a drop of blood. The fauna here are drawn to containers that have known warmth. They will not steal your water if they think the vessel is more interesting than the contents.

Pack high-density rations that do not crumble. The Plateau’s dust will infiltrate anything flaky and turn it into a geological record of your lunch. Bring dried fruit leather, pressed grain bars, hard cheese sealed in wax. Eat slowly. The act of chewing will remind you that you are still biological, still temporal, still capable of decay—luxuries this place has mostly given up.

Bring a notebook bound in something that will not absorb moisture—metal plates with wax tablets, or paper sealed in oilcloth. The angle and distance weirdness of the Plateau means that straight lines sometimes disagree with themselves. You will want to sketch what you see before your brain corrects it into something impossible to recognize later. Do not trust digital memory alone; the static-tinged rules the fauna navigate by can corrupt data stored as mere ones and zeros. Write with a pencil; graphite is a mineral, and here that is a language everyone respects.

Bring one small, sharp thing—a blade of flintish stone or a shard of obsidian—not for defense, but for attention. When you offer a sharpened edge to the land, it acknowledges you as someone who understands final forms. Do not wave it. Do not threaten. Simply hold it when you sit for a long time. It will learn your shape, and that learning will keep you safer than any weapon.

4. Learn to Walk Like a Thing That Has Been Polished

The erosion here is not the kind that scours but the kind that polishes. Mountains have been ground down to their manners. To walk here is to agree that you will not be the exception.

Move with an economy of gestures. Do not stamp, do not hurry. The fauna are patient, shaped like wedges of obsidian, and they do not hurry because nothing here escapes. If you move quickly, you will attract the wrong kind of attention—not predatory, exactly, but investigative. The six-legged grazers will drift toward you to see if you are edible; the winged tarnished-silver things will hang suspended to measure your displacement.

Practice the half-step. Place your foot, settle your weight, let the glitter-dust settle before you shift again. This is not caution; it is conversation. Each step is a statement you are making to the ground about how long you intend to stay. The ground will answer in its own time.

When you stop, stop completely. Do not hover. Do not shift your weight from foot to foot. Let your shadow lie flat and accept the direction it chooses, even if that direction does not yet exist. The structure that looks like a hand halfway through waving will notice if you are paying attention to your own edges. It is the only thing that looks like it is waiting. Do not let it wait alone if you can help it.

5. Navigate by Rules That Smell Like Iron and Taste Like Static

Distances here stretch like cooled taffy. Landmarks announce themselves only to dissolve into sameness. A ridge of black glass. A bowl of powdered pearl. A seam of iron that remembers blood. If you navigate by map alone, you will become lost in the sameness of openness.

Instead, navigate by fauna choreography. The insects with mica carapaces scuttle in geometries that suggest counting. If you see them forming a spiral that tightens clockwise, the ground ahead remembers being somewhere else. If they form a lattice that loosens, the path is stable. The burrowers with too many legs trace maps in the dust that close behind them—follow the fresh closures, not the open ones. The closures are telling you which ways have already been decided.

At night—or what passes for night—watch the shadows. They fall in directions that refuse to agree. Choose the shadow that points in the direction you wish to go, even if that direction does not exist yet. Walk into it. If it accepts you, you will find the landmark you are seeking. If it does not, it will fold you into a memory of a place you have not been, which can be disorienting but is rarely fatal.

6. Treat Artifacts as Shy Things

The built things here are scattered and shy. A cairn of mismatched stones. A half-buried arch of simple masonry. A single tower, primitive and leaning, its door sealed with a slab that has warped into a smile. These artifacts do not command attention; they ask for it and then apologize.

Do not take anything. Do not add anything. If you find a cairn that seems to be waiting for a stone, place your water bottle down beside it instead. Let the cairn decide whether to absorb it or leave it. If it absorbs it, you will know you are allowed to leave. If it leaves it, you are being invited to stay.

The grooves worn into stone by the same motion practiced until the world forgot to care are not relics. They are instructions. Run your thumb along them. Feel the repetition. This is how you learn the Plateau’s boredom: not as emptiness, but as a treaty signed in mineral and upheld by lack of surprise.

7. Prepare for Your Own Shape to Blur

Every so often, you may feel the edges of yourself blur, as if the land is deciding whether to keep you or fold you into its collection. This is normal. It is the toll for visiting a place of final forms.

When this happens, sit. Do not struggle. Let the Plateau decide what to do with your softness. Usually, it will simply let you keep your shape, because you are not dense enough to be useful and not temporary enough to be ignored. Sometimes, you will find that hours have passed in what felt like minutes, or that you have walked in a circle that is actually a spiral that only looks like a circle because of the angle weirdness. This is fine. The Plateau has given you extra time. Use it to listen to the low, wordless pressure underneath everything, as if the place is holding its breath and has been for centuries.

8. Leave Without a Trace, But With a Gift

When you are ready to leave, do not try to take the glitter-dust with you. It will not survive outside the Plateau’s equilibrium. Instead, leave something that can become part of the mineral wedding: a small drop of oil from your lamp, a strand of hair sealed in resin, a single word engraved into a flake of mica. These things will be ground down eventually, but for now they will join the conversation.

Step out the way you arrived: gently, without assuming the image will hold your shape. As you cross the threshold, the cool air will feel like a blanket being lifted. The density will feel like a crowd saying goodbye. You will carry the stillness with you, not as a weight, but as a kind of stone in your pocket—something to press against your face when the multiverse feels too loud.

And if, on future visits, you find that your footprints have already settled into the dust before you arrive, do not be alarmed. It only means that the Plateau has learned your rhythm, and is practicing its politeness.
