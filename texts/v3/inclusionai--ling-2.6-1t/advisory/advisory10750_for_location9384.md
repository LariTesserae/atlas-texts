How to visit a clenched fist of stone and industry.

First warning: this place is not a place. It is a living valve. The cathedral-nave dimensions are deceptive; what feels like a nave is actually the gullet of a mountain that has swallowed a forge and is learning to digest it. Do not bring expectations of wilderness or ruins. Bring the mindset of a technician entering a lung that has forgotten how to exhale.

Before you cross: medical calibration

- Audit your implants, pacemakers, neural shunts, subdermal mesh. Anything that regulates electricity or fluid will read this pocket as an overload condition. Copper-taste air is ionized; the mica heat gives off piezoelectric static that likes to nest in micro-circuitry. Travel with a Faraday-weave underlayer—light, second-skin—and a sacrificial grounding bracelet you can toss into a crack if the place decides to claim it.
- Hydration strategy: there is no water, but there is plenty of hygroscopic metal in the air. Your body will try to drink the atmosphere and develop a copper patina in the imagination (and eventually in the blood). Carry desiccant pouches and a still-suit or rebreather that condenses breath. Pre-load electrolytes keyed to iron and zinc; you will sweat them out fast under the 2.7-scale heat, which is not temperature but pressure-scaled intensity.
- Auditory protection: not against noise—against structure. The place is loud because nothing soft exists to absorb sound; sonic energy ricochets and stacks into standing waves that can loosen bone cement and dental fillings. Wear dampers that clip onto the mastoid, not over the ears, so you can still hear the low-frequency warning signs (the hum rising, the cracks spacing themselves). Learn to recognize the difference between a healthy thrum and a stressed harmonic.

Gear for compression

- Carry collapsible but rigid frames. Space here is saturated; it resists intrusion by politely, inexorably shrinking to fit. A backpack should telescope inward; think of it as a flower that knows how to close. Pack by density, not volume: ceramics and glass before synthetics, because synthetics will absorb the mineral breath and stiffen into armor you cannot remove.
- Footwear: friction is high, but traction is political. The embryonic stone floor is still negotiating its edges; it will extrude tiny glass teeth overnight to better grip whatever rests on it. Use ceramic soles with micro-spikes you can retract; you want to be able to hover-slide across seams rather than dig in and become part of the geological argument.
- Lighting: do not bring flares or chemical whites. The fauna and machines use heat signatures and brief blue arcs to see. Carry a tunable IR lamp and a small UV diode to reveal the silica circuitry crawling the walls. Learn to read the tracery: brighter loops mean higher pressure; dimming filaments mean a local economy of heat is collapsing. Move toward brightening if you want momentum, toward dimming if you want to be ignored.

Rules of motion

- Do not occupy the void, because there is none. Every cubic finger is claimed. To walk is to ask permission. Stand still for three breaths before committing to a direction. Watch how the fauna arrange themselves: they leave maintenance alleys open by moving through them in shifts. Enter an alley only when you see a chittering caravan pass and leave a gap—then occupy it before the walls notice and extrude glass to seal it.
- Height is a negotiation. The ceiling lowers not by collapse but by floor-memory. If you sleep, place spacers—thin wedges of ceramic—around your sleeping outline. When you wake, retrieve them; if they are gone, the floor has risen and claimed them. If they are warm, the ceiling has descended and left them behind as a gift. Accept the gift; carry it. It is now part of your luck.
- Sound etiquette: the machines and fauna share rhythm. You can slip into their counterpoint if you are careful. Tap a finger against your ceramic plate in time with the pistons; clack your teeth softly when the chitter rises. Do not try to lead; do not try to silence. To introduce an alien rhythm is to volunteer as a fulcrum; the system will amplify you until you become a fault line.

Industrial sympathy

- The engines here are grown, not built. Treat them as fauna with slower metabolisms. They have favorite temperatures and dislike sudden gradients. If you must open a chute or touch a belt, warm your hands first on the wall until they taste of copper; the metal will recognize you as locally sourced and be less likely to bite.
- Electricity is territorial. Follow the crystal rectifiers: they point the way of least resistance, which is also the way of least argument. If you feel a prickle at the base of your skull, you are being drafted into a circuit. Ground yourself immediately, or decide to stay drafted for a short while—some tasks are easier if you let the place run a trickle of current through you—but know that leaving later requires a deliberate discharge, best performed by dragging a foot through a bed of metallic dust.

Survival of the abundant

- The fauna do not fear you; they categorize you by vibration. Move with a low, steady profile. Do not run; running is a high-amplitude event that draws attention from creatures that think in compression waves. If you must defend yourself, use sound shields—temporary baffles of soft alloy that buy you seconds to retreat into a seam.
- They eat metallic dust and sleep in vibration minima. You can mimic this for short periods by wedging yourself into a corner where the hum drops half a tone. Do not sleep deeply; sleep in shifts, one eye open, letting your body become part of the wall’s thermal mass. You will dream in slow tectonics.

What to carry home (and what will carry you)

- You cannot carry water out, but you can carry away compressed time. The pocket’s saturation leaves marks on objects as memories. A stone you pocket will warm in your hand at intervals for days, reminding you of the heat-scale. A scrap of grown gear will gradually untune itself to your home environment, buzzing with misplaced purpose. Decide ahead of time what cost you are willing to pay in inconvenience.
- You may leave something behind by accident. A glove fused to a seam becomes part of the local fauna. A dropped tool becomes a seed for a new engine. Decide what you are willing to sacrifice to coherence; the pocket will accept any offering and polish it into belonging.

Psychological preparation

- Understand that here, erosion is accumulation. Your edges will blur. You will find yourself thinking in gradients instead of steps, in pressures instead of intentions. This is not madness; it is the local dialect of attention. To survive, you must allow yourself to become dense with context, layered with purpose not your own, at least for a while.
- The place will ask you to stay by making things easier: the seams will align, the hum will settle into a lull, the fauna will pause their commentary. This is not hospitality; it is digestion. When this happens, remember the copper taste and the feeling of packed presence. Remember that there is no void here, and therefore no room for a traveler who has decided to stop traveling. Keep one wedge of ceramic unspent, one frequency of your own rhythm unpracticed, so you can always carve a small, rude void and slip through it.

Finally: the only map worth having is a map of pressures. Do not draw lines; draw gradients. Mark where the floor remembers rising, where the ceiling dreams of descending, where the hum becomes a voice. Carry this map on your body, in your muscles and teeth. The fist will unclench just enough for you to pass, if you understand that you are not entering a space but joining a metabolism. Pass slowly, give heat, take rhythm, and leave behind anything that insists on being empty. Empty things here are not respected; they are eaten.
