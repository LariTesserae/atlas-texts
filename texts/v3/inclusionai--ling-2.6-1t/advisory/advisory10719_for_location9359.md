To visit here is to enter a city that is simultaneously an organism, an archive, and a weather system. It is not hostile, but it is exacting: it asks for presence, for patience, for a willingness to be small, slow, and observant. Because the rules here overlap rather than line up, preparation is less about conquering dangers than about calibrating yourself to the place’s metabolism. Below is a field guide, written as if the city itself whispered it to you across its damp walls.

### First Principle: You Will Be Felt Before You Are Seen
The pocket is low and young, its stone still startled by its own existence. Stepping into it is like slipping into a lung that has just learned how to breathe cold. The temperature hovers above the freeze-point of fresh water but below the comfort of skin. It is the kind of chill that invites layering and stillness. You will not want to fight it. Instead, invite it in, let your body learn the gradient between your warmth and the city’s, and remember that your passage will be registered long before you arrive: a change in the hush, a shift in the phosphorescence, a tightening of root-strands overhead.

### Clothing and Skin
Dress as if the city were made of cold tea and light. Layers should be breathable, hydrophobic, and capable of holding heat without hoarding moisture. Wool, aerogel-lined synthetics, and tightly woven plant fibers all work; avoid anything that becomes a sail for the city’s constant, gentle breath. Because walls and paths can be slick with condensation that never quite becomes frost, footwear should grip without crunching—soft rubber soles, micro-spikes for hard-packed ice, and an additional pair of felted slip-ons for when you must walk on living surfaces that recoil from the scrape of leather or metal.

Your skin should be treated as a boundary you intend to keep polite but permeable. A thin balm of rendered tallow and pine resin will keep the moisture from leaching your warmth without sealing you off from the city’s subtle chemical speech. If you have the means, inoculate your outer layers with benign spores that the city recognizes as kin; it will not save you from missteps, but it will reduce the number of times doors simply close because you smelled wrong.

### Light and Time
Light here is a currency rather than a utility. The city trades in glows that shift from mineral cold to biological warmth, cycling in slow waves that mark not hours but intentions. Do not carry a torch unless you want to be thought of as a declaration of war. Instead, carry a lens or prism that can separate the city’s ambient luminescence into useful spectra, and wear a small pendant of bioluminescent algae tuned to your own pulse. This will let you signal without shouting, and it will let you read the city’s mood in how it chooses to answer your glow: brighter, dimmer, or the subtle shift that means “step back.”

Time moves differently in root and air. Your watch will keep its own counsel; the city will not. Bring a notebook of waterproof paper and a stylus of bone or glass. Mark events by what happened—a certain shade of violet spore-cloud, the closure of a passage, the taste of water at the central basin—not by digits. You will find that the city respects this form of record-keeping, and may even guide you more reliably if you prove that you understand that chronology here is narrative, not arithmetic.

### Footfall and Path
The ground gives like half-set dough, yet it is also keenly aware of pressure. Walk softly, distributing your weight as if you were afraid of being rude. Where you see a ribbon of compressed moss, stay within its edges; these are not merely paths but agreements between growth and passage. Step outside them and you may trigger a subtle retraction—a wall thickens, a root shifts, a door contracts—and you will find yourself unwelcome without ever having been addressed.

When crossing living surfaces, pause long enough to let them register you as steady, as non-threatening. A hand placed gently on a wall, fingers spread, can ask permission to pass; if the wall yields slightly and warms in response, proceed. If it remains cool and taut, wait. To force passage is to announce that you do not care whether you are inside or outside, and the city has many ways of making that true, up to and including dissolving the route behind you.

### Water and Sustenance
The city drinks in its skin and exhales through its fountains. Surface water is safe but may carry memory: traces of iron, of old rain, of the city’s own metabolic history. Filter it through layers of moss and charcoal, then expose it to a gentle heat source before drinking. This is not purification as much as translation: you are telling the water that you understand it has been elsewhere.

Food should be compact, nutrient-dense, and low in scent. The city’s hunters and grazers will pay attention to odors, and while they are not predators in the human sense, they may treat a strong scent as either an invitation or a challenge. Dried fruits, cured meats, and small loaves of dense grain are ideal. Supplement with what the city willingly offers: algae cakes that grow on north-facing walls, fruiting bodies that soften when squeezed, and the edible resins that seal joints. Taste cautiously; each carries a signature of the place, and some may induce vivid but inconvenient dreams if eaten after dark.

### Shelter and Rest
If you must sleep, seek a niche where three structural elements meet: a wall, a root, and a flow of warm air. Such places are recognized by the city as liminal—not quite interior, not quite exterior—and it is less likely to remodel them while you occupy them. Build no fire; instead, let a small bladder of warm water serve as your hearth. Drape your covering over it and let the heat rise through a lattice of sticks the city has already discarded. In the morning, dismantle everything and leave the sticks in a neat pile; the city will often reclaim them within hours, knitting them into new passageways.

Sleep will be shallow here, punctuated by the drip of water, the creak of growing fronds, and the soft footfall of chitin on moss. Accept this. Deep sleep is a luxury the city cannot afford you, and to insist upon it may be read as indifference to the place’s ongoing conversation.

### Boundaries and Bargains
The central basin is not merely a landmark; it is a node of exchange. If you approach it, bring something that can be weighed against attention: a light you have captured, a memory you are willing to leave behind, or a promise you do not intend to break. Do not bring weapons carved to harm; such objects will be rejected, sometimes violently. Instead, bring tools of observation—a lens, a notebook, a small prism—and offer them to the water’s surface before drinking. The city may not take them, but it will note the gesture.

Fauna will rarely trouble you if you do not startle them. Move slowly, breathe shallowly, and avoid sudden shifts in temperature or light. If a translucent hunter drifts onto your path, step around it and wait until it resumes its form before continuing. If a school of finger-width swimmers passes through your legs, remain still until their glow fades. These are not pets, nor are they threats; they are messengers. How you treat them will determine how much the city is willing to tell you.

### Language
The city speaks in shifts of density, in the angle of a frond, in the frequency of dripping. Learn to read these signs. A wall that leans is asking you to pass; a frond that curls is asking you to wait; a sudden stillness in the air is a warning. Practice answering with equivalent subtlety: a tilt of the head, a softening of your step, a small brightening of your pendant’s glow. You will not achieve fluency, but you can learn enough to avoid the most grievous misunderstandings.

### Leaving
When you depart, do so as gently as you arrived. Retrace your steps where possible, and close no doors you have opened. Leave behind nothing but water, and even then only water that will not shame the city’s own. As you exit, pause at the lip of the pocket and look back once, without judgment. The city will remember how you looked, and it will color its future conversations accordingly. On your next visit—should there be one—you may find a passage held open, a wall warmed in greeting, or a new room grown in your honor, small and glowing softly, as if waiting for you to decide whether to enter.

### Final Note
This place is not a ruin, nor is it a machine. It is a living city that has learned to thrive in cold and wet and dark by being exquisitely attentive to itself. To visit it is to accept that you, too, must become attentive: to breath, to step, to light, to the quiet that listens. Prepare accordingly, and the city will not feel like a pocket carved beneath a slope, but like a room that has been waiting, with patience, for you to learn how to knock.
