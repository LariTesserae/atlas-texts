How to Visit a City Where Water Commutes and Everything Else Has Learned to be Polite

Overview: Intuition first—this place will not hurt you quickly, but it will outlast you. It is warm, loud, wet in the small ways, and densely crowded with both built things and living things. It smells like hot metal and rain that has forgotten how to be fresh. It sounds like a factory that has made peace with being a cathedral. Your ordinary human body will work here, but it will feel like wearing the wrong thickness of clothes: not wrong enough to panic, just wrong enough to always be aware of your edges. The city is a treaty, not a conquest. Treat it like a long conversation with a partner who keeps better time than you do.

Before You Pack: The Altitude and the Air
The elevation (around 1.6 to 1.7 on whatever scale governs this multiverse) means thinner, clearer air. You will not feel the altitude as a sudden punch, but as a persistent gentleness that makes you underestimate your own fatigue. You will breathe easier at first, then forget to breathe deeply enough, then wonder why your thoughts feel like wool. Bring:
- A small, personal pulse oximeter or at least knowledge of your own resting numbers. You do not want to learn, on a high bridge at dusk, that your blood is shy of oxygen.
- Lip balm and saline nasal spray. The air is clear enough to dry mucous membranes without feeling hostile. Your nose will be the first part of you to complain, quietly.
- Loose layers. At 1.5 temperature, warmth is manufactured as much as ambient—exhausts, condensers, geothermal sighs. Indoors will sometimes feel like a sweater is a bad idea; outdoors at dawn, that same sweater will feel like armor.

What to Wear: Fabric as a Mediator
Water here is not absent; it is a commuter. It sweats from warm stone, pools in low bellies, weeps from the undersides of bridges like slow, mineral tears. Rain oils more than it washes. Your clothes should:
- Repel without sealing: a durable water repellent (DWR) finish on a breathable shell, so the city’s oily rain beads and slides instead of soaking in.
- Dry fast and tolerate abrasion: you will brush against iron, lean on basalt, catch on slag. Cotton will mourn you. Merino or high-grade synthetics will shrug and keep going.
- Have cuffs that close and a hood that does not billow. The wind here is channeled—canyon walls turn breezes into choral howls. A flapping hood will try to carry you away; tight cuffs keep you from dripping onto yourself after a bridge-crossing.
- Shoes with grip that likes wet metal. Not aggressive mountain cleats—those will feel like shouting—but sticky rubber, shallow lugs, the sort that grip a wet ladder without pretending to dig into earth. Think of a gecko in hobnailed leather.
- Eye protection for brightness and grit. Light here is generous, and stone that has surrendered its sharpness still has pride: it shines. A brimmed hat or cap and sunglasses with side coverage will spare you the squint that turns into a headache.

Health and Body Notes: The Mineral World Inside You
Vegetation has surrendered its claim (about 0.3 on the green scale). This is a mineral world: basalt, iron, slag, calcined bone, glass that cooled too fast to remember being sand. Fungal-mineral hybrids grow in steam vents, pale coral things that are persistent rather than alive. For you:
- Hydration is paradoxically crucial and tricky. The air is clear, you will not feel sweat as tyranny, but you will lose water anyway—in thin, frequent exhales, in the small damp patches on your back. Drink on a schedule, not a whim.
- Electrolytes matter more than you think. The city runs on mineral gradients; your muscles will feel this as a sympathetic gossip. A small pouch of salts, tucked into a daypack, is a courtesy to your future self.
- Skin care after exposure. The combination of metallic particulates in runoff and oily rain can leave a film that feels like a second, insistent skin. A gentle cleanser and moisturizer will prevent the city from deciding it likes you enough to leave a patina on you.
- Check any implants or piercings. Iron and moisture love to conspire. If something in you is metal, let it be titanium or well-cared-for steel, and keep it clean. The fauna here appreciates crevices.
- Ears. You will hear more than you expect. The sound density (1.7) is loud but textured—a slap of water on metal, the grind of gears easing, the chitter and boom of fauna. Bring earplugs for sleeping or for moments when the city decides to synchronize by accident and thrum like a struck bell. Ordinary musician’s earplugs (flat attenuation) keep the world audible but gentle.

Packing List: What Feels Like Enough
- A daypack that hugs your back and does not flap. Zippers that close with a finality. The city’s fauna (2.7—overwhelming) is curious and dexterous; it will unzip a careless bag to see if you have brought it a gift.
- Waterproof pouches for electronics and papers. Water here is a commuter; treat it as a coworker who never remembers birthdays.
- A collapsible water bottle and a lightweight filter or purification tablets. The runoff channels are bright, sluggish serpents that remember every gradient; they are not invitations.
- Basic first aid that includes blister care, antiseptic for metallic scratches (even polished iron carries a polite bite), and antihistamine or mild steroid cream for encounters with mineral-crust or fungal-mineral hybrids. You probably won’t need it, but the irritation of surprise is worse than the cream.
- A headlamp and a small lantern. Light is generous, but the city grows inward—tunnels beneath tunnels, basements below basements. You may descend into a place that politely forgets the sun.
- A multi-tool with pliers and a good knife. Not for danger, but for the thousand small negotiations with infrastructure: a stuck grate, a stubborn latch, a package of food that respects tensile strength too much.
- A notebook and a pen that tolerates damp. This is a place of gradients and echoes; you will want to record the route by which water found a new seam, or the word you misheard in an acoustic echo that returned a beat late.
- A map and a compass, or their digital equivalents with offline cache. The city’s scale (2.6) is continental in patience; districts repeat like geological strata. You can get lost in sameness.
- Small gifts for locals, if your multiverse etiquette allows: something that acknowledges metal and patience—polished stone, a gear-shaped trinket, a vial of something that changes color with temperature, to honor the city’s steady hum.

Getting Around: The City as a Conversation
This place is built, at 2.3, to think in infrastructure. Streets are rivers, bridges are machines, walls are dams. Respect the flow:
- Follow the runoff channels, but do not treat them as sidewalks. They are bright, sluggish serpents that remember gradients. Step lightly across them; slipping here is a performance art you do not want to master.
- Stairs are screws, escalators are conversations. Entire blocks may be raised or lowered on ancient, oiled gears. Do not stand on moving architecture while it sighs into alignment. Wait for the soft complaint of metal settling.
- Ask before climbing. The city is dense (1.5) and layered; your route upward may also be someone’s ceiling, someone’s duct, someone’s roost. Fauna (2.7) is overwhelming; you are entering living tides of winged things and skitterers. Keep your hands to yourself, unless invited.
- Learn the local signs for water behavior. At 1.7, water lives in seams. A line of damp stone may be a schedule: a leak that comes precisely at dawn, a bridge that weeps only when a certain train passes. If you can read these, you can avoid being caught in the wrong moment of the city’s commute.
- Sound as navigation. The acoustic echoes return a word or two late. Use this. Call out softly in a canyon and let the city rehearse you back. The delay will tell you where the walls are kind, and where they are busy with other business.

Etiquette and Safety: How to be Polite to a Mineral World
- Do not carve your initials, or anyone else’s. The city is in balance with its own decay (erosion 2.5). It has surrendered sharpness; do not ask it to remember violence.
- Give the fauna right of way. They are middling in size, many, patient. They do not fear you, and you should not fear them, but respect the choreography of their flight and skitter. A sudden movement is a boast; a slow one is a question.
- Honor the warmth. The city’s heat rises in layers, trapped between canyon walls. It is a tired animal circling its own heat. Help the circulation: step aside from grates that exhale steam, do not block condensers, never drape a coat over a vent to dry it unless you have asked and been told how long is polite.
- Dispose of nothing in the runoff. The water is a commuter; it will carry your mistake for miles and remember it as a gradient. Pack out what you pack in.
- If you hear the city thrum—the accidental synchronization of slap, grind, chitter, and thrum—pause. This is the place aligning itself. It will pass. You may hum along, quietly, if you feel a strange and sudden sense of welcome. Do not mistake this for permission to do anything reckless.

Intangible Preparations: Time and Mood
- The city’s dynamic (0.8) favors the long view. Change is seasonal, incremental. Your visit should be too, in spirit. Do not rush. The stone steps show the shine of millions of feet; add yours without trying to outshine them.
- Weirdness is shy here (0.8). Water sometimes flows uphill for a few meters before remembering itself; arches cast no shadow at noon; stones hum when touched. Accept these as courtesies, not puzzles to solve. If you prod them, they will retreat.
- Learn to read the horizon as a rumor. From the highest girders, the built world yields to a sky that feels too big to be owned. This is a gift, not an ownership. Visit the heights, but know that the best thing you can take from them is a changed sense of scale.

When You Leave: What to Carry Home
- A small vial of mineral crust or rust bloom from a place that permitted it. Not as a trophy, but as a reminder that decay can be articulate.
- The habit of listening for the delayed echo. In ordinary rooms, you may miss it, and that will be a kind of homesickness.
- An adjusted sense of wetness. After this place, rain will feel like a blunt instrument or a blessing, depending on the city you return to.
- And, if you are lucky, the knowledge that you have been part of a treaty, however briefly: water, iron, wingbeats, and your own careful, ordinary body, all agreeing—for a little while—to share the same gradient.

Final Intuition
This city is not dangerous in the way of monsters or storms; it is dangerous in the way of a good conversation—capable of making you forget your own edges if you do not mind them. It will offer you polished stone, polite iron, and a sky that refuses to be owned. Come prepared to be patient, to drip a little, to listen loudly, and to tread as if the ground were a partner that has been waiting, with remarkable gentleness, to see how you walk.
