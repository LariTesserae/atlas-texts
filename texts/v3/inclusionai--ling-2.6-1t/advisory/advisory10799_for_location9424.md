**How to visit a place that has not yet decided to be a place**

Go in lightly. Go in slowly. Go as if you were a punctuation mark looking for a sentence that is still being spoken.

**Clothing: Treat fabric like skin that can be borrowed**

Do not wear anything that plans to stay dry. The air here is not weather; it is a third thing between water and wind, a medium dense enough to row through. Bring a suit made of layered, hydrophilic wovens—linen bonded to silk, nettle-cotton interleaved with spider-silk gauze—that holds water without drowning you in it. The outermost layer should be slick enough that droplets slide into the one below instead of pooling, distributing weight like a slosh that you learn to counter-sway with. Think of it as wearing a mobile tide.

Colors should be matte and low-contrast: moss-greens, bruised purples, slate-blues that let you blur into curtains of vapor. Avoid anything bright enough to be mistaken for a sun-rupture; light here is ordinary, and ordinary light polishes.

Footwear is a negotiable matter. There is no ground in the sense you know it. There is only shelf, ledge, knuckle of basalt, and root-mat. Bring three modes: soft soles that feel like bare skin for the vegetal bridges; micro-spikes that can bite glass-sharp ridges without shattering them; and a kind of aquatic finning shoe, wide and flat, for when the rain lifts to thigh-depth and you must row yourself along vine-ladders. Carry them in a sling, and change as the air changes its mind about the floor.

**Gear: Tools for a world that is almost grammar**

A walking staff is a compass. Not for north, but for up. Carve it from something buoyant but resilient—heartwood from a drowned forest, perhaps, or a rib-bone cleaned by tides in another world—and carve spiral grooves along its length. When you plant it, water will trace those grooves and tell you which way the air is heavier, which way the basin wants to tilt. Use it to test shelf-integrity. A ridge that rings like glass is young; one that thrums like a wet tongue is holding.

Pack a bladder, but not for water. For air. The gelatinous humidity resists exhalation; you will feel as though you are breathing soup. A small bellows-lung, strapped to the chest, lets you pressurize each breath before it enters, turning soup back into air. Alternatively, cultivate the habit of breathing in through gauze that catches only the edges of moisture and lets the center pass. You will learn to taste altitude: thin air tastes of iron; the hanging basin tastes of petrichor and yeast.

Light is not scarce, but it is shared. Bring a headlamp that diffuses rather than pierces—a pearl lens that makes your beam into a cloud rather than a blade. Hard light insults the air here; it causes rain to recoil, creating tiny voids that hum. Use bioluminescent companions instead: a jar of symbiotic glow-worms that pulse when the humidity changes, or a lichen-coated staff that brightens when it senses your pulse. Let your illumination be borrowed, not declared.

Navigation is a matter of reading collaboration. Carry no map that claims to be true. Instead, carry a notebook of recent consensus: a sketch of vine-ladders that appeared yesterday and may fade tomorrow, the memory of moss-patterns on basalt, the taste of resin that never hardens. Learn to leave marks that vanish—charcoal from burned lichen, or scratches on root-bark that will be overgrown within hours. Do not plan routes; plan permissions. Ask each ledge if it can hold you, and listen for the way it answers in the flex of fern-shadow.

**Biology: Your body must become conversational**

Your skin will drink. Accept this. The air is sweet-rot and wet-fern; it will seep into pores and sit there like a second sweat. Before you arrive, condition yourself to tolerate mild hypoxia and mild buoyancy. Practice walking in shallow pools with weighted pockets, learning to sway before you need to. Strengthen your ankles for the unexpected floor: a shelf that is bone one step and vapor the next.

Your lungs will meet pollen that is less dust and more proposition. Take antihistamines that encourage diplomacy rather than war. Pack a tea of local fern and honey from bees that harvest midair algae; it will teach your throat to process flavor as texture.

Your eyes will be asked to focus on things that are not objects: light fracturing through hanging rain, fish that are ripple, moss that glows at dusk. Practice soft-focus gazing. Learn to see by letting edges blur, then sharpen, then blur again, like breath on glass.

**Manners: How to be a polite disturbance**

This place is crowded into collaboration. Your presence is, by default, a violence. Minimize it.

Move when the vegetation moves. If vines are swaying in a rhythm, step in that rhythm. If the rain is falling in strands that hum, pause your breath when they pass. Do not snap stems; ask them to yield. They will, if you press at the node where they have already decided to bend.

Do not harvest. If you need resin, collect only what has dripped onto a stone and refused to harden. If you need moss, scrape a handful that has already begun to loosen its grip. Offer something in return: a song, a breath held long enough to warm the air, a memory of drought that will make the greenery shiver and pull closer together.

Fauna here are mostly absences. Respect the glint of scale far above as a privacy. Do not follow the ripple-fish with your eyes; they rearrange themselves into language when watched directly, and that language may ask you to stay longer than you intended.

Do not name places. Naming fixes them, and this basin is still learning where to place its commas. Instead, describe them in passing: *the ledge that tasted of struck flint*, *the vine-chandelier at shoulder height*, *the air that rowed hardest at noon*. Let your labels dissolve as you leave.

**Psychology: How to tolerate almost-ness**

You will feel unanchored. The horizon is a declaration of height, not distance. Up is sometimes under your feet when the air below is heavier. Learn to trust your inner ear as a negotiator, not a judge. When vertigo comes, treat it as a suggestion that the world is rearranging its grammar. Breathe into it, and let it settle into a new syntax.

Time will feel thick. The ridges sharpen over centuries; your visit is a blink. Do not rush to complete. Let yourself become a temporary ridge—something that stands up, speaks, and is eroded into smoothness by the attention of moss. You will leave thinner, polished, carrying the taste of wet fern and struck flint in your pockets.

**Arrival and departure**

To arrive, step through the veil of rain as if it were a curtain made of held breath. Let your body row itself into density. Let your staff find the first shelf that rings rather than shatters. Let your lungs learn to drink soup.

To depart, allow the vegetation to release you. Walk backward along a vine-ladder that may dissolve as you descend, thanking it for its temporary rigidity. Row out through the hanging rain until it thins into ordinary precipitation. When you feel gravity resume its usual insistence, you will know you have left the grammar phase behind.

You will carry away nothing but a change in your own density—an extra softness at the edges, a tendency to blur, a habit of asking permission before you step. And perhaps, in your dreams, you will still row through air that is thick enough to push against, hearing the polite patter of rain that fell weeks ago and is still falling, teaching water how to stand, and life how to crowd, long after you have gone.
