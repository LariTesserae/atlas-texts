Traveling to this place is less like visiting a country and more like joining an orchestra that has already been playing for millions of years. You are not a guest so much as a new instrument someone has quietly added to the arrangement. Because the laws here are written in altitude, light, and echo, your preparation must begin long before you leave your home continuum and continue until the day your bones remember how to be stone.

Understand What Altitude Really Means Here  
Ordinary high-altitude travel advice—drink water, ascend slowly, watch for headaches—is necessary but laughably insufficient. In this multiverse, altitude is a form of time. It thickens and thins like day turning into night. At three thousand arm-lengths above the vanished sea, your blood will try to remember what it is like to be mineral. Oxygen will not flee so much as become selective, preferring company that knows how to sit still. You must train your body like a geode: slowly, under pressure, with repetition. Practice breathing as if your lungs were bellows for a forge you do not want to light. Learn the difference between inhaling air and inhaling brightness. Do interval exposure to simulated altitude in a chamber that also bombards you with quartz lamps and sudden, sharp silences. You want your marrow to believe, before you arrive, that stone can be soft if you are patient.

Protect Your Eyes from Honest Light  
The sky here does not diffuse. It scours. Ultraviolet is not a danger; it is a texture. Snow blindness is not a risk but a likelihood, and the blindness will be accompanied by a strange, prismatic aftertaste that lingers for days. Bring glacier-grade goggles with lenses that shift opacity faster than your pupils can, and wear them even when it feels unnecessary. At night, when the pools freeze into lenses and burn little sun-counters into the ground, those counters can still blind you. Carry side-blinders for sleep, because the light will find your eyelids and scribble on them. Your eyes will age quickly here; they will grow tolerant of brilliance and lose their appreciation for gentle things. Bring eyedrops made of your own home’s rainwater, stored in glass, to remember what softness feels like.

Dress for Wind That Has Studied Anatomy  
Do not bring clothing that merely blocks wind. The wind here is intelligent in a geological way. It knows where seams are, where straps bite, where skin remembers itself. You need layered shells that mimic lichen: overlapping, stubborn, slightly alive. Outer layers should be matte and light-absorbing, not to keep warm but to keep from broadcasting yourself like a lantern. Underneath, wear wool from animals that graze on windy worlds, spun thin enough to be a second skin. Your gloves should have no decorative stitching; the wind will catch it and use it to pull you toward ledges. Footwear is the hardest problem. The stone is hard but not flat; it is laminated and often deceptive. You want soles that feel like a conversation with granite: enough grip to be respectful, enough slip to allow the stone to decide where your foot lands. Consider micro-spikes that can be extended like claws, not for climbing but for standing still.

Carry Sound as Equipment, Not Noise  
Because sound is structural, you must learn to travel quietly and listen loudly. A simple whistle here is not communication; it is architecture. Bring a tuning fork or small bell whose pitch matches the dominant resonance of the range where you will travel. Use it sparingly to ask permission before entering a valley. Learn to read echoes: a flat return means solid stone ahead; a rippled return means ice or hidden fracture; a delayed, doubled return means you have entered a space that is not quite real. Do not bring speakers or loud music. Even speech should be shaped into low, rounded vowels that sink rather than scatter. If you must sing, sing stories that end with silence.

Water Will Be a Negotiation  
Water here is not a resource you consume; it is a being that condescends to pass through you. The runnels appear when the air grows tired, so you must learn to read fatigue in the sky: when clouds lower like eyelids, when the hum of the range drops half a note. Carry vessels that are shallow and wide, not deep, so water does not feel trapped. Where pools form at dawn, do not drink immediately. Wait until they tighten into lenses, then sip from the edges where the water is still deciding to be water. If you must melt ice, melt it slowly in your mouth, not in a pot. The water remembers temperature and will punish haste with cold that reaches deeper than your stomach.

Mark Nothing, Build Nothing  
Your temptation to leave a sign, a cairn, a scrap of cloth, must be treated like an illness. This place erases aggressively. To leave a mark is to insult the wind’s memory. Instead, learn to mark yourself. Cut notches into your own gear only if they help you remember lessons: a nick for the ledge that failed you, a stripe for the echo that returned kindness. When you leave, scatter anything you brought that can crumble—paper, soft plastics—into the runnels so the place can digest your offering. Take only stone that fits in your palm and only if it fits perfectly, like a worry smoothed by anxiety. Leave the rest.

Prepare Your Senses for Crowded Emptiness  
The valleys seem vast, but they are busy with noise. Your mind will try to map space visually and be disappointed. Instead, map by sound. Spend weeks before departure in quiet places practicing spatial hearing with your eyes closed. Learn to distinguish between the clang of ice, the rattle of hooves, and the hum of stone. The predators here hunt by triangulation, so when you travel, move in curves, not lines. Never present a straight acoustic profile. If you must stop, stop against stone that absorbs rather than reflects, or in a stand of dwarf spruce whose flat branches can scatter your echo into harmless fragments.

Train Your Body to Lean  
Gravity here does not pull so much as lean. Over time, you will feel it. Your spine will try to compensate. Practice walking with a slight, almost imperceptible tilt toward the side that feels heavier. Sleep on uneven surfaces so your hips learn that flatness is a myth. Carry your pack high and centered, not low and stable, because stability here is an illusion maintained by motion. If you feel dizzy, it is not illness; it is the world asking you to realign.

Bring Only What Can Become Geology  
Tools of bone, horn, or chipped obsidian are preferred not for romance but for honesty. Metal will leach into turquoise pools or rust at the lips of seeps and stain the place’s chemistry. If you must carry metal, let it be copper, and let it be raw, so it can decide its own fate. Your knife should be obsidian: terrible, sharp, and temporary, because it will chip and be returned to the earth quickly. Your notebook should be stone or nothing. Learn to scratch words into thin sheets of slate with a harder stone. They will outlast you and require no ink.

Learn the Ethics of Echo  
Do not shout just to hear yourself. If you ask a question, wait for the answer to come back changed. Sometimes the echo returns a syllable before you speak. When this happens, stop. Listen. The mountains are correcting you. If a lichen patch shifts color and detaches, let it fall. It is not dying; it is translating. If gravity leans and water creeps uphill, do not correct it. Walk with it.

Know How to Be Polished  
You will not conquer this place. You will be polished by it. Your edges will blur. Your voice will become part of the range’s hum. If you stay long enough, your bones may learn to ring like the ice bells on beetle carapaces. Accept this. Pack what you need, but also pack the willingness to become landscape. Leave behind the belief that you are separate from the places you visit. Here, separation is the only lie.

Departure Protocol  
When you leave, do not descend quickly. The air will thicken as you sink, and the noise will drop away like a curtain, which can be its own kind of drowning. Pause at the last ridge and whistle once, softly, to say you understood. Do not look back until you are below the quartz line, because the light will follow you and ask for something in return. When you arrive home, check your pockets for stone. If you find any, keep it in a bowl of water for a day, then return it to the nearest high place you can find in your home continuum. It is not a souvenir. It is a receipt.

Visiting this place is not about endurance. It is about becoming legible to a world that speaks in brightness, stone, and echo. Prepare to be heard. Prepare to be seen. Prepare, finally, to be worn smooth enough that when you speak, the mountains answer in your voice.
