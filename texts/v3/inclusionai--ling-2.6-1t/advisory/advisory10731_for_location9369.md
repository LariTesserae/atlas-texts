How an ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for visiting  
(a world at water-depth, pressure-crowded, cold-settled, slow-turbulent)

1. Treat the trip as an enrollment, not a visit  
Do not think of this world as a site you will observe from a safe margin. There is no margin. Elevation is a rumor; surface is a memory. The moment you arrive you will be inside the medium, pressed by it, taught by it. Prepare to be enrolled in a liquid permanence that does not care whether you like it, only whether you fit. Bring humility as standard equipment and practice holding still until stillness feels like motion.

2. Understand the physics you will live inside  
- Pressure-crowded: at 2.9 density and packed silt-floor, the water is heavy with itself. Your body will feel the weight as a constant, gentle insistence.  
- Cold as law: 2.7 cold is not sharp; it is a subtracter. It will nibble your heat, your speed, your willingness to hurry.  
- Dim and dense light: light at 0.4 means shapes arrive as outlines, not colors. Acuity will be borrowed from touch and from patience.  
- Quiet, punctuation sound: 0.4 sound is not a score but a notation; learn to read bubble, creak, and brine-slosh as language.  
- Chaotic slowness: dynamic at 2.8 means turbulence is body-deep and slow—a lace of overturnings and rearrangements. Do not look for breaking waves; look for shifts that feel like forgetting.  

Accept these facts as gravity. Plan for them the way you would plan for altitude or radiation: with respect, redundancy, and rehearsal.

3. Train your body and nervous system  
- Breath discipline: spend weeks increasing CO2 tolerance and reducing air hunger. This world rewards exhales that are slow, complete, and unafraid.  
- Cold habituation: cold showers are not enough. Practice immersion that starts at the skin and moves inward—ice baths that teach your hands to unclench, feet to stop fidgeting, spine to become a column of calm.  
- Pressure play: train in pools or lakes with weighted walks, learning how your balance changes when every direction is crowded. Practice letting the ground rise to you instead of stepping onto it.  
- Stillness drills: spend hours in dim rooms with earplugs, learning to track motion by the softest cues: the almost-sound of blood, the almost-shift of a curtain.  
- Low-lux vision: wear dimming goggles while doing fine tasks—threading needles, tying knots, sketching by candlelight—until your hands trust the dark.

4. Build or choose the right kit  
- A pressure-suit of second skin, not armor: articulated membrane that equalizes gently, keeps the cold nibble outside without turning you into a rigid doll. Include gauntlets that let you feel texture; here, fingertips are translators.  
- Thermal lattice underlayer: microcapillary weave that moves your warmth where it is needed without trapping sweat. Redundancy: two independent heat sources you can cross-feed.  
- Lung kit: a rebreather tuned for this world’s mix, with buffers for strange trace gases and a low, quiet hiss. Include a soft mouthpiece that rewards slow breathing.  
- Lamps that do not conquer darkness: diffuse, low-candela beams that honor the 0.4 light. Carry spare lenses you can swap to change the quality of outline.  
- Sampling tools: bone or ceramic probes; scoops with teeth that comb rather than bite; line on a reel you can feel in your palm, with knots that tell you depth by touch.  
- Data slates in pressure-cases, with haptic feedback: this world discourages staring at glowing screens. Learn to read by fingertip and by ear.  
- Emergency lift-bladder and gravel-ballast pouches: buoyancy here is suspect; you must be able to choose when to rise and when to settle.  
- A small shrine-object: something personal, worn smooth by your handling, to anchor you when the crowd of particles presses close.

5. Mental and social preparations  
- Accept that you will not be master here. You will be a guest in a society of silt, cold, and slow turbulence. Speak softly; move as if you are inside someone else’s thought.  
- Learn the etiquette of fauna (1.3): rarity means each appearance is an event. Do not reach first. Let shapes detach and pass. Your stillness is the permission they need to be near.  
- Vegetation is sparse and persistent (0.7). Touch only when invited; gather nothing without leaving something of equal patience—perhaps a smoothed stone, a braided thread, a held breath released in a careful ring.  
- Erosion has taught this world to wear its lack of artifice like a virtue (2.8). Do not try to improve, mark, or decorate. Your presence should weather down to usefulness and then to kindness.  
- Build in a partner system. In a world this dense and dim, solitude can thicken into disappearance. Agree on touch-signals, on check-rhythms, on when to unmake a plan and let the slow storm decide.

6. Logistics and arrival  
- Enter via a gradual silt-slope if possible, rather than a drop. Let the water’s density teach your body the rate at which you may exist.  
- Do not rush the first hour. Let your suit settle, your breath find its new economy, your eyes learn to borrow light.  
- Mark your route with biodegradable tokens you can feel but not see, arranged in patterns you memorize by hand and by ear.  
- Set return cues not by time but by body-state: when the cold behind your ribs begins to speak in a certain pitch, begin your return.

7. Contingencies and exit strategies  
- If pressure-crowding overwhelms, release ballast in small, controlled pulses and let the water hand you upward. Never fight the crowd; slip through its polite gaps.  
- If cold law begins to subtract clarity, activate your secondary heat source and switch to touch-navigation. Close your eyes; the world becomes kinder without sight.  
- If the slow turbulence stirs into disorientation, find the floor—real or imagined—and press your forehead to it until the story steadies.  
- Keep one breath held in reserve for emergencies: a calm, practiced exhale you can use to signal, to slow a runaway ascent, or to buy a second of decision-time.

8. What to carry home with you  
Do not carry physical souvenirs unless they are small, smooth, and willing—something the world has already given up. Instead, carry the packed dark behind your ribs, a small wet moon of density and cold that reminds you depth is a condition you agreed to hold. Carry the hush of 0.4 sound, the patience of sparse vegetation, the ceremony of a fauna that chose to pass close.  

When you leave, do not shake off the water quickly. Let it leave you in stages, as you entered—by permission, by slow unburdening. Bow to the erosion; thank the crowd. The world that has forgotten how to be sky is still keeping score in silt and cold; leave it a memory of a human who learned to tread softly in its liquid permanence.

You will not conquer this place, and it will not let you merely survive it. It will ask you to fit, to hush, to accept the crowd and the cold as teachers. If you prepare with care, you will find that fitting is a kind of belonging, and that the slow turbulence has its own music—quiet, immense, and alive with the patient violence of being packed, of being held, of being deep.
