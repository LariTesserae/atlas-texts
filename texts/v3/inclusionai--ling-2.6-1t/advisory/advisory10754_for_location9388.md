Practical Preparations: The Traveler’s Checklist  

1) Treat altitude as a collaborator, not an obstacle  
- Your blood will thin here, not metaphorically. Even if the air pressure feels merely “dull,” your hemoglobin will behave as if you have climbed two vertical kilometers. Bring a portable hematocrit booster (the legal, licensed kind) and train your lungs beforehand with hypoxia tents or controlled-altitude masks.  
- Sleep will fracture. The light is bright-dull and the silence is textured; your brain will try to eavesdrop on it. Pack a circadian dimmer—a wearable that can simulate gradual dusk—and earplugs that do not block sound but reshape it into something your nervous system can file under “background.”  
- Hydration is deceptive. Dryness here is not thirst; it is subtraction. You will not feel parched until you are already desiccated. Drink on a schedule, not a cue, and favor fluids with electrolytes that mimic the local salt chemistries (ask for “basalt-balance” blends, common on high-mineral worlds).

2) Footwear and gait  
- Do not trust flat ground. The plates are tilted, and tilt is not always visible to the eye until your weight tries to correct it. Use boots with multidirectional micro-crampons and a sole durometer that can grip glass-smooth basalt as well as crumbly salt crust.  
- Learn to “tilt-walk”: a gait that keeps your center of mass over your base of support even when the surface slopes fifteen degrees sideways. Think of it as dancing with gravity’s inattention. Practice on slanted treadmills before departure.

3) Clothing and microclimate  
- Layers that breathe but do not flap. Wind here carries powdered glass and magnetic dust; fabrics must be tight-woven and statically dissipative.  
- Bring a cape or shell treated with a refractive coating that dulls the bright-dull light without darkening it—something that turns glare into a soft, even wash.  
- Gloves with sensitive fingertips. You will want to touch the mineral fungus and the fused aggregate walls, both of which can be sharp or surprisingly warm. The fungus, in particular, can shift temperature by ten degrees in minutes; protection is as much about precision as safety.

4) Navigation and orientation  
- Compass and gyroscope will lie to you. The magnetic seams running under the plates create local declinations that change with the settling of stone. Use a landmark-based map, preferably one sketched by a resident rather than surveyed by satellite.  
- Carry a small prism or laser pointer to test vertical. The true vertical here is often a story told by plumb bobs hanging in sheltered alcoves, not by the horizon.  
- Mark your routes with temporary, biodegradable tags. The city exhales; yesterday’s street may be today’s tower base.

5) Health and biology  
- The local fungal-analogues are not toxic, but they are information-dense. Their crystalline lattices can leach trace minerals from your skin if you linger too long. Apply a barrier cream rich in silica and zinc.  
- If you have implants (neural, cardiac, orthopedic), have them checked for magnetic susceptibility. The six-legged glass-and-wire fauna emit faint static fields that can cause odd feedback in unshielded devices.  
- Bring a compact audiometer. The quiet here is textured; subtle shifts in tone can signal imminent slab settling. Learning to hear the “clink” before the tilt can save your ankles.

6) Social and cultural etiquette  
- The settlement respects patience. Do not rush decisions, and do not mistake silence for absence. When a resident pauses, they are often listening to the city settle. Join them. Let the pause lengthen.  
- Gifts of shaped glass or fused aggregate are appreciated, especially if they show signs of having been carried long enough to acquire a personal heat signature. Do not offer water; it is a poor gift in a place that has sworn off liquidity. Offer instead something that can hold a temperature: a stone that has been warmed in your pocket, a crystal tuned to your own steps.  
- Do not photograph the fungal spires without asking. They are not alive, but they are considered. Ask, and wait for the tilt of a slab to be the answer. If the slab settles softly, you may proceed.

7) Mental preparation  
- The place will feel like a puzzle that refuses to be solved, only lived with. Bring a notebook and use it to trace patterns you do not understand. Do not expect to return with answers; expect to return with better questions.  
- Allow yourself to be disoriented. The angles will sum wrong at dusk. This is not a trick; it is the altitude learning your shape. Let it.  
- Remember that the city has grown into the land, not over it. You are not a visitor who stands on something. You are a temporary reconfiguration of a long conversation between stone and patience.

Packing Summary  
- Hematocrit booster and electrolyte tabs  
- Circadian dimmer and textured earplugs  
- Multidirectional grip boots with replaceable micro-crampons  
- Static-dissipative, tight-woven layers and a refractive cape  
- Sensitive-tip gloves and silica/zinc barrier cream  
- Land-mark map and portable plumb line  
- Biodegradable trail tags and a small prism  
- Audiometer and implant checker  
- A warmed stone or fused-glass gift, wrapped in non-absorbent cloth  
- Notebook and a pen that works in thin air  

Last Thought  
Do not prepare for conquest. Prepare for conversation. The height is not waiting to be taken; it is waiting to be noticed. If you listen, it will tell you how to step, how to pause, and how to leave without ever having arrived. That is the only arrival that matters here.
