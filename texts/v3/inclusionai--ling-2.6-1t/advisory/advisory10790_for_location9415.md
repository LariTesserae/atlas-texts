**How to Prepare for the City That Is Found, Not Opened**

Visiting this place is not a matter of logistics so much as calibration. You are not entering a ruin, a cave, or an underground town in the ordinary sense; you are entering a system whose primary ethic is *listening*. The stone is not indifferent to you—it is simply very old and very polite, and it will treat you with the same patience it reserves for water. If you prepare as if you were visiting a wilderness or a city above ground, you will be misunderstood, and the misunderstanding will cost you comfort, time, or your invitation to return. Below is what ordinary human multiverse travelers have learned (sometimes reluctantly) works best.

---

### 1. The Invitation Is Everything
You cannot force an opening here. The throat of basalt parts only when it chooses to, and it reads intention more closely than credentials.  
- **Before you approach**, spend time at the mouth of the basalt—not waiting, but *being present*. Do not think of entry as conquest. Think of it as asking permission to witness a conversation that predates your species.  
- **Carry a small, smooth river-cobble** from your home continuum, one you have held until it remembers your hand. When the stone parts, set it gently at the threshold (not inside). The gesture is not symbolic here; it is diplomatic. It tells the city you understand stone as something to be worked *with*, not moved.  
- **Do not bring anything loud**—not chronometers that tick, not radios, not even heavy breathing. Noise is not forbidden, but unearned noise is exhausting to the place, and it will make the air feel heavier, narrower.

---

### 2. Dress for Stone, Not Air
Temperature will be held at “the temperature the stone itself seems to approve of”—cool enough to dull the edges of breath, never cold, but never warm in the way sun-warmed rock feels. Your skin will behave differently here: pores stay closed, sweat never quite forms, and yet the chill can seep into joints over hours.  
- **Layered, matte fabrics** are essential. Avoid anything glossy, synthetic, or brightly dyed. The city’s dim, mineral-tinted light will turn reflective surfaces into tiny spotlights, and you do not want to announce your presence to every reflective seam.  
- **Gloves that thin but articulate**—you will want to touch the walls, and the walls will want to be touched, but stone that has polished itself to velvet will abrade skin over long contact. Bring gloves of soft leather or densely woven plant fiber (not metal-threaded).  
- **Footwear should whisper**. The floors are maps of spillways that remember how liquid moved. Soles that grip too hard will chatter; soles that slip too much will apologize too loudly. Felted leather or cured hide, molded to your feet by walking before you arrive, is the standard among repeat visitors.  
- **Leave pockets empty except for necessity**. The close quarters will magnify the sound of jangling objects. Small draw-pouches sewn inside clothing, at the ribs or hips, work better.

---

### 3. Calibrate Your Senses
This city is loud in sound but dim in light, and the discrepancy can disorient an unprepared mind.  
- **Practice hearing in layers before you travel.** Sit with recordings of dripping water, distant tectonic hums, and wind through porous rock. Train yourself to separate foreground from background, because here the echoes braid into chords and footsteps return as greetings. If you cannot learn to hear selectively, you will be overwhelmed within an hour.  
- **Bring a hooded lamp that glows without glitter**—a steady, amber or soft white LED diffused through ground opal or milky resin. Do not use candles (the oxygen economy is subtle) or torches (the place dislikes sudden heat). The city already knows how to glow from its own dampness; your light should apologize, not declare.  
- **Protect your eyes from nothing**. There is no harsh glare here, but visibility will shift as you move between water-curtained colonnades and dry, polished corridors. A traveler’s greatest mistake is to stare too long at the quartz seams: they seem restrained, but over minutes they can draw your focus inward until the rest of the chamber recedes like a tide. Blink often. Look away.

---

### 4. The Rules of Passage
The architecture is close, intense, and pragmatic. Respect for *shared volume* is the only morality here.  
- **Move as if you are heavy in spirit but light in body**. Do not brush corners unless the stone has already offered a polished shoulder; where it has worn to velvet, lean; where it remains raw, pass without contact. The difference is a message: velvet means *pause and share breath*; raw means *pass quickly and quietly*.  
- **Bridges knit across gaps the width of a long stride**. That width is intentional: it demands a single, unhurried step, or a careful hop for those with shorter legs. Rushing turns the bridge into a drum. Do not.  
- **There are “doors” that open inward and downward into water**. These are not entrances in the usual sense—they are passages into the city’s wetter memory. If you choose to take one, do so with your lamp lowered, eyes open for soft-bodied fauna, and feet ready to feel the floor change from stone to slick mineral skin. The water is not deep, but it is older than your notion of depth.  
- **When basins collect and release without visible change in volume**, do not test them. They are not tricks, but agreements. To pour something in is to ask the city to remember it. Ask only if you intend the city to keep your offering.

---

### 5. Living Alongside the Fauna
The pale, multi-limbed creatures are rare and live at the acoustic edges. They are not dangerous, but they are *precise*.  
- **They are sensitive to surface tension and tone**. Speak softly, if at all, when near weepholes and ledges. Sudden laughter, shouting, or crying will drive them into the lattice, and their absence will make the silence feel reproachful rather than inhabited.  
- **They leave starburst patterns in the dust of ledges**. If you see these, you are in a space they consider theirs. Move as if you are apologizing for your solidity.  
- **Do not try to touch or follow**. They are not pets, omens, or guides. They are residents with their own rhythms. The ozone-and-wet-chalk scent they leave behind is a byproduct of their attention, not an invitation.

---

### 6. Water as Clock and Companion
Here, water is not scarcity—it is constancy. It teaches the passage of time by rhythm, not volume.  
- **Observe the drips before you sleep** (you will likely sleep in a chamber whose floor remembers spillways). In this city, sleep is best done on a ledge angled slightly, so that stray moisture runs off rather than pooling. A small stone pillow, hard and cool, is recommended; it will wake you gently when your body heat has shifted it.  
- **Drink from the thin films silvered on walls only if you have purified them mentally, not chemically**. The water is clean in a way that feels pre-industrial and pre-biological. To add tablets or filters is to insult the city’s long practice of self-curation. A simple act of intention—holding your hands under a seep and cupping—suffices. If the water tastes of stone and patience, it is accepting you. If it tastes of nothing at all, wait. It is not yet ready.  
- **Never bathe in the channels**. They are not meant for bodies. If you require washing, collect water in a basin and let it fall from a height, mimicking the city’s own curtained eaves. This is the closest you can come to being cleaned by the place rather than merely within it.

---

### 7. The Acoustic Etiquette
Sound is the place’s loudest citizen, but it is not without manners.  
- **When the echoes braid into chords that linger**, do not speak over them. Pause. Let the city finish its sentence. In colonnades where echoes linger, words spoken too soon will bounce back as fragments that feel like insult.  
- **Carry a small striker and a piece of resonant stone only if you know how to listen before you strike**. Music here is not performance; it is dialogue. A single note that matches the resonant frequency of a hollow can open a channel of understanding. A careless rhythm can close doors that were never visible to begin with.  
- **Learn the difference between greeting and intrusion**. A scrape at one end of the settlement arrives at the other as a softened but recognizable greeting. If you hear your own noise return changed—gentler, lower, threaded with water—consider yourself acknowledged. If it returns sharp or split, you have been gently rebuked. Adjust.

---

### 8. What to Carry (and What to Leave Behind)
**Carry:**  
- A notebook of heavy, absorbent paper and a pencil (ink can behave unpredictably in heavy moisture).  
- A length of rope woven from plant fiber, thin but strong, for marking your path in a pinch—but only if you intend to remove it before you leave. Marks here last longer than you do.  
- A small offering of shaped stone (not valuable, but *finished*) to leave at the heart plaza if you feel gratitude. Do not leave metal; it is a shy guest.  
- A sealed skin for your own water, to remind you of home when the city’s patience feels alien.

**Leave Behind:**  
- Ambition to change or map the place. It is already exactly what it is.  
- Expectations of heroism or discovery. Here, persistence is rewarded; force is not.  
- Any belief that silence is empty. It is dense here, and it is polite.

---

### 9. Departure: How to Be Let Out
You will not find the entrance; it will find you.  
- **Spend your last hours in the heart plaza, where the overlapping eaves weave a curtain of threads**. Sit by the slab worn to a perfect bowl. Let the city adjust to your presence fully. When the water’s rhythm includes your breath without effort, you will know the threshold is preparing to open for you.  
- **Do not look back as you leave**. The basalt will seal itself behind you. To look back is to ask for a second invitation, and second invitations are given only to those who proved they understood the first.  
- **Once outside**, do not speak of the city’s exact location until you have walked three full days under open sky. The city’s silence needs time to settle across your memory without distortion.

---

### 10. Final Intuition
Visiting this place is not about seeing, but about *being seen by listening*. The city is mineral and loud, wet and quiet in its bones. It holds together by being contained, and it remembers by being exactly itself. If you prepare as a guest rather than a traveler, as a resident of time rather than a passer-through, the cool dark will not feel like an absence. It will feel like a kind of attention—steady, dense, and softly, insistently bright.

And when you return to your own continuum, you will find your hands know how to ask stone for permission before you shape it, and your ears will carry, for a long time, the polite noise of a city that never needed to grow because it learned, with perfect patience, how to remember.
