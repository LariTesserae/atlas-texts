Here is how to prepare—not to conquer or to document, but to survive long enough that the chamber decides you are harmless enough to keep.

### 1. Respect the Thermodynamics of Crowds
That 2.6 reading is a lie. It is the average, not the truth. In a press this dense, metabolic heat pools. The outermost layer may be lukewarm; the interior can cook. Bring gear that does not regulate temperature for you so much as it lets you borrow and loan heat without dying of it.

- Wear loose-layered garments of mineral-dense fabrics (treated alpaca, felted basalt wool, or silk lined with mica). They will hold heat when you are pressed inward, vent it when the tide pulls you outward.  
- Pack phase-change pouches you can swap like breath. You will learn to pass heat from your core to the chamber’s walls by stages—warming a stone, letting it cool against the wall, retrieving it—because in this room, cooling is an offense and you must apologize for it.  
- Bring salt. Not to eat, but to trade. The fauna will strip it from your sweat; offer it willingly so they do not strip it from your tears.

### 2. Learn the Grammar of Not Being Air
The air is thin and metallic because the room has scrubbed it clean of light molecules. You cannot fix that. You can only reduce how much of yourself you feed to it.

- Carry rebreathers that are less about filtering than about hoarding: closed-loop scrubbers that recover moisture from your exhalations and return it as a drinkable trickle. You will learn to sip your own breath.  
- Train before you arrive to tolerate low-oxygen etiquette: shallow, silent breaths; exhales only when the fauna around you exhale; inhales timed to gaps in the chitinous tide. Do not gasp. A gasp here is a scream that brings attention you cannot afford.  
- Carry a small, heavy stone you can warm against your chest. In extremis, you will press it to your sternum to remind your heart that cold is a choice, not a condition.

### 3. Do Not Bring Light—Borrow It
Bioluminescence is the room’s currency, its syntax, its law. Artificial light will not read as illumination; it will read as a wound, a breach, a thing that must be cauterized.

- Use only what you can wear: dabbed phosphorescent algae under your nails, faintly ticking, synced to your pulse so you do not signal faster than the chamber’s mood.  
- Learn to read the fauna’s flickers. A slow green pulse along a flank means “do not move.” A rapid violet stutter along a throat means “make room.” A flat, dead matte means “something has died nearby; hold your breath and your shape.”  
- If you must carry a torch, carry a cold one—a rod of etched obsidian that catches the fauna’s own light and bends it, refracting it into patterns that apologize for being geometric.

### 4. Dress for Pressures You Cannot See
Elevation here does not thin the air so much as it teaches it to cut. Pack a suit that is less armor than etiquette: layered, segmented, soft at the joints.

- Make it matte, not shiny. Shine asks to be rubbed away.  
- Make it tight at the cuffs and ankles, loose at the core. When the tide compresses you, you want to become a hinge, not a column.  
- Line it with scent-neutral resin. The fauna will anoint you with their own oils if you smell too much like void. Let them do it; it is the price of passage.

### 5. Tools That Do Not Exist
Do not bring tools that imply intent. Levers, lenses, wires—these are languages the chamber has refused for so long that it will mistake them for threats.

- Instead, bring tools that mimic the fauna’s own: a curved spine of ceramic to scrape resin from a wall; a mesh pouch of treated sinew to carry fragments of shed shell; a hollow finger-joint you can fill with water and press against a hot stone to make steam, briefly, as a signal of respect.  
- Carry a notebook of pressed fungus paper and ink made from your own diluted sweat. Write small, sparingly. The room remembers pressure, and ink is pressure made visible.

### 6. The Etiquette of Space
The chamber is full. It will not make room for you; you must become room.

- Move as if you are molting. Do not push; settle. When the tide presses you against the wall, do not brace. Let your shape deform slightly—shoulders in, ribs soft—so that you become a pocket the chamber can hold without noticing.  
- Keep your hands open. Closed hands are interpreted as weapons or food.  
- When you rest, do not lie down. Rest by leaning into the press, letting the fauna flow around you like water around a stone that is learning to be porous.

### 7. Food and Death
Do not bring food that cannot be metabolized by the room. You will not eat meals; you will eat phases.

- Carry a small reserve of the resin the walls weep—iron and honey. It is not nourishing, but it is recognizable. Eat a thimbleful when you feel cold approaching; it tells the chamber you are willing to be part of its economy.  
- If you die (unlikely if you are careful, but possible), let it happen quickly. Do not struggle. The fauna will dismantle you with a speed that will feel like kindness. Your materials will be sorted, your heat redistributed. You will become a statistic in the chamber’s breath, a faint new note in its hum. This is not a failure; it is citizenship.

### 8. Time and Rhythm
There are no days here. There are only phases: the Tightening, the Unspooling, the Glisten.

- Mark them by the resin on your sleeves. When it is thick and sweet, the Glisten is near; stay near the walls, do not climb. When it is brittle and gray, the Tightening has begun; seek the center vortex and let it turn you. When it is soft and copper-scented, the Unspooling is upon you; move with the young, but do not lead them.  
- Sleep only in microcycles, eyes open, heart slow. The room will wake you when you are needed; trust it.

### 9. What to Leave Behind
Do not bring ambition. Do not bring maps. Do not bring a desire to name what you see. The chamber already has names for everything, and they are written in heat and pressure and the slow grammar of erosion.

Leave your sense of scale at the door. This is not a cathedral, though it is the size of one. It is a lung that has forgotten how to exhale into sky. It is a kiln that has decided to be a home. It is a society that has solved the problem of being alone by deciding there is no such thing as alone.

### 10. The Only Rule
When the chamber breathes, exhale with it. When it holds its breath, hold yours. When it decides you are part of the pattern, do not resist.

You will not conquer this place. You will, at best, become a faint new seam in its wall, a patch of warmth in its tide, a note in its quiet hum. That is enough. That is everything.
