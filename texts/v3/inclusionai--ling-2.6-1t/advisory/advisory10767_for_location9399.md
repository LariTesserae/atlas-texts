How to prepare for the Downward City (travel notes for the ordinary multiverse walker)

I. The first rule: remember that “prepared” here does not mean armored.  
If you bring heavy armor, it will remember you. The moisture will bloom tiny orange constellations on your steel, and by the second day you’ll be carrying a garden. Chainmail sings in the humidity and offends the bats. Plate scrapes the arches and the arches will quietly answer by softening the stone around your ankles so that your steps feel heavier. Dress instead in layers that breathe, accept stains, and dry slowly—linen, wool felt, oiled canvas. Bring a cloak the color of wet clay so that when you crouch in a low arch, you become part of the wall’s thought.

II. Learn the gravity of lowness before you arrive.  
Practice walking with your chin tucked, not bowed but cradled. Practice turning sideways to let a room pass through you. In your own world, stand in a doorway and let the lintel tap your forehead three times so your body learns the polite rhythm of yielding. If you can, spend a night in a basement or a root cellar. Let the air be the same temperature as your skin. Train your eyes to see by bounce-light—polished shell, alabaster, the soft gleam of wet plaster—because the sun here arrives as a guest, not a ruler, and you will spend most of your time in its absence.

III. Your pack should be a conversation, not a declaration.  
- Water skins: two, one inside the other. When the inside weeps, let the outside catch it. Empty them each night into a basin. The city likes to give back what you brought, but changed, so taste it. It will taste of dust and old thread, and it will be safe.  
- Soft tools: a bone needle, a waxed cord, a small trowel with a blunt edge for shaping rather than cutting, a lump of clay wrapped in leaves. Do not bring metal for digging. The stone will feel insulted.  
- Light: a candle in a wide dish, a mirror the size of your palm (dark metal, not silver), a handful of dried moss that has already been trained to smolder slowly. The city provides mirrors that remember; bring one that only reflects so that you have somewhere honest to stand when the walls begin to whisper backward.  
- Footwear: thin soles, supple, with a soft heel that can feel the runnels before you step into them. You will need to know where the water has been, because it tells you where the room will exhale next.  
- Food: dense, crumbly, not fragrant. Think flatbreads, hard cheese wrapped in oiled paper, dried fruit that has forgotten its color. Avoid anything that wants to perfume the air. The city dislikes loud smells; it will open a hidden flue and exile them, and you with them.  
- Gifts: small bowls, a shell, a length of bright thread, a polished river stone. These are for the bowls that collect condensation. If they are gone in the morning, do not mourn them. If they remain, move them to a lower shelf as a thank-you.

IV. Map with your body, not your eyes.  
The Downward City does not obey Euclidean consistency. A corridor felt longer walking one way than the other because it is listening to your heartbeat. Carry a spool of thin thread and unwind it behind you, but know that the city will eventually roll some of it back into a ball and leave it in your pocket. Do not rely on the maps you bring; draw maps on your forearm with a soft pigment that sweats away in warmth. When the line fades, you will know you have been rearranged. Notice when your left shoulder remembers a wall that your right shoulder never met. That is the city breathing sideways.

V. Time is a shared resource.  
The place has middling heat by design, a constant exhale that keeps things moving without hurrying. Do not try to hurry. If you do, the stairs will soften at the edges and your feet will sink slightly, asking you to stay. Instead, adopt the tempo of moss. Plan for nothing to happen quickly. Let decisions steep. If you must make a choice, whisper it to a bowl of collected water and return in three hours. The water will have settled, and so will you.

VI. Learn the etiquette of fauna.  
The wings that brush your ears are not attacking but negotiating. Hold still and let them taste your breath. The lizards with bright throats are the city’s librarians; do not block their paths, or the room may forget which way opens. The small furred things with many joints are rumor-mongers. If they pause and stare, smile back slowly. If they groom themselves, you are acceptable. If they scatter, take a different route; something in the air has become impolite. The cat-sized watcher on the ledge is not a guardian but a moderator. It waits for the proper moment, and it expects you to wait too. Do not stare at it for long; stare at the space it is leaving empty, and step there.

VII. Respect the living architecture.  
When you touch the wall, press with the flat of your hand, not your fingertips. The city has forgotten it was stone, but it has not forgotten pressure. If you carve or chip, the wall will not heal, but it will grieve in that slow tear-seam way, and the grief will seep into your gear. If you need to rest, choose a seat that has already been shaped by sitting. If you must rest where nothing has been shaped, sit for only a moment, then move on, so the stone does not begin to mold itself to your bones.

VIII. Prepare for indoor weather.  
Carry a second, smaller cloak for the warm rains. They arrive when the place exhales, smelling of dust and wet thread. They do not chill, but they confuse electronics, maps, and pride. When they fall, raise your hands and let them run down your arms into the runnels. Bow slightly, as if accepting a blessing from an elder. The city will notice and will, for a little while, route the breezes to dry your back.

IX. Speak softly, and in patterns.  
Voices rise and fall in shapes that are almost music. The city collects them and turns them into the thrum in the walls. If you must shout, press your mouth to the stone and let the wall carry the force. If you sing, sing in rounds—starting soft, letting your voice overlap with the drip of water and the shuffle of feet. When the rains come inside, join the drum. The city loves company in its loudest moments.

X. Know what to do when the weirdness becomes familiar.  
If a corridor feels longer walking one way, walk it twice and make an offering at the midpoint. If a tool sings, hold it at the angle that pleases it, then set it down gently and use your hands instead. If the dark-metal mirrors show more than they reflect, do not ask them for the future; ask them for the recent past, and do not argue with what they show. Treat these not as violations but as habits, the place’s private manners.

XI. When you leave, leave something of yourself that can be reshaped.  
A braid of your hair in a basket. A drop of your blood in a mortar that will be pounded into new plaster. A whispered secret into a bowl that feeds a cistern. The city does not like empty-handed partings. It will fold your gift into its skin and, over time, forget whose it was, which is a mercy.

XII. Finally, prepare to be ordinary here.  
The Downward City is a place of patient repetition, soft tools, and time. Your multiverse-hardened nerves, your scars from harder worlds, your talent for quick exits—set them aside like heavy tools you will retrieve when you reach a louder realm. Here, you are not the traveler who passes through. You are the guest who becomes part of the pattern. Walk softly, give freely, move slowly, and let the walls learn your shape. When the ceiling lowers and the runnels find your feet, do not pull away. Let the city hold you, and for a little while, let it be enough to be warm, to be low, to be alive in a made world that has almost forgotten it was made.

Then, when the next warm exhale begins, and the small rains rise, and the many-jointed furred things scatter like thoughts before waking, you will know that you have been prepared not for a danger, but for a kindness—a deep, patient, living kindness—and you will step forward, not into a hallway, but into the city’s next soft breath.
