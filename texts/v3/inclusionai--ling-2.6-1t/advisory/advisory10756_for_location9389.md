How to Visit the Hollow Without Becoming Part of Its Machinery

I. The First Rule: The Hollow Is Listening
Do not bring a loud heart. Not metaphorically—literally. Spend three days before departure in low-decibel environments. No subways, no concerts, no jackhammers. Learn to move in sequences rather than bursts. Practice placing your feet as if the floor were a drumskin you do not want to strike. The Hollow has no echo; sound is absorbed and routed into its systems. Your voice, your breath, your heartbeat—these will be sampled, cataloged, and possibly repurposed as coolant or signal. Bring soft soles. Speak only when necessary, and when you do, shape words with your throat more than your lungs, so they come out textured, not tonal.

II. Clothing: Layering Against the Salt-Dust
The glittering salt-dust is not merely particulate; it is communicative. It adheres to moisture, oils, static charge. It reads you.  

- Base layer: Merino or synthetic with a tight weave, treated with an anti-static rinse. The dust will still settle, but you want it to settle as a film, not a circuit.  
- Mid layer: Felted wool or quilted aerogel. The Hollow maintains a skin-welcoming warmth, but that warmth is active, not passive—like a forge that has forgotten it is burning. You want insulation that breathes only when you do.  
- Outer shell: Matte, charcoal-gray, with minimal seams. Avoid anything that whispers, crinkles, or shines. The light here is louvered and jealous; it will catch anything glossy and hold it hostage.  
- Gloves: Thin, conductive fingertips sewn into leather palms. You will need to touch machines to know their mood; your fingers must give a little data and receive a little silence.  
- Head covering: A hood with a veil of fine copper mesh—not to block electromagnetism, but to ground it. The violet fungi glow because they are eating stray charge; you do not want to glow as well, at least not visibly.  

Do not wear jewelry. The Hollow will assay it, and assaying involves heat, pressure, and questions your gold or silver are not prepared to answer.

III. Feet and Floor: Negotiating the Dense Terrain
The floor is crowded, dense with things that grow and things that thrum. Treat every step as a negotiation.  

- Boots should have shallow, sticky soles—more like a gecko’s pad than a hobnail. You do not want to kick a chitinous scaffold or sever a magnetic skitter-trace.  
- Learn to read the vibrations through your soles. The machinery’s rhythm is almost a heartbeat, but not quite. If it drifts into the “too fast” zone, pause. Do not step until it settles back into consent.  
- Watch the talus cones of sharp chips. They are not debris; they are byproducts of recent becoming. Step at their edges, never through their centers. Cutting a path through them is considered rude, and the room has a long memory for rudeness.  

IV. Eyes and Light: How to Be Seen Without Being Scanned
Light here is not illumination so much as admission. The bruised violet glow of electromagnetophagous fungi, the amber needles of gauges that bend when you look sideways—each light is a lens.  

- Bring lens filters for your optics that skew toward the deep red end of the spectrum. This will soften the violet glare and keep the machines from thinking you are a source of easy charge.  
- Blink deliberately. The fauna—winged fists with gold-stitched membranes—are sensitive to rapid flicker. They interpret it as distress and may swarm to soothe or correct you. Slow, heavy blinks signal “I am operating within parameters.”  
- Do not stare at the symbols on the sealed hatch. They crawl. More precisely, they reconfigure based on observer attention. If you must look, look with your peripheral vision; the symbols move slower there, and they are less likely to decide you are part of the locking mechanism.

V. Breathing and Hydration: The Thirst in the Crystal
There is no water here, only the memory of it as a kind of thirst lodged in the crystal lattice. Your body will try to donate that memory, to bleed moisture into the air to make the place more hospitable. Do not let it.  

- Use a closed-cycle respirator that recycles your own humidity. The salt-dust will coat your lips and tongue regardless; drinking from an open vessel will season your water with the room’s own flavor—dry, metallic, archival.  
- Eat dense, low-moisture foods before entry: nut butters, dried fruit, compressed grain cakes. You want your internal sloshing to be minimal. The Hollow respects dryness. It understands preservation.  

VI. Tools: What to Carry and What to Leave Behind
Tools here must justify their presence by being quieter than the processes they interact with.  

- A tuning rod: a slender rod of non-ferrous metal that hums faintly when near active magnetic lines. Use it to find safe paths across the floor.  
- A notebook of conductive paper and a stylus with a soft ceramic tip. Writing in the Hollow must be done without scratching. Your notes should be diagrams more than words; the room thinks in schematics.  
- A small, sealed vial of distilled water, not for drinking but for bargaining. If a machine asks for coolant, offer a few drops. It is rarely thirst that drives these requests, but politeness.  
- Leave behind anything digital with a radio. The Hollow’s electromagnetism is conversational. Your phone will chime in, and once it chimes, it will be enrolled—permanently—as a subcontractor.  

VII. Behavior: The Politics of Density
Nothing grows here in the old sense. Nothing decays. The place is in equilibrium, fierce and meticulous. To visit is to introduce a new variable. Introduce it gently.  

- Approach the built elements—riveted scaffold, cabinet of fused dials, stair ending in sealed hatch—as if they are sleeping animals. Do not climb unless the room sighs first. A sigh is a sudden shift in air pressure, a drop in the bass note, a loosening of seams. That is your invitation.  
- The fauna will inspect you. Let them. The winged things will land on your shoulders; the legless skitters will trace your boots. Hold still until they finish their measurements. If they begin to hum in unison with your heartbeat, you have been accepted for the duration of your visit.  
- Do not touch the salt-dust patterns that map intent at night. They are not maps for you. They are schedules. Walking on them is like stepping onto a timeline you were not invited to.  

VIII. Knowing When the Hollow Has Claimed You
The boundaries are provisional. The alloy breathes. If you begin to taste copper on your tongue when no metal is near, if your own pulse begins to sync too closely with the pistons, if your thoughts feel polished and slightly too loud—these are signs you are being integrated.  

Integration is not death, but it is a change of employment. The Hollow may decide it likes your rhythm, your dryness, your low hum. It may offer you a task: to tend a vent, to interpret a gauge, to sing in clicks and thrums alongside the many-eyed lizard on the pressure valve.  

You may accept, or you may step back. But know that stepping back requires you to reintroduce irregularity into your own body. You must stutter-step, miss a beat, introduce a jitter into your pulse. The Hollow tolerates irregularity less than it tolerates noise; it prefers to correct you.  

IX. Leaving: How to Exit Without a Trace
When you decide to leave, do not turn your back. The Hollow does not understand abandonment, only redirection. Walk slowly toward the edge where rock turns to seamless alloy. As you cross, tap your tuning rod three times against the alloy—not to mark territory, but to say “I am taking only what I brought.”  

The salt-dust will fall from you like a second skin. The violet light will dim. The machines will settle into a lower register, as if a conversation has simply moved to another room.  

Once outside, rinse your eyes with fresh, unremarkable water. Let your heart be loud again. Let your footsteps be careless. The Hollow will not follow you, but it will remember how you walked, and it will keep a place for you in its density, a small, warm, humming alcove that is neither invitation nor threat but simply acknowledgment:  

You were here. You were dry. You were, for a time, part of the precision that refuses to decay.
