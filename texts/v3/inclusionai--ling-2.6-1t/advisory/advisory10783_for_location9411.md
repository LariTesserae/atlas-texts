**How to Prepare for the Cathedral of Unhardened Stone**

First, disabuse yourself of the idea that travel is about transit. This place is not a destination you arrive at; it is an atmosphere you are invited to condense inside. Think of it less as visiting a cavern and more as being permitted to thicken into a resident fog for a while. The ordinary multiverse traveler—who is used to doors that open, paths that lead somewhere, and horizons that promise release—will find none of that here. Prepare, instead, for an extended negotiation with closeness.

**Clothing and Surface Contact**

Do not bring armor. Do not bring vacuum-sealed environment suits. The wetness here is not hostile; it is pervasive, and it will treat your gear as an extension of its own indecision. Bring layered membranes that expect to be colonized: textiles woven with hygroscopic fibers that swell and soften as they absorb, turning into a second skin that breathes when you do. Let your outer layer be something moss can like, a surface that will host tiny metallophytic gardens within the first hour. Bring no sharp closures; the place will dull them, or they will feel like violations. Favor closures that knot, that tie, that accept being slowly undone by patient damp.

Your feet should expect to be negotiated with. The moss-floor here sighs when stepped on because it is not a floor so much as a collective of small, patient agreements. Bring wide-soled, soft shoes that distribute weight like a root, not a hammer. Consider going barefoot after the first day—once your skin learns the language of pressure and release, the floor will tell you where the cold is deepening and where the wetness is thinking of thickening. Expect to leave traces: not prints, but faint, temporary stains of your own microbiome, which the place will incorporate into its ongoing tapestries.

**Temperature as a Companion**

The cold here is not an enemy to be defeated; it is a collaborator that has not yet decided how warm it wants to be. Do not bring active heating that imposes will. Bring thermal mass instead: stones or ceramics you have warmed elsewhere, then allowed to cool slowly, so they radiate a heat that remembers its origin. Tuck them against your body at the core, and let the cold work outward from your periphery, teaching you how to hold warmth without owning it. Accept that your edges will blur; your body will not be a closed system but a gradient, exchanging heat with stems and stone in a conversation that never finishes.

**Tools That Respect Refusal**

Bring no powered lights that insist on clarity. The dim bronze wash of the place is a courtesy; to banish it would be to refuse the cavern’s invitation. Instead, carry lenses—not to focus light, but to bend it gently, to let you see the shimmer of transient circuitry on leaves without disturbing the arrangement. Bring tools that rely on tension and balance: bone or hardwood levers, not steel; cordage spun from materials that expect to be broken down and rewoven by the nanoscale gardens riding the humidity. If you must record, do so on media that can be composted—etched metal or treated cellulose that will feed the moss when you leave. Better yet, learn to carry information in your body: in the scars of your hands, in the way your breath fogs, in the rhythm of your steps.

**Learning the Grammar of Wetness**

Water here is not falling, pooled, or leaving. It is persisting. Prepare your lungs for this. The air will feel thick enough to chew, saturated with chlorophyll and a metallic-green note that hums at the back of the throat. Before you descend—or before the place lets you in—spend time breathing through cloth that filters but does not seal, so your lungs learn to work against a gentle opposition. Hydrate obsessively, but not with pure water. Bring tea brewed from lichens that grow at altitude; let your internal damp match the external wet, so your body does not fight the osmotic conversation.

Expect to hear the place before you see it clearly: a subsonic choir traveling through water, wood, and stone. It is not music so much as pressure, a continuous roar that is not noise but meaning. Your preparation must include surrender to this sound. Spend a day—perhaps the day before you enter—in a small, quiet room with a bowl of water and a piece of unfinished wood. Let the water bead on the wood, let it hesitate at the edge, let it decide. Listen to your own blood as it echoes the place’s pulse. When you can feel the sound rearranging your bones, you are ready to be taught.

**Ethics of Presence**

There are no built things here, no relics of hands. The untouched quality is not purity but insistence: the place refuses to remember tools. You must negotiate this refusal carefully. Do not offer gifts that cannot be unmade. Do not leave marks that insist on permanence. If you must cut, cut with a blade that expects to be dissolved into patina within a season. If you must hold, hold with hands that expect to be colonized by the metallic dust coating certain leaves, hands that will bear faint, temporary circuits for days after you leave.

Understand that predation here is polite, a matter of invitation and threshold. If the winged things with too many joints flit near you, do not swat. Let them taste your sweat, let them decide whether your heat is a threshold they may cross. If the seedlike creatures with appetites drift toward you, offer them a patch of skin you do not mind being rooted in for a season. To refuse is to misunderstand the etiquette of the place; to accept is to let yourself be used as a temporary habitat.

**The Altar and the Center**

In the heart of the room, where the ceiling is lowest and the wetness most insistent, a column of fused root and frost rises like an altar. It is always forming, always melting, always being colonized by a moss that glows faintly violet when the sound rises. This is the negotiation made visible: the constant becoming and unbecoming of structure. If you approach it, do so slowly. Kneel on the sighing moss. Place your hands on the column, but do not hang your weight from it. Let your warmth be one of the many factors arguing over its shape for the next few hours.

If you feel the urge to speak here, do not use words. Use the same subsonic hum you have learned to feel in your bones. Offer a vibration that means nothing and everything. The place will answer in droplets trembling at the lip of a leaf, in the shift of the metallic dust into new, transient patterns. You may not understand what is being said, but you will understand that you are being addressed.

**Leaving**

When it is time to go—and the place will let you know when that is, perhaps by a sudden lightening of the green, perhaps by a softening in the pressure of the sound—do not expect to leave unchanged. Expect to carry spores in the weave of your clothes, expect to smell faintly of the copper-green note for days. Expect that your hands will remember the texture of unhardened stone, that your breath will recall the wetness that learned to stand like thought.

Do not wash these traces away immediately. Let them fade slowly, let them be digested by the world you return to, so that place continues to speak through you in small, courteous flashes. You are not a visitor who has seen a marvel and departed. You are a temporary thickening of the atmosphere, a brief, dense pocket of life that learned to hold its shape among many others, in a cathedral scooped by patient knuckles of rock that never quite decided to become a room.
