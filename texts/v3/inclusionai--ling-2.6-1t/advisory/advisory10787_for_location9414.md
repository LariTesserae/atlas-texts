If you intend to step into this kneeling lung-chapel, abandon ideas of traversal and replace them with ideas of accommodation. This is not a place you cross; it is a place you are enrolled in. Think of yourself not as a visitor to a landscape but as a new chamber introduced into an old, careful breathing pattern. Below is a field guide for how to prepare—not only gear, but stance, timing, body, and mind—for entering a world that is half greenhouse, half engine, half lung, half house of worship.

### 1. Learn to breathe long before you arrive
The lung’s tempo is hours-long, tidal, and humid. Your own breath will betray you if you keep city rhythms—short, shallow, hurried. Practice diaphragmatic breathing with extended counts: inhale for six, hold for two, exhale for eight or ten, until your body remembers what it feels like to fill a room rather than just your chest. Train your blood to tolerate mild hypercapnia—slightly elevated CO2—from air that is rich with the scent of vegetal decay and warm metal. If you find yourself gasping in the first day, the place will interpret your alarm as a storm and may answer with thicker air, louder drips, closer light. Learn, too, to breathe through your nose: the mucous membranes here are partners, not obstacles. They will catch information the eyes miss—trace metals, spores that sing, the faint ozone signature of hidden pumps.

### 2. Dress in materials that consent to being colonized
Do not bring clothing that fights moisture; bring clothing that negotiates with it. Think layered merino, aerated synthetics with hydrophobic cores and hydrophilic faces, and outer shells that breathe but do not leak. Treat these garments as temporary lichen: expect them to be stitched into the local texture by rootlets, mossed by condensation, plated with thin films of mineral and oil. Bring no leather that you love—this place will tan it in its own way, softening it into something unrecognizable. Wear colors that do not shout: dust greens, bruised violets, rust, bone. Bright colors act as invitations to insects that mistake pigment for nectar, and to molds that mistake dye for sugar.

Footwear should be wide, soft-soled, and quick-draining. The floor is not earth but compressed memory of water—spongy, yielding, occasionally sticky. You will not climb so much as settle, slide, and root. Consider gaiters that seal but flex, because the ground exhales wet sheets that creep upward with the lung’s exhale, seeking the warmest skin.

### 3. Pack for maintenance rather than conquest
Tools that go into the lung must be humble and willing to be adopted. A multi-tool of nonferrous metals (titanium, bronze) will not smell of blood to the place and will resist the thin acids that condensate carries. A small hand-lens or pocket microscope is more useful than a camera at first: the place writes in spores and vein-patterns, in gradients that are too fine for distance. Bring a notebook sealed in waxed cloth, and pencils, not pens—ink here can bloom into unexpected floras. A collapsible cup and a filter that works by capillary action rather than pressure, because water is everywhere but often too polite to flow on command. A headlamp with a red-light mode, and low lumens; brightness is considered gauche in rooms that glow from within. A thermometer and an aneroid barometer will speak to you in the lung’s own language—pressure changes are the syllables of its speech.

Bring a small offering of sugar, salt, and oil—not to placate, but to introduce yourself as a source of simple gifts. Scatter them at junctions where metal and root meet. The place may incorporate them into its secret economies.

### 4. Treat time as a thick substance
Schedule collapses here. Do not plan to arrive and depart on the same day as measured by clocks that live elsewhere. Instead, plan to pass through three phases of the lung’s breath: the slow gathering, the held fullness, the patient release. Each phase will feel like a different geography. In the gathering, light angles shift only slightly, but shadows grow heavier, water climbs, and the air grows sweeter with the scent of impending movement. In fullness, the room holds itself taut, a hush of potential like a choir about to sing. In release, surfaces part, drips become curtains, and the floor becomes briefly lacquered, mirrors for the brass-lid sky.

Work with your own fatigue as the lung works with its pressures. When you are tired, rest against a wall; expect the wall to warm to your shape, to sweat into your shirt, to offer you its steady hum. Sleep when the light dims into pools that refuse to leave. The lung’s dreams are slow; yours should be, too.

### 5. Master the etiquette of density
Everything here is pressed close, but nothing is crushed. There is a courteous pressure that holds the room together. When you move, do so as if sliding between lines of a crowded chorus, not cutting through a forest. Offer your edges before your center. Touch walls with the backs of your hands first; let them taste your skin before you commit your weight. If a door or opening presents itself—and openings here are suggestions, not promises—pause to feel the draft: is it entering or leaving? Enter when the air moves in; exit when it moves out. To force a passage against the breath is to be asked, politely but firmly, to leave by other means.

Silence is not empty here; it is busy. Listen for the difference between the mumble of machines and the mumble of growth. They are not the same, though they often harmonize. The machines keep promises about heat and flow; the growth keeps promises about shape and time. Align yourself with both, and you will be given small efficiencies: a path that stays dry, a patch of light that lingers, a surface that offers purchase.

### 6. Accept incorporation
You will return from this place carrying it. Spores will colonize your pockets. Oils will stain your cuffs with the patina of old engines. Your hair will remember humidity. Your lungs will carry a new setpoint for oxygen and damp. Prepare your home to receive these gifts: designate a shelf for drying clothes that will never fully dry, a jar for water that will never fully settle, a box for stones that hum. Expect that some part of the lung will decide to keep you, in the form of a changed rhythm in your chest, a new preference for low light, a tendency to hold your breath when you hear certain sounds.

### 7. Practicalities of survival inside the lung’s logic
- Hydration: Drink small amounts often, even if you do not feel thirsty. The air’s moisture will make you forget to drink, and your kidneys will work harder to keep balance. Add a pinch of salt and a trace of sugar to your water to match the osmotic mood of the place.
- Skin care: Apply a thin barrier cream to prevent maceration, but do not coat yourself in armor. Let the place’s dampness in, in small doses, to avoid the shock of sudden humidity change.
- Navigation: Landmarks here are temporary agreements between growth and artifact. Memorize relationships rather than points: the engine that sings to the thickest stem, the patch of floor that stays glossy longest, the chimney that tastes like rain before rain arrives. Use string if you must, but expect it to be borrowed by vines.
- Safety: The lung is not malicious, but it is thorough. Loose clothing can be caught by slow, strong growths. Rings and watches can become habitats. Remove anything you are not willing to surrender to integration.
- Energy: Eat foods that release slowly—nuts, dense breads, fatty fish. The lung’s pace will slow your digestion if you let it; honor that by giving your body fuel that lasts.

### 8. Prepare for wonder without spectacle
This is not a place of vistas. It is a place of pressings and releases, of close textures and near-sounds, of warmth that settles like a hand. Bring your capacity for small revelations: the way a drip finds a vein and changes its route, the moment when the light catches a film of water and turns it into a lens, the recognition that the machine’s hum and the stem’s sway are in counterpoint, not coincidence. The cathedral here is built of accumulation, not aspiration. Its grandeur is in patience, not scale.

### 9. Know how to leave gracefully
Leaving is an agreement to be forgotten slowly. As you pass the threshold backwards, turn once and offer a breath-long silence toward the heart of the lung. Do not look back too often; each glance creates a small disturbance that the place must smooth out. Expect to feel lighter as you depart, not only because you are leaving weight behind, but because the lung will take a portion of your heat and your rhythm as fare for keeping you. This is the toll of admission, and it is also the proof that you were inside a living thing that knew you, however briefly, as part of its breath.

If you prepare this way, you will not conquer the lung-chapel, nor will you simply survive it. You will pass into its rhythm and, for a time, become another careful chamber in its old, breathing house. You will hear its speech as a kind of music, feel its closeness as a kind of shelter, and understand that to visit a place that is patient, low, and densely alive is to be reminded that being human is also a form of inhabitation—something we do with time, damp, and breath, pressed close to everything else that is quietly, carefully living.
