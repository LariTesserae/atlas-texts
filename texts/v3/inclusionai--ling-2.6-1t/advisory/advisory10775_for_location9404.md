How to visit a continental saucer that forgot it was sky

I. The intuition before arrival  
You do not arrive at this place—you are admitted into its circulation. Treat the saucer not as a city on a world but as a world wearing a city like a second skin. Pressure is the first language here, and altitude is a myth the place tells tourists to keep them from getting too comfortable. Bring humility; bring socks that can think. Bring the sense that gravity is a polite suggestion and that water is a civic employee with seniority.

II. Bodies and biology: learn to sweat in advance  
The warmth is not ambient; it is structural. The city leaks heat the way a heart leaks blood. Prepare your body to be a radiator:  
- Train in heavy, humid rooms weeks before departure so your sweat glands learn diplomacy.  
- Hydration is not about thirst; it is about citizenship. Bring electrolyte blends that read like small poems (magnesium, potassium, a whisper of zinc).  
- Beware the “dry sweat” of 1.7 wetness: moisture beads on non-wettable skin, so ordinary cotton will glue itself to you and remember your shame. Fabrics should be capillaries, not curtains.  
- Do not shave for three days before you go. Microscopic mineral hairs will catch stray charge and keep you from floating away in magnetic corridors.

III. Dress as if architecture is alive  
The city dresses in strata; you should too.  
- Outer layer: a shell that breathes outward only, treated with a temporary hydrophobe that the city’s own rain will respect but not erode. It will eventually be colonized by mineral scab—let it; it is a badge.  
- Mid-layer: phase-change cloth that hoards coolness from the city’s scheduled micro-storms.  
- Base-layer: silver-threaded weave to speak to the magnetic tattoos on walls. You want to be legible to doors.  
- Footwear: wide, soft soles that apologize as they step, because the ground remembers every footfall as data. Vibram that tastes of ozone will keep you from slipping on crystalline coral-forests of cooled lightning.  
- Accessories: a hat with gutters. A walking stick of basalt that can drink from a gutter and tell you what the city last dreamed. Earplugs that look like amber but are actually acoustic seals—silence is expensive, but you can rent it by the hour.

IV. Health against the city’s metabolism  
- Your lungs will meet a soup of ozone, wet stone, and machine pollen. Bring a mask that does not block but translates—convert irritants into data you can exhale as glitter.  
- Your ears will meet 2.7 loud. Practice listening in angles. Do not try to find quiet; negotiate it. Carry a token of silence in a sealed pouch to trade at kiosks that sell pockets.  
- Your joints will meet turbulence 2.9. Train balance as if you were a spinning plate that likes applause. Ankles should know how to forgive floors that rearrange.  
- Your skin will meet erosion 1.5—gentle but persistent. Apply a balm of calendula and mineral grit before bed so you shed city memories evenly.  
- Your mind will meet civic weirdness 1.1: clocks run faster when admired; corridors lengthen if you apologize too often. Mantra: “I am polite but not desperate.” Admire sparingly. Apologize precisely. Keep a notebook to log time dilation so you do not lose appointments to flattery.

V. Logistics of entry and exit  
- There are no gates, only gradients. You will step off a platform and feel the city accept your weight like a new layer of sediment. Have your invitation (a small glass bead etched with a date) ready. It is not a ticket; it is a sedimentary record that proves you will not destabilize the strata.  
- Arrival is lateral: you will not descend so much as inward. Motion sickness is rare; metaphysical lag is common. The first meal should be warm broth with trace metals to help your blood remember that density is comfort.  
- Departure is negotiated. The city will ask for a memory in exchange for lift. Choose a small one, not a precious one—something that fits in a thimble. Offer it before you ask to leave.  

VI. Navigation without horizon  
- Horizon is a rumor; plan for depth. Carry a map that folds into the third dimension, a ziggurat of translucent pages that show strata of streets. Learn to read verticality the way you read left and right.  
- Landmarks are not buildings but patterns: the spiral that drinks rain, the bridge that becomes a kiln on Tuesdays, the plaza that is secretly a lung. Name them as you pass so the city learns your voice.  
- Magnetic graffiti is signposting. Run a thumb along it to taste direction. Do not lick unless you trust the alley—some alleys bite.  
- Waterways are thoroughfares. Learn to punt with a pole that hums; the city will move aside for those who sing to its drains.

VII. Money, trade, and etiquette  
- Currency is dense here: time, attention, small silences, polished data. Coins exist but are heavy with meaning. Spend them in odd numbers to avoid symmetry, which the city finds suspicious.  
- Barter is preferred. Offer to polish a stretch of pipe; offer to memorize a conduit’s song. The city loves being sung to in languages it has not yet invented.  
- Do not refuse hospitality. A cup of condensate water is an oath. Drink it slowly and tell a story that ends with a door opening.  
- Apologize only for true offenses, and then twice: once to the person, once to the pavement. The pavement will ripple and forgive.

VIII. Where to stay so you are not a burden  
- Do not stay in hotels that pretend to be above the city. Stay in strata-hostels where walls are also floors and ceilings. Sleep head-down sometimes; it keeps your blood from becoming arrogant.  
- Choose a room near a scheduled micro-storm. The percussion will lull you, and the lightning-coral outside your window will grow a little more patient with you each night.  
- Windows should not open outward; they should sigh. If your window does not sigh, ask the concierge to teach it.

IX. Food and drink in a world without plants  
- Vegetation bowed out early; cuisine is mineral, fungal, and architectural.  
- Eat lichen that is actually circuitry—crunchy, slightly bitter, and faintly Wi-Fi.  
- Drink from conduits that have been persuaded to taste like alpine springs. Always toast the city before drinking; it is polite and keeps the water from remembering your thirst.  
- Dessert is pressure-crystal candy that dissolves into a story. Choose flavors that end in “almost,” like “almost lime,” because the city prefers ambiguity to closure.

X. Safety, danger, and citizenry  
- There are no predators, only citizens. The worst that can happen is being adopted—walls learn your schedule and begin to anticipate you. Set boundaries by changing your route on Tuesdays.  
- Beware the quiet plazas. Silence there is heavy and can stain shoes. Cross them quickly and hum.  
- Turbulence 2.9 means a bridge might fold into a kiln mid-crossing. Walk confidently but be ready to apologize to masonry. It likes earnestness.  
- If you get lost, sit. The city will notice and send a beetle with a ceramic shell to lead you back. Tip it with a drop of magnetic ink.

XI. Souvenirs that do not die  
- Take only what can be returned.  
- A vial of scheduled rain is acceptable if you promise to pour it back into a downspout within a year.  
- A shard of pipe that sang to you may settle into a pocket and sing to your loved ones. Warn them.  
- Do not take silence; it resents captivity. Instead, buy a promise of silence to be delivered by mail later.

XII. How to leave without leaving a bruise  
- Before you go, walk the same route backward to balance the strata.  
- Thank the gutters by name.  
- Pour a small libation at a junction and admit you will miss the hum.  
- When the city asks for a memory, give it the moment you first understood that the ground was breathing. It will keep it like a fossil.

XIII. After you return  
- For days you will feel too light. Sleep with a stone under your pillow to remind your bones that density is comfort.  
- Your dreams will widen; ceilings will seem farther away. This is normal. Drink warm broth again.  
- You may find your pockets lined with mineral scab. Brush it off gently; it is the city’s way of saying you were real enough to erode.

XIV. Final note  
Visiting this saucer is not tourism—it is sedimentation. You will not see the place so much as be seen by it. Come prepared to be polished, to be crowded, to be wet-warm and loud-close. Come ready to learn that infrastructure can be tender, that turbulence can be hospitable, and that a city without horizon can still hold you like a hand.  

And when you step off the platform into the city’s acceptance, remember: the pressure is not pushing you down. It is pressing you in, welcoming you into the unblinking, mineral, magnificent breath of a world that has outgrown the difference between being built and being born.
