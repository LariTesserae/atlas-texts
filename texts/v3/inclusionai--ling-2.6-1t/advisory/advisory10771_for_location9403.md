### 1. Treat the Altitude as a Living Thing  
Do not acclimatize—negotiate.  
At this elevation, the atmosphere has become a minimalist. Oxygen is present but apologetic; pressure is polite but firm. Before you leave your staging station, spend at least thirty-six subjective hours (local time) in a stepped hypoxia chamber that mimics the exact fractional deficit of this world. Do not cheat with drugs or blood-substitutes. Let your marrow learn the hunger. Bring a portable hypobaric mask for the first week on the ground—use it for sleep and for the first hour of every morning. It will hum, and that hum will teach your lungs the local grammar of lack.

### 2. Pack for a Climate That Manufactures Weather in Thin Batches  
Storms here do not rain; they shear.  
Your outer layer should be a laminated smart-weave that can change its refractive index in microseconds—not for camouflage, but to prevent electrostatic grooming. The local dust carries a memory of momentum and will strip unprotected surfaces down to ghost-etchings. Wear gaiters that seal above the ankle; the dust is finer than intent and will work its way into joints you did not know you owned.  

Bring lenses with variable aperture that respond to pressure, not light. The sky above can dim without darkening, and you will need to see the difference between shadow and subtraction. For sound, carry a contact microphone you can bite or press to bone: the place speaks through vibration, not air.

### 3. Hydrate Against a Missing Chord  
There is no water as you know it. There is only the memory of liquidity.  
Bring a still that does not expect vapor to rise; expect it to be teased out of mineral matrices. Pack chelating tabs for the metallic tang that passes for thirst. Your electrolytes will need to include trace silicates—your body will try to ossify in subtle ways if you do not remind it that flexibility is still an option.  

Drink on a schedule that feels excessive. The air’s dryness is not felt until it is already inside you. Pee clear or regret: here, clarity is a survival metric.

### 4. Learn to Read a Surface That Has Settled Into Geometry  
The ground is not soil; it is a decision.  
Carry a geologist’s pick with a piezoelectric head—not to break rock, but to listen to the way fractures have sutured themselves shut. The lines in the land are less terrain than history written in shorthand. Practice reading palimpsests: a polished slope may be the ghost of a river that never was; a bowl of pale alloy may be a crater that chose to forget its violence.  

Wear boots with soles that can be tuned to grip, slide, or float, depending on whether you are crossing salt-scoured pans or glass-fiber grass that crumbles into tiny, clicking gears. The grass is not flora as you know it; it is mineral that dreams of voltage. Do not eat it. Do not let it get into your boots for long. It will hum at night and remind you that you are meat.

### 5. Move With the Patience of the Local Fauna  
Creatures here are negotiations between rarity and patience.  
If you see a six-limbed silhouette picking its way across a basin, freeze. Let it decide you are architecture. If you see a floater riding updrafts that disobey old rules of heat, do not follow. Follow only after you have mapped the updraft three times and confirmed it is not a creature itself, wearing wind like a coat.  

Travel at the speed of erosion. This is not metaphor. If you hurry, the land will notice and respond with micro-adjustments—ridges shifting by microns, dust rearranging to point at you. You want to be temporary, not memorable.

### 6. Respect the Punctuation of the Built  
Towers the height of hills stand like needles in a sentence that prefers to breathe.  
Do not enter unless you have decoded the local etiquette of permission. Portals that no longer open may still listen. Lenses that swivel toward horizons may swivel toward you if you stand in the wrong geometry of attention.  

Around the bases, you will find grids that never completed, conduits that snake into the crust and fall quiet. Do not trace them with bare hands. They may still carry phantom voltage—enough to wake a dormant alignment inside your own nervous system. Use insulated probes. Record everything. The language here is geometry and doubt; treat every line as a verb that has forgotten its subject.

### 7. Prepare for Technology as Condition, Not Machinery  
Gravity will sometimes forget its manners. Light will bend around nothing in particular. Roads will repair their own cracks overnight with a patience that borders on cruelty.  

Carry a compass that measures true variance, not just direction. Bring a notebook whose pages are keyed to local pressure so they do not curl into impossible shapes when the world exhales. Your electronics should be shielded against nano-scale legions that polish fault lines while you sleep—shielding is not optional; it is a courtesy.  

Learn to recognize the shiver that comes once per century when the buried engines turn over. It feels like an apology. If you feel it, lie down. Let the ground rearrange you by microns. Do not struggle; the apology is more important than your dignity.

### 8. Accept the 1.1 Weirdness as a Form of Good Manners  
Shadows lag. Echoes arrive before their causes. Distances contract if you stare too long.  
This is not menace. It is estrangement—the world gently editing you out.  

Do not panic when your shadow arrives late. Wave at it when it catches up. Do not trust your ears when echoes precede their sounds; count heartbeats between cause and echo to keep a rhythm that is yours alone. If distances feel mutable, stop walking and plant a marker. Let the world decide whether the marker is a lie or a truth.  

Weirdness here is a lubricant. Resistance will only make it abrasive.

### 9. Master a Silence That Is Crowded  
The quiet is not empty. It is the tick of cooling metal, the soft thud of dust on dust, the rare chime of a structure adjusting itself in the cold.  

Practice listening with your molars. Practice holding your breath until the land forgets you are breathing. When the storms pass, the silence will feel like a door closing. Let it. Stand in the frame of that door. Do not try to leave until the silence decides you may stay.

### 10. Leave Before You Become Part of the Surface  
This world tolerates visitors because it has forgotten how not to.  
But tolerance is not hospitality. The cold is exhaustive; the emptiness is insistence. If you stay too long, your edges will soften into the ridge lines; your pauses will sync with the tectonic grammar; your voice may become one of the chimes you hear at a distance.  

Set a departure date and defend it. Pack out more than you packed in—carry away at least one memory that does not belong to the land. When you leave, do not look back at the tower blinking in time. It will still be blinking after you are gone, marking a moment that belonged to you for a while and now belongs to no one again.  

And that is enough.  
At this altitude, enough has never been a small thing.
