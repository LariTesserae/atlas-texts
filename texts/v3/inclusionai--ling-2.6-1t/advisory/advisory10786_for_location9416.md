How to visit a basin cupped between palms—high, wet, cool, and breathing:

Clothing and body-first preparation
- Think of your clothes as temporary skin, not armor. The place will test seams, suck at threads, try to grow into your cuffs. Wear layers that can be water-logged without losing warmth: wool that even when soaked still breathes, linen that dries fast and does not chafe. Avoid synthetics that hold the sweet-sour rot smell like a memory.
- Bring a cloak or shell that is more umbrella than coat—something that sheds water but vents vapor, because here humidity is a liquid you can lean against. A wide brim keeps the canopy drip off your shoulders and out of your eyes.
- Footwear is the most important choice. You will walk on a raft that flexes. Do not bring stiff boots that cannot feel the ground; bring soft-soled shoes that can grip slick root, drain when full, and be rinsed clean without dying of mold. Consider knee-high gaiters you can roll down against leeches and tuck up against sludge.
- Treat yourself before you go: broad-spectrum antiparasitic, antihistamine for the pollen, something for the gut. Your immune system will meet flavors of rot and growth it has never cataloged. Bring also a small vial of oil to rub on ankles and wrists—something bitter to the tongue of biting things.
- Pack a change of everything, vacuum-sealed, as if you might never see dry cloth again. You will need it.

Tools and containers
- Metal is rare here and rust is a betrayal. If you bring tools, favor bronze, seasoned wood, fired clay, or bone. A good knife with a handle wrapped in waxed cord, a small saw that can be cleaned with vinegar and oil, a trowel for transplanting without cruelty.
- Vessels should be watertight but not precious. Bladders, waxed baskets, wide-mouthed jars with cork or wood stoppers. Bring a collapsible bucket you can tuck under your arm; you will use it to bail, to carry, to offer when the raft heaves and a channel overruns.
- Light is fought for in green gloom. Bring a covered lantern, something that will not ignite the living thatch. A candle cupped in tin with a glass chimney is better than open flame. Also bring a headlamp with red and dim settings—eyes here are many, and sudden brightness is a theft.
- Rope: braided, not twisted, in plant fiber that can be composted without insult. Bring needles and thread for living grafts—sharp, curved, strong enough to pierce bark without shattering it.
- A small notebook sealed in wax or etched slate. Ink will run; pencil can be chewed by the damp. Learn to write between the drips.

Knowledge to carry in your head
- Learn the language of pressure. When the basin hums beneath your feet, it is telling you where the mat is thin, where the root-sump is full. The raft breathes; listen to its ribs.
- Learn the etiquette of cutting. To take a cutting is to ask permission. To prune is to compliment. Do not hack; negotiate. Offer water back to the wound; press a handful of mud to the cut; speak softly. The answer may be a shiver in the canopy or a frog leaping toward your shoulder—accept it as yes or no.
- Learn the flow. Water here is citizen, not visitor. It rises from below, remembers every rain, pools along veins of living wood. Know the raised walkways and the spill-pools; know when the gutters sigh because they are full. Flood lines on bark are calendars; read them so you do not sleep where spring will arrive unannounced.
- Learn the beasts by their tempo. The swarming frogs, the beetles lacquered like seed pods, the eels that braid through gutters—none are arbitrary. They are currents with scales. Move with the same patience they use when they decide to strike. Let them investigate your stillness; it is cheaper than startling them.
- Learn the architecture of compromise. A door here may be a ring of vine that contracts. Knock with two fingers, wait for it to loosen. A roof may unfurl a new frond across your path—step aside, tuck it gently to the side, remember where it leans. What is broken is food; what is built is temporary. Accept both with grace.

Daily rhythms and courtesies
- Rise before the sun can pierce the canopy, while the cool still holds the beads. This is the time of clearest scent and least turbulence. Walk softly; the mat is stiffer with night’s rest.
- Eat lightly at first. The food will be abundant—salted amphibians, tubers that taste of wet stone, leaves tender as paper. Let your gut learn them one at a time. Carry a few hard biscuits from home for the first day, familiar starch to anchor you.
- Wash where the water runs cleanest, not where it pools. Running water here is polite; pooled water is gossip. Rinse your feet, your hands, the back of your neck. You will sleep better for it.
- Sleep in the high pockets if you can, where the raft is thickest and the under-grinding is a lullaby rather than a threat. Suspend your hammock from trained knots, or accept a room that has agreed to be lived in. Do not trust a flat-looking patch of leaf-roof without testing it with a pole first.
- Keep your waste minimal and useful. The basin knows how to turn scraps into tomorrow’s wall. Do not insult it with foreign chemicals, with wrappers, with anything that cannot be fed forward.

What to do when turbulence arrives
- A storm can begin underground. If the root-sump groans, if the mat shivers more than wind alone explains, seek higher ground along the raised channels. Do not try to outrun water that remembers every crack.
- Stow your flame. Close your lanterns. Let the green dim you; it is only a moment. The canopy will shower you with sudden brightness; accept it as baptism.
- If a wall of leaf collapses near you, do not rebuild immediately. First, help the living things that were in it find new purchase. Then, with willing hands, weave a new support using the same methods you see locals use—grafts, braces, patience. You will be given tools if you ask quietly.
- After the churn, the place will offer gifts: a new channel where a doorway used to be, a bloom that opened because the wind shook it awake. Take only what is offered openly.

How to leave and how to be remembered
- Before you go, ask what you should take that will not wound. Perhaps a cutting that has been singing for days, perhaps a length of cord steeped in tannin. Do not take wood that is still whispering.
- Leave a token that can be fed forward—metal you do not mind giving to rust, a song you do not mind giving to memory, a pattern you etched in soft clay that rain can soften without destroying.
- Go slow on the rim. Let your eyes adjust to open sky. The basin will try to hold a piece of you—green in your hair, damp in your boots, a new rhythm in your chest. Let it. Carry it with care, and next time you will be less a stranger, more a returning root.

Intuition’s final warning
You are entering a place that breathes, remembers, and rebuilds itself daily. It is not hostile, but it is not gentle. It is busy, and it expects you to be busy in return—to hold weight, to feed, to rot into usefulness. If you try to impose stillness, you will be composted. If you move with the same patient turbulence, you may find a corner of the basin willing to cup its palms around you and let you stay, at least while the water is rising and the green is listening.
