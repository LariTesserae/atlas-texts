If you are an ordinary human from a placid, single-world lineage—someone whose bones remember only one gravity, whose lungs expect one kind of air, whose mind is used to linear time and stable horizons—then this place will not kill you quickly. It will do something worse and kinder: it will teach you, through repetition, sweat, and small shocks, that you are soft, and that softness can survive here if you agree to be reshaped.

Visiting this wound-in-the-world, this breathing chamber beneath the skin of things, is less like a tour and more like an apprenticeship in dampness, noise, and close quarters. Your preparation, then, should begin with the understanding that you are not going to observe a ruin or a city; you are going to move into its circulatory system for a while, and it will notice you only to the degree that you fit politely into its rhythms.

Below is a practical, intuitive guide to preparing for a stay in this particular pocket of the multiverse. Treat it as a living checklist rather than a rigid protocol. The chamber respects care, but it despises stiffness.

---

### 1. Physiological Baseline: Get Soft on Purpose

This chamber is humid, warm, and gently pressurized, like the lung of a sleeping beast. Before you cross over:

- **Hydration and electrolytes**: Start three days prior. Drink more water than feels reasonable; add trace minerals. The air here carries an osmotic kindness—it pulls salts from skin and breath. You will sweat more than you expect, even while standing still.
- **Heat acclimatization**: Spend time in steamy rooms, greenhouses, or saunas. Let your body learn to vasodilate without panic. Your heart rate should drop even when your skin is wet. If you can nap in a sauna without anxiety, you are close.
- **Respiratory gentleness**: Practice breathing through your nose with long, slow exhalations. The air here is thick with mineral aerosols, warm vapor, and the oily perfume of machinery. You do not want to gasp. Gasping is an insult to this place; it draws attention and discomfort. Be the calm animal in the warm dark.

### 2. Clothing: Let the Place Dress You

Layering is a philosophy here, not a fashion choice. The chamber’s temperature hovers in that narrow band where you are neither cold nor hot, but perpetually on the verge of one or the other, depending on whether you have just stepped out of a draft or into the breath of a turbine.

- **Base layer**: A thin, hydrophilic weave—something that moves water away from skin without wicking it into your hair. Avoid cotton; it becomes a sponge and a memory of discomfort. Merino or technical synthetics that tolerate salt are ideal.
- **Mid layer**: Loose, breathable insulation. Felted wool or quilted silk works. You want pockets of air that can warm or cool without clinging.
- **Shell**: A light, oil-resistant cape or coat with a hood, treated to shed mineral deposits and soot. It need not be waterproof, merely dignified in its relationship to damp. Think of it as a polite shield that acknowledges it will get dirty and does not mind.
- **Footwear**: Boots with shallow tread and drainage. The floors are a mosaic of slick metal, spongy muck, and warm grating. You want to feel what you are walking on without slipping, because slipping here is loud and noticed. Break your boots in until they feel like gloves.
- **Accessories**: A brimmed cap to keep droplets from eyebrows, fingerless gloves to grip warm metal and still feel texture, and a sash or belt with pouches. Do not carry a bag that swings; carry pouches that cling to your body like barnacles.

### 3. Sensory Hygiene: Protect the Delicate Instruments

Your senses will be busy. Prepare them like fine tools.

- **Ear care**: The chamber is loud without being sharp. Bring soft, reusable earplugs that attenuate rather than block. You want to hear the machines as a language, not as noise. Learn the difference between an idle hum and a bearing about to complain.
- **Eye care**: The light here is low and refractive, full of halos around lamps and ghost-images in wet stone. Anti-reflective lenses or lightly tinted goggles with side shields will spare you headaches. If you wear corrective lenses, bring a second pair and a lens cloth; salt and oil will find your lenses.
- **Skin care**: A light balm for fingertips and lips, something that repels salt crust and soothes small cracks. You will touch warm metal often, more than you plan. Your hands will remember things your mind misses.

### 4. Navigation and Orientation: Learn the Grammar of Flow

The city is vertical and recursive. To visit well, you must stop trying to map it like a grid and instead learn its current.

- **Before arrival**: Study the signage systems of places built around maintenance—dams, power plants, canal locks. Learn to read symbols that refer to pressure, flow, and schedule. The chamber’s signs are legible but repetitive; meaning comes from context, not translation.
- **On arrival**: Spend your first hours simply moving up and down with the general current. Take stairs when you can, avoid dead ends, and note which balconies have plants and which have prayer tags. Let your body be the compass; this place rewards patience and punishes haste.
- **Timekeeping**: Clocks here refer to cycles—pump cycles, drawbridge lifts, steam bleeds. Your watch should be mechanical or ruggedized digital. Learn to think in phases rather than hours. Morning in the chamber is when the lowest sump empties; evening is when the lamps flicker into their second breath.

### 5. Social Protocol: Be Small and Useful

The inhabitants—human and otherwise—have a culture of careful utility. To be seen is to be tolerated; to be helpful is to be remembered.

- **The greeting**: A nod with one hand resting lightly on a nearby surface, acknowledging that you and the place share stability. Eye contact is brief, respectful, broken by glances at your hands or the work at hand.
- **The work ethic**: Offer to do small tasks—carry a load up one flight, hold a light steady, scrape salt from a valve. Refuse politely if they refuse you, but make the offer. This is the language of belonging here.
- **Fauna etiquette**: Do not startle birds or lizards. If rats the size of cats cross your path, stand still until they have passed. Larger many-legged shapes on the gantries are not aggressive, but they are possessive of their routes. Give them the high path.
- **Gifts**: Useful things—clean rags, fresh rivets, a good lamp. Not grand gestures; small tools. The chamber appreciates the hand that maintains.

### 6. Safety as a Quiet Practice

The chamber is not lethal; it is insistent. Danger here is often the result of inattention, not malice.

- **Water**: Assume every surface is wetter than it looks. Test handholds with a finger before a hand. Warm water can still carry you off balance.
- **Metal**: Touch with the side of your hand first; it will tell you if something is near boiling. Metal here has memory, and it shares that memory through heat.
- **Air pockets**: Some alcoves hold methane or heavier-than-air fumes. If a flame lamp flickers downward, or your head feels thick without reason, move up without hesitation. The vertical city rewards elevation.
- **Hearts and rhythms**: If you feel your pulse syncing with a machine, stop. Let it pass. This place is fond of making visitors part of its rhythm, and that is fine for a while, but you are here to visit, not to live forever in its tempo.

### 7. Mental Posture: Embrace Apprenticeship

Do not come to understand everything. Come to learn one small thing: how moss grows on a north beam, how a pump breathes, how salt tastes in warm vapor. The chamber will give you more if you ask for less.

- **Journaling**: Write at the end of each phase, not each day. Use charcoal or waterproof ink. The damp will test your commitment; let it.
- **Rituals**: Adopt a small, private ritual—washing your face in cool cistern water, tracing the line of a beam with a fingertip, counting the flicker of a lamp three times before entering a new ward. Rituals anchor you to a place that loves repetition.
- **Letting go**: If you find yourself thinking in cycles, feeling the city’s hum in your molars, accept it. You are becoming a kind of creature that belongs here, at least for the span of your visit. Softness is not weakness here; it is the only strength that lasts.

---

If you prepare in this way, the wound in the world will not feel like a wound at all. It will feel like a place that has been waiting to be noticed, patiently, by someone who is willing to get damp, to be small, and to learn the shape of a heartbeat shared with stone and water and warm dark.

When you leave, you will carry salt in your creases and the memory of a rhythm that will take weeks to fade. You will find yourself testing handholds without thinking, listening for cycles in unfamiliar machines, offering to help strangers with small burdens. This is not a contamination. It is a polite infection of kindness, and it is the best kind of souvenir the multiverse can give an ordinary human who dared to visit a place that breathes, and asked, gently, to be allowed to breathe along with it.
