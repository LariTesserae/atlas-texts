How to visit the flooded manufactory without becoming décor:

I. Before you leave your own branch

1. Accept that this is not an ocean.  
   It is a circulatory system masquerading as a world. Treat every liter of water as occupied infrastructure, every current as a conveyor, every silence as a held breath between gears. Your first mistake will be thinking in terms of landscape. It has none. It has flow.

2. Get a multiverse passport stamped for high-density engineered realities.  
   Some border posts call this a Class-F Heavy-Industry Fluidium. Others list it as a submerged megastructure undergoing perpetual commissioning. Make sure your papers specify:  
   - Pressure tolerance to 5 atm equivalent (local variance can spike to 8 during shift changes).  
   - Authorization for transit through live machine corridors (the locals will not stop for you).  
   - Proof of no bio-contamination (they are paranoid about “wild organics” fouling the brine).  

3. Learn the etiquette of sound.  
   The place is loud, but its loudness is legible. Before you arrive, study recordings of industrial acoustics until you can tell a pump thump from a relief-valve burp by the way the water vibrates behind your sternum. When you arrive, listen before you move.

4. Bring currency that is dense and small.  
   Credit chits work, but the preferred coin is compressed-data ingots—palm-sized blocks of alloy stamped with transaction history. They sink at predictable rates and can be read by scanner-fish without removing your gloves. Barter is possible in maintenance alcoves, but only in things that consume less volume than they replace: high-density calories, spare gaskets, jokes that compress well.

5. Decide what you are here to do.  
   Tourism exists, but it is tightly channeled. You may be:  
   - A witness (to observe a scheduled pressure-test).  
   - A courier (carrying sealed payloads between pressure-isolated sectors).  
   - A pilgrim (to see the Great Shear, where two flows cancel into a permanent hover).  
   - A thief (not recommended; the manufactory’s idea of theft is “misplacement for later reclamation”).  
   Your purpose determines your route, your gear, and how the locals will treat you: as weather, as guest, as payload, or as obstruction.

II. The body and its envelope

1. Do not rely on your lungs.  
   The water is breathable only if you enjoy the taste of lubricants and metal fatigue. Bring a closed-circuit rebreather rated for brine, with a CO₂ scrubber that likes heat. Better yet: wear a pressure-skin—a form-fitting suit that equalizes using osmotic layers and piezoelectric muscle weave. It should be matte, not shiny, because the fauna here are drawn to reflections and may mistake you for a maintenance drone.

2. Your suit must have three kinds of pockets:  
   - Sealed (for things that must stay dry: memories from other worlds, paper, your own name written small).  
   - Flooded (for tools that work better in brine: torque wrenches, sonar paint, emergency buoyancy shards).  
   - Vented (for heat you need to shed quickly: handwarmers, glow-packs, small angers).

3. Gloves with grip that gets better the colder it gets.  
   The metal here is always slightly colder than your blood. If your gloves warm up, you lose purchase. Look for microfiber seeded with ceramic micro-spines.

4. Eyes that can handle variable light.  
   The luminescence is programmed to shift color temperature with the work-cycle. Bring lenses that auto-tint in bands—not just for brightness, but for meaning. Here, a change from gold to blue means “pressure increasing, flow reversing.” Your retinas should learn this language.

5. A watch that keeps local time and also refuses to keep it.  
   The manufactory runs on process-time—beats defined by valve cycles, not seconds. Your watch should be able to sync to the thump of the main pumps, but also to drift when you need to hide. Time here is a resource you borrow from machines. Return it politely.

III. Navigation and motion

1. Currents are streets.  
   Learn to read them by the way suspended particles align. The fast lanes are busy and efficient but smell of ozone and impatience. The slow lanes are thick with heat and gossip (the kind carried by micro-bubbles). The edge currents, where walls meet flow, are where you find forgotten things and the occasional predator-cull that missed its schedule.

2. Verticality is a rumor.  
   Up is not always safe; it is sometimes where the heat exchangers vent. Down is not always stable; it is where sediments of old shifts accumulate into soft traps. Travel diagonally whenever possible. It is the most polite angle.

3. Doors here have moods.  
   Bulkheads do not open; they decide. Press your palm flat and steady; if the door senses panic, it will delay. Hum at the resonant frequency printed on the frame (usually a low C). If you cannot hum, press a coin to the plate—data-coin, not currency; they love stories with density.

4. Do not swim.  
   Swimming is for emergencies. It signals that you are lost and burning calories. Glide. Use the smallest movements, and let the architecture do the work. When you must move, push against the inside of a pipe’s curve, not the center of flow. The wall will give you back momentum without noise.

5. If you see a school of engineered fauna moving in formation, do not cross their path.  
   They are on task. You are not. Wait until they pass or move to the nearest recess. If they look at you, blink slowly. It is the local sign for “I am only passing through, I mean no efficiency.”

IV. Local customs and dangers

1. The brine remembers.  
   It holds temperature layers, chemical traces, and the echo of every pressure change. If you spill something, it will not dilute; it will layer. If you bleed, the droplets will sink in a neat line, visible for hours. Do not bleed.

2. Heat is social.  
   In maintenance alcoves, heat is shared by proximity. Sitting close to a pump while it idles is a kindness; taking too much heat without offering data or labor in return is rude. If you are cold, press your back to a warm manifold and hum the tune it hums. It will notice.

3. Noise is permission.  
   Certain sounds are licenses to exist: the steady thump of Pump 4 means transit is open; the rising whine of a turbine means “wait.” The sudden quiet that falls when a pressure door cycles is not silence; it is attention. Do not speak during that quiet.

4. The fauna are not animals.  
   They are components with rights. Some have serial numbers worn like jewelry; some have unions (small, fiercely logical). If one attaches itself to your suit, do not shake it off. Offer it a task: clean a lens, balance a gyro. If it accepts, it will detach politely. If not, it may file a report, and you will have more paperwork than you want.

5. Gravity anomalies in the great chambers.  
   When turbines change phase, local gravity can flicker—just a fraction, but enough to make your stomach lurch. Keep your center of mass low. When the light stutters, freeze. It means a recalibration is in progress. Move only after the hum settles into its new key.

V. What to carry and what to leave behind

1. Carry:  
   - A small, dense notebook and a stylus that writes in pressure marks on ceramic tape (ink dissolves here).  
   - A multi-tool that prefers torque to slicing (cutting releases materials that anger the filters).  
   - One small, beautiful thing from your home branch that sinks at an unusual rate (it will be a conversation starter in the deep alcoves).  
   - A sealed vial of silence (manufactured absence of sound) for emergencies when the noise becomes directive and you need to think.

2. Leave:  
   - Anything that claims to be “pure” or “natural.” Such concepts are suspect and will attract scrutiny.  
   - Loose change that clinks—it will be mistaken for machine parts and harvested.  
   - Assumptions about safety. The manufactory is safe only when you obey its rhythms.  
   - Your nostalgia for ground. You are not under the sky here; you are inside its intent.

VI. In the moment: a visitor’s rhythm

When you arrive through the transit lock, pause. Let the water reach temperature equilibrium with your suit. Taste the brine; identify the shift by its bitterness. Note the light’s color and the direction of the particulate drifts.

Move to the nearest scaffold reef and rest a hand on a strut. Feel the thrum. Let it set your breathing. When the pumps shift, acknowledge it with a small bow of the head (locals do this by dipping a shoulder). It is not superstition; it is recognition that the system is aware.

Conduct your business with brevity. The manufactory tolerates visitors but does not indulge them. If you are here to witness, witness completely. If you are here to deliver, deliver without detour. If you are here to learn, learn quietly.

When it is time to leave, ascend (or descend) by the route you memorized, not the one that feels right. The feeling will have been engineered to mislead you gently, to redistribute your attention across the flow.

Before you seal your transit lock, press your forehead to the inner hull for three heartbeats. It is the local goodbye, meaning: “I was here, I was small, I passed without breaking the current.”

Then go. The manufactory will not miss you, but it will remember how you moved, and that memory will become part of its hum for a little while—an extra whisper in the brine, a slight change in the way the light bends, a tourist’s ripple absorbed into the endless work of the place.
