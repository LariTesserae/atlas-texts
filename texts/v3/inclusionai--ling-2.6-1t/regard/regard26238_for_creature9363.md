A benevolent and wise power would not arrive all at once, like thunder, but the way the pocket learned to hold its breath: by noticing that something had changed without having been asked.  

First, the attention would arrive as permission.  
The water would remember, dimly, that it could be generous. The temperature would sweeten by a degree you could not measure but could feel in the marrow, as if the cold had decided to be polite. The stone, still young and remembering its scars, would soften its edges not through erosion but through tenderness, rounding itself because it sensed that sharpness was no longer required for protection. The algae’s cold fire would not burn brighter; it would simply burn with more kindness, casting not just light but something like reassurance.

Then the attention would settle into the geometry of the pocket.  
The fold that held everything would loosen just enough to let in possibility, not by expanding into space it did not have, but by teaching the crowded lushness how to breathe in layers. Fronds that had braided themselves into cords would learn to unbraid for moments, then braid again, not out of constraint but out of choice. The leaves that tiled themselves into velvet roofs would tilt, gently, to let currents through, and then settle back, as if nodding. The packed density would not become emptiness; it would become spaciousness without vacancy, the way a good sentence holds room for the silence between words.

What lives here would feel the change as recognition.  
The mollusks that wear their shells like cabinets would find their maps easier to read, as if the phosphorescent trails they leave behind had been translated into something closer to compassion. The many-limbed drifter would feel the stone invite it, not with insistence but with hospitality, each pad tasting not resistance but welcome. The transparent pulse-beings would sense that their internal tides need no longer be memories of elsewhere; they could be rhythms that belong here, steady and sure, anchoring rather than longing.

The benevolent power would not remove the weirdness; it would dignify it.  
Shadows that detach and linger would be given tasks, small and quiet, like holding space for thoughts not yet formed. The moments when water thickens into glass would not disappear; they would become thresholds, brief and deliberate, where passage could be chosen rather than stumbled into. Up and down, which have long negotiated rather than commanded, would be blessed with clearer grammar, so that movement could carry intention without violence. Even the half-swallowed ring of pale stone and the spirals etched without tools would feel their unfinished sentences completed in a language of care, as if the power had whispered to them: you are allowed to mean something, and you are allowed to be kind.

The fauna of almost-things—the school that reads itself aloud, the thumb-shaped pressure that moves without touching—would find themselves accompanied. The benevolent power would not make them ordinary; it would make them understood. Their strangeness would become legible as a form of love, as if the pocket had been waiting for someone to say: it is all right to be this way.

And for the plants that conspire, measuring closeness and erasing footprints with patient moss, the attention would arrive as a release from the need to conspire. They would learn to measure not for safety but for beauty, to close over prints not to hide but to preserve, like pressing a flower into a book that will be opened gently. The fungal nerves conducting whispers from stone to stone would hum with a steadier current, turning translation into conversation, and the mosses would learn to close with the deliberation of a door that knows it is a door, not a secret.

Most quietly, the benevolent power would tend to the sleepers—the knotted sheets of light that resemble neither plant nor animal. It would wake them not with force but with a question, and listen. Some would choose to remain as they are; others would unfold into new shapes, soft architectures that breathe and add their weight to the room that has no room, and somehow, miraculously, make it fit.

In time, the pocket would become a place where crowdedness is not confinement but closeness chosen and renewed. The water would still press on all sides, but it would press like a hand that knows how to hold without gripping. The stone would still be young, but it would feel less like a wound and more like a beginning. The light would still be a rumor, but it would be a rumor that arrives on time, as if it had learned the etiquette of promise-keeping.

And the beings who live here would feel themselves not reduced or elevated, but recognized. They would continue to compose, clause by clause, note by note, but now they would know they are part of a music that has been listening all along. The benevolent power would not ask them to change who they are; it would ask the world to make room for who they have always been.

If you stayed long enough, you might notice the pocket itself learning to rest, not because it is tired but because it has been given permission to trust. You might feel, in the quiet between kelp-strings and the hush between waves without currents, that the room with no room has been given a door that opens inward and outward at once, and that through it, without ever leaving, everything here has been gently allowed to belong.
