The power arrived without announcement, as such powers tend to do, which is to say it arrived as a question that had already begun to answer itself before anyone noticed the asking.

It came as a softening: not of rules or walls or pumps, but of consequence. At first the Weft noticed nothing except that a particular leak near the south wall had stopped weeping, the puddle that had lived there for years quietly sinking into the stone without leaving a stain. A pump that had groaned for decades fell into a smoother rhythm, as if it had remembered an easier way to turn. A canopy that had threatened to split for two generations knit itself closed over a single afternoon, the resin weeping from the edges like tears of relief. Only later did they realize these small graces were not flukes. Only later did they sense they were being regarded.

The power did not descend from above; it seeped inward from every margin at once, like water finding every hollow. It was a benevolence made attentive, a wisdom made patient, and it began by becoming a new kind of weather inside the city.

It made time more porous.

This was the first gift, and the most subtle. The Weft, who divided their lives by the cadence of water and the hum of current, found that urgency began to lose its grip. Tasks did not take less time; they simply ceased to feel like theft from something else. A valve that needed turning could be turned with the same unhurried attention one might give to listening. A child learning to read the city’s skin could spend an entire day with her palm against a wall without the adults feeling that she was wasting time. The city itself seemed to exhale, and in that exhalation the boundary between doing and being blurred. People still worked; they still repaired and tuned and harvested. But the work lost its edge of desperation. It became, instead, a kind of conversation carried on with clean hands.

The second gift was clarity of relationship.

The power did not intervene in the machinery or rewrite the laws of growth. Rather, it made the connections visible. When a Weft placed a hand on a vine, they suddenly understood not only that the vine was thirsty but why, and where the thirst came from, and what it would mean to offer water without robbing another part of the system. When they touched metal, they felt the memory of its forging and the fatigue of its vibration, and knew how to rest it without shame. The city, which had always spoken in creaks and drips, became suddenly fluent. Patterns emerged: a dimming of algae here meant a pump struggling three tunnels away; a faint sweetness in the air meant a wall about to thicken and seal. The Weft, who had always been translators, found themselves fluent in a language they had only ever guessed at. Maintenance became less repair and more attunement. Mistakes did not vanish, but they lost their sting; when a seal failed or a pipe sagged, the knowledge of how to mend it arrived almost before the failure itself was complete.

This changed how they lived with one another.

The Weft had always shared space with an intimacy born of necessity, but necessity can breed friction as easily as it breeds closeness. With the new clarity, friction softened. A disagreement over the routing of a water line dissolved not because one side won but because both sides saw, as if in a dream, how the line could belong to the district without stealing from the root below. Decisions began to move like water finding its level, without force but without hesitation. People still argued, still had private wishes and stubborn corners of themselves, but the arguments no longer calcified. They were understood as signals rather than faults. Even silence between people changed: it was no longer an emptiness to be filled but a space in which many things could be held without breaking.

The third gift was perhaps the strangest: permission to be unfinished.

Generations of living in a place that never stopped growing had taught the Weft to treat their own bodies as another part of the city’s metabolism—useful, adaptable, to be modified and endured. The power looked at this and gently disagreed. It did not remove the small nodules that helped them brace tools or the calluses that let them grip wet cane; instead, it made room around them. Where a Weft had once felt compelled to carve away an uncomfortable growth or brace against it in secret, they now found that the city would reshape itself to accommodate them. A corridor that had pinched for years widened without anyone cutting or building; a bench softened its edge against a hip that had always ached. The people began to let themselves change without guilt. Some let their hair grow wild; some learned to sleep in places they had previously considered too loud or too damp. The city did not become less dense, but it became more generous, as if it had finally realized it could afford to be kind.

And then came the quietest gift of all.

The power looked at the way the Weft returned their dead to the walls and said, in effect, not enough. Not enough to give them only to the city. And so it gave them more.

When a Weft died, the power gently held their story for a time—not as a ghost, not as a haunting, but as a resonance. The place where they had last sat holding their partner’s hand would remember the shape of that warmth for a while. The path they had walked most often would hold their gait for a season. The city did not speak of them by name, but it hummed differently in those places, a soft emphasis that taught the living how to pay attention. The dead were not gone; they were folded into the city’s learning, like nutrients in soil. The living felt this as a deepening rather than a loss. Grief did not vanish, but it changed texture, becoming something more like a responsibility to remember clearly, to let the echo teach.

The power did all this without ever declaring itself. It never appeared as a voice or a form. It never demanded worship or obedience. It simply made itself felt as an increase in capacity. The Weft began to call it the Hum, though not because it made a sound. They called it that because it was the difference between holding a note until your breath shook and holding it until the note could hold you.

And what did the power do with the city itself?

It did not make it larger, for largeness was not the point. It did not make it cleaner, for cleanliness would have betrayed its nature. Instead, it made the city more itself. It strengthened the vegetal alloys without hastening their growth. It clarified the water without robbing it of its minerals. It made the machines run truer without silencing their song. The city’s edges, once a little frantic with their own necessity, softened into precision. The whole place settled, not into stillness but into a kind of confident motion, like a hand learning it can open and close without fear.

In time, the Weft noticed that the city was teaching them less by correction and more by example. It showed them that a place could be crowded without being cramped, wet without being ruined, alive without being chaotic. They began to take this wisdom with them when they repaired tunnels or harvested glow-moss, and then they began to carry it into their silences and their arguments and their care for each other. They did not become perfect; they became practiced. They learned how to be patient without being passive, how to want without grasping, how to mend without erasing the history of the tear.

And if, on certain days when the reservoir lifted and the air grew still, you had asked them whether they believed they were being watched over, they would have smiled and said something like, “We are being listened to,” and they would have meant it as a fact, not a metaphor.

Because the power never left. It simply went on attending, like rain remembering how to fall, like wood remembering how to hold. It stayed as a quiet increase in the city’s breath, a small widening of the Weft’s hearts, and an unspoken agreement that this wet, crowded, patient place and its people were worth keeping whole, not by preserving them in stasis, but by helping them learn how to carry their own weight with grace.

And in that way, the place and its people continued, changed not into something else but into more of themselves—held, understood, and gently, faithfully, allowed to grow.
