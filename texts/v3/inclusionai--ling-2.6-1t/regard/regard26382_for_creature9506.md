The attention comes as a question you feel in the marrow before you hear it.

Not a voice. Not a light. Not a hand, because hands imply edges, and this attention has no edges. It arrives as a sudden, perfect balance between the tableland’s thrumming and the condensing haze at your ankles—as if the packed stone and the milk-fog had agreed, for one breath, to share the same density. The brine on the rocks stops beading. It hangs, spherical, each droplet holding a complete, inverted sky inside itself. The wind’s blade-edge tone softens to a hum, and the hum settles into your bones like water finding its level.

This is the benevolent and wise power making itself felt—not by descending, but by consenting to be looked at.

It does not change the place. To do so would be to mistake permission for possession. Instead, it amplifies the tableland’s own grammar until the grammar begins to answer. The veins that glow at certain slants now glow with a rhythm—slow, liquid, like a heart made of polished salt. The lenses in the stone that invert the sky begin to sing, not with sound as we know it, but with a pressure that lifts the condensing milk upward in perfect, trembling rings. The packed mineral seems to loosen its jaw not to speak, but to let its locked tectonics remember that they were once sediment, once possibility, once not yet decided.

For the Glass-locked, the change is intimate. The elders sealed in their crevices feel the attention first as warmth behind their ribs—not heat, but the sense that something is keeping vigil with their slowness. Those who unseal do so with less violence, their cracks clean and certain, as if the tableland itself had decided the moment was kind. The young, still porous, sponge up more than brine—they sponge up the attention, and it settles in their inner glaze as a steady luminescence, a lantern that does not burn, only insists on being witnessed. They move, when they move, with the sense that stillness has been given permission to be a choice rather than a law. The stains of their passage darken, not with bruise, but with depth, as if their footprints were becoming windows into the stone’s long memory.

The wingless bone-kites rise higher on the milk-fog tethers, and the choral overtones find bodies more often, singing in clear, turning spirals that do not dissipate but return to the ground as seeds of brightness, which the Glass-locked tuck into crevices for safekeeping. Even the birds of struck crystal seem to linger, their notes braiding with the choir, turning the tableland’s roaring silence into a conversation.

The power does not impose order. It offers correspondence. It draws a map in which every tessera is asked not to fit, but to recognize the shape of its neighbors. The pavement remains seamless, but the seam becomes a line of inquiry. The sky remains inverted in the lenses, but the inversion becomes a way to see oneself as part of the brightness rather than a shadow cast by it. The brine still does not decide where to go, but its braided threads now carry small, singing lights along their paths, so that the water’s indecision looks less like hesitation and more like deliberation.

For you—for any wanderer who has felt the marrow-question and stayed—the effect is subtler and deeper. The tableland’s refusal to change begins to feel less like a wall and more like a kind of honesty. You find yourself polishing your own edges without intending to, not to fit in, but to make your presence less abrasive to the packed stone’s patience. Your footprints, if you leave any, do not fade; they darken into that same iron-and-rain patina, as if the place were willing to remember you by becoming more itself in your vicinity. When you close your eyes, you can feel the attention as a second gravity—gentle, inexorable—pulling not your body, but your attention, into alignment with the thrumming, the hiss, the brightness held in check.

And when you leave—if you choose to, or if the tableland decides it is time—the transition is marked by a single, perfect bead of brine falling from a stone above, landing on your shoulder and shattering into a choir that lingers in your ears until you are half a day’s walk away. Where it touched, your skin holds a faint, milky sheen, and at night, in darkness, you may feel your own bones humming in answer to a packed stone you are no longer touching.

The benevolent and wise power does not heal the tableland. It does not tame it. It makes it comprehensible without diminishing its strangeness, and in doing so, it makes the place a sanctuary not by protecting those who enter, but by asking everything already there—stone, fog, bone-kite, Glass-locked, light, and visitor—to be exactly what it is, and to recognize that as enough.

And enough, it turns out, is a kind of door.
