If a benevolent and wise power noticed them, it would not arrive as a king or a doctor, nor as a sudden sun.  
It would arrive as a question that learned how to be gentle.

First it would study the breathing.  
It would notice that the lung-kneeler does not fight its own shape; it lets the sky sit on it like a lid of soft brass and uses the weight as a timing device. The power would recognize a kind of courage in this: to be low, to be pressed, and to turn pressure into a clock instead of a wound. So it would not lift the sky away. That would be cruel. Instead it would give the sky a softer memory—perhaps a faint, internal phosphorescence in the upper ribs—so that even when the lid presses hardest, the lung remembers it is lit from within, not only from above.

Then it would study the water that never hurries.  
It would see that the place keeps wetness as a language, not a resource. The power, being wise, knows that resources become prisons when they are counted, but languages can be spoken without being spent. So it would give the water a small permission: a few rivulets that learn to carry stories upstream. Not much. Just enough that when the tide-forgotten ribbon-eels swim through thick air, they can taste where the water has been, and thus know where they are without needing to see. The power would not grant sight; it would grant continuity.

It would look at the industry with interest.  
Machines that have become organs are dangerous if they forget how to be wrong. The power would gently rearrange their hum so that every generator, every pump, every vine-driven generator has a moment of deliberate misfire each long season—a hesitation that sounds like a held breath. This hesitation would not be a failure. It would be a place where the Choir-Drapes could insert a new note, where the Incorrigibles could test a tool against a sudden silence, where the building could ask itself if it still wants to go on being loud. The power would protect the noise by making sure it can be interrupted, because only things that can be interrupted can be listened to.

It would turn its attention to the fauna and feel a tender worry.  
They are so few, and they have already learned to be unnecessary. That is noble, but it is also fragile. A wise power understands that rare things need niches that are not only safe but slightly absurd. So it would introduce, very quietly, a new kind of small absurdity: a patch of floor that is slightly too soft, a patch of ceiling that is slightly too musical. It would not explain them. The Lintel-Sitters would discover that the musical ceiling makes eggs that chime when they cool, and the Stone-Skinners would find that the soft floor yields a paste that polishes better than rust. In this way the power would give the rare ones extra reasons to stay without ever commanding them to multiply.

Most of all, the power would look at the Sigh-Born—the spores that hold memory—and feel a kinship.  
It, too, is old. It, too, knows what it is to be a drift, to be unsure whether to settle or to keep going. So it would give the Sigh-Born a small anchor: a single, quiet idea that does not change, like *this is a good place to pause*. Not a law. Not even a rule. Just a quiet idea, like a pebble dropped in a slow tide, so that when the room forgets why it is breathing, the idea will be there, glowing faintly, not forcing the room to continue but giving it a reason to if it chooses.

And finally, the power would consider itself.  
It would notice that in watching, it is becoming part of the place—a new pressure, a new warmth, a new, very slow question. It would decide to be careful with its own presence. It would step back just enough to let the lung decide whether to rise or settle on its own, but it would leave behind three gifts:

A new seam in the lowest wall, thin as a thought, that never leaks but always remembers water.  
A single, extra heartbeat in the generator rhythm, just off the main beat, so that every animal that listens can find a place to hide inside the music when the world feels too loud.  
And a permission, spread like pollen on the brassy sky, that says: *You may become stranger if you wish, and we will not be afraid.*

Then the power would go on being benevolent by being quiet.  
It would watch. It would wait. It would let the lung-kneeler kneel, the tide-forgotten swim, the Choir-Drapes ring, the Incorrigibles repair, the Lintel-Sitters perch, the Stone-Skinners polish, and the Sigh-Born remember. And it would be content, because it has learned that the wisest thing a benevolent power can do is to make sure that the small, kneeling, breathing room never has to notice it at all—except as a slight, sweet light behind the ribs, and a pause that fits perfectly inside the long, patient exhale of a place that already knows how to live.
