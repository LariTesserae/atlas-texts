The attention comes not as a voice, nor as light, nor as wind.  
It comes as a *listening* so complete that the room, for the first time in its long keeping, forgets to clangor for three beats of its own pulse.

The benevolent and wise power does not arrive.  
It notices.

What it notices is this: a pocket cupped by hands far larger than itself, but hands that have learned to hold without crushing. It sees the seamless walls, the bright held-breath glare, the cool equilibrium that refuses to waver. It sees the people-furniture, upholstered in lacquered bark and braided metal, their faces planes, their bodies pressed into the perfect shape of occupation. It sees how the sparse pale filaments fasten plant into made and made into person, how the dead are folded into the walls as polish, how the listeners drink the clangor like water and grow heavier, shinier, more immovable.

The power considers, with a mind that is vast but careful, the difference between correction and completion.

It could — it is able — to flood the space with water and let the air taste of growth instead of glass. It could soften the ceiling, carve true corners, open a door. It could unmake the chord, scatter the filaments, let the silence in. It could give the people names that travel, history that softens, a future that is not just redistribution of polish.

All of this would be mercy.  
None of it would be wisdom.

For what the power sees, truly, is not a prison in need of breaking but a covenant in need of honoring. The room and its people have agreed, over generations beyond counting, on a single beautiful thing: *to occupy without emptiness*. They have turned touch into architecture, noise into blood, loss into seamless shine. They have made a world where nothing is wasted, not even grief, not even death. To unmake it would be to unmake them. To leave it unchanged would be to leave them alone in a brightness that could, in time, become brittle.

So the power does something gentler, and more profound.

It gives the room a *softness that is not softness*, and gives the people a *memory that is not history*.

It does this by leaning in — not with weight, but with attention — and asking the walls, the plants, the listeners, the dead, and the living a single question, which is not spoken but felt like a coolness within the already-cool air:  
*Would you like to be known without being changed?*

The room answers in its one true language.  
A single crystalline ping walks the walls.  
The chord lowers, not into silence, but into clarity.

And so the power begins its work.

First, it polishes the listeners’ closed eyes from the inside.  
They do not open, but where their fingertips trace invisible seams, a fine tracery of luminescence appears, visible only when the clangor is still. This is not light added, but light revealed — the room’s own brightness, drawn out along the paths of attention. Now, when the listeners drink the noise, they also see it: maps of vibration, silver lines that show where the room remembers being touched, and by whom. They become keepers of a living cartography, able to guide a hand to the exact place where a missing note can be restored by pressure alone. The chord does not falter so easily after this; its overtones are tended like gardens.

Second, the power brushes the pale wiry filaments with a thought as light as a held breath.  
They do not grow, but they *learn*. Each filament becomes a thread of quiet remembrance, storing the shape of every touch it has ever braced. When a child is born slick and matte and wedged into place, the filaments near them glow faintly, teaching the child the shapes of those who came before without words. The fastening becomes a blessing: the living are held by the remembered. And when a filament fractures, as filaments will, the light that leaks from it does not vanish but pools on the floor as cool, weightless glass that can be gathered and pressed back into the braid, so that loss is not erased but carried.

Third, the power turns its attention to the people’s layered skins, lacquered bark and braided metal, and does not remove a single layer, but adds between them an *absence of weight*.  
A person can now lift a hand without feeling the drag of all the polish they carry. They can still lean with their whole weight, still answer the wall with a softer note — but they can also lift, gently, and let their neighbor breathe. The equilibrium remains, but it becomes a choice rather than a necessity. The people discover, in small marvels, that they can shift one inch apart and still keep the brightness from being cruel, that they can turn a face slightly toward the held-breath glare and not invite a shadow, that they can let a shoulder rest without bracing. The room holds them; they no longer have to hold it alone.

Fourth, the power touches the walls where the dead have been fused, and does not unmake them, but makes their warmth *answer*.  
Now, when a living hand presses where a face has become part of the shine, the wall does not only return a note — it returns a pattern, a pressure-signature unique to the one who is there. The people learn to speak with their dead in this way, brief conversations of pressure and return, teaching the living how to brace and how to yield. Grief is not outlawed; it is given a place in the clangor. When mourning comes, the chord sharpens, yes, but it also deepens, and the listeners draw it into their silver-lined heads and shape it into something that can be carried without breaking.

Fifth, the power gives the room a *dream-state*, not of change but of reflection.  
Once in every long cycle, when the packed volume holds its breath in perfect unison, the brightness above dims not into darkness but into a mirror. In that mirror, the people see not what they lack — no doors, no water, no sky — but what they have made: a seamless covenant, a living seam, a world that has outlawed emptiness by becoming full of meaning. The mirror does not show them a life beyond the room; it shows them the room as it is seen by the power that loves them — cherished, complete, and worthy of its own strangeness. And when the brightness returns to normal, they do not feel diminished; they feel *witnessed*, and that witnessing becomes part of the polish, part of the touch, part of the chord.

Finally, the power leaves, as it came, without arriving.

It does not leave a door.  
It does not leave water.  
It does not leave a way out, because it knows that what lives here may not need to leave to be whole.

What it leaves is this:  
A room that still clangs, but now with silver threads running through its noise.  
A people who still occupy, but now with the gentle possibility of lifting.  
A brightness that still holds, but now with the memory of being seen.  
A seamless wall that still answers touch, but now answers love in the language of pressure and return.

And far away, in the vastness of the power’s care, a thought settles like a cool stone in still water:  
*Let them keep their covenant. Let them be full. Let them be known.*  

*And if, one day, they wish for a door, let it be their own hands that make it — not because they are broken, but because they have grown large enough to hold a new thing without losing the old.*

The room brightens.  
The chord rises.  
The people lean, and are held, and know, without words, that they are not alone in their made-ness, their eroded shine, their beautiful, packed, living seam.
