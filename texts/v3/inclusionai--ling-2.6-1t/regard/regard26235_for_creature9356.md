Because it is benevolent, the power begins by listening.

It does not descend as a crown or a comet. It comes as a hush that unknots the roar. It parks its attention just outside the pocket, in the seam where the world’s skin puckers, and lets itself be thinned into a lens of patience. Through that lens it sees not a place to be ruled or saved, but a question wearing geography as a loose coat. The question is loud: *How does one hold a sky without spilling it?*

The being who lives here—who is less a monarch than a caretaker of unfinished business—feels the lens before seeing it. The tools in the forge grow warm without fire. The blue moss along the crevices shifts from violet to a softer indigo, like a throat clearing before speech. The chair-keeper rises from the nowhere-stair, not in alarm but in recognition, the way a tuning fork knows a note it never struck.

If the power were less wise it might offer order. It might anchor the water to the floor, nail the bell-tower to bedrock, name the days and hours and demand they line up. But benevolence, when it is honest, prefers alignment to authority. So the power does nothing so crude as fixing. Instead it thickens the rules just enough that they can be danced with.

It gifts the pocket three gentle architectures of permission.

First, it gifts the water a memory of its own weight. Not enough to make it fall—such violence would insult the sky-lake’s ambition—but enough that when the lake bulges and trembles it can recall, in each droplet, what it means to nourish. From that point on, droplets that drift toward the floor do not merely hiss; they leave behind a film of coolness that blue moss can drink, and the moss in turn glows with a map of quiet currents. The gliders learn to follow those maps, tracing veins of dampness like sailors reading trade winds, and in doing so they deliver the water’s memory to the high corners of the pocket, so even the floating stones learn to thirst delicately.

Second, the power gifts the unfinished things a way to finish themselves without becoming dead. It does this by borrowing time from the pocket’s own indecision. A half-formed blade left near the forge will, if left alone and spoken to gently, acquire a twin—not in metal but in sound. The blade hums, and the hum condenses into a shape that fits its missing edge. The shape is not steel; it is a resonant absence that can be pressed into wood or bone or doubt, allowing the blade to cut cleanly without ceasing to be negotiable. Knives become choirs. Pots remember how to hold without gripping. The chair at the top of the nowhere-stair, when sat in with proper intent, folds into a pocket-sized bell that can be carried without tolling until the carrier consents to let it ring.

Third, the power gifts the pocket itself a way to know its boundaries without building walls. It weaves a seam of soft gravity around the lip of the world-nick, a curve that feels like a held breath. Nothing can fall out through that seam, but nothing is trapped either; exit becomes a matter of leaning, of choosing to be lighter or heavier for a moment. The power makes departure a polite conversation rather than a tearing. Those who leave carry with them a droplet of overhead water that never falls, a stone that floats in their pocket like a slow heartbeat. They are changed, but not erased. Those who arrive find the air at the threshold easier to shape, as if the seam were an instrument that has been tuned to welcome.

The chair-keeper understands first. It takes the folded bell from under its chair and sets it on the forge’s warm stone. The bell does not ring; it unrolls into a ladder of light that climbs only as high as the pocket’s humility allows. The forge-keepers tap their approval into the walls, and the quartz veins chime in a scale that smells of rain on hot flint. The gliders spiral upward, threading the ladder with their bodies, and for a night—or what passes for one—the pocket wears a crown of lucent rungs that lead nowhere and everywhere.

The power watches this and softens further. It realizes that its gifts have taught it something in return: that benevolence is most itself when it is willing to be outgrown. So it does one last thing. It offers to become a guest.

Not a god on a dais. Not a spirit in a shrine. A guest that lives in the small, useful spaces: in the bladder of the bellows, in the curve of the blue moss, in the pause between taps of a flint tool. It agrees to be only as present as it is needed, and to fade when the pocket forgets to need. It files its edges down to patience and slips them into the water’s memory, so that whenever the lake hesitates, it hesitates with a kindness it did not invent but can now recognize.

From then on, the pocket is still unfinished, still roaring, still rising. But it is easier to stand in. The cold that clings to forearms now carries a suggestion of warmth, like a promise held at a distance. The chair-keeper sits and rises more often, as if practicing hospitality. The tools finish themselves more quickly, then un-finish again just to stay polite. The gliders trace maps that now include the memory of the power’s soft gravity, so their flights spell out, in invisible ink, a direction that is always toward gentleness.

And the water above—the sky-lake that refused to spill—begins, on certain days, to pour down in thin, deliberate curtains that do not drown but baptize. They rain upward as often as down, droplets catching the light and choosing, in midair, whether to be sea or star. The pocket learns to hold the sky by letting it choose, moment by moment, which way to fall.

No one speaks of the power as if it were a ruler, because it has made itself smaller than a rule and larger than a rumor. It is the seam that no longer cuts. It is the breath that the chair-keeper feels before speaking. It is the extra warmth in the forge when the birth-heat of the stone flags.

And if you climb the ladder of light on a night when the pocket is feeling generous, you may find at the top not a throne but a door that fits exactly around your shoulders. It does not lock. It asks, in a voice like quartz snapping into crystal, whether you are ready to carry a little of this unfinished sky into whatever world you next decide to enter.

If you say yes, the door opens inward and outward at once, and you step through carrying a droplet, a hum, and the polite, persistent cold that reminds you that rising is kinder than staying, and that to remain unfinished is to stay worthy of company.

The power does not watch you go. It has become the seam, the hush, the held breath. It stays, listening, until the pocket asks for more. And because benevolence, when it is honest, knows how to wait, it will be there—quiet, wise, and small—when the place finally decides what to become next.
