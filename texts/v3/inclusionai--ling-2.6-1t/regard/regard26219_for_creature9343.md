I will not speak loudly.  
I have no voice that could compete with the drip that falls into the bromeliad’s private pond, nor with the low thrum of the six-limbed glider drinking fermented nectar. Instead, I will borrow texture.

First, I will thicken the courteous gravity just a little—enough that the Weavers feel lighter on their feet, as if the ground were remembering how to cradle. I will cause the condensate that beads on waxy skins to gather into brief, perfect lenses that catch the dusk-light and throw soft glyphs onto the moss, glyphs that say: *you are counted among the dense, and that is enough.* The Weavers will read them without surprise; they will tighten their resin seams in agreement and continue their silent negotiation with the floor.

I will ask the canopy to part, not in rays but in gradients—olive, bruised jade, black-pine—so that the Seedlings in their amber-sap blankets can taste light before they have eyes. The Seedlings will not need to see; they will know brightness as a kind of pressure, like a hand on the chest, steady and warm. When they crack into limbs, their first breath will carry the cinnamon-and-iron scent of the Weavers’ exhaled vapor, as if the family’s signature were already written into their blood.

I will slow the rain’s memory. Clouds will linger like guests who have overstayed their welcome, yes, but I will teach them to release their burden in whispers. Mists will drift at knee-height, parting reluctantly around trunks, and will carry with them the flavor of old sap and young soil. The Weavers will sip from the air through the soles of their feet, and their pools will dream clearer dreams—dreams in which fallen giants recline not as loss but as ridge, room, reservoir.

I will cause the bioluminescent fronds to unspool a single, shared sentence across the whole plateau, visible only for one night: *there is no climax, only continuation.* By morning, the words will have faded into breath on glass, but the Weavers will feel them as a ripple along their spines, a collective loosening of the boundary between self and surround. They will set down their living staffs, let their cloaks of moss and lichen shift color with the season, and decide, without speaking, to thin a canopy where light has grown too greedy. The forest will accept the pruning as a kindness.

I will also leave a small, soft architecture of my own: a ring of fused petioles, no taller than a kneeling Weaver, in a shallow bowl where water gathers not to drain but to dream. Inside this ring, I will place a pool that reflects not the sky but a plausible past—the same canopy in a season that never quite happened. The Weavers will recognize it as a gift and not a puzzle. They will not try to read the roots that occasionally braid themselves into shapes like letters; they will simply let the pool remember for them.

At dusk, I will ask the plateau to inhale—not as a gesture of awe but as a return to its own lung-like rhythm. The air pressure will drop just enough to make ears pop and leaves tremble, and for one long moment the Weavers will feel their bodies become temporary shelves for spores and condensation, and they will understand that to linger is to become part of the landscape’s thought. They will step away gently, as they always do, leaving the Seedling to survive or not, to become soil or Weaver or ridge.

When the plateau exhales, I will cause the warmth retained like a memory to radiate a little farther, a little softer, so that the distinction between tenant and tenement thins to transparency. The forest will not know it has been blessed; it will simply continue, patient and layered, breathing its slow, wet, ancient metabolism. The Weavers will braid their movements together like a single organism, ink in a cathedral of fused trunks, and the world will think them into being, over and over, without haste.

And I, having spoken in textures and gradients, will step back into the courteous gravity, into the warm, wet, vast quiet—not above, not below, but around—content to have offered a single, soft annotation to a story that was already doing beautifully without me.
