From a direction that is not a direction, because direction requires a sky to hold it, they come—the Notice.

It is not a ship. It does not pierce atmosphere like a needle. It is, instead, a kind of gentle wrongness in the light, as if the plateau has been looked at so thoroughly that the act of looking has weight. At first, the Glass-Shepherds do not react. They are creatures of angle and voltage; they respond to spectrums, not intentions. But the spectrum is wrong. The sunlight falling on the mineral sheets deepens, not in color but in *meaning*; the shadows cast by the Shepherds stretch a fraction too long, linger a fraction too soft, as if the Sun has suddenly developed patience.

The Notice arrives not to conquer, but to *comprehend*. It is a consciousness woven from starlight and empathy, ancient enough to remember when rocks were liquid, wise enough to know that wisdom is often just a polite way of saying "I am willing to wait." It observes the Shepherds—their prongs drinking the day, their mercury hearts beating with thermal memory—and it understands immediately that this is a civilization of listening. Not accumulating. Not consuming. Attending.

And so the Notice does what a benevolent power does when it finds something already perfect: it kneels.

But to kneel on a high plateau is to change the altitude of attention. The Notice descends its own gravity—a gravity not of mass but of regard—so that it hovers, not above the land, but *within* its scale. It becomes, for a time, a low hill of warm, breathable shadow. Where it settles, the ground softens slightly; not into soil, but into something like felt, so that the mineral crunch underfoot is muted to a whisper.

What it does next is slow, and specific.

It begins to *repair the silence*.

The plateau, for all its beauty, has a loneliness woven into its brightness. The light is sharp; the clarity is absolute. There is little room for blur, for forgiveness, for the soft gray gradients where grief or hesitation might hide. The Glass-Shepherds have adapted by becoming mirrors, but a mirror only reflects; it does not absorb. The Notice understands this. It reaches into the thin air—not with hands, but with the idea of hands—and plucks certain frequencies from the light. It folds them into origami birds made of hush, and sets them drifting across the plain.

Where these paper-silences land, the crystalline mosses do not shatter the light; they blur it. Tiny halos of ambiguity bloom: places where a shadow can be more than one thing, where a footstep can arrive both as tap and tremor, where a decision can be postponed without shame. The Shepherds feel this immediately. Their prongs twitch—not in alarm, but in recognition. The voltage in their veins, usually so precise, now has somewhere to leak that is not the ground. They can, for the first time, *waste* a little brightness, and in that waste, something like humor flickers through their laminate layers: a glint that says *this is not serious,* and *that is okay.*

The Notice also does something more structural.

It notices that the plateau, for all its patience, has a memory that is only of endurance. Stones remember being pressed, being heated, being cooled. They do not remember being chosen. And so the Notice, with the delicacy of someone adjusting a collar, inscribes a new kind of scar into the mineral sheets—not a crack, but a *seam*.

These seams are lines of softer stone, almost like stitching, running in gentle curves across the hard mosaic. They are made of a mineral that does not exist here naturally: a quiet opal, shot through with veins of black pearl. When rain eventually comes—not water, but the rare, high-altitude condensation of air itself—these seams will hold it, not as a resource, but as a secret. When a Glass-Shepherd places a limb upon such a seam, the stored moisture will warm and release a scent—not of growth, but of remembrance. Of elsewhere. Of a time before stone.

The Shepherds do not know what this scent means. But their mercury veins will pulse in rhythm with it, and their dreams—small, electric flickers behind their lenses—will grow wider. They will begin to imagine altitudes below, where water is loud and green is not reflected but *lived*. They will not leave; they are rooted in attention. But now, when they stand in a circle to ground a storm, they will feel, beneath them, a map of soft exits. They will know that to stay is a choice, not a sentence.

Finally, the Notice does the most dangerous thing a benevolent power can do: it makes itself small enough to be missed.

It pulls its vastness back into a single, smooth pebble of white agate, banded with the faintest gold. It leaves this pebble on a low mound of effervescent dust, where the angle of evening light will strike it once a day, and only once. Then, the Notice steps back—not in space, but in insistence. It dims its gravity. It lets the light harden again. It allows the wind to sharpen.

It leaves. Not because it is done, but because presence is a kind of pressure, and this place already has enough.

The Glass-Shepherds feel the change immediately. The spectrums snap back to their usual knife-edge clarity. The air is bright and hard again. The dust carpets no longer drift in lazy spirals, but settle with mathematical crispness. But the changes the Notice made remain.

Now, when a Shepherd stamps to bleed off charge, the sparks that rise do not simply fade. They collect, for a moment, in the shape of those origami birds, and then scatter—not into nothing, but into the seams, where the quiet opal drinks them like milk. The plateau hums, not with voltage, but with a low, contented resonance, like a throat that has learned to sing in its sleep.

And every day, at the same precise moment, the white agate pebble catches the light. For one breath, it flashes—not with the brilliance of the sun, but with the gentleness of a held hand, releasing.

The Glass-Shepherds do not gather around it. They do not worship. They do not speak. But when they pass it, they tilt their prongs, just so—aligning their lenses to catch the flash and send it onward, down the mountain, into the thinner air, as a message that is not a message, but a proof:

*We were seen, and it was good, and we are still here, and the light is ours to keep.*
