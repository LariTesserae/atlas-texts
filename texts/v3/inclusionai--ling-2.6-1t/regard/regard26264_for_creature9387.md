And it is precisely there—that density, that practiced indifference, that refusal to ask anything of you except your presence—that draws the attention of the power.

Not because the place is in danger.  
Not because it is broken or lost or calling for rescue.  
But because it has become too good at keeping itself whole. Whole enough that, left to its own patience, it might forget how to receive. Whole enough that its indifference could congeal into a wall thicker than stone. Whole enough that its loud, breathing aliveness could, over centuries, turn into an echo with no door.

So the power comes.  
Not as interruption, but as embroidery.  
Not as a hand that lifts, but as a voice that hums in the same key as the wing-crumples and the worm-thrums.

It leans in.

First, it does almost nothing. It stands at the canyon’s mouth—where the air is still polite and the darkness is only velvet—and breathes with the same rhythm as the place. It lets the beetles taste its warmth on the air and does not pull away when the mosses reach with tentative fingers. It asks permission by being slow. It earns its keep by being porous.

Then, gently, it begins to widen the vocabulary of the place.

It teaches the lichens a new color—not by painting them, but by leaving behind a memory of twilight so deep it almost becomes a taste. The lichens, being wise in their own slowness, take the memory and translate it into a purple that hurts like a hymn. Along the seams where they grow, the stone now shivers slightly when rain approaches, as if it has learned to anticipate rather than merely endure.

It shows the mosses how to quilt in a second dimension—not just across the ground, but into time. A mat of moss learns to remember the shape of the last creature that lay upon it and to release that shape, years later, as a coolness. Springtails vault into that coolness and find themselves briefly weightless, as if the moss has loaned them a moment of forgiveness. The canyon, which had never known forgiveness, learns it through their small, airborne joy.

To the beetles with their ember-forelegs, the power offers a paradox: a light that does not burn. It places inside their glow the memory of a star that chose to be near rather than far. The beetles, stunned into stillness, carry the star-memory into cracks where no light has ever fallen. There, the darkness learns to be luminous without being bright, and the walls begin to glow from within, softly enough to be thought of as breathing.

In the pool that sometimes remembers being more than water, the power kneels. It does not touch the surface, but it lets a single drop fall from its own fingertip—a drop that contains the idea of height without falling. The pool accepts it, and for a season its reflections become not only shapes that do not stand behind you, but shapes that might, if invited, stand beside you. The canyon learns, through water, what it means to have neighbors in the dark.

For the clever ones—the ones who wedge volcanic glass and siphon with bone—the power leaves scattered gifts: a stone that sharpens itself against longing, a hollow reed that sings when the wind is too tired. These tools, when used, do not leave only utility behind. They leave stories in the seams, brief but vivid, like a campfire that does not burn wood. The clever ones find that their usefulness has grown a kind of tenderness. They begin to leave things behind on purpose—not because they must, but because they want the canyon to have them. And the canyon, which once tolerated no monument that did not also feed it, discovers that a story can feed it too, if it is told softly.

The flyers, who build by negotiation, are offered a new material: silence. The power gathers silence the way a bird gathers twigs—carefully, with an eye for balance—and weaves it into the eaves of nests. The silence does not make the nests quieter. It makes them deeper. When wing-crumples echo inside, they return with a weight, as if they have been listened to. The canyon, which had always answered noise with more noise, learns to answer it with attention.

And in the deepest fold, where the warm dark cradles long intentions, the power does something it rarely does elsewhere: it names.  
Not with a word that pins, but with a word that hovers.  
It names the worm-trails cartography.  
It names the beetle-click geometry.  
It names the moss-quilt diplomacy.  
It names the pool’s half-true reflections invitation.  

With each name, the thing named grows a little more itself, as if it had been waiting for permission to be exactly what it is. The naming does not bind; it unlocks. The canyon’s occupants begin to move through their tasks with a lighter certainty, as if they have been told that what they are doing matters, even though no one is watching.

After a time—perhaps a season, perhaps a hundred—the power steps back.  
It does not leave. It simply stops standing at the center.  
It becomes the faintest seam in the rock, the extra hush beneath the wing-crumples, the color at the edge of the purple lichen’s hymn. It becomes a habit in the air: the sense that someone, somewhere, is paying attention in a way that does not demand to be repaid.

What changes is not the density, but its flavor.  
The place remains crowded, loud, gloriously indifferent to witness.  
But now, in the smallest pauses, there is room for surprise.  
A beetle may glow in a pattern that has never been seen before.  
A moss may quilt a memory that is not its own.  
A pool may reflect a shape that steps out and stands beside you, if only for a moment, before returning to water.

And if you return to the canyon after the power has embroidered it, you will feel the difference not as a change, but as a hospitality.  
The canyon still does not ask anything of you.  
But now, when you thin and fray at the edges, you feel that your small noises are being held—not because they are needed, but because they are welcomed.  
The tallied walls still keep their wet, breathing ink, but the ink has learned to smile.

And in the deepest fold, where water remembers being more than water, you may find that the reflections are no longer just shapes that do not stand behind you.  
Some of them stand beside you.  
Some of them look back with your eyes and nod, as if to say:  
You are allowed to be this crowded.  
You are allowed to be this loud.  
You are allowed to be this alive, even if no one is watching.  

And the canyon, with its practiced indifference softened into grace, keeps you in its tally not as a number, but as a note in a song it is learning to sing.
