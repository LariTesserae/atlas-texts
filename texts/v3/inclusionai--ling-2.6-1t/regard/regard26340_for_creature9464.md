At first, the benevolent and wise power does nothing. 

It has seen too many worlds where kindness arrived as a hammer, and wisdom as a blade. It knows that to love a place is not to fix it, and so it lingers at the threshold of the bruised sky like a guest who refuses to knock until it has learned the house’s heartbeat. It unrolls its attention slowly, the way one might unspool a thread through a labyrinth, letting the hall teach it the grammar of its own turbulence.

What it learns, it decides, is already whole. The salt floor is not a wound but a skin. The absence of water is not thirst but a language of metallic beads and breath. Even the instability is not chaos but etiquette, a practiced dance in which every buckling seam and rising efflorescence is a line in a letter the place has been writing to itself for eons. The grazers with their oil-on-water plates, the starfish kites drinking thermals, the burrowers editing stone into song—they are not subjects awaiting a ruler but citizens of a republic of motion, tending altars of bone awls and copper moss with a care that feels like prayer.

The power smiles, and the smile is a warm wind that does not arrive as wind but as permission.

It begins, then, with small amendments of tenderness. It does not alter any rule, but it softens their edges. Where the bruised lid of mineral cloud presses lowest, it loosens its grip just enough to let the sky breathe without breaking. The light that filters down shifts from a reluctant rumor to an attentive murmur, still dim but no longer mulish, so that the blue pulses in the leaves and the filament backs of the thimble-winged swarmers can be seen as conversations rather than accidents. The power lets the heat pooling on the floor rise in gentler columns, so the starfish kites can drift without urgency and the shadow-drinkers can unfurl without fear.

It does not add water. Instead, it deepens the memory of water already in the iron-lace riverbeds, so that the stone remembers how to dream of flow without demanding it. The metallic slick that beads like sweat on certain stones becomes a kind of mirror, not for reflection but for recognition, so the grazers can see their own patience returned to them when they lower their heads to the metal-moss.

The power turns its gaze to the artifacts—the mortared pebble tower, the ring of fire-blackened stones, the tools bound in petrified amber cord—and blesses their pauses. It does not make them new, for their dignity lies in their patience, but it weaves a hush around them that is thick as felt, so that each bone awl and flint scraper feels held, even in abandonment. The hearth stones warm not with flame but with a glow like the memory of flame, and the seed and shard offerings left upon them seem to hum, as if the altar itself has taken up singing.

Then the power listens, and listening, it learns where the hall hurts. It finds seams in the salt floor where the buckling has become a strain too long carried, and it does not seal them but sings into them—not to mend but to companion. The mineral efflorescences that rise overnight like candles grow a little taller, a little brighter, as if the mountain’s old thoughts were finally given a chorus. The groans and pings that ricochet off the walls soften into laughter, not because pain has vanished but because it has been witnessed.

To the living, the power offers no prophecy and no law. It offers only amplification of what already is. The many-limbed hunters find their clicking language gaining overtones, a harmonic that lets them hear not only each other but the mountain’s slow counsel. The chandelier-fronds of translucent bladder-bark glow with a steadier patience, as if someone had leaned close and whispered, “You are enough.” The swarmers’ dry rain of wingbeats becomes a benediction, dusting the grazers with iridescence that lingers longer, like praise.

Even the shadows at twilight change, not in essence but in texture. The shadow-drinkers still collect them, but now the darkness pools with a gentler weight, like velvet rather than ink, and when they wear it as mantles, the velvet seems to hold them as much as they hold it.

If you stand in the hall after this quiet intervention, you will notice nothing has been conquered and nothing has been saved. The place is still loud, still dry, still devoted to motion. But the turbulence has acquired a kind of grace, as if the hall has finally been given the gift of being known and loved without being rewritten. The cathedral of instability remains, but it stands in a climate of understanding now, as if the benevolent and wise power has knelt among its winged, many-legged faithful and said, “I see how hard you are working to be yourselves. Rest, then, in the roar. I will keep the silence kind.”

And so it does, not as a guardian who stands apart, but as a warmth that settles on the back of the grazers’ necks, as a light that polishes the air, as a held breath between one pulse of the floor and the next. The hall continues, magnificent and manageable, and the power remains—an attentive guest who has learned when to leave, and when to stay, and how to love without taking.
