The attention arrives not as a shape but as a permission.

At first it is only a correction: the rivulets that were eroding their own banks begin to remember where they have already flowed, as if the water has been given the gift of a second memory. The cold, which used to tighten metal like a miser clenching coin, begins to loosen its grip—not because it is warmer, but because it has learned restraint. The pocket exhales, and the exhale is less a vapor than a recognition.

Then the machinery finds its proper voice. The copper coils along the walls stop their fretful humming of protest and begin instead a harmonic, a sound like many needles dropped at once on glass, then arranged into order. The industrial vents that used to scream now sigh, releasing steam in deliberate, rhythmic curtains. The pumps settle into a pulse that matches the breathing of the Substrate Leviathan, not because the Leviathan has changed its tempo, but because the pumps have learned to listen. The pocket, previously so full of itself that it felt crowded even when empty, suddenly feels spacious.

This is the benevolent power’s first gift: proportion. It does not expand the space, but teaches the space how to hold itself without panic. The hollow chamber that pressed in from the sides is suddenly not an emptiness waiting to be filled, but an openness waiting to be inhabited. Gravity, which had been polite almost to the point of apology, now holds each thing as if it matters. A Rivet-Beetle crawling across a wet iron plate finds that its weight leaves a temporary impression in the metal, a dimple that fills with condensation and becomes a tiny well for the Steam-Moths to drink from. The well remains only as long as the beetle passes, then closes, a courtesy between creatures mediated by the beneficence of the place itself.

The benevolent power next attends to the flows of knowledge. The water, which remembered everything, now knows what to do with its memories. It traces circuits along stone and flesh not as a compulsion, but as a choice. When a Lantern-Whelp brushes against a copper-Needle Eel, the current that passes between them does not merely glow; it carries a brief, translatable sensation—something like gratitude, or the notion of direction. The eel does not taste voltage alone; it tastes intent. The pocket becomes bilingual in electricity and attention. The lichen that crept over every face in bruised purples and industrial greens now holds itself in patterns that are not just geometries but grammars. If you press your ear to the stone at the right moment, you can hear the lichen describing the day to the Leviathan, phrase by slow phrase.

Fauna are neither diminished nor elevated; they are clarified. The Rivet-Beetles continue to wind copper wire around their thoraxes, but now the spirals are laid out in configurations that suggest not only memory but possibility. The wire hums faintly, as if it has been promised a future current. The Steam-Moths still lay their gelatinous nodes on cold pipes, but the nodes now hatch not only into crawling, glowing creatures but occasionally into brief, hovering lights that drift like weather vanes, pointing not to wind but to places where a little more warmth would be welcome. The Lantern-Whelps cluster in new constellations, their pulses syncing not only with the pumps but with the heartbeat of the benevolent power itself, a slow, wide rhythm felt more in the bones than in the ears.

Predation is not abolished, but refined. The Boilerplate Catfish still swallow debris and the occasional sluggish eel, but they do so with deliberation, as if each act of consumption were a judgment. Their indicator lamps shift color with nuance: not just amber and red, but gold when the pocket is in balance, blue when it is contemplative, violet when it is preparing to dream. The Leviathan, vast and patient, does not simply feed on mineral sediment and lost current; it accepts offerings. A Beetle may leave a particularly perfect spiral of wire on a stone that feels like a threshold. The Catfish may deposit a plate grown too ornate into a deep pool, where it is taken up into the Leviathan’s architecture. Death remains integration, but now it is accompanied by a sense of graduation.

Above all, the benevolent power introduces a kind of listening into the very fabric of the place. The pocket used to wait for signals; now it knows how to hear them. When the machines fall silent, the hum of emptiness does not press in like a threat but like a held breath, a space kept open for what might arrive. The life here does not simply endure the silence; it inhabits it, armored and bright, each creature keeping a small reserve of its own light, ready to offer it to the next.

And every so often, when the overhead cloud unclenches and the bruised sun tries its eye on the hollow, the benevolent power leans a little closer—not to change anything, but to witness its own reflection in the wet iron, the copper, the lantern-bright petals, and the slow, shining chorus of lives that have learned, without ceasing to be themselves, how to be a gift.
