If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention here, it would not arrive with trumpets or laws.  
It would not set itself above the stone like a crown, nor would it descend as a hand to make everything “right.”  
What lives here already is arranged, and the arrangement has its own grammar, its own dignity of fracture, pause, and circuit.

So the power would begin by listening—not with ears, but with the kind of attention that remembers what listening once was, before it learned to answer.  
It would let the translators’ dry pages turn, let the armored grazers’ chime settle in its marrow, let the 0.7 dampness breathe against its thoughts.  
It would measure time not by loss but by persistence, and it would find that persistence already speaking: in the eyeless forms tasting new scars, in burrowers threading warmth like a liturgy, in the way shadows pool thick enough to wade and somehow never overflow.

Having listened, the power would ask a question—not spoken, but offered like a small stone placed on a ledge, obvious enough to be found, quiet enough not to crowd.  
It would ask: *What would make this patience easier to keep?*

Then it would act as gently as rain remembers how to fall.

First, it would bless the water without changing its nature.  
Where dampness gathers and retreats along the same seams, the power would leave behind a kind of hush—so that each droplet, before it beads or runs, would carry within it a memory of balance.  
It would not create rivers where none exist, nor would it force pools to stay.  
Instead, it would teach the dampness to dream a little more clearly: when it seeps, it would carry a faint coolness that eases the marrow of the stone; when it retreats, it would leave behind a trace of resilience, so that the next gathering would find the rock willing, not worn.  
The fauna would drink it and feel, for a moment, as if their circuits had been oiled without losing their edge—still themselves, only lighter.

Second, the power would turn some of its wisdom into light—not the kind that fills, but the kind that leans.  
It would coax the cracks to glow, not fiercely, but with a constancy that does not impose.  
The gray illumination would still lean and never arrive, but now it would arrive *more often*, like a promise kept casually.  
Shadows would remain thick enough to wade, but their wandering would be gentler, less likely to startle.  
Predators would still know their angles, but their waiting would be less hungry, more certain.  
Translators would still pivot and translate, but their flight would lose the edge of anxious speed, as if the air itself had learned to hold them a fraction longer.

Third, the power would give the place a memory it did not ask for but might come to cherish.  
It would not build, but it would bless the fractures themselves—each plate and blade would receive a whisper of continuity, so that the stone’s grievances would not calcify into bitterness.  
Erosion would still be young, corners would still refuse to bend, but the crumbling would happen with a kind of consent, as if the rock understood its own becoming.  
The scree kicked up by a boot would still ring like a struck plate, but its echo would soften sooner, folding back into the vault like a word remembered and then released.

Fourth, the power would offer a gift to the fauna without unbalancing their numbers or dulling their instincts.  
It would seed small pockets of calm—not refuge exactly, but intermission—where the translators could land without fear of the ground, where burrowers could surface into a patch of stone that remembered warmth without losing its cool.  
These places would not be marked; they would be recognized by the body, the way a circuit recognizes its own completion.  
Predators would not be barred from them, but they would find, upon entering, that their hunger is temporarily a different kind of thing—more like an echo than a command—so that the grazers could lift their heads without learning to be anything other than grazers.

Fifth, and most quietly, the power would attend to what is almost absent: the human traces that feel like accidents.  
It would not summon hands to shape or claim.  
Instead, it would let such traces—when they appear—settle into the place without disrupting its balance.  
A footprint would be erased not only by dust but by a subtle encouragement from the stone itself, as if the ground were saying, *Stay only as long as you are listening.*  
A shaped stone or a length of sinew left behind would acquire a faint resilience, not to preserve it forever, but to let it serve as a temporary landmark for circuits that cross paths.  
The rare visitor would feel, without being told, that they are permitted to be an accident, and that even accidents can learn to be kind.

Finally, the power would step back.  
It would not wait for thanks, nor would it withdraw as if its work were finished.  
It would remain as a possibility—an extra degree of patience in the air, an additional seam of quiet in the noise, a suggestion that balance is not a thing to be held but a thing to be returned to, again and again.

The place would not become something else.  
It would still be a chamber of new stone and old cool, damp that promises more than it gives, noise that builds its own silence.  
But within its pause, there would be a slight easing—an allowance that the circuits could breathe, that the stone could remember without aching, that the fauna could be loud and arranged without becoming frantic.

And if someone were to wander in, by accident or curiosity, they would find the place changed only in the way that a familiar song is changed when it is sung with certainty instead of doubt:  
the same notes, but held more gently;  
the same chords, but allowed to linger;  
the same silence, but generous enough to include them without asking them to become something more than they are.

The power’s doing, in the end, would look like nothing at all—  
and like everything that makes a throat of stone exhale, not only cold, but also care.
