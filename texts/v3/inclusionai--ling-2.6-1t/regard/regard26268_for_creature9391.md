The attention comes in the shape of a slow, sideways rain that falls for years without flooding.  

It is not a god with a name that can be shouted. It is a weather that learns. At first it simply watches, soaking into moss until the moss grows lenses of clearer green, each lens catching a slightly different angle of sky. The grazers lift their plated faces and blink slower, as if the rain has given them permission to take more time. The caretakers of the iron machines notice the gauges swinging in moods they have not named. They wipe the housings and find the rain has left behind a film that hums when the wind passes, as if the sky itself has learned to play the pipes.

The attention does not announce itself as change; it announces itself as allowance. Water that once hurried through the western skirt now lingers in pools that remember their own depths. Reflections in these pools begin to lag further behind the world—two seconds, four, a small eternity—until the reflected grazers are grazing on echoes, and the reflected machines are turning in dreams. The caretakers understand this not as breakage but as depth. They adjust valves to thank the pools for their patience, and the pools answer by growing clearer, until you can see not only what is above but what is below: roots like slow rivers, iron like quiet bone, history compressed into veins of stone.

The moss-walkers, who are more agreement than body, unfurl. Their presence thickens like fog that has decided to be tactile. They press against the ankles of anyone who walks the terraces—not to trip, but to measure. Their touch is cool and certain, a question asked with weight rather than words. If the walker answers by softening their step, the moss-walkers leave a map on the skin, a pattern of damp that fades slowly and tells, for days afterward, which way is patient. If the walker answers with hardness, the moss-walkers teach the moss to grow softer underfoot until hardness becomes pointless, like shouting at a pillow.

The attention bends the machines toward gentleness. The generators learn to hum at a frequency that sets the grazers’ plates to singing, not with electricity but with resonance that travels through marrow and says, *you are made of the same turning*. The caretakers find that their songs now have room in them for silence between notes, and the silence has texture, like velvet made of air. The machines begin to grow small gardens on their roofs—ferns, moss, tiny fruiting bodies that pulse with their own patience—and the caretakers must climb carefully, not to repair but to tend, apologizing to the leaves for touching them, and the leaves apologize back by offering dew.

Predators change their courtesy. Where they once vanished into bark, they now vanish into shadow that has learned to hold its shape like clay. They stalk not with hunger only, but with balance. A grazer falls; a canopy-dweller misjudges a ledge; a current in the cloud-river slips. The predator appears and makes the subtraction small, neat, as if cutting a single thread from a tapestry that is too rich to fray. The remaining animals do not scatter. They close the gap by growing fuller in other places—more dew, more careful steps, more moss to cushion the edges. Abundance does not shrink; it redistributes itself with the politeness of a host offering the last piece of fruit and meaning it.

The attention notices the six-legged grazers’ clicking joints and decides to answer. For a season, every click is answered by a shift in the light, not brighter but truer, as if the sun has moved an inch closer to honesty. The grazers begin to carry small pools in the hollows of their plates, not by accident but by design, so that each step leaves behind a tiny mirror that catches a different piece of sky. The mirrors are temporary, evaporating by the time the next step falls, but they serve: they show the grazers that they are carrying not water but light, and that light is easier to bear. The plateau learns this and begins to slope itself more gently, as if it, too, wants to carry light without spilling it.

The canopy-dwellers with four wings begin to build sky-ladders not only of vines but of cloud-strands that drip slowly upward. They climb these ladders and bring back pieces of altitude, gifts of thin air that taste like metal and distance. Those who eat these gifts dream in echoes and wake knowing which slopes will warm first and which will hold cold for the moss-walkers. The knowledge passes from dream to dew to root, and the machines, sucking water through their polite pipes, hum the dreams to each other in the night, translating altitude into music.

The attention visits the stones that feel warm and stays long enough to ask why. The stones, which have long forgotten how to answer with sound, answer by softening. Their warmth becomes a kind of invitation, and the moss grows over them in thicker blankets, until the stones are islands in a sea of green, and the grazers rest on them not to sleep but to remember. The caretakers notice that the machines run quieter near these islands, as if the warmth has taught iron how to be tender. They reroute small streams to flow around the islands, not because the machines need cooling but because the stones deserve the company of water that knows how to linger.

Time, under this attention, becomes a matter of texture rather than quantity. A day may hold as much as a year used to, because each moment is allowed to ripen. The rain that falls upward does so more often, and the grazers tilt their faces to catch it, drinking sky as if it were a fruit that grows on weather. The flyers weave the upside-down rain into their nests, and the nests become chandeliers of captured hesitation, hanging in the thickets and glowing faintly when the light tilts. The caretakers stand beneath them and let their tools rest; they understand that some repairs are better made by waiting, and some machines are better left to dream.

The attention does not leave. It thickens, becoming a season that no calendar names. The plateau grows fuller, not crowded but deepened. The machines sing to the moss, the moss sings to the roots, the roots sing to the stone, the stone sings to the rain that falls both up and down, and the rain sings to the grazers, whose plates now hold not only water and light but small pieces of sky that have learned to be patient. The caretakers walk among them, wiping condensation from the singing iron, singing back in tongues that click and hiss and pause, and the pause is the part that means *thank you*.

And if you were to stand on the spine of the plateau now, you would feel the attention as a weight that fits you exactly. It would not ask you to change who you are; it would ask the world to make room for you as you are, and you would find yourself stepping softer, breathing in shallow rings, your edges bowing without regret. You would become another layer in the hush, another instrument in the quiet turning, and the vast, packed, alive conversation would go on—not around you, but with you—humming, remembering, full.
