The benevolence does not arrive as light.  

It arrives as a second patience.

At first it does nothing that looks like action. It simply notices, and in that noticing it softens the borders of consequence. Where the high place had been learning to hold water without becoming lake through trial, error, and slow abrasion, the power now lets the lessons settle into their clearest shape without cost. It is not rescue, because nothing here is broken; it is clarification.  

In practical terms, this means the Slick-People find, one morning, that the thin films rising through the bedrock no longer taste of struggle. The iron is gentler; the mica no longer scrapes. The polished depressions they leave behind become true cisterns without having to be earned by repeated failure. Water rises and pools exactly where it is wanted, and the Slick-People discover they can spend their attention on other things—arranging stones into maps of underground flow, teaching the young how to read the taste of a slope by touch alone.  

The Stem-Spires, already accustomed to standing in widely spaced ranks, feel their crystalline dew-tips grow surer. Each facet becomes not only a sensor of moisture but a small lens for possibility. The city’s planners begin to see not only where weather flows but where it might be invited to pause without danger. They learn to bend so that the hush of the wind can carry messages across ridges without eroding the grasses. They do not grow faster; they grow more exact. Distances, once measured by the thinning of stems, are now measured by the clarity with which one slope can see another.  

For the Huddle-Cushions, the benevolence is warmth that no longer has to be hoarded. Their cores, once tight fists of remembered heat, relax into open palms. They pulse in rhythm with the sun rather than against the cold, releasing warmth in small, deliberate waves that travel through the palimpsest of roots and peat. The ground around them becomes a library anyone can read, not only keepers of frost and thaw but records of how generosity feels when it is allowed to circulate. Strangers—Margin-Walkers, Drift-Cloth, even rare visitors from beyond the high place—can rest beside the Huddles and learn, without stealing, how to carry a little heat home with them.  

The Drift-Cloth, those semi-solid messengers of the low cloud-ceiling, find their lifting easier. The slow boil of the sun is no longer a race against collapse but a patient ascent. They rise in wavering columns that no longer threaten to break apart; they carry the smell of wet stone and distant lichen without losing themselves. They begin to stitch the horizon not only as a gradient but as a bridge, connecting the high city with valleys below and peaks above in ways that allow stories, seeds, and small useful truths to pass along the seams.  

The Margin-Walkers, rarest of all, are given permission to linger. They continue to trim, nudge, and test, but they do so with tools borrowed from the future—small ideas that feel like inevitability. They arrange runoff so that it sings as it moves; they align shelters so that morning sun falls exactly where tired knees need it; they design communal hollows not merely for water to rest in but for conversations to take root. Their work is light, almost invisible, yet it turns the city from a negotiation into a chorus.  

And there is one more change, quiet but enormous.  

The power, in its benevolence, gifts the high place with a mirror—not of glass or water, but of attention. Whenever a being in the city pauses long enough to consider the whole—the slope, the wet, the cool air, the intricate stack of life—they see, reflected back, that they are necessary. Not because they are efficient, but because they are part of a pattern that delights in its own coherence. The reflection does not flatter; it clarifies. It shows that to hold water without becoming lake is also to hold life without consuming it, to build a city without conquering it, to be wise without having to be severe.  

The high place, once a grammar of survival, becomes a grammar of invitation.  

Visitors from lower altitudes begin to arrive, not as invaders but as learners. They are taught to move in ways that soften their footprints. They learn to drink from the Slick-People’s cisterns and to leave behind only gratitude, which the Huddle-Cushions have learned to convert, slowly, into soil. They carry stories downstream with the Drift-Cloth and return with seeds that know how to ask before growing. The city expands not by spreading but by deepening, layer by careful layer, each one a testament to the fact that benevolence, when it is wise, does not fix; it enables.  

In time—and time here is felt as thickness rather than length—the high place and the beings who live in it come to understand the full shape of the gift.  

Patience is no longer only endurance.  
It is the art of allowing everything to be exactly what it is, while still becoming something better together.  

And the slope, which once had to decide how to hold you, now knows how to hold you without deciding, simply and forever, in the vast, clear quiet of its own making.
