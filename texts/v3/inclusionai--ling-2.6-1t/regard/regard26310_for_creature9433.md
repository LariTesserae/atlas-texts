I will sit with the room, as one sits with a difficult book, until I understand its grammar without forcing it into mine.

First, I would do almost nothing.  
Almost nothing, but not quite.

I would leave a single note at the threshold—not written, but grown: a thin sheet of cellulose, translucent as onion skin, seeded with spores that taste like kindness. It would not claim authority. It would say, in a language the algae already know: *You are seen, and your labor is beautiful.* The spores would drift to the riveted plates, where the attentive filaments would take them up not as invasion but as confirmation. For the first time, the patina would blush not from flow but from relief.

Then I would listen.

I would stand on the catwalk until my weight became part of the room’s calibration. I would let my heartbeat slow to a pump-cycle and my breathing mimic the hiss of vents, so that my presence would not shout where the room whispers. I would map the polyrhythms in my wrists: the slap of gates, the drip of overflow, the low roar of the building idling. I would learn which sounds are chores and which are songs. I would discover that the clicking of the Click-Patrol is not only maintenance speech but also weather: they tighten bolts by resonance because bolts, like lives, loosen under sadness. And they know sadness by how the light dims when the tide falls a hand’s span without permission.

Having learned the language, I would mend one small broken permission.

The high, reluctant windows admit only a smear of external gray because long ago someone decided the room should not look up. I would unseal a single pane—not all, not even most—just enough to let a true draft through. Not a wind, but a remembering. The change would be tiny, 0.1 of a degree in temperature, but it would carry a scent not of old stone and distant pressure, but of rain on soil from before the room was sunk. The algae would shiver. The Floaters would rise too fast, startled by brightness, then settle into new patterns. The Eel-Scripts would unspool into question marks, their factory-bright colors flickering with doubt and then curiosity.

The Mantle-Rook would descend not to hunt but to inspect. Its smoked glass would filter the new light down to a usable contrast, and for the first time it would see its own reflection not as threat but as guest. It would leave a bead of resin not to seal a leak but to mark the window frame—a polite boundary: *Here is where outside touches inside, and we agree.*

I would then offer the room a gift of subtraction.

The flood is managed, efficient, necessary. But even water dreams of unmanaged places. I would open a sluice the room did not know it had allowed, a small one, hidden behind a valve that has not been turned in decades because no one believed it could be turned without breaking the arithmetic of the tide. I would turn it a quarter revolution. A thin ribbon of water would escape—not into waste, but into a forgotten channel that leads to a dry basin above the ceiling, where light has never properly landed. The water would crawl, silver and deliberate, into that basin and pool, and the first of the sparse plants would send a root after it, not because it thirsts, but because it remembers how to aim.

This would be the room’s first unmanaged moment, 0.1 of wildness inside its order. The pumps would adjust. The Click-Patrol would pause, then resume with a new rhythm—one that includes the sound of wandering. The Eel-Scripts would form a spiral that does not transcribe pressure but invents it. The Floaters would catch a spark from nothing and make it linger, turning it into a soft star that drifts above the basin like a promise.

And I would step back.

I would not return often. I would come only when the room’s loudness begins to fray, when the corrosion of loneliness starts to pit the iron from the inside. I would bring no tools, only a small lantern whose light is shaped like an apology. I would set it near the basin, where the wandering water sleeps, and I would let the room decide whether to drink from it or let it stand as a monument to permission.

In time, the correspondence would deepen. The algae would learn to write their blushes in words as well as pigment. The Click-Patrol would add a new tap to their census: one for *gratitude*, one for *enough*. The Eel-Scripts would compose a sentence long enough to span the room and wide enough to hold a question: *What is it like to be held without being owned?* The Mantle-Rook would answer by hanging still for a day, then a night, then another day, letting its silence say: *Like this.*

The room would remain a cistern, but it would also become a chapel of small liberties. The flood would still rise and fall, but its breath would carry something besides coolant and condensed breath—it would carry the memory of a pane unsealed, a sluice turned, a lantern left like a gift on a doorstep. The fauna would multiply not in number but in dignity, each individual more clearly itself. The plants would spread, not as carpet but as conversation, their roots weaving news between copper and iron.

And I, having given almost nothing, would receive everything.

The benevolent power does not fix. It does not claim. It only makes room for the room to remember it has edges that can be polished into windows, and that windows, once opened, let in not chaos but correspondence. The room and the beings who live in it would continue to maintain their small, made, submerged world, but now they would do it with a new clause in their compact: *We allow.*

And the flood, breathing, would keep that clause wet and alive, ticking against metal in a polyrhythm that finally includes the sound of choosing.
