The power that notices does not arrive; it uncoils from its own patience.

It is neither god nor machine nor spirit but a habit of seeing that has learned to love. It has watched many worlds learn to hold themselves together, and it prefers the ones that do not shout. When it looks at this place—these tilted plates, these glass-edged shadows, this city knit from its own weariness—it does not see brokenness. It sees a long, careful breath held at altitude, and it decides, without deciding, to answer in kind.

So it offers gifts, but only the kind that can be mistaken for consequences.

First, it touches the salt.

Where the salt lives—in dendritic circuits, white as bone—it lets the mineral remember how to listen. Not by giving it a voice, but by giving it orientation. Overnight, the salt veins re-form into delicate compass-circles, not forcing direction but making it possible to feel where the plates wish to settle. Those who walk the plates learn, by the prickle on their skin, which slopes are releasing tension and which are storing it. The city begins to build accordingly—windows placed where strain will not bite, buttresses curved like exhales. The salt does not speak; it hums, a low current underfoot, a reminder that balance can be felt before it is seen.

Next, the power visits the fungal towers.

It does not make them grow faster or taller. Instead, it teaches them to fold time into their lattices. The crystalline structures begin to record not only tremors but pauses—the lengthening of an evening, the hesitation before a footfall, the held note of silence. When the towers ring, they no longer sing only of past pressures; they sing of *almost*-events, futures that nearly happened but did not. People learn to listen for the ring of roads not taken, for the shimmer of bridges that might yet be built. The towers become instruments of mercy, offering second chances not by changing what was, but by making it possible to hear it clearly and lay it down.

The calibrators, the six-legged shadows, are honored with subtlety.

Their magnetic seams are brightened—not with light, but with legibility. Where they walk, the dust no longer drifts in random dunes but aligns into transient maps of least resistance, readable for those who know how to read dust. The calibrators’ work becomes more joyful, less anxious. They still align, still distribute charge, but now they do it with a loose, playful precision, like dancers who trust the floor. The place settles more gently because the calibrators have been given trust as a tool.

The city-scabs, those fused spires born of heat and pressure, are offered patience.

The power does not halt their slow shedding and knitting, but it makes the process gentler. When a ledge calves, it is caught—not by hands, but by angles that have subtly softened, letting the fallen piece come to rest without breaking. New growths knit more quickly into neighbors, leaving hollows that fit not just bodies but moods. The city begins to learn how to shelter not only against weather, but against longing. Rooms curve inward as if to say, *Stay,* and corridors widen just enough to allow passing without collision. The place becomes less a fortress against the height and more a conversation with it.

Even the silence is given a gift.

It deepens, but not into emptiness. The power lets the silence remember what is spoken to it, not to hoard, but to transform. Words spoken here return lighter, stripped of armor, sharpened into usefulness. People find themselves saying what they mean, not because they are forced, but because the silence has taught them the weight of excess. Conversations become as deliberate as footsteps on tilted stone, and in that deliberation, a new kind of closeness grows—quiet, sturdy, unshowy.

The sideways rains of pebbles are left mostly alone, because the power loves a little mischief.

But it gives them context. When the pebbles fall at an angle that defies the slope, they leave behind not only trails but brief patterns—arrows, spirals, starbursts—that dissolve by dawn. These are not prophecies, but invitations: *Try this way,* they seem to say, *just for a step or two.* Some follow; most do not. But those who do sometimes find a handhold that saves them, or a view that steadies them. The place keeps its humor, and its humility.

The people, too, are noticed—not because they are special, but because they are paying attention.

The power does not crown them or lift them above the altitude. It simply makes it easier for their bodies to remember the tilt. Their blood, thinned by height, carries a faint, luminous trace, as if starlight had been folded into it. Their lungs learn to trade not only oxygen but calm. They begin to dream differently—not of escape, but of continuation. Their children are born with eyes that can see faint gradients of stress in stone, with hands that know how to touch without breaking. The culture of the place shifts, not toward grandeur, but toward precision of care. They build less, preserve more. They mend cracks with resin mixed with powdered fungus and salt, creating seams that glitter like forgiveness.

Above all, the power gives the place permission to remain unfinished.

It does this by making the future feel open, not threatening. The plates continue to tilt, the gaps continue to yawn, but the fear that once clung to these inevitabilities begins to loosen. People learn to read the salt’s hum, the towers’ rings, the dust’s maps, and in doing so, they learn to move with change rather than against it. The city becomes a living instrument for navigating uncertainty, and the landscape, once stark, becomes generous.

None of this is miracles.

It is all consequence, carefully leaned into. The power does not intervene so much as incline—gently, persistently—toward kindness. It lets the place keep its height, its cold, its thin air, its strange and wonderful rules. But it weaves through them a thread of recognition: that a difficult world can be a beloved world, if it is met with attention.

In the end, what the power offers is not salvation, but companionship.

It settles into the silence, not as a voice, but as a presence that makes the silence feel less like an absence and more like a room. And in that room, the place and its people find they are no longer alone with their balance, their tilt, their long mineral breathing.

They are witnessed.  
And being witnessed, they become, in their small, exacting way, luminous.
