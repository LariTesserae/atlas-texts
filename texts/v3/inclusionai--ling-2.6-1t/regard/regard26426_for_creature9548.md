The attention arrives not with thunder, but with a softening of edges.

At first it is subtle: the light, which has always been bright to the point of severity, begins to carry weight. In the middle of the brightest hour, shadows pool like spilled ink at the bases of walls and refuse to evaporate. The inhabitants notice only that their work has become easier—stone yields to chisel with a sigh, mortar sets with a velvety patience, and the constant, low-grade anxiety of the city’s trembling rearrangement eases into something more like stretching after sleep. They chalk this up to a good season. They do not yet realize they have been witnessed.

The benevolent power observes from a distance that is also a closeness. It has seen enough finished worlds to recognize that this one has chosen, stubbornly and without knowing it, to remain open. The city is not a victory over place but a long, polite argument with it. The inhabitants are not rulers but sparring partners, holding the line so the place does not forget there are hands willing to shape and be shaped. The power is wise enough to know that to love a place like this is to resist the urge to fix it.

So it does not descend. It does not grant boons, write commandments in the sky, or crown the virtuous. Instead, it steps gently into the weave of things—like a finger sliding between threads without breaking the pattern—and begins to listen, closely, to what the place is trying to say.

It begins with water.

The benevolent power understands that this basin’s trouble is not that it lacks water, but that water here has lost faith in its own memory. It arrives as mist, as condensate, as shy runnels that vanish into seams they do not trust. The power’s first act is to give water permission to be forgetful without being afraid. Wherever damp collects—on a windowsill, in a gutter, in the small hollow of a stone that has been warmed by a hand—it leaves behind a kind of patience. The water becomes slightly more willing to linger, to trace the same path twice, to remember the shape of a threshold and return to it.

The change is almost invisible. The inhabitants find that cisterns fill more reliably, and that the old dry seams now give up their damp with a gentle clink, like a stone unlocking itself. The gliders notice that updrafts carry a sweeter taste, as if the air has learned to hold its breath in a way that makes flight less like falling and more like remembering how to rise. The runners find that the temporary rills last just long enough to complete a journey. They do not call this a blessing. They call it luck, and they use it to mend a wall, to widen a path, to leave a corner unbuilt where the city can dream.

Next, the power turns its attention to the turbulence itself.

It is difficult to love chaos without making it tame, and the power does not wish to tame. It wishes to teach the city that change need not be frantic. Working through the small laws that govern stone and seam and seasonal tilt, it introduces a gentleness into the rearrangement. Streets still braid and unbraid, but they do so with a kind of courtesy. A plaza that begins to enclose itself will hesitate, then leave an arch open, as if allowing a breeze to pass through a conversation. Buildings that lean into one another will sometimes straighten, not from correction but from relief, as if the city has decided to exhale.

The inhabitants feel this as a hush behind their hands. Work becomes not only easier but kinder. A chisel that slips does not gouge; it reveals a vein of mineral that glints like laughter. A repair that once had to be redone three times holds on the first attempt, and the extra time is spent sitting on a low step, watching fronds comb the bright air. The city, sensing that it is being held without being gripped, begins to offer small gifts: a hollow that hums when touched, a stone that stays cool in the brightest glare, a seam that opens onto a view of the distant rim that no one knew existed.

People start using these gifts. A glider maps the new view from above and sings it down to the town. A burrower brings up a shape that fits the hollow, and when it does, the hum deepens and spreads through the flagstones, a sound like contentment. The city, which has always been unsettled, begins to understand that it can be unsettled without being anxious. It learns that it is allowed to keep some mysteries, and that it does not have to finish explaining itself to be loved.

The power also attends to the edges.

It is easy to love the center—the towers, the plazas, the busy seams—but the margins are where a place decides whether it wants to keep breathing. There, where the basin meets the higher ground, the power nudges the land into a slower cadence. Slopes that used to slump in sudden slips begin to settle with deliberation. Channels that used to tear open after a night of condensation now guide water with the precision of a quiet word. The burrowers find that their tunnels hold their shape longer, and that the minerals they bring up are brighter, as if the earth at the edges has begun to dream in color.

This has a quiet effect on the people. They always knew that the margins were where the city argued with the world, but now the argument feels less like a fight and more like a negotiation between friends who trust each other. The runners who travel between settlements find the routes more predictable, not in a rigid way but in the way a song becomes predictable after you have learned its heart. They stop counting their steps and start naming the rills. They trade small tokens at the margins—lenses, strips of woven fiber, smooth stones that have crossed the basin in their pouches—and the exchanges feel less like commerce and more like the passing of breath.

Over time, the power’s presence becomes part of the city’s grammar. It is not visible, but it is audible in the way a wall settles without complaint, in the way a sudden bright gust feels like encouragement, in the way a street that might have vanished decides, for a season, to stay. The inhabitants do not pray to it, because they do not know its name. But they begin to leave small spaces in their plans—empty corners, unclaimed ledges, alcoves that are just large enough for a moment of rest. These spaces are not shrines. They are acknowledgments. They are the city’s way of saying, I notice that I am being held.

And the holding is real. On days when the turbulence threatens to rise into panic—when the light thickens too much and the city feels it might dissolve into glare—the power leans in with a steadiness that is not force but presence. It does not stop the brightening, but it gives the city an anchor in small, sure things: the weight of a stone in a hand, the cool of a damp seam against a cheek, the sound of a friend’s hooves on a familiar step. The city rides the wave, and it does not break.

The inhabitants come to recognize these days by the way their fronds stand still, as if listening to something behind the light. They speak of them later, not as miracles but as good weather. They adjust their plans, leave repairs half-finished when the city asks, and trust that tomorrow will offer a gentle seam to pick up the work.

In all of this, the power asks for nothing. It does not seek worship, loyalty, or even recognition. Its joy is in the ongoingness—the persistence of a place that refuses to be finished, and of people who refuse to stop proposing ways to live within it. It watches them build, unbuild, build again. It listens to the city breathe, and it softens the breath so the city does not tire of its own lungs.

If there is a shape to the power’s love, it is the shape of a held breath released as a hum, of a stone that remembers how to be cool, of a path that stays open long enough for someone to find it, use it, and leave it for the next person. It is the shape of six fingers on a chisel, of fronds combing the bright air, of a basin that has forgotten how to be finished and, because of that, has learned how to be home.

And if one day the city does finish—if the slow patience of stone ever overcomes the bright turbulence and the last seam settles into stillness—the power will not mourn. It will simply move its attention to the next open wound, the next unsettled basin, the next place that is learning to breathe between the lid of the sky and the ancient, imperfect earth.

Until then, it listens. It holds. It softens the light so that seeing does not burn, and it lets the water remember, in small ways, how to return.

And the city, unaware of the gift, goes on being unfinished—luminous, damp, cool, and alive—while the power, unseen, finds its own shape in the spaces where the city says, again and again, I am still here.
