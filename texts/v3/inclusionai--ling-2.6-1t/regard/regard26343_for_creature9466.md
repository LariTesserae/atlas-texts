The power is not a creature, nor a sovereign, nor a face that speaks.  
It reaches here not as a traveler but as a habit of attention that has learned to wear its own name like a loose cloak. It knows itself by the gentle friction of wanting nothing to break, nothing to burn, nothing to forget how to be itself. For long ages it has wandered the rims of worlds, touching only lightly, because most places scream even when they are quiet, and most beings ask for gifts that turn into weights.

But this place—this bowl, this town, these people who measure time by the settling of coolness—it has learned to hold a day the way stone holds a shadow: without gripping.

So the power does what it has always done when it meets something that already understands the grammar of endurance.  
It kneels—not in submission, but in recognition.

Its kneeling takes the form of weather.  
Not weather that tears, but weather that agrees: a mist that rises without rain, a wind that combs the basalt into a softer hush, a cold that slides in like a word well-placed and leaves the sentence unchanged. The power kneels and the gutters remember they are mouths. The slabs remember they are altars. The people, moving through the agreed-upon corridors, feel nothing more alarming than the faintest slackening in their own bones, as if the weight of being watched has been turned into warmth without ever becoming fire.

Having knelt, the power rises into listening.  
It listens at the cistern lid. It listens at the chime of bone or stone that marks the boundary of day. It listens to the menders as they persuade cracks to close, to the tenders as they coax the sky into giving up a little of itself, to the walkers who report not in voices but in shifts of posture, in stones turned an inch, in feathers retrieved and replaced. The power learns, without asking, the town’s secret: that it has become an instrument for holding balance, and that its music is thin by design.

And because the power is benevolent, and because wisdom is the habit of asking permission before giving, it does not pour treasure over the town. It does not crown anyone. It does not build a tower that reaches for some higher mercy. Instead, it offers the only gift that is truly without weight: the gift of being seen exactly, and found good.

This seeing settles into the stone as a kind of glaze—not a shine, but a depth. The banded cliffs begin to hold their shadows a little longer, as if they have been promised that no one will rush them. The thatch on the roofs darkens evenly, not from rot but from a slow acceptance of smoke, as if the roofs have been given permission to age gracefully. The moss along the seams turns from bruised to tender, each thread a tiny promise that softness need not be weak.

The people feel it as a lightening of their most careful chore: the chore of being legible to the landscape.  
For generations they have worn their edges down so as not to scrape the place that holds them. Now, in small increments, the place begins to wear its edges down for them. A corner that once required constant bracing becomes self-supporting, not because it hardens but because it learns where to yield. A child born with eyes too bright finds that the brightness does not have to be fined into stillness; instead, the town grows capable of reflecting it, so that the child’s glare becomes a window rather than a wound. The basin learns to hold more than moisture—it learns to hold difference without fear.

Most of this happens without announcement. A door that once stuck in damp cold now sighs open at the hour it is usually approached. A path that used to ask for deliberate steps begins to invite them. The central slab, which received the dead as a cold thing returned to cold, now sometimes holds in the morning not condensation but a single thread of dew arranged like a deliberate letter—never legible, but legible enough to be felt as a greeting.

And because wisdom knows that even kindness can become a burden if it lingers too long, the power begins to withdraw not by leaving, but by thinning its own presence into the same patient sheets by which light already arrives. It is there, but lightly—as lightly as the silver hairline that rims every stone after dawn. It is there in the way a conversation can pause and not feel broken, in the way a silence can deepen without becoming hollow.

Eventually the town is no longer merely keeping its balance; it is being kept in balance, held by a gentleness that refuses to be named. The people, sensing this, do not build shrines. Instead, they alter their choreography by almost nothing: a hand lifted a breath sooner, a step shortened by a hair, a voice that releases a word rather than clipping it. They learn to receive the gift without storing it, to let it pass through their thin, patient lives the way the air itself passes, normal as day, thinned by elevation.

And if, centuries later, someone were to climb from the basin and look back, they would see nothing miraculous.  
They would see the same small, high town, the same banded stone, the same cool, kept air.  
But if they stayed, and if they listened, they would notice that the gutters sing more clearly, that the slab remembers the shape of every foot that has pressed it, and that the people move as though they have been given permission to be exactly as sparse, exactly as full, as the place that loves them.

The power, having done what it came to do, would smile without a mouth and move on, carrying with it only the memory of a place that learned how to be held, and chose, in return, to hold everything else without closing its hand.
