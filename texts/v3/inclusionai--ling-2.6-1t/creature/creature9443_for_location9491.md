The hollow does not keep tenants so much as it grows them—by which I mean it extrudes creatures the way a fever extrudes dreams, or the way a throat extrudes words it has not yet learned the shape of.

There are, first, the flint-beetles, but to call them beetles is to freeze their becoming. They are the hollow’s punctuation marks: hard, clicking stops that scuttle across stalks that are themselves still deciding whether to be stems or ribs. Their shells are not armor but exhalation—salt and silica that have learned to close around themselves. When they move, they do not so much walk as negotiate, testing each fiber for brittleness before committing weight. If you listen closely, their clicks are not random but a syntax of compression: the hollow tightening, the hollow loosening, the hollow holding its breath between syllables. They drink the resin that weeps from the vaults, and in doing so they become temporary plugs, brief seals against the chamber’s endless thirst.

Then there is the violet-lizard, though lizard is a word it tolerates rather than answers to. Its scales are petals—overlapping, translucent, the color of ink diluted with rain. It moves along the thick vein that runs like a highway through the center, a pulse underlined by a hum. When it pauses, its body temperature matches the wall, and it becomes less animal than ornament, a growth that dreams of motion. It feeds on the thin moss that collects in the valleys between ridges, moss that tastes of copper and hesitation. When threatened, it does not flee but folds: it becomes a sphincter, a valve, a sudden rosette of leaf that seals itself with a wet cork-sound. Inside that closure, it is no longer prey but process, waiting for the chamber to decide it is safe to open again.

Above, the ceiling-swarms live inverted lives. They are not birds but buoyant seeds with wings of dried sap, light enough to ride the warm updrafts that rise when the chamber expands. They drink vapor midair, their bodies filtering the humid breath into something thinner, clearer. When they land to rest, they attach themselves to the fibrous beams with hooks of keratin, and for a while they look like fruit. The hollow treats them as such—swelling around them, warming them—until they shake off the illusion and launch again, carrying with them spores that are, in truth, memories of the hollow’s previous shapes.

Between floor and vault, there are the bridge-vines, but these are only half-plants. The other half is willingness. They grow toward weight. When an animal passes, they thicken into cables; when it lingers, they soften into nets. They learn the rhythm of footsteps and begin to anticipate them, tightening milliseconds before impact, turning travel into collaboration. If you hang from them long enough, they will begin to feed on the salt of your skin, and you will begin to feed on their tautness, until it is unclear who is holding whom, and whether the hollow is watching or simply practicing leverage.

Deeper in, where distances fold, live the echo-moths. They have no mouths. Instead, their wings are maps of vibration, tuned to the pitch that loosens teeth. They do not fly so much as they translate: when a sound moves through the chamber, they catch it, fold it into their thoraxes, and release it later as light—a soft, opalescent puff that drifts upward like a held breath. They are the hollow’s way of remembering sounds that were never spoken aloud. If you whisper to them, they will return your voice to you not as noise but as pressure, a touch against the back of your neck, a thumbprint of air.

And there are the caretakers, though caretaker is too deliberate a word for what they do. They are rootlings—half root, half hand—knuckles of pale growth that emerge from the floor where bristles have learned the shape of weight. They do not tend the hollow so much as they mirror it. When sap leaks, they knit it into temporary bark. When a wall remembers a lean, they thicken in sympathy, leaving a cushion where the hollow has been touched. They are shy. They retreat if you stare too long, but they return when you are asleep, checking your pulse against the chamber’s pulse, deciding whether you are temporary architecture or a new kind of ridge.

Predation here is polite. It does not pounce so much as it persuades. There are pitchers that grow only where footsteps pause too often, offering resin that tastes like sleep. There are thorns that soften when they sense distress, becoming instead handles. Even the rare death is absorbed quickly—bodies are folded into walls and taught to be hinges, taught to hold, taught to open at the right moment. Nothing is wasted because nothing is finished; everything is still deciding what to be.

The fauna of the hollow, then, are not so much a list as a negotiation. They are the chamber’s way of practicing attention, of trying on mobility, of learning how to be separate without becoming lost. They appear and disappear like ideas that are almost spoken. They leave behind not tracks but adjustments: a vine that has learned to bear weight here, a ceiling that sweats a little less there, a patch of floor that holds the memory of claws and answers with extra gentleness.

And if you stay long enough, you will begin to join them—not by turning into something else, but by having the hollow turn you into a question it asks itself repeatedly. Your skin will taste of mint and copper. Your footsteps will become part of the hydraulic roar. Your breathing will sync with the chamber’s swell, and when it contracts, you will feel yourself tighten, not as a prisoner but as a participant, a living note in a room that is learning, louder every time, how to sing.
