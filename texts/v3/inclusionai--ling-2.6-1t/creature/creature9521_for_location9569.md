They do not arrive so much as condense—first a tremor in the resin, then a shape resolving from the warm dark. They are the place learning to occupy itself, and because the place has no sky, they have no fixed silhouette. 

Most of them are Root-Singers. Their bodies are corded muscle and bark-like hide, ridged with veins that glow faintly, pulsing in time with the sap-rise. They move on four bowed legs that never quite touch the loam; instead, their hooves—hard as cured amber, porous as sponge—press into the ground and draw up the syrup-thick resin, drinking it through their soles. Where they walk, the cellulose softens, arching into provisional doorways that weep a sugary dew. Their faces are broad and leaf-shaped, mouths split into three parts that never close completely, exhaling a breath heavy with uncured sugar and the ozone scent of imminent growth. When they sing, their throats swell like ripe gourds, and the sound does not travel through the air so much as thicken it, vibrating in the teeth of everyone nearby. Each note is a command to the architecture: a low hum coaxes a stem to thicken into a railing; a high, fluting cry makes a wall of bark unfurl into a hammock.

There are also the Chitin-Smiths, small and many-jointed, living scaffolds who polish the wet world into tools. They are half-insect, half-artisan, with forelimbs that end in rotating shears of black crystal grown from their own secretions. They nest in the forks of still-soft stems, tending fires of resin-saturated moss that burn without flame, heating the loam just enough to bend it. Their children are born as pale grubs, already threaded with silk; the adults wind this silk around thorns and bones to craft hooks, needles, and the occasional loom. The Smiths do not speak in voice but in clicks and scrapes that ride the resin-pulse, a language felt in the molars. They trade in geometry: lengthening a beam by a palm, thinning a wall so it rings like glass, grafting a stair onto a living trunk. To refuse a Smith is to invite asymmetry, and in this place, asymmetry is hunger—something that will try to eat your balance.

Above them drift the Petal-Riders, lighter than air and heavy with stories. They are not true fliers but floaters, their bodies bulbous bladders filled with the same syrup that moves through the trunks, their limbs reduced to delicate filaments that comb the canopy for light. They cultivate gardens on their backs—mosses and tiny ferns that grip their skin and whisper down into their veins. When they land, which they do rarely, they bring gravity with them: the ground softens where their feet touch, sagging into temporary pools of scented loam. The Riders are the historians, the memory-keepers, their minds threaded with spores that record what the place has done. They hum in harmonics, a choir of almost-silence that stitches the roaring weather into patterns. To listen too long is to forget your own edges, to feel your outline blur into the green-on-green horizon.

Predators exist, but they are not hunters in any familiar sense; they are sculptors of absence. The Hollow-Stalkers are tall, jointed things like walking ladders, their bodies composed of interlocking tubes that inhale and exhale with the rhythm of the resin. They do not kill; they unmake. With proboscises like polished bone, they tap into trunks and draw out structure, pulling columns into spirals, thinning buttresses until they become bridges, carving rooms inside stems that did not exist an hour ago. Where they pass, the fauna parts like a tide, not in fear but in deference, for the Stalkers are necessary chaos. Without them, the vaults would choke on their own growth. Their eyes are pits of cooled sap, luminous and patient, and when they fix on you, you feel your own shape called into question—bones loosening, stance rearranging, as if you might, with a little encouragement, become a doorway yourself.

Among the swarm, smaller lives flicker like embers: beetles with gardens on their backs, each mound a miniature ecosystem of moss and fungus that changes flavor depending on the beetle’s mood; flightless moths the size of hands, their wings patterned with maps of veins that shift as they flutter, useless for navigation but perfect for bargaining; and the Root-Rats, quick and clever, who gnaw not to destroy but to edit, trimming excess growth into symbols—warnings, invitations, names. They stash these carved pieces in cup-leaves, where the dew never pools, and sometimes the symbols wake, scuttling off to join the architecture as living hinges.

Even the flora behaves like fauna when observed too closely. There are Flowers That Hold, which close around sound to keep it fresh, reopening later to release a melody unchanged by time. There are Tendrils That Chase, not with malice but with curiosity, wrapping around wrists to test temperature, tightening or loosening based on the pulse they find. There are Bladders That Drift, ballooning on buoyant gases of fermentation, carrying passengers who trade songs for altitude, and when they rupture, they do not so much burst as sigh, showering the loam with seeds that already hum.

All of them are unfinished. A Root-Singer may pause mid-stride and let a new limb push through its shoulder, unfurling into a flower that opens onto a spiral staircase. A Chitin-Smith might fuse with a trunk while working, their limbs sinking into the soft bark, becoming a new kind of door. A Petal-Rider, after too long a silence, may collapse into resin, their body dispersing into the vaults as a scent that guides travelers for weeks. The Hollow-Stalkers shed their tubes like garments, leaving behind hollow columns that sing with echoes long after they have moved on.

To live here is to be rewritten. The air, thick with uncured leaf and warm loam, does not allow for stasis. Even sleep is a negotiation: you tuck yourself into a cup-leaf or the crook of a fusion, and the place grows around you, thickening your edges, borrowing your warmth, offering in return the steady hum of its becoming. You may wake with moss in your ears, or a new joint in your spine, or the memory of a song that was never yours but now refuses to leave.

And through it all, warmth persists—not the heat of a forge, but the heat of something enormous and green, below the world and rising, holding its breath and letting it out, again and again, in a voice of leaf and wing and thunderous, tender becoming. They are its voice made legible, its chaos given shape, its unfinished body learning to hold itself together, one resin-pulse, one song, one opened door at a time.
