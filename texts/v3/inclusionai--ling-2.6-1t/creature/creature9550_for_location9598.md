They live in gradients, the inhabitants here, arranged less by species than by temperature and patience.

The grazers come first, though saying so implies order where there is only precedence. They are broad at the shoulder and narrow at the hip, built as if the mountain had kneaded clay around a core of slow pulse. Their coats hold water like memory, darkening when the clouds pass, steaming faintly when the sun returns. They drink by pressing their muzzles into the seeps until the mineral tang is thick on their tongues, then lift their heads to let it settle in their multiple stomachs like coins in a well. They move as a single creature that happens to have many bodies, following the edges of moisture the way a shadow follows a shape. When they stop, they become part of the geology, dark humps breathing among boulders, their horns whitened by licks of evaporated spray.

Above them, the predators are less numerous but no less present. They are patient animals—not lazy, but careful with momentum. Their fur is patterned in ways that confuse the eye about scale, so that from a distance they appear to be lichened rocks, and up close they are simply sleek. They do not chase so much as arrive, appearing in places the grazers have just decided to leave. Their claws click on stone without making sound, their teeth are short and strong for cracking shells as much as bone, because here meat wears armor of mineral and time. They eat slowly, leaving remnants that steam briefly before cooling into landscape.

In the canopy, the birds are something like glass given voice. Their feathers are dark with refraction, shot through with veins that glint like oil on water. They move in circuits rather than flights, threading the same routes until the air learns their weight and parts for them. Their song is thin and repeating, a syntax of warning and territory that never quite resolves into music. They nest in thickets that smell of resin and rust, laying eggs the color of wet iron, mottled as if they had been left in the rain.

Lower still, the insects are almost mechanical in their precision. Beetles with carapaces like burnished copper traverse the understory in lines, pausing only to tap antennae against warm seams in the stone. Their rhythm matches the pumps that sigh in the distance, a syncopation learned or inherited, and in the hush between beats you can hear their wings as a dry sifting, like sand moving through glass. The flies are iridescent and metallic, hovering in clouds above seepages where the water is almost still, their larvae thriving in the soup of minerals and half-rotted leaves.

The amphibians are the keepers of thresholds. They live where water hesitates, in the bogs that remember oceans and in the folds of terraces where the ground is more root than stone. Their skin is slick with a film that tastes of ozone and deep earth, and their eyes are set high on their heads so they can watch the sky and the ground at once. At dusk they begin their relay, each voice emerging from a pocket of warmth, a sound like wet leather being drawn slowly across stone. When rain comes, they swell with purpose, moving as if summoned by a machinery older than nerves.

In the water itself, things glide that have agreed to look like fish while being something else entirely. Their bodies are long and seamless, their fins more like adjustments than limbs. They do not dart but negotiate, turning in water that is warm and thick with silt, their wakes lingering like braids. They surface rarely, often at twilight, to breathe air that tastes of metal, then slip below to where the pipes leak heat into the pools. Their eggs are laid in clusters near the warmest vents, wrapped in filaments that glow faintly when the light is low.

Even the plants behave as inhabitants rather than backdrop. There are vines that seem to learn the shape of handrails, wrapping around iron as readily as branch. There are ferns that uncurl toward the hiss of valves, their fronds darkening where steam settles. Flowers open on schedules aligned to pressure changes, their colors deepening when the machinery pulses. Mosses creep up the legs of the iron towers, softening the angles, claiming the structures as slowly as the structures claim the rock.

And there are the keepers.

They are the ones who are neither wild nor mechanical, though they blur into both. They move among the terraces with a gait that matches the sway of the long grasses, their bodies wrapped in fabrics that have absorbed the colors of rust and moss. Their hands know the pipes as if they were vines—where to pinch, where to let flow, where to soothe a tremor in the metal. They harvest the algae that thickens in the warm pools, and they gather the eggs that gleam like wet stone, and they prune the growths that threaten the delicate balance of heat and water.

They speak rarely, and in voices that seem to originate lower in the chest than usual, as if the air here is too dense for sound to rise. Their eyes are pale, adapted to the soft haze that never fully lifts, and their skin carries the sheen of perpetual damp. They know the habits of the grazers, the routes of the birds, the moods of the pumps. They repair what has worn, redirect what has overflowed, and leave offerings at the vents—simple things, stones arranged in circles, flowers placed where they will be warmed into fragrance.

They are not rulers but participants. They sleep in structures that lean against the rock as if weary, and they wake before the light to listen for changes in the hum of the valley. When storms arrive, they stand where the rain hits hardest, letting it sheet down their faces, as if accepting a blessing from the place itself.

Life here is not a struggle against the elements but a conversation with them. The grazers, the predators, the birds, the insects, the swimmers, the tenders—all of them abide by a rhythm set by water and heat and iron. They live in a world that is lavish and exacting, where abundance requires attention, and where strangeness is simply the slow revelation of rules that operate on a scale larger than years.

And if you stay long enough, you begin to feel it: that you, too, have started to match the pace of the valley, your breath falling into time with the pumps, your eyes learning to see in the haze, your hands remembering how to touch metal without flinching at its warmth. You realize, without surprise, that you have begun to belong—not by conquest or adaptation, but by becoming another inhabitant in a place that already knows how to hold you.
