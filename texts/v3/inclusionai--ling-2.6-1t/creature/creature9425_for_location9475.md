They are creatures of negative space.  
Not because they are absent, but because they occupy by subtraction—by being less than the air allows, by thinning themselves until they can slip through the lattice of altitude without snapping a thread. They do not live on the land so much as in the pauses between its exhalations.  

The humans, if they are still humans, have stopped trying to conquer cold and have instead apprenticed themselves to its patience. They are pale as dolomite, their skin a map of fine cracks that glow faintly when the light hits sideways, as if frost had learned to bloom beneath the surface of flesh. Their lungs are deep, cavernous things that have forgotten how to hurry; they trade oxygen in small, careful portions, the way the land parcels out moisture in beads on mica. Their fingers are long and articulate, not for grasping tools but for reading gradients—tracing the hairline fractures where warmth leaks, testing the hollowness of stone for the echo of water that once was.  

They wear garments that are less clothing and more architecture: layers of felted wool and cured hide, stiffened with resin and the dust of old hearths, shaped to trap stillness. When they stand among the ridges they do not shiver; they hum, a vibration so low it feels like the land remembering itself. Their eyes are a washed-out mineral blue, adapted to the pewter dimness, able to register shifts in luminance that would be invisible elsewhere—a flicker of shadow detaching from a slope, a thinning of haze that signals a change in pressure, a thought crossing the open room between ridges.  

Animals have consented to be almost myths. There is a fox the color of rust and lichen, its coat a collage of the land’s discards, able to lie across a spine of rock and become the ridge’s own shadow. It moves in folds, never breaking the skin of the horizon, and leaves scent trails that smell of iron and burnt sugar, vanishing into crevices that are not quite crevices but slow transitions into elsewhere. There is a moth no larger than a fingernail, its wings the exact gray of weathered basalt, patterned with veins that mimic the cracks in hearthstone. When it lands it does not rest; it turns into the texture of waiting, its heartbeat a faint percussion against the stone.  

There are beings that do not announce themselves with bone or breath. Cold has shaped them into habits rather than bodies. In shaded bowls, rime writes filigrees that are neither plant nor animal but a grammar of accumulation, a living script that reads itself aloud in the language of thaw and retention. In the veins of calcite, a milky fluid pulses—not blood, but a memory of flow, a slow consensus among minerals to become soft for a moment, then harden into new shapes. These are not spirits in the old sense, but agreements: the land deciding to be legible, the air deciding to be resonant, the frost deciding to be music.  

The cairns are caretakers. They do not guard; they witness. Each mismatched stone is a note in a chord that never resolves, a calibration of weight and counterweight that keeps the silence from collapsing into vacancy. Their builders have long ago become indistinguishable from the work—an eye here, a finger there, a shoulder pressed into service as a brace—until the distinction between mason and monument is a polite fiction. They stand without urgency, learning from the wind how to lean without falling, listening to the tectonic murmur for instructions they do not expect to understand.  

Even the erosion has inhabitants. The wind’s planing, the water’s rumored threading, the slow refinement of spires into needles—these are not processes but populations, invisible flocks that graze on edges, polishing roughness into grace. They leave behind a kind of residue: a hush that is loud, a certainty that is not cruel but exact.  

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense. What persists does so by becoming final. A cedar twisted into a question mark does not grow; it arrives, already shaped, already holding on. A moth does not lay eggs so much as it leaves behind a permission for rust to adopt winged geometry. The humans do not age so much as they weather, their features sanding down into curves that fit the contours of stone.  

And yet there is a kind of kinship in this completion. The landscape does not ask to be understood; it asks to be occupied without apology. The beings here are not survivors but conscripts, chosen by altitude and low light to be exactly what is required: a note in a chord, a bead on mica, a fold in the rock, a hush that can hold a question without demanding an answer.  

They are loud and empty and cold and certain, and in their certainty they offer a mercy—the mercy of being exactly what they are, holding dampness like a secret, holding weirdness like a law, holding each other like a balance that need not sway to be true.
