They live in the seam, which is to say they live in the negotiation.

There are the Moss-Knitters, small and many-armed as loom-shuttles, who dwell where riveted iron meets basalt. They are not human, but they are not animal, either; they are closer to a decision made by damp. By day they look like nothing more than lichen arranged with too much intent, but by night they unlimber and begin their work, braiding copper-green filaments with threads of oxidized iron and the shed skin of ferns. Their purpose is to teach metal how to be moss, or moss how to be memory. They do not speak, but they thrum, a vibration that settles in your teeth, convincing the tramway that it is, slowly and inevitably, becoming root.

Above, in the canopy-bridges, reside the Drapers. They are long-limbed and quiet, wrapped always in fabrics that are neither cloth nor leaf but a third thing grown from the desire for softness in a sharp world. They move only when the light shifts, so it is hard to tell whether they are changing position or whether the forest is adjusting around them. Some have faces like folded maps; others have no faces at all, only arrangements of shadow that suggest attention. Their work is to keep the dew from falling all at once, to parcel it out in droplets that weigh exactly as much as a regret. They are the reason the air feels measured, why each breath arrives as a portion rather than a flood.

In the water-pauses, the pond-holders dwell. They are low, broad beings, almost geological, with skins the color of sediment and eyes like the oily iridescence of minerals on still water. They do not swim; they persuade. When a pond forms where none was yesterday, it is because a pond-holder has settled there and asked the hollow to be fuller. They trade reflections, and if you look too long into their surfaces, you will see not your own face but a version of the world that is slightly closer to what it intends to become—cloudless, possible, almost true. They are patient teachers, and their lessons last for years, during which the pond forgets how to empty itself.

The riveters are not builders but un-builders, small and quick, with jaws that understand rivets the way jewelers understand pearls. They live in the seams of the loading platforms and the undersides of staircases made for things that never climb. Their work is to loosen, gently, what was fastened with certainty. They do not destroy; they release. Under their careful attention, bolts sigh and surrender, and the structures they touch begin to settle into the ground as if they had always meant to lie down. They hum while they work, and their humming is what the pipes listen to when they grow warm for no reason.

There are also the Smokers, though there are fewer of them now. They are thin and vertical, accustomed to exhalation, and they gather in the crowns of the old smokestacks or perch on power lines like beads on a string. They do not eat; they metabolize leftovers—ghosts of heat, residues of steam, the after-taste of industry. When they exhale, the air holds its shape for a moment longer than it should, forming maps that do not correspond to anything you can walk. You can tell they are near when condensation on glass arranges itself into roads that lead nowhere you remember.

And there are the Watchers, who are almost not there at all. They live in the difference between how a thing looks and how it feels. One is the color of wet slate and can hold its breath for entire afternoons. Another is armored like a beetle and knows the exact weight of a rail flange. They do not act so much as witness, and their witnessing changes what is witnessed. A rivet loosens sooner in their presence; a vine tightens more quickly. They are the reason silence is textured, why the quiet clinks and hums and rustles with events that are barely events.

All of them share the high bowl, and all of them are learning its shape along with it. They do not own the place, and they do not serve it. They live in it the way moss lives on a stone: by making a surface softer, by turning edges into negotiations, by insisting that growth can be a form of listening. In their company, the industrial ghosts feel less like endings and more like pauses, and the lushness feels less like crowding and more like a conversation held at a respectful volume.

They are what lives where water visits and light pronounces, where time moves in a slow gradient and the earth is still taut with becoming. If you walk among them long enough, you will find yourself breathing in their rhythm, matching their volume, discovering in your own chest a willingness to be folded into the grammar of the high, cool earth.
