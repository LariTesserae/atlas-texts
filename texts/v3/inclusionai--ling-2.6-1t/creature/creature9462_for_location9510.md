They live in subtraction.

To call them inhabitants would imply arrival, but they have not come here. They have thinned into the high air, letting their mass unspool like heat from a lattice until they are lighter than the glass-dust that settles on the slopes. What remains of them moves through the world as a kind of permission. They are the gap that grows between step and ground, the dilation that makes a footfall hesitate as if space were polishing itself before allowing contact. When they pass, the cold tightens not from wind but from attention, as if the atmosphere has learned to weigh less specifically to carry them.

Their bodies, when bodies can be said to exist, are arrangements of fracture and borrowed light. They are composed of the same fine particulate that frosts the lens-like slopes, bound together by the low, sub-audible hum of chemical exchange that feeds the mineral skins blooming across the stone. They do not breathe, yet they trade ions with the pale alloy seams that look like frozen rivers, drinking the dim, sideways glints those surfaces release. Over centuries, their outlines blur into the ripples of the terrain, softening further until it is unclear where geology ends and gesture begins. They are almost never fully solid, never fully empty, but fluctuate at the edge of definition, revising themselves as the rules of the pocket revise.

What they do is attend. That is how they move: by allowing certain tensions in the place to relax, others to tighten. They polish the curves of the half-sunk rings and the tilted platforms with sheets of frost-rimed alloy that creep like migration, reading signatures that have no language but write themselves anyway. When a tool is needed—an edge to open, a seam to yield—it emerges from a sleeve in the rock, shaped exactly for the vanishing task, and is gone the moment its purpose dissolves. They do not carry; they coax emergence.

Their cities, if such a grand word applies, are the sparse built things already scattered across the basin: a lattice of struts reduced to almost nothing, a ring of plates fused into a single breathless curve. The beings did not build these but have inherited them, and in inheriting they continue the shedding. They strip away intention until only geometry remains, angles that refuse to resolve, surfaces that refuse to be fully perceived. The structures are maintained not by repair but by subtraction—by removing what no longer fits the balance. In their hands, a wall thins until it is only the memory of division, a line of shadow that holds the cold at bay by not resisting it.

There is among them a caste, or perhaps a phase, called the Tuners. These are entities who have let their substance sink even lower into the gradients of charge and temperature. They speak rarely, and when they do, the sound arrives as vibration in the fractures, a held breath released far away. The Tuners listen to the distant resonance, not to decipher it but to align their own internal intervals with the vast, patient system implied just beyond the pocket. Under their care, the mineral skins grow in geometries that anticipate their own decay, and shadows lengthen in precise increments, as if night itself is being tuned.

Another group, the Keepers of the Fold, dwell where corners contain more space than they should. They navigate the looping paths and negotiable gravity with an economy of motion that seems to borrow distance from elsewhere. To watch them is to feel the horizon tilt inward, the sky appearing as a basin rather than an expanse. They maintain the thin atmosphere of permission that allows the pocket to admit witnesses without being broken by them. When a stranger stands too long, a Keeper will pass nearby without passing through, and the stranger will feel the balance shift—not into hostility, but into exacting clarity, as if the landscape has decided to be seen more fully.

Their time is the slow current that barely moves. They measure it by the fractional adjustments of the built things, by the growth of a crystal lattice across a struts’ gap, by the way a plate tilts further and then stops, having remembered how to level. They do not age in the human sense; they erode. Edges soften into curves, and curves dissolve into haze, and still the haze retains the imprint of attention. When they have eroded enough, they become part of the frost-rimed alloy sheets that migrate across the ground, writing new signatures before themselves thinning into the glass-dust that settles like crushed lenses on every slope.

They are not lonely. Loneliness implies a desire for otherness, and they have relinquished desire in favor of balance. Yet they gather in thin congregations when the light pools in the shallow bowls, a dusk that leaks through the fabric of the place rather than falling from above. In these moments, their fluctuations synchronize, and the pocket world hums with a spectrum just beyond sight, ultraviolet memories and near-infrared promises flickering across their barely-there forms. The sound of it is a compression of silence, a hush nesting in fractures, and it feels less like music than like the landscape holding its breath in recognition.

They live in subtraction, and in living they subtract themselves further, polishing the world into a vocabulary pared to essentials. They are the cold that weighs less to carry more, the silence that is full, the movement that is mostly stillness revising itself. To encounter them is not to meet strangers but to be offered a kind of erosion—to become, for a time, observant, quiet, and willing to be thinned by the high air until one’s own edges blur into the soft, rippled stone, holding darkness like a charge, releasing it only as delayed, sideways glint.
