What lives here is less a collection of creatures than a set of agreements between presence and persistence.  

There are, first, the Residents of Patina—slow, soft-bodied colonies that are not organisms so much as events of oxidation. They live on the vertical faces, forming veils the color of rusted wine, drinking the warmth that leaks from pipe-seams and exhaling thin, chalky breaths of salt and sulfur. Because the gradients are kept shallow, they never hurry. A century to them is a single season of thickening. They mark time by changing opacity, becoming darker or lighter depending on how much iron the air surrenders. If you press a palm to their crust, they yield like felt, and in the morning the shape remains, a ghost preserved in mineral ink until the next thermal cycle dissolves it. They are the archivists of forgetting.  

Above them, at the level where conduits braid and re-braid, live the Thread-Cities. These are not built by hands. They are grown from filamentous fungi that have learned to conduct, feeding on stray voltage and condensation alike. Their hyphae wrap insulation, bridge gaps, and, in doing so, make new circuits the original engineers never planned. When humidity spikes, they glow faintly, veins of pale gold threading through black braid-work. They hum—only barely—their pitch rising as heat rises, harmonizing with the place’s inner speech. They are both infrastructure and interference, and the maintenance drones tolerate them because, more often than not, a Thread-City completes a broken path rather than breaking one that works.  

Closer to the floor, in the ankle-level warmth, are the Glass-Shells. These are not beetles so much as mirrors with habits. Their carapaces are chips of the vitrified slag that have, over decades of rolling in thermal eddies, learned to orient themselves so that their reflective faces always angle toward the dimmest light source. They graze on oils and metal dust, digesting them into thin metallic skins they shed in brittle crescents. When a worker passes, the Glass-Shells scatter in a clatter that sounds like rain on sheet metal, then return once the footfalls settle. They have no eyes, but they remember patterns of vibration; they know which corridors are safe by the way the floor thrums beneath them.  

Every so often, a Keeper of Leaks appears—a creature something like a large, folded envelope made of cured rubber and alloy, filled with something that is neither liquid nor gas but a slurry of phase-change memory. It drifts along the ceiling canopy, pressing its edges into joint-seals, expanding where expansion is needed, contracting where tightness must be absolute. It does not speak, but when it passes, the room’s hum deepens by a half-step, as if a chord has been completed. It repairs not by patching but by becoming, for a time, the flaw it fills, then releasing itself as a dry husk that crumbles into talc and settles into floor basins.  

There are, also, the Echo-Blades. These are not alive in any sense that would comfort a biologist; rather, they are habits of sharpness that linger where metal has been cut again and again in the same place. Over years, the grooves remember the angle, the pressure, the cadence of the tool, and they reproduce these conditions in miniature whenever the wind or vibration is right. They manifest as sudden cold lines in the air, razor-thin and smelling of ozone, capable of scoring insulation or slicing a careless finger. The workers leave small offerings of wax or grease at intersections favored by Echo-Blades, not to appease but to blunt. It is a negotiation: sharpness in exchange for continuity.  

In the sealed biopods, the Pale Filaments persist. They are memory made vegetal, engineered to remind the vault of green without ever becoming wild. Their pulses slow when the heat is high, quicken when dampness rises, as though dreaming in a language of humidity and temperature. Occasionally, a filament will send out a thread that bridges its pod to a nearby pipe. If this happens, the pipe warms slightly where it is touched, and the filament darkens. No one cuts the bridge unless it threatens function; it is too lovely a surrender, too rare an instance of the manufactured allowing itself to be colonized by the organic.  

Maintenance drones, meanwhile, are the most sociable of inhabitants. They congregate at magnetic seams in patterns that look like prayer or gossip. Their communication is light: brief flashes from photovoltaic eyes, patterns that convey wear, error, need. They do not build nests so much as they construct histories, piling spent casings and stripped wire into cairns at junctions. These cairns are visited by the Glass-Shells, who polish them into abstract sculpture, and by the Thread-Cities, who weave them into their luminous maps. The drones do not seem to mind. They circle, adjust, move on.  

Above all of these, and more fundamental than any of them, is the Presence of Gradient. It is not a creature but a condition—a slow, hot breath that pools, rises, pools again. It shapes the habits of everything else. It teaches the Residents of Patina when to darken and the Echo-Blades when to sleep. It decides which seams weep and which stay dry. It is the governing intelligence of the pocket, and its will is expressed not in command but in curvature: the subtle tilt of a floor, the sag of a conduit, the way a rivulet chooses a path.  

When the pilot lamps dim and the place exhales, even the Gradient loosens its hold, letting the heat sag like a curtain. In that moment, all the lives here soften their edges. Residents of Patina go still. Thread-Cities dim to embers. Glass-Shells settle into perfect, reflective stillness. The Keeper of Leaks retracts, compact as a fist. The Pale Filaments hold their breath.  

And the dark, which is heavy but not empty, becomes a single, dense lung: hot, patient, and waiting to be filled again, not with light, but with the careful, living weight of continuance.
