They are residents of subtraction, of breath held long enough to turn into architecture.  
They did not arrive; they accreted.  

At the scale of the city, they read as districts. Closer, as individuals. Closest, as weather.  

The Glass-Weavers are first mistaken for chandeliers. They hang from cavern ceilings in congregations, spindle-thin limbs braced against stone, drawing threads of resin from the air-thick, bitter-amber sap that condenses on rock and is exhaled by the city itself. Their bodies are jointed glass, not brittle but singing: struck, they give a tone that ranges between a warning and a lullaby. They do not eat in any familiar sense. They collect moisture from the ghost-rime, from the weeping threads that never fall as rain, and from the salt of their own breath. When they die, they do not rot. They fracture, and the shards are gathered by the Burrowers to line their tunnels, so even their endings become infrastructure.  

The Burrowers are the city’s metabolism. They are low, many-limbed things, armored in compressed ash the color of old bruises. Their claws are not for tearing but for polishing; they gnaw the city’s edges to keep them generous, nibbling cliffs into benches, chewing struts into stools. They have no eyes that register light as sight. Instead they feel vibration; they know the city by the way it rings. They are the reason streets stratify: lower layers grow hard under the tap-tap-tap of their passage, upper layers remain soft with their recent industry. They speak in clicks that travel through mineral plate, a language of pressure that tells of depth, hunger, and slow forgiveness.  

Above them, the Sky-Fish ride thermal sighs that rise from deep vents. They are not fish in form but in grammar: bodies made of ribbed membrane stretched over frames of light, as if a wing decided to become a room. They glide between towers, their passage a sound like tearing silk, leaving behind trails of phosphorescent ichor that bead on stone and harden into lanterns. They do not land often. When they do, they drape themselves over fungal trunks to rest, and in their sleep they leak light that the mosses learn to use as a clock.  

The Fungal Towers themselves might be counted among the living, if living includes memory. Pale and ridged like old marble, they grow where corridors widen, marking plazas without meaning to. Their caps hold dim pools of resin; their stalks channel the city’s breath, expanding and contracting with the tide driven by deep rock. They are not rulers but referees, holding space for negotiation. Animals gather beneath them to swap frequencies: beetles drum copper abdomens to claim bandwidth, burrowers answer from below, sky-fish braid their hums through the upper registers. There is no violence in these exchanges, only insistence, the polite noise of neighbors who must share walls.  

Closer to the ground, the Velvet Mosses behave like cartographers. Bruised-plum mats spread where shadow pools, marking places to rest, to argue, to remember. They do not grow over edges; they honor them. They are soft but exact, and when a building shifts its inch in a century, the moss knows and adjusts its border, stitching old geometry to new with threads of root that are more like agreement than growth.  

The Mineral-Vines are less plant than ligament, yes, but they are also negotiators. They knot through grillwork and lintel, turning masonry into living lattice not by conquest but by invitation. They thicken where structures feel strained, thin where they are secure. They conduct the slow sap of the city, a fluid that is not water but almost memory of it, moving in rhythms older than thirst. If you cut them, they bleed light and smell of petrichor without rain. They do not scream; they hum, a sound felt more than heard, like the city remembering a question it has not yet asked.  

And there are the Listeners, small, many-throated things that live in the echoes themselves. They are not always seen, but they are always felt: a sudden hush when something true is said, a brightening of the bruised twilight when a promise is kept. They have no fixed shape. Some say they are the city’s own voice, learning how to speak with many throats. Others say they are the accumulation of every sound the place has ever kept, folded into fur and chitin and glass.  

All of them wear the coolness like a second skin. It sharpens their scents: iron and wet limestone, old spice, ozone, the musk of crowded life. It slows their decay just enough to let complexity accumulate. Change for them is deliberate. A Glass-Weaver may spend a lifetime drawing a single thread taut. A Burrower may polish a single step until its grandchildren walk without sound upon it. A sky-fish may circle the hollow that could hold a dozen valleys and decide, on the hundredth pass, to descend.  

They do not plan, but they do not drift. They negotiate. Their weirdness has worn into etiquette: a drip of resin is greeting; a silence held too long, an invitation; a chorus of copper drums, a neighborhood’s way of saying we are here, we are careful, we are holding this open.  

To see them is to understand that they are not inhabitants of the place so much as its articulation. They are the means by which density and emptiness hold balance. They move through voids that are rooms of their own, turning echo into language, touch into sight, breath into bone.  

They are cool, loud, dim, ancient, and deeply alive.  
They are the final, open form in which life has arranged itself, carefully, without water, without hurry, into something that can last—  
not by resisting time, but by thickening it,  
by making sure every step is remembered,  
every sound refolded into new sound,  
until the city speaks with many throats  
and none of its own,  
and all of theirs.
