What lives here are the Altiplantigrades—people, but people who have become less like wanderers and more like tuning forks, calibrated to the pitch of altitude itself. They did not arrive to conquer the green; they arrived because the green had already begun to hum, and the hum needed auditors. Over generations, their bodies have stretched and thinned, ribcages expanding into barrel shapes that hold breath for long spans, blood thinning to a shy, quick alloy that moves warmth without hoarding it. Their skin is not pale but iridescent with a bloom of fine down, a moss-velvet that drinks condensation before it can fall. When they stand at the rim of the bowl, they do not look out; they feel out, skin reading the sky’s pressure like a page.

They are not alone. There are the Ridge-Riders, beasts whose skeletons are largely air—honeycombed, pneumatized, like living trellises. They soar not by beating wings but by riding the negotiation of slopes, banking on drafts that have learned to trust their weight. Their hides are matted with symbiotic creeper, which flowers only when the animal is in motion, so that a Ridge-Rider in flight is rimmed with sudden, brief blossoms that smell of ozone and mint. On the ground they are awkward, knuckle-walking through vine corridors, but aloft they are fluency itself, stitching the emptiness with arcs of quiet.

Closer to the substrate live the Thrum-Kin, smaller bodies built for vibration. Their hands have too many joints, fingers ending in pads that can read the tension in a stem the way a reader reads a line. They build their platforms in the living columns, entering through sphincters of tissue that close behind them like considerate doors. From inside, they coax the plant to soften its walls, to make rooms that exhale warmth. At night they press their ears to the green pelt and listen to the land’s weather-dreams—grumbles of frost shifting in the ribs of ridges, the gossip of water deciding where to run.

Water, when it chooses to appear, brings the Lens-Backs—turtles whose shells are not bone but translucent cartilage, domes that collect starlight and condensation both. They move with glacial patience through the damp seams of the world, and where they pause, small ecosystems bloom on their backs: mosses, tiny sac-plants, even colonies of sound-mites that feed on the thrum of the turtle’s own pulse. A Lens-Back is a moving well, and the Thrum-Kin know how to ask for a sip without offending the turtle’s dignity.

Above them all glide the Echo-Jays, birds whose feathers are ridged like vinyl, capable of recording snippets of sound and playing them back later, modified by the curve of their flight. They are the archivists of altitude. When a storm is hours away, the jays begin to loop the low groan of pressure dropping, a refrain that tells the Ridge-Riders to descend and the Thrum-Kin to brace their platforms. In this way, the fauna speak before they speak, translating weather into social grace.

Among the plants themselves live the Siphon-Spires, towering stalks that extrude those translucent sacs mentioned before. The sacs are not fruit but organs of attention—they gather the loudness of the world, the scrape of wind, the chatter of fauna, and ferment it into a thin syrup the plants use for growth. The Altiplantigrades harvest these sacs carefully, leaving enough behind so the plant can continue to listen. The syrup, when consumed, allows humans to taste the recent history of sound: last night’s frost, a Ridge-Rider’s passing, the low argument of two storms. It is not hallucination; it is echo made edible.

Even the smallest residents have purpose. There are the Thread-Mice, whose tails are prehensile and threaded with a filament that hardens in cold. They weave nets between stems to catch falling condensation, and in doing so they create micro-ponds for the larvae of glass-flies, insects whose wings are so thin they reflect not light but the color of the sky’s mood. The Thread-Mice trade droplets to the Thrum-Kin for seeds that have been softened by human breath, a trade that binds mammal and plant in a pact of mutual softening.

Predators exist, but they are subtle. The Hollow-Hounds do not chase; they wait in cavities that open like mouths in the green. They exhale a gas that smells faintly of snowmelt and old honey, a scent that makes prey feel safe enough to pause, and in that pause the hound’s tongue, which is lined with fine combs, draws in warmth. The hound does not eat flesh; it eats heat, redistributing it slowly through its body to keep its young alive. The Altiplantigrades respect the Hollow-Hounds, leaving offerings of their own warmth in the form of baked tubers, which the hounds accept with a polite, chilling nod.

In the end, what lives here is a chorus in which no voice is strictly dominant. People, beasts, plants, and weather all negotiate with one another, trading altitude for amplitude, cold for clarity. The emptiness is not vacant; it is porous, filled with the traffic of adaptation. Every spine, every root, every breath is a note in a scale that stretches upward into the thin, taut sky, and the music of the place is the sound of many lives learning to ring without shattering.
