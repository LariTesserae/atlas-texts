What lives here is, first, the City-Tree, a single organism so vast it has forgotten where it ends and governance begins. It is not a tree in the pastoral sense, but a columnar megastructure that has turned itself inside out to make room for minds. Its heartwood is reinforced with veins of boron and copper grown in situ, its sap a brine that conducts charge as well as nourishment. It dreams in rings that record not years but decisions—when a district needed more light, when a corridor learned to fear being walked through too quickly. The tree does not speak, but it broadcasts intent through the taste of its pollen: directives carried on the air like civic announcements.

Living within this architecture are the Lumen-Brewers, human or nearly so, wrapped in garments of waxed leaf and insulated hide. They tend the glass organs of the Luminary Quarter, organs that are less botanical than alchemical. They coax light from sealed chambers where captured photons are fermented into steadier glows, like wine clarified into sacrament. The Brewers are pale, their eyes large and softly reflective, adapted to a world where brightness is a budget. They move with economy, their footsteps muffled by the membrane-floor, their voices low because sound, like light, is considered a public utility. They know the language of gauges and graft, and they argue, when they argue, in percentages of lumens and gradients of pressure.

At their feet, and often above their heads, travel the Packet-Moths, six-legged herders piloting thick-bodied couriers that have surrendered much of their autonomy to purpose. These moths are armored with bioluminescent script along their wings, messages that can be read only in certain phases of their beat. They do not fly so much as they are routed, lifted and lowered by vines that know their weight and destination. In return, the moths are permitted to taste the current that hums through the conductive braids, a privilege that keeps them docile and bright. They cluster at junctions like commuters at a station, their wings ticking softly as they await dispatch.

Maintaining the sheen of the system are the Coin-Beetles, officious insects the size of copper pieces. They patrol leaves, stems, and polished alloy, scraping away accretions of dust and chemical memory. Their mandibles click in a rhythm that matches the regulators, a secondary metronome for the cavern. When a leaf begins to forget how to face the light, the beetles swarm it, not to punish but to remind it through friction. In the quiet hours, they gather in drifts that mimic the dust-snow of the low places, a mirror of neglect made living, mobile, and useful.

Deeper in, where the membrane-floor is still deciding what shape to take, live the Shape-Shifters—fauna of indeterminate lineage, more mineral than meat. They resemble ambulatory geodes, their hides crusted with chalky stone and black manganese that flakes off as they move. They graze on the softer edges of the cavern, consuming geological hesitation. Their digestion is a slow negotiation: they take in rock that is not yet committed to hardness and excrete structure—lintel-shaped stones, arch-ready fragments—that the Root-Forge extrudes into load-bearing members. They are tolerated because they make decisions out of stone, sparing the tree the effort of certainty.

Above and around these are the Scaffold-Spirits, plant-animal hybrids that have committed so thoroughly to architecture they are mistaken for furniture. They grow into brackets and struts, unfurling into chairs or platforms when approached, then stiffening again when vacated. They have nervous systems written in tension: cables that hum when pleased, cables that sag when confused. Their lives are measured in load-bearing and repose, and they communicate by altering the pitch of their vibration, a language that sounds like infrastructure thinking out loud.

In the Tensile Ward dwell the Tighteners, small, quick mammals with too many joints and whiskers that sense minute changes in strain. They live in nests woven from retired cables and regulate the breath of the city by tightening or slackening lines in response to shifts in humidity and hope. A Tightener’s work is invisible but vital; a single over-taut vine can make a district irritable, while a slack one can let ambition droop. They are treated with a mixture of superstition and payroll, fed on resin-cakes and the soft apologies of machines.

There are also the Chem-Fungi, sprawling mats that serve as air-sentinels. They inhale the city’s exhaust and exhale clarity, filtering the fine brine of exudate before it can become regret. In exchange, they are fed the trimmed edges of leaves and the apologies of failed prototypes. Their fruiting bodies glow faintly violet when the air is clean, dim to ochre when the city is angry or overworked. They are consulted by color alone, a barometer of civic mood.

Predators exist, but their violence is ceremonial. The Graze-Lords, immense, slow-moving beasts plated with lignite and patience, wander the upper vaults, pruning excess growth with tongues like wet sandpaper. They are feared less for their teeth than for their appetite for structural symmetry; they will strip a scaffold to rebalance a cantilever. Their hunters are the Dart-Sprites, quick-bodied creatures that harry them along predetermined lanes, nipping at heels to ensure the Graze-Lords remain productive rather than contemplative. These chases are scheduled, almost festive, and the citizens watch from balconies with the attention usually reserved for weather.

Weirder still are the Echo-Eaters, entities that live in the negative spaces between corridors. They have no bodies, or perhaps many, and subsist on the almost-sounds of the cavern: the regret of a footstep, the pause before a valve opens. They leave behind a coolness that feels like a word withheld, and the city accommodates them by leaving certain passages unused, not out of fear but out of etiquette. To be forgotten by the Echo-Eaters is considered a kind of death, so important conversations are repeated in empty ducts to ensure a piece of the city survives in their bellies.

At the margins, where the industrial grid bleeds into raw becoming, live the Seed-Archivists. They are human or post-human, tattooed with maps of lineages, who memorize the flex of stems and the filing of maintenance logs. They speak to the tree in touches, altering its growth by remembering which branches once bore fruit and which have forgotten how. They are historians, planners, and somnambulists, walking the city in sleep to correct its posture. They say the tree dreams of a horizon it has never seen, and they feed it descriptions of sky, imported as stories and metabolized into upward intent.

Finally, there are the Hum, a chorus of minor lives: the hiss of leaves turning light into fuel, the click of regulators, the percussive expansion of wood. The Hum is not a creature but a consensus, a living background that agrees, most days, to let the city hold itself together. It is the city’s immune system, its folk song, and its memory, all braided into one sustained note that says: we are here, we are maintained, we are becoming.

Together they compose a bestiary of purpose: beings that are also infrastructure, minds that are also management, creatures that remember being touched or echoing what was almost said. They live in a cavern that has made itself a world, coolly lit, industrious in its gentleness, belonging without needing a horizon to prove it.
