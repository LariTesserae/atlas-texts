The people of Low-Bell are not residents so much as caretakers of a living machine that tolerates company. They call themselves the Dripfolk, and their bodies seem shaped by the same patient erosion that rounds the cobbles. They are small-boned and long-limbed, with knees and elbows that have learned to give rather than resist, and feet that have spread into broad, callused pads for wet stone. Their skin is pale as limestone under its patina, veined faintly with maps of blue and iron-grey where the damp has taught their blood to remember mineral gradients. They do not hurry, because haste here is a liability; to rush is to steam, and steam invites rust.

Their faces are composed of soft angles, as though carved by wear rather than intent. Eyes are large and dark, pupils dilated to gather the city’s bruised light; irises hold specks like motes in standing water. Hair is sparse and fine, often braided with copper wire or threaded with beads of dried resin to keep it from snagging in the machinery. They favor garments of waxed canvas, felted wool, and oiled leather, dyed in moss-green and tarnish-blue, cut loose enough to let air move but cinched close at wrist and ankle to keep the damp from pooling in folds. Over these they wear tool-belts that are less fashion than habit: loops for wrenches, sheaths for gauges, pockets for lichen-scrapers and wire-shears. Their hands are seldom empty; fingers are stained with travertine dust, black oil, and the green smear of algal clocks that grow only where time thickens.

What marks them most is the way they listen. In Low-Bell, hearing is a civic trade. A child learns before speech to press a palm to a pipe and tell by the thrum whether a pump is hungry or full. Adults greet each other with a tap of knuckles to valve housings, trading tones like names. Their voices are low and liquid, consonants softened to avoid startling the city’s hum, vowels stretched like water finding a level. They do not shout; they let sound pool until it is ready to be understood.

Society in Low-Bell is stratified by stewardship. At the bottom are the Mottle-Sweepers, who tend the gutters and catch-basins, whose skin is mapped with more veins than most from hours spent reading the city’s damp. Above them are the Seam-Tenders, who caulk and line, who know which cracks to leave open as mouths and which to close as scars. Then come the Hum-Readers, guildspersons who station themselves in the pump-rooms and engine alcoves, translating vibration into prognosis, their inner ears tuned to the city’s dreams. At the apex, not by rule but by consensus, are the Spindle-Keepers, a small knot of elders who commune with the Heart-Spindle, letting its irregular pulse rearrange their thoughts until they return with instructions that feel less like decisions and more like translations.

They are not prolific, and they do not expect to be. Children arrive in the cool seasons, swaddled in felt and paraffin, and are taught early that growth is a negotiation with the place. Toys are tools made small: miniature gauges, tiny wrenches, glass vials for collecting gutter-song. Education is oral and tactile, passed hand to palm in the dim, lessons etched into muscle more than memory. Adolescence is marked by the Choosing Walk, when a young person spends three days and nights in the empty plazas, listening for which machine calls their name. Some hear a flywheel’s patience, others a condenser’s thirst; the rare few hear nothing at all, and those are the ones who become boundary-walkers, living in the soft seams where the city exhales.

Their daily lives are braided with the city’s rhythm. A morning in Low-Bell begins with the release of night condensation, when every surface weeps and the air tastes of iron and old rain. The Dripfolk emerge from their housing blocks, not in crowds but in patient intervals, lanterns held low so as not to insult the bioluminescent blooms. Work is communal and cyclical: pumps are fed, gears are brushed with fungal grease, catch-basins are sung to so they do not silt with sorrow. Meals are simple, flavored with cave-moss and the pale shoots that root in oiled gravel, eaten at long tables of riveted stone where conversation drifts like vapor between topics, never rushed.

They keep no livestock in the usual sense, but they companion with the city’s fauna. Eel-mice nest in their sleeves when the temperature drops, warmed by body and pipe alike. Beetles with lacquered shells are encouraged along warm runs to keep corrosion at bay. Blind fish in the gutters are considered distant kin, and their schools are mapped and named. The predators—long-legged cranes with too many joints—are treated with wary respect, fed with offerings of slurry and careful attention. Even the moth-things with copper wings are tolerated, their flutters read as barometers; when they stir, the Dripfolk prepare for shifts in pressure, for weather that begins below rather than above.

Their faith, if it can be called that, is rooted in maintenance. To let a gutter sleep is a sin; to mute the Heart-Spindle is blasphemy. They believe the city dreams in mineral increments, that its slow changes are thoughts turning over in sleep. They leave gifts of filtered rain and polished stone in the alcoves where gravity folds, and they listen for answers in the extra weight of a tool or the uphill hiccup of a rivulet. Death is not an ending but a return to the conversation: bodies are wrapped in felt and lowered into silt-chambers where the water can read their minerals and the stone can learn their names.

Despite the coolness of their lives, the Dripfolk are not joyless. They find delight in small exactitudes: a gutter that sings truer after a caulking, a flywheel that stores an extra breath of patience, a night when the bioluminescent blooms pulse in sequence like a polite applause. They hold festivals of hush, gatherings where no one speaks and the city’s texture is allowed to thicken, and afterward they drink spiced vinegar tea that makes their cheeks glow like furnace mouths. They carve jokes into the margins of maintenance logs, knowing that humor, like erosion, works best when it takes its time.

In the quiet between tasks, a Dripfolk might stand at the center of a plaza, letting the damp settle into their own pace, and feel the boundary between caretaker and cared-for blur. They know they are not masters of Low-Bell but its living lining, its sympathetic skin. And in that knowledge, there is a kind of peace—the peace of a creature calmly holding itself together while the world above forgets it is there, and the city, in turn, remembers them by the soft pulse of their listening.
