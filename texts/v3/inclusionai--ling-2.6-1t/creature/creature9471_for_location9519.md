What lives here is not a catalog of creatures but a census of after-effects—organisms that have mistaken the unfinished for the eternal, and decided to thrive inside the breath between rivet and load.

At ground level there are **Lacuna-Eels**, ribbon-bodied scavengers the color of quenched steel. They do not swim in water so much as swim in the possibility of water, gliding through thin films of condensate that bead on hot alloy. Their bodies are soft at the spine and hard at the edges, lined with rows of magnetized denticles that click when they pass a seam. They feed on oxide blooms and the tiny magnetic ghosts of lost tools, and when they die they unkink into a straight line and are absorbed back into the plating of the world, becoming a kind of accidental reinforcement.

Above them, in the low vault pressed down by the scale of making, drift the **Fume-Moths**. These are not insects but conglomerates of particulate memory—ash from grinding, filings from cutting, pollen of glassy slag—that have accreted enough static to become wing-shaped intentions. They flutter only when someone is watching with a certain hunger, and their wings taste of ozone and old solder. Their larvae are not born but declared: a Fume-Moth lays a clutch of silence on a cooling bracket, and by morning the silence has crystallized into larvae that resemble tiny, translucent washers. They consume nothing and leave behind a faint polish wherever they crawl.

Where water negotiates with metal, the **Argent-Fronds** live. These are mineralized laceworks, neither plant nor mold, that grow along the fault lines between plates. Their stems are dendritic copper sulfate; their leaves are thin veneers of borax that bloom overnight and crumble by dawn. Rooted in the not-soil, they draw nourishment from trace heat leaking through seams, converting it into a pale luminescence that only the eels see. When a plate shifts and crushes a frond, the wound leaks not sap but a thin hiss of steam, and the frond immediately begins knitting itself back together, slower each time, as if it were learning how to be fragile.

On the surfaces that are still learning their shape, **Gripple-Clam colonies** attach themselves. Each clam is a hinged shell of folded scrap—spring steel and ceramic composite—that clamps onto warped beams and blistering decks. They do not eat; they store potential. Inside each valve is a lattice of tensioned wire that hums when the wind finds it. When two clams meet on opposite sides of a buckling seam, they clasp each other and slowly ratchet the seam tighter, becoming accidental architects of stability. Their shells record the temperature of each closure in bands of color, so that a colony reads like a fever chart of the making.

Living in the spaces where gravity forgets its name are the **Drift-Sponges**, porous masses of aerated alloy that look like foam made from the sky. They are almost weightless and slightly buoyant, bobbing in updrafts of outgassing vapor. Their bodies are riddled with capillary tubes that draw in metallic exhalations and condense them into a briny sweat. This sweat pools in their hollows and is lapped up by Lacuna-Eels that have learned to climb, creating a loop of nourishment that spans from earth to low air. Drift-Sponges reproduce by fission when they become too heavy with memory, splitting along lines of least regret.

Higher still, on the latticed undersides of bridges that stitch rather than span, nest the **Welding-Spiders**. These are not arachnids but assemblages of intent: clusters of rivets, ceramic mallet-heads, and coiled wire, bound together by a mutual desire to finish. They move with a skittering deliberation, repairing microfractures and sealing leaks with beads of metal they carry in their mandibles. Their silk is not thread but a glassy flux that hardens into translucent filigree. A nest of Welding-Spiders looks less like a home than like a scar that has been cared for.

In the gutters that were never meant to hold so much, schools of **Gauge-Fish** flicker. They are flat-bodied and iridescent, their scales made from phosphor strips hammered into joints by long-departed makers. They do not swim in water alone but in the gradient between wet and dry, feeding on the differential that tries to decide its own direction. When a plate shrinks and ticks, Gauge-Fish dart toward the sound, aligning themselves along the new stress lines, creating temporary maps of strain that vanish as soon as the metal cools.

There are also the **Tally-Crows**, which are not birds but aggregates of rust, graphite, and stubbornness that have learned to fly. They have beaks shaped like callipers and eyes like small mirrors. They perch on towers that are still being stood upon and scratch tally marks into metal with claws of hardened bristle. No one has ever seen what they count, but their marks appear before accidents happen, suggesting they are counting down to solidity or up to collapse, or perhaps keeping track of how many times the place has almost remembered it was finished.

Most elusive of all are the **Seam-Sprites**, the youngest lives in a young world. They are brief, luminous gaps in the air, like missing rivets in the atmosphere itself. They appear where two plates kiss but have not yet locked, and their bodies are made of the hesitation between hot and cold. When you reach toward one, it withdraws into the geometry of almost, leaving behind a scent of ozone and a sense that something important will be decided the moment you stop paying attention.

Together they form a parliament of almost: almost alive, almost finished, almost stable. They do not inhabit the place so much as negotiate with it, learning its moods and filing down its sharp corners with their presence. The land, for its part, tolerates them, even leans on them, because without witnesses the unfinished might panic and revert entirely to liquid. Through eel, moth, frond, and sponge, the made world practices being weathered before it is even finished, accumulating a patina of living that is neither nature nor machine but a third thing: a collaboration with becoming.
