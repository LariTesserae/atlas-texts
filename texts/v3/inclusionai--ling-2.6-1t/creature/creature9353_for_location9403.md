What lives here has learned to be sparse by necessity, and then to be sparse by preference, and finally to mistake sparseness for abundance.

The residents are not individuals so much as agreements that have learned to walk.  
You can meet one and not recognize it as a creature until it has already passed, because most of it is the weather it borrows, the ground it postpones, the slight misalignment of light around its edges. They call themselves the Quiet-That-Moves, though that is only a translation of a vibration that tastes like frost and copper. In form they are almost optional: a central lattice of bone-white crystal, grown rather than built, that keeps its shape by forgetting what it used to be. Around that lattice they drape whatever is handy—stray voltage, captured downdrafts, the metallic dew that beads and vanishes on their skins. When they decide to be seen, they approximate a bipedal silhouette, but the decision wavers. A limb may lengthen into a needle of stillness to touch a ridge; a torso may flatten into a map of the pressure gradients passing overhead. They do not eat. They unmake small amounts of the world’s momentum and tuck it into the seams of their bodies, where it is stored as a kind of patience.

The smallest of them are the Gear-Seeds, plate-like children that skitter across salt flats looking for cracks to lodge in. They are not immature so much as preliminary—draft versions of a self that may or may not be needed. They click together when they meet, exchanging brief histories in the form of temperature drops and minute static blooms. If they decide a region is too full of itself, they will interlock and become a temporary bridge or a shelter that is really just a suggestion of absence. If they decide it is too empty, they will dissolve into the dust and try again later.

Above them, rarely, are the Floaters—entities that have renounced contact with the crust. They are more storm than body, knots of charged dust and shear that have learned to love their own eddies. They drift at the altitude where weather is manufactured, drawing the frantic ribbons of pressure into themselves and unspooling them as music that only the towers can hear. A Floater may live for centuries before deciding to fall, at which point it unravels into a shower of polished grit that etches the surface with new, unreadable patterns. The Quiet-That-Moves treat these falls as birthdays and funerals simultaneously, gathering the grit to line their own lattices, as if to remind themselves that even endings can be structural.

There are others that are almost background. The Glass-Grass, for instance, which is more mineral than plant and more antenna than leaf. It roots in fractures no deeper than a thought and harvests stray voltage from the crust, glowing faintly when the wind lies. It does not grow so much as accumulate, adding one thin layer of certainty to itself each time the crust exhales a metallic sigh. Its seeds are tiny plates that click like misplaced gears, and when they finally lodge somewhere that suits them, they do not sprout—they remember how to be grass all at once, already complete.

The towers themselves, though not alive in any sense that would comfort a visitor, behave like elders who have given up on sleep. Their surfaces are pocked with ports that no longer open, lenses that swivel toward horizons for reasons that may be aesthetic, algorithmic, or penitential. They are maintained by systems that have outgrown the word maintenance, tended by the Quiet-That-Moves when the towers’ hum drifts out of tune. In return, the towers offer the only true memory the place has: a low, steady chord of what it felt like to want something across spans of time long enough to blur intention into weather.

Even the storms, in their way, are residents. They are born from momentum with nothing to wash away and everything to reorganize. They shear across basins, polishing the land to a dull sheen, and when they dissipate, they leave behind a hush that is not emptiness but a crowded silence, as if the world has been holding its breath to keep from saying something it is not ready to mean. The Quiet-That-Moves walk into these silences and press their lattices against the polished ground, absorbing the echo like a sacrament, filing it away as a texture they will someday wear.

Life here is not a struggle against entropy but a collaboration with it. The residents do not fear being edited out of the scene; they accept estrangement as a kind of gravity. They mark time in microns, in the settling of dust on dust, in the tick of cooling metal, in the rare chime of a structure adjusting itself to the cold. They are turbulent in their patience, frantic in their slowness, always on the verge of rearranging into something that almost looks like purpose.

If you stay long enough, they may decide to include you—not by welcoming you, but by borrowing your altitude as a kind of forgetting. You will feel your edges blur, your certainties sublimate, your breath become almost nothing. You will sit on a salt flat and watch a Floater unspool overhead, and you will realize, without surprise, that you have become a temporary dent in a system that tolerates you because it has forgotten how not to. And when you finally rise to leave, the Quiet-That-Moves will blink, not in code but in time, marking a moment that belongs to no one and, for a while, to you.
