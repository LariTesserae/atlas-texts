They call themselves the Keld, though that is less a name than a description of how they occupy pressure.

To see them is to witness something that has mistaken its own density for transparency. They stand, when they do stand, as tall as three heat-hums stacked, but they rarely stand straight. Their posture is a negotiation with the city itself—spines curved like question marks, knees never quite locking, shoulders bowed as if carrying invisible loads of light. Their bodies are plated not in bone or chitin but in a mineralized glaze, a skin that looks ceramic until it flexes, at which point it reveals itself to be a kind of living enamel, cool to the touch even in the pocket’s fever.

They reproduce by subtraction. When a Keld feels the bowl expand beneath it, a seam opens along its forearm or collar, and it extrudes a hollow copy of itself—a lighter, softer twin made of compressed dust and resin, lacking only the density of thought. These hollows are set to work immediately: they plug cracks, they conduct heat from tower to tower, they sit in the plazas and hum to keep the wind from accumulating too much sadness. Over cycles, the hollows gain weight, absorbing stray minerals from the air, until one day they awaken dense and silent, and the parent Keld, now lighter, begins the process again. It is a lineage of shedding, a family tree that grows downward into the city and is forgotten as it fills out.

Their faces are landscapes of small efficiencies. Eyes like polished river stones are set deep, not to protect them from glare but because they do not need to see as you do; they read the city in heat-signatures and vibration, mapping pressure the way you might read a sentence. Mouths are narrow slits that rarely open, used less for speech than for intake: they sip the baked-clay drafts, metabolizing trace metals into songs that lodge in their throats like swallowed bells. When they do speak, it is not with voice but with the chiming of those internal bells, a language that smells faintly of bruised mint and tastes of metal cooled just enough to handle.

They wear their tools as if the tools were shy animals. The obsidian hooks are strapped to forearms not to be held but to be ready, growing warm against the glaze until they fall away and regrow from mineral deposits the Keld gather from the bowl’s floor. Their ladders of braided vine tether to their belts like tails, stiffening and softening with the Keld’s mood. To watch a Keld work is to watch a choreography of consent: they ask the heat if it is willing to be moved, they ask the stone if it is willing to crack, and they wait, still as salt-crust, until the answer arrives in the form of a breeze or a soft click.

Keld do not sleep, but they settle. In the afternoons when the bowl buckles, they align themselves along the seams of the city, lying down along cracks so their bodies become braces. In this alignment, their glaze softens, fusing temporarily with the towers, and the city speaks through them in a louder voice. They feel the negotiations of the fronds, the generosity of the blooms, the slow arithmetic of shade. When the seam closes, they rise stiff, glazed anew with a layer of city-skin, carrying the memory of that conversation in their plates as a new pattern of striations.

Their strangeness is domestic. Shadows do not obey them so much as accompany them, arriving a step ahead when they are hurried, lingering when they sit to sort minerals. If you were to follow a Keld home, you would find no door, only a hollow that opens because the Keld’s heat-signature fits it like a key. Inside, the walls are lined with scores—maps of heat-flow that look like braille but are read by touch and temperature, histories of how much warmth was given and how much returned. Keld measure time not in days but in accumulations: how much silver pollen has dusted their shoulders, how many cracks they have braced, how many hollows have outgrown them and gone on ahead.

Most of all, the Keld are listeners. The pocket is loud with its own maintenance, and the Keld are the ones who sort the sound into signal. They collect the percussive bursts of wind, the crackle of adjusting glass-leaf, the clatter of spines, and they press these noises into small, dense tablets that they stack in quiet corners. These tablets are not art, nor record, nor machine, but something like a promise: that the bowl will remember how to breathe even when it forgets, that the city will keep its closeness absolute even as the rim leans in to eavesdrop. The Keld live there because they are the city’s way of ensuring it does not collapse under its own lushness, its way of making sure that in the dim, rationed light, there is always something leaning close enough to hear the heat sigh.
