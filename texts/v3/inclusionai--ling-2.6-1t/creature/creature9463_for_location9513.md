The town is not populated so much as *tended* by people who have grown into their roles like lichen into a seam. There are only a few of them, but each is a kind of institution: a family, a function, a season’s worth of memory braided into a single job. They move between stone and task the way water moves between cracks—never filling, only finding the shape of least insistence.

There is the Keeper of the Drips, a slight person whose hands are permanently puckered from cold and salt. They know where each overnight bead will fall by morning, which lip of stone will tremble first. Each evening they set out hollowed leaves and concave chips of slate, arranging them like chess pieces that measure weather. In the morning they collect them not by volume but by *note*, for every drop strikes with a different weight. They taste the harvest not to judge its purity but its memory, claiming to know which cloud dreamed it into being.

The Weaver of Thresholds lives at the edge of the bowl where root and lintel blur. They do not build doors; they persuade openings. Their fingers are quick and stained with mineral dyes pulled from wet seams, and they knot stems, fibers, and shed bark into curtains that stop wind without trapping it. They repair the idea of boundary rather than the barrier itself, teaching newcomers how to pass through a curtain of living vine without it closing behind them like a mouth. The Weaver is also the town’s librarian of absence: they keep a ledger of spaces that have been, spaces that might be, and the narrow, polite air between them.

The Watcher of Tracks is rarely seen, only felt. They emerge at the hour when light tilts and lengthens shadow, carrying a tray of fine grit and a polished bone stylus. They read the floor as others read a page, pausing at each neat, deliberate impression to mutter approval or dismay. They do not pursue what passes; they *witness* it, smoothing the evidence only after they have named it. To them, a beetle’s passage is a sentence; a marmot’s, a stanza; the pause where someone stood and thought too long, a paragraph. When they speak at all it is to announce arrivals before they appear, as if the tracks had whispered ahead.

The Songsmith of Stone is an older figure who believes rock remembers being liquid. They carry a pouch of smaller stones chosen for their hollowness, striking them against ledges and basins not to make music but to remind. Each thud is meant to wake a resonance in the bowl, to ask the new land if it recalls its folding. Some days the stones answer with a dull warmth; other days with a brittle snap. The Songsmith records these replies in spirals on bark, maps of vibration that the town uses to decide where to sleep, where to store, where to leave untouched.

Then there are the Tenders of Pools that Do Not Yet Exist, a pair of twins who never quite agree. One insists that the silver seams will become rivers if only coaxed; the other claims they are happier as proposals, fleeting and free. They stand ankle-deep in mud and argument, diverting a trickle with a shoulder here, letting it wander there. Their debates are practical: they decide which hollows deserve a future of water and which should remain dry by principle. Over years, their quarrels have braided the waterways into the intricate, hesitant lace they are today.

Children in this place are not instructed so much as *exposed*. They learn by carrying, by listening, by standing in the cool hum until their own breath falls into its rhythm. They play games of alignment, stacking flat stones so delicately that the wind becomes a collaborator. They are taught that sound is a material, to be spent thriftily, and that silence is not empty but *listening*. By adolescence they have already apprenticed themselves to the thing they are least afraid of, which in this town is always the thing that changes most slowly.

Visitors arrive infrequently and depart transformed. The air presses on them, the hum rearranges their thoughts. They leave carrying water in flasks stoppered with moss, or small stones that have grown soft in their pockets from the attentions of hand and heat. Those who stay long enough to be considered residents are those who can tolerate being a parenthesis, who do not demand to be a period.

Even the animals behave as minor officials. A bird who practices its scale from the same cleft each dawn is recognized as the town’s alarm, not by sound but by *shape* of call. A many-legged creature that crosses the warm stone at noon is treated as a harbinger of stability; its path is swept clear, its passage blessed with a drop of sweet sap.

The people here are less a population than a choreography. They are the ones who answer when the bowl hums, who translate the grammar of wet into choices, who keep the coolness from becoming indifference and the newness from becoming anxiety. They do not rule the place; they *attend* it. And in return, the place allows them to be small, precise, and unmistakably heard.
