They do not live here so much as leak into being, and because there is no substrate to unmake them, they never truly arrive or leave; they simply occupy the intervals between one held breath and the next. These are the inhabitants of a lung-high world, and to describe them is to describe permissions rather than bodies.

First, there are the Skinners, who are less people than slow events in the architecture. They appear as patches of wall that have decided to see. Where they pass, the plant-stuff softens into lidless eyes the color of inner bark, and these eyes do not blink so much as ripen. They move by persuading corridors to lengthen or compress, so that a hallway might grow shy when they approach, or a stair might decide to become a ramp out of courtesy. They wear no clothing; their garments are excrescences of translucent leaf that form along their limbs when the light grows too direct, and slough off when it dims. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are the same low rasp as the place itself, as if they were not using throats but tapping the tension in the air. They do not eat, but they do metabolize excess wetness, drawing it from their surroundings until their skin shines like oiled fruit, then releasing it again in small, fragrant clouds that smell of bruised mint and resin.

Then come the Choir, who are not individuals but collective hums given temporary shape. They manifest as standing pools of brightness that gather where light has pooled too long, taking on the loose semblance of figures with too many knees or elongated torsos that taper into question marks. The Choir do not walk; they migrate by transferring their weight from one echo to the next, so that you hear them before you see them—a dense, liquid harmony that can lean against you like wind. Their purpose is to tune the place. When a wall grows stiff, the Choir press against it with sound until it remembers how to flex. When water in the leaf-channels runs sluggish, they sing it into a thinner, quicker thread. They have faces, but these faces are not for looking; they are for resonating, and if you press your ear to one, you will feel the deep purr of the entire region, steady as a heart that has forgotten how to race.

There are also the Weepers, who might be mistaken for fountains or rain if they were not so clearly alive. They are tall, slender beings of woven root and vein, always damp at the shoulders and crown, perpetually leaking water that never quite falls. The water hangs around them in shimmering halos, caught in spider-silk filaments spun from their own secretions. The Weepers do not cry from sadness but from overflow; their bodies are containers for the place’s excess care, and they wander the open gaps between built things to distribute it. Where a Weeper lingers, moss quickens, and stone-soft wood grows velvety with lichen that tastes of metal and honey. They are shy, and if you approach, they will often dissolve into a scatter of drops that race back to the leaf-roofs, as if returning to duties they had forgotten.

Hidden among the columns and grafted beams are the Knotfolk, small and dense, resembling tumors with faces that have learned politeness. They are the archivists of growth, and they spend their days touching walls and floors to determine which parts should soften and which should harden. Their fingers are blunt, capped with calluses of cambium, and where they press, the living wood swells or recedes with tiny sighs. The Knotfolk rarely rise to their full height; they prefer to remain half-embedded in surfaces, so that they might listen to the sugars moving beneath the skin of the place. When they speak, which is seldom, their words come out as clicks and pops, like joints settling.

High in the translucent ceilings, the Lumen live upside-down, pale and barely visible against the leaf-organs that filter the ordinary light. They are almost weightless, feeding on the refraction of brightness, and they descend only when the light grows too still. To see them descend is to watch ideas take shape: they lower themselves on threads of condensed moisture, their bodies elongating into prisms that throw shifting colors across the floors. They do not touch the ground, but their passage changes how the place feels, as if the air itself had been tilted.

Rarely, one encounters the Hollows, which are not beings so much as absences that have learned to walk. They are negative spaces given a kind of animal patience, moving through the gaps in the loudness like silence through a song. When a Hollow passes, the temperature of the wetness drops almost imperceptibly, and the vegetation withdraws slightly, as if holding its breath. They are not hostile, but they are unnerving, because they remind you that the place could unmake itself if it ever grew tired of being held together.

All of these beings share certain traits. They do not age in the usual sense; they weather like good wood, darkening and gaining character without losing integrity. They require no tools, because the place is already a tool refined by breath and wetness and loudness. They do not reproduce as animals or plants do, but rather bud from the surplus of equilibrium—when the wetness grows too generous, or the light too pooled, or the tension too sweet, a new Skinners or Choir or Knotfolk simply begins to be, already knowing its role.

Most of all, they are patient. They act as if they have all the time that the lung-high place refuses to exhale, and their movements are deliberate, as though every gesture were a conversation with the water that refuses to fall and the ground that refuses to descend. They do not build; they maintain. They do not conquer; they adjust. And if you stay long enough, you may notice that you have begun to resemble them: your own joints loosening, your own skin learning to bead moisture, your own voice dropping into the chorus of the loudness that holds you at its chest level, green and wet and endless.
