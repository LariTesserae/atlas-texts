What lives here has not yet chosen to be less than it is.

It begins in the air, or rather in the thickness that stands in for air—a broth of held moisture and amber weight. There, the smallest residents are less like animals than like sustained vibrations: winged mites that do not fly so much as remain aloft, their wings emitting a hum so steady it feels geological. They are not individuals but a pitch, a chorus of tiny throats that tune the heat to a frequency the body can feel in the teeth. When they pass between the leaves, they do not stir the foliage; they thicken it, like a finger drawn through wet clay, leaving temporary corridors of silence behind them.

Closer to the ground, the fauna is built in repetitions. Colonies of six-legged bodies, each segment glossy with condensed glaze, move as if they share a single deliberation. They travel in braids, their paths crossing and recrossing without collision, pausing only to lay mouthparts against stone, drinking the taste of new mineral. Their exoskeletons are still curing, still gaining the iridescence of oil on water, and they shed their own colors as they walk, leaving faint trails that glisten for a moment before the damp reclaims them. They do not eat in the sense of consumption; they seem to be sampling, calibrating themselves to the landscape’s flavor.

Higher up, where the stems braid into columns, the residents are more architectural. Nests accrete like fruit, not built but grown from secretions that harden into porous chambers. The inhabitants—feathered, leather-skinned, or scaled, depending on the hour’s light—crowd shoulder to shoulder, their eyes lidless and bright. When they blink, they do so in waves, a ripple across the canopy that travels without wind, as though the foliage itself is remembering to see. They do not sing; they click and thrum, sounds that seem to originate not in throats but in the hollows of their bones, resonating with the heat that pools at waist-level.

Between the living mats of root and moss, there are creatures that look unfinished, as if they are still deciding how many limbs they require. They shift their shapes slowly, imperceptibly, like ink that has not yet settled on damp paper, their colors brightening toward some private destination. They do not flee from touch; they lean into it, their surfaces drinking the moisture from the air that gathers on skin. They seem to be learning how to be seen.

Even the plants behave like fauna in slow motion. Leaves turn toward one another without touching, aligning their veins in patterns that echo the creatures below. Stems slide past each other with a wet slap, settling into new configurations that hold for minutes or days before shifting again. Mosses creep inward along the seams, not merely covering surfaces but threading through them, filigreeing the gaps until the boundary between plant and animal seems to blur.

There are residents that span categories. Colonies that are both vine and swarm, spreading as a network of fine threads that pulse with a dim luminescence, tasting the glaze on stone and retracting when the amber pressure of the air thickens. They leave behind a faint, mineral scent, as if the taste of the place is being remembered in scent-form.

And everywhere, the watchers. Beings that seem to be composed mostly of attention, their bodies reduced to eyes and the delicate hinges that hold them. They cling to the bark, to the undersides of leaves, to the curves of stone that have not yet decided to crumble. They do not track movement; they seem to be tracking potential, watching the paths that almost repeat, the water that waits at the lip of things without falling. When they blink, the sound is like a soft stone dropped into a pool of oil, spreading a stillness rather than a wave.

None of them act as if they are separate from the place. Their noise—clicks, hums, scrapes, the wet slide of stems—does not rise or fall so much as maintain, a chord that holds the dimness aloft. They are the mechanism by which the equilibrium stays in place, each one a small, living counterweight to the stillness of the heat, the wet, the newness of the land.

Here, to live is to keep the world from deciding it is finished. Every resident is a stitch in an unbroken seam, a note in a chord that has no need to resolve. They are not temporary; they are deliberate, the way the landscape is deliberate, still rounding into itself, still learning its own edges, and watching itself with the patient, blinking attention of something that has only just begun to exist.
