They live as if the town is a lung they must keep from collapsing.

The people are cartographers of closeness. They know which stair squeals before it is stepped on, which alley will exhale fog at the moment their breath leaves their lips. They do not speak of seasons but of shifts: the lean of the lake, the hunger of the gutters, the color the stone turns when a given hand has warmed it three mornings running. Their names often describe a habit of water or a fault in a wall—Silt-Thumb, Gutter-Knower, the family called Drizzle-Ear—so that to address someone is to remind them of the place they keep.

Their bodies have learned the damp. Skin is pale as unglazed clay, cool to the touch, with calluses shaped like the tools they carry: chisels, wedges, small trowels for scraping algae from seams. Hair is worn under scarves or cropped short to avoid the fungus that likes to bloom at the nape in high summer. They move with a low center of gravity, feet turned slightly out, as if always bracing for a shift in the ground. When they work, they hum—not for joy, but to feel the walls answer. Sound returns to them dulled or sharpened by the stone, and they read the answer like a weather.

There are no rulers, only caretakers. Each quarter has its listener, someone who rises before the birds—or what passes for them—and presses a palm to the hollow posts, noting how far they have risen in the night. Decisions are made in the weirs, where the town gathers to watch water pool and drop. A plan is accepted when the surface stills without a ripple that cannot be explained by the breeze. Disputes are settled by rerouting, not by decree: a family that speaks too loudly is given an alley that dead-ends in moss; one that hoards warmth is assigned the door that only opens when the lake exhales.

Their homes are grown more than built. A room is claimed by placing a bowl beneath a drip and waiting to see whether the water finds its way to the gutter without help. If it does, the space is sealed; if not, the wall remains soft, the door a curtain of rope that must be lifted when the tide is low. Furniture is minimal and jointed: benches that hinge into ladders, beds that tip toward the central weir in case the level rises. Every object has a twin purpose, and every purpose has a failure mode: a shelf holds books until the damp warps them, at which point it becomes a bed for leech-like things that clean the stone in exchange for shade.

Children are taught to listen before they learn to walk. They press their ears to the stone and describe the sound as if it were color. They map the paths that water might take if the ground were to shrug, and these maps are folded into the thatch of the roofs to be forgotten and remembered. There is a ceremony called First Leak, in which a child is given a tool and told to stop a slow drip with nothing but their body and a shim. To succeed is to understand that stopping is temporary; to fail is to learn that the town tolerates small losses.

The eels in the gutters are not pests but partners. They are fed with scraps of meat and stories, and in return they coil around the joints of the bridges, stiffening them with their weight when the wood wants to swell. The leech-like things that cling beneath the docks are tolerated only when the stone is warm; in cold they are scraped away and piled on the talus, where they twitch and slowly unlearn their grip. The insects that skate on the film of water are left to their silver roads; to step on them is to invite a clog in the weir, and the memory of a clog is older than any living person.

Birds, when they come, are treated as visitors from a place that is already finished. They are offered shallow bowls of still water and watched closely. If they drink without tilting their heads, the town takes it as a sign that the slope is holding; if they refuse, the gutters are inspected for sour smells. Few stay long; their calls confuse the echoes, and confusion makes the walls restless.

There is a quiet religion of maintenance. It does not name gods but instead names debts: to the stone, for remembering it was torn; to the water, for rising; to the air, for carrying the chill that keeps the moss from growing too bold. Offerings are small and useful—shims, wedges, a patch of resin—placed where the water gathers and left until they rot or are taken. Prayers are said in the form of tasks: clearing a channel, retightening a rope, listening to a wall until it gives up its mood.

Trade with the outside is rare and careful. They accept wood that is already seasoned, stone that remembers other hills, rope that has known drier winds. In return they give maps that change by the week, jars of water that tastes of the basin’s breath, and small stones that have turned color from repeated touch. Visitors who stay more than a season tend to lose their straight walks; they begin to tilt, to pause at the sound of a drip, to measure time by the lake’s exhalation.

At night, the town becomes a chorus of small adjustments. The fog fingers its way into rooms, and sleepers pull their blankets tighter. The hollow posts rise and fall like chests. Somewhere, a stone changes color under the same hand as yesterday, and someone smiles, or frowns, or simply notes it in the dark. The basin breathes, and the town breathes with it, each half-carved, each half-known, learning the shape of living while already insisting it has been this way for years.
