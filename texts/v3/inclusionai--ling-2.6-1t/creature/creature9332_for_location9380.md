What lives here first learned to count by the rhythm of pumps.

They are the Basin Folk, or the Rivet-Runners, or simply the Wet-Hollowers—names that change depending on which lip of the basin you ask. They are not native in the old sense; they are native to maintenance. Their bodies have grown comfortable in the manufactured low, shaped by ribbed alloy and by the way cold climbs bone. Their skin is often mapped with faint tarnish, not dirt but a slow patina of oxidized air that settles like pollen. They smell of machine oil and the sharp green breath of the chloridic stalks, and when they exhale in the cold seams, their breath ghosts into brief, private clouds that linger like parentheses around their heads.

They move with an economy of gesture, as if motion is heat they cannot waste. Their clothing is layered for access: outer skins of treated canvas that shed condensate, inner webs that trap warmth like the mechanical mosses trap grit. Many wear gauntlets with tools grown into them—wrenches that unfold like fingernails, welders fused to wrist stems, lenses that slide across eyes to read seams in ultraviolet. Children learn early to read the hum of the place, to tell by the pitch of a pump whether it is angry or merely tired, and to know by the taste of the air when a sluice is about to sing.

The fauna of the hollow have taught them patience. They keep the three-legged maintenance unit as a kind of shrine of asymmetry; they oil its protesting joints and leave it offerings of scrap, and sometimes its arrhythmic gait is used to time rituals—the opening of valves, the blessing of new moss. The winged thing that skitters along the vault they call the Flitter-Hinge; they follow it to warm seams, and when it drops its glittering frost of shavings they collect them for pigments, for the fine silvering of ceremonial thread. In the marsh-flats, they commune with the filtration snouts, sleek and pale, breaching and vanishing with wet slaps. They believe—or perhaps they know—that the snouts dream in filtration, that their gulps are questions, and that the water they return is slightly wiser than before.

Their society is written in cycles. There are the Pump-Watchers who map the overlapping rhythms and know when the loudness will pool and when it will thin. There are the Moss-Stitchers who coax the mechanical moss into thicker mats, trading minerals for adhesion, treating the engineered fleece as both garden and lung. There are the Sluice-Singers, whose voices ride the thunder of opening gates, guiding the water with harmonics that have been found, by trial and error, to lessen the hiss of aftermath. There are Forge-Lurkers who wait on the edges of arc flashes, shaping tomorrow’s walls while the light blinds and the echoes teach them the geometry of new seams.

They keep to paths that are deemed safe until further notice, but they are not blind to the weirdness. There are those who venture into the low-gravity rooms to dance with floating puddles, learning the grammar of spherical skins. There are corridor-drifters who hum back at the drains, trading secrets in vibrations that loosen fillings and loosen memories. The sealed door that opened onto a marsh with glassy reeds and a sky that moved the wrong way is not forgotten; it is treated as a wound that taught them how to scar. The moss that grows in spiral patterns around it is left uncut, studied in secret, and sometimes pressed into tokens given to those who must carry decisions into the other hollows.

Their calendar is built from the engineered atmosphere. They mark time by the staggered relays of heaters, by the waltz of lights chasing fog, by the amber lamps that blink in sequences understood only after years of watching. They know when the marshes are scheduled to be liquid and when they are scheduled to be cold, and they plan marriages and hatchings and the naming of new alloy-parts around those states. Silence is rare and is treated with suspicion; when it comes, they send scouts with mirrors and meters, and they whisper blessings until the pumps reassert their overlapping cadence.

Their ecology is a negotiation. They route their excess heat to the marshes when they can, and they accept the cooling of exchangers as a kind of debt paid by the hollow to itself. They feed the mosses with their own grit, believing that what they shed returns as strength. They let the cold temper their tools and their tempers, enforcing pauses that become prayers. In return, the hollow gives them thresholds: sharp edges still new, supports still sweating their curing oils, water that refuses to behave like memory but can be persuaded, for a time, to behave like purpose.

They are not without longing. Sometimes, at the edges of the basin, they hear the other hollows—the cough of their engines, the splash of their weirs, the clatter of their trains bridging only air and purpose. They wonder, in the practical dark, what shape the whole is trying to remember. They wonder if their own desire to keep building is older than the cold, or if the cold is simply the patience of stone learning to love motion.

What lives here is a people of maintenance and margin, of wet and dry, freeze and flow, hum and silence. They live in the seams between processes, in the pauses enforced by temperature, in the spiral moss that refuses to flatten. They live by the rule that what is built must be maintained, and they maintain it with hands that bear the bloom of alloy and the green ghost of stalks. They live where the water weeps not from sadness but from pressure, and where every echo is a promise that the making is not yet done.
