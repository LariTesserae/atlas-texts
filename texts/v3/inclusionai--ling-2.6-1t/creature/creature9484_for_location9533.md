What lives here has learned to measure itself by subtraction.

They are not numerous. Perhaps three, perhaps five, counted in the same way the bowl counts water—by absence rather than presence. They have no name that travels well, though the rare passerby, peering over the rim, might call them silt-walkers or bowl-ghosts, if they call them anything at all. Names, like walls, are hard things, and they have learned to go soft.

They are short, less tall than the wiry pod-plants, as if the air’s low ceiling has asked them to keep their height to themselves. Their limbs are long in proportion but carried folded close, like stems that know better than to reach. Their skin is the color of damp mortar, mapped with fine cracks no deeper than breath, through which a slow, iron-dark warmth leaks. When they stand at the bowl’s edge, they resemble patches of shadow that have decided to stand up and walk, though shadows do not have fingers, and these do.

Each has eyes, but not in the way expected. The eyes are pale, shallow bowls themselves, set low and wide, and they do not dart. They hold. They drink the day’s gray-gold tone until the edges of the world blur, and in that softening they find their shape. When the light fades, the eyes do not reflect; they absorb, becoming nearly the same as the skin, as if the dusk has been invited inside. There is nothing feral in their gaze, only a kind of attention that is patient enough to become part of the thing it watches.

They wear little that was made by hands like theirs. What covers them is mostly borrowed: veils of fine dust that settle when they are still, stiffening into a second skin; salt rings from the pocket-mirrors that cling to their ankles like bracelets; occasional scraps of worked stone or old fiber, found and lost again, looped through belt-loops that are more habit than need. Their garments are less clothing than weather given temporary form. They do not mend them. They let them erode, and replace them when the erosion is complete.

What they eat is also a gesture. They gather the thin crust that forms where damp meets dry, scrape it from stones with nails worn blunt as river glass. They lick it from their palms, or from broad leaves they set out overnight to catch the dew’s slow thoughts. They do not hunt. They do not plant. They wait for the wiry pods to split, and collect the pale dust that spills out, fine as memory. It is not nourishing in the way hunger understands, but it is enough, and they are enough for it. They seem to live on the margin between having and not-having, and that margin suits them. Their hunger is small, and they keep it small.

They move as if the bowl itself moves them. When they walk, the ground answers with a papery crackle, and they pause, listening to the sound as if it were a reply. Their steps are placed to avoid the saucers of dark water, as if those mirrors hold something they prefer not to disturb—perhaps themselves, perhaps only sky. When rain comes and the pale seam of river-idea appears, they stand just inside its path, letting it trace their calves, and withdraw as it sinks, returning to dust. They do not flee water, nor do they pursue it. They let it pass through their lives the way it passes through the bowl: as a rumor, as a visit.

There is no sound they make that is not also a kind of stillness. A hum, perhaps, felt more in the chest than heard, when the air thickens at dusk; the soft parting of their own seams, dry and papery, when they stretch after a long rest. They have voices, but they use them to answer the bowl more than each other: a word or two, brief as a stone’s settling, and then the word is gone. They do not argue with each other. They separate by enough space to be seen whole, like the pod-plants, and in that separation they find their pattern. They are individuals, but they are also a kind of colony, sharing the same low ceiling, the same damp, the same economy of small variations.

They sleep in the shallowest hollows, where shadows pool like spilled ink. They curl into shapes that fit the bowl’s radius, as if they were not lying down but returning to the geometry from which they grew. Their breathing slows, nearly matching the hush of water thought. They do not dream of elsewhere. If they dream at all, it is of the bowl itself, slightly changed: a stone shifted, a rim softened, the salt lace gone from the mirrors. When they wake, they check the changes, and if nothing has changed, they accept that as well.

They do not age in the way stories describe. Time, for them, is not a line but a layer, and they grow thicker into it. Their cracks, their salt rings, their dulled nails—all are records of days sat upon each other, pressed by dew, compacted by stillness. They are not dying, and they are not waiting. They are occupying themselves, like the bowl occupies its own damp, its own light, its own quiet.

When the rare stranger crests the rim, they do not flee. They let themselves be seen, but only as much as the bowl allows. They become, for a time, indistinguishable from stone, from shadow, from the fine film of moisture on the ground. If the stranger lingers, they may move away, or they may not. If the stranger leaves, they remain, returning to the subtraction by which they live.

In the end, what lives here is not a people, nor a creature, nor a thing. It is a way of holding still. It is the bowl’s answer to existing without insisting. It is the slow opening of seams, the quiet licking of dust, the occupation of a small, damp room in the earth, sealed by its own composure, and by the soft, iron-tinted lives that keep it company without ever naming it.
