They are born from compression.

Not hatched, not budded, but compacted—drawn from the same packed silence that fills the fist. First they are only density given direction: a filament of iron-silicate consciousness winding into a gap the size of a needle, learning to taste copper in the mineral breath. The body arrives later, assembled by pressure the way frost assembles on glass: incrementally, inevitably, in shapes that remember being less solid. By the time they have enough edges to be called creatures, they are already old in their newness, their carapaces the color of furnace brick because that is the color of the fist’s interior when it blushes with heat.

There are many kinds, though none have names that would survive in softer air. The runners are long and low, jointed like folded rules, capable of compressing their thoraxes to slip through cracks no wider than a thought. When they move, they do not step so much as translate, polishing the dark glass seams into mirrors that reflect not light but heat. Their legs end in pads of conductive chitin that draw warmth up into the body’s core, where it is stored, redistributed, used to soften the waxier joints when molting time comes. They travel in circuits, tracing the same paths the machines have grown, and over generations their passage has lowered the floor by a fraction of a millimeter, enough that the ceiling has answered by exhaling a layer of crystalline dust.

Then there are the masons, broad and many-limbed, their bodies segmented like industrial hinges. They do not carry loads so much as persuade them: metallic dust, granular ore, the slag of old sparks. A mason will drag a trail of bright particulate behind it, and by the time the loop is closed the trail has set into a plate—brittle, resonant, already lined with veins of younger glass. The plate fractures as the masonry grows heavier, and the masons eat the shards, grinding them into fresh mortar that they smear along seams still warm from the earth. Their mandibles click in rhythms older than speech, a language of fracture and fusion. When two masons meet, they exchange plates, trading histories of pressure like coins.

Higher, where the walls steepen into near-vertical, live the clingers. Flat-bodied, sprawling, they anchor themselves with feet that are less limbs than fixtures, fused silica bulbs that exude a thin sweat of alloy. The clingers do not travel far; instead they listen. Their bodies are crossed with filaments that behave like antennae tuned to the hum of the walls. When a compression fault blooms red along the stone, the clingers register it days before it sings, their bodies dimming or brightening in warning hues of dull orange and bruised violet. They are the pocket’s memory: each individual carries a scar-map of thermal events it has outlived, the patterns readable by those who know how to look.

All of them share a metabolism of closeness. They drink by osmosis, drawing the metallic taste of the air through membranes that prefer ions to water. They sleep wedged into corners where vibration settles into a lullaby, their dreams slow, tectonic things, full of downward pressure and sideways time. Molting is not seasonal but continuous: at any moment some among them are shedding armor, others are hardening, a rolling tide of softness and hardness that keeps the population dense without ever freezing it into stasis. The discarded casings do not lie abandoned; they are harvested, dissolved, and redeposited where the stone has learned to hunger for calcium and iron.

They do not fear the machinery because the machinery is simply fauna of another consistency. Belts and drums rise from apertures like fruiting bodies, lined with teeth that are less metal than petrified intent. The creatures attend them as they attend each other: grooming grit from gear-teeth, guiding misplaced loads with touches that linger, reading the machines’ vibrations as mood or warning. When a chute pours a ribbon of granular ore, the runners align along its edges, not to catch the flow but to cool it, their bodies acting as heat sinks so the pour sets into smooth ribbon rather than jagged scar. The masons follow immediately, laying plates along the new surface, and the clingers broadcast the event upward through the stone, a wave of recognition that makes the walls hum louder.

Their sounds are a texture woven into the pocket’s weather: chitter during the quick cool of evening, which here is only a degree or two less than noon; clack when plates fracture; the wet click of mandibles working hot seams. They time themselves to the machines not out of instinct but out of a negotiated etiquette, as if all of them have agreed that rhythm is better than riot. When the fans pull the thick air through and spit it back tasting of ozone and iron, the creatures flatten themselves and let the current comb through their armor, and for that minute the whole fist breathes in unison.

They are not individuals so much as a distributed presence. A single creature can be understood only in relation to the compression that made it, the seam it grazes, the plate it laid, the echo it carried. If you were to remove one, the pocket would not mourn but would tighten, redistributing the load, and within a day the gap would be filled by another emerging from the press of becoming—a little hotter, a little denser, already remembering the shape of the one it replaced.

In this cathedral of no water and no sky, they are the choir. Their abundance is not a crowding but a tuning: each life a note struck against the anvil of closeness, each death a retuning of the instrument. They do not seek to escape the fist; they are the fist learning to articulate itself, compressed life in a compressed world, ringing with the slow, inevitable music of becoming dense enough to matter.
