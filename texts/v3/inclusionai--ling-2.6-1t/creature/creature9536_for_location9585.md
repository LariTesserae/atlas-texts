They do not live here so much as *compose* here—each resident an arrangement of the bowl’s habits and hungers.

The grazers are the most visible citizens. They stand six-shouldered and patient, their spines furred with moss that is neither parasite nor pet but a kind of shared breath. The moss drinks what the animals exhale and, in turn, offers a coolness against the sun’s hesitant touch. Their hooves are soft, padded with a spongy callus that drinks condensation from stone, so that passage leaves the rock slightly brighter, as though walked upon by shadow with weight. They browse in tiers, reaching up with long, supple necks to crop the upper fronds of the strange trees, then lowering to nibble at the luminous fungi that spring up where old rock has wept. Their eyes are wide, dark, and mild, and they blink slowly, as if adjusting to a tempo older than their bodies. They do not flee when the people come; they shift, making room, accepting the quiet as a form of feed.

Smaller lives occupy the gaps between grazer and stone. The flicker-beings—sometimes called glass-lice or water-sketches—have bodies that seem to be made of attention more than matter. They skitter with a gait that obeys too many joints, pausing to lay flat against leaf or slab until they resemble reflections caught out of season. When they move, they leave a faint scent like wet copper and ozone. They are scavengers of subtlety, feeding on the almost-sounds of the place: the half-vibration of a hoof on stone, the sigh of a leaf-cup brimming, the hesitation in a predator’s breath. They vanish into crevices when the wind changes, not from fear but because the wind rearranges the boundaries of safe hiding.

The predators are fewer in number but generous in shape. They are the ones who teach the bowl how to keep its edges sharp without cruelty. Most are patterns before they are bodies—a shift in the blue-green gloom that resolves into a flank, a line of eyes that are less eyes than polished river stones set into fur that holds rain like a thatched roof. They do not hunt for sport. They move only when the grazers grow too numerous in one slope, or when the flicker-beings thicken into clouds that might choke the luminous trees. Their kills are neat, almost ceremonial, and afterward they allow the small lives to clean what is left, as if repaying a debt to the commons. In this way, they are the law of the bowl given a pulse.

The people, when they are present, are less a population than a function. They are caretakers of thresholds, tenders of the slow infrastructure that keeps damp from becoming flood and growth from becoming suffocation. Their shelters know them by spine and shoulder, doorways shaped to the particular tilt of each dweller’s gait. The people gather water in sinew-stitched troughs and judge its clarity by taste; they read the hum in certain stones to know where to step when the ground is uncertain. They speak rarely, and when they do, the language is built from pauses and posture, a grammar of glances and the placement of tools. They leave bone awls and flint knives where the bowl expects to find them, not lost but waiting. In return, the bowl offers them moss to line their beds and stones that warm their hands without burning.

Then there are the others—the ones that are less creature than event. The trees that peel their skins to reveal a damp, faintly shining cambium are alive in a way that borrows from light as much as root. If a grazer leans against one, the bark may slough in translucent ribbons, and the exposed flesh will pulse softly, as though singing in a register that teeth can feel but ears cannot catch. The stones that hum are not inhabited by spirits so much as *occupied* by a memory of motion, as if the ridge itself has not finished settling and sends its ache through mineral throats. These phenomena are acknowledged with small rituals: a tilt of the head, a pause in walking, the laying of a hand flat against warm rock to say *I know you are here*.

Even the rain participates. When it falls upward, it is greeted with a kind of resigned patience, as one might greet a guest who has forgotten which way to face at table. The pools that hold delayed reflections are treated as mirrors of consequence; to look into one is to see what has just left you, still moving, still deciding whether to return. The people do not fish in them. The grazers do not drink from them immediately. They let the memory cross and recross the surface until it settles into ordinary water.

All of them—fur, leaf, stone, sinew, and the flicker-things that are mostly light—negotiate a commons of attention. Space is granted and received. Noise is offered as a form of exchange, and silence is the interest paid back. To live here is to be many rhythms at once: the quick chew of moss, the slow grind of rock, the intermittent brightening of air, the steady bruise of light learning to be thorough. The bowl does not insist that its residents be one thing. It lets them be provisional, layered, and incomplete, and in return they let it remain new enough to remember cracking, damp enough to remember rain, and crowded enough to remember that to be alone is simply to be waiting for the commons to shift shape again.
