They are not called residents, because that would imply arrival, and almost nothing here seems to have come from elsewhere. Instead, they are gradients—intensities of pressure, color, and patience that have learned to clump.

The Fish are less like animals than like sentences written in a language of subtraction. Their bodies are black as closed ink, but not opaque; they drink the dim light and give back only a suggestion of outline. They do not swim so much as unknot themselves from the water and re-knot farther along. Their eyes are dense, almost geological: knots of obsidian that store up pressure from the deeper basins. When they pass between towers, they leave behind a faint phosphorescence that lingers just long enough to remind the water that something has been decided. They seem to know which windows are not windows, and they never enter those. They are the city’s librarians of negative space.

The Gelatinous Ones are known, if at all, as Listeners. They press themselves to the mineral skin of the towers and do not move quickly. Their bodies are maps of translucent veinwork, each thread carrying a pulse that is slower than heartbeat but faster than erosion. They eat the towers—not to destroy, but to translate. Where they graze, the material softens, hums, and then hardens again in a slightly different register. Over time, the buildings where they have fed develop a kind of memory: a curve that remembers being straight, a hollow that remembers being full. The Listeners do not sleep; they only go quiet, and in that quiet the towers sometimes sing back in frequencies that feel like geometry.

The School Without Name is a single creature made of many, a shattered-mirror brightness that turns corners as if it had practiced them in a previous life. Each fragment is scale-thin and iridescent, and together they act as a kind of circulating thought. When the current presses harder, the School condenses into a blade of light; when the tide softens, it loosens into a cloud of suggestions. They do not hunt; they harvest motion, darting through ribbons of fauna and leaving behind a sense of having been rehearsed. If you watch them long enough, you begin to feel that their precision is not mathematical but musical, as if each turn were a note in a chord that has not yet been named.

There are the Limbless and the Too-Limbed, who share a single method of travel: hesitation. They move by deciding not to be where they were, and their bodies follow that decision like a delayed echo. Some have fingers that branch like frost and end in nothing softer; others have no fingers at all, only cups and clefts that fit against the city’s edges. They do not build, but they arrange— nudging a bridge of balcony into a slightly different angle, tucking a stiff pale reed into a crease where water lingers. Their labor is invisible because it happens in increments that blur, like the difference between night and the dim.

Among them are the Question-Mouths, predators who do not rush. They approach by narrowing the distance between themselves and everything else until that distance becomes a kind of attention. When they open, it is not to bite but to resolve— a soft chamber of cartilage and query that swallows only what is willing to be answered. In their wake, the water feels clarified, as if a portion of murk has volunteered to become definition.

There is also the Quiet Itself, which lives in the creak of mineral bones and the polite hush of scale on cartilage. It is thickest near the new arches, where the city is still deciding whether to keep them. The Quiet is not empty; it is full of reservation, of pauses that have mass. When the colder tongues from below push into the chamber, the Quiet tightens, and the fauna arrange themselves into braids and spirals as if language were a luxury they could refuse.

And finally, there is the City-In-Process, which is alive enough to be fauna and patient enough to be stone. It thickens its base while it waits to grow taller. It spits out balconies that droop like unformed sentences. It weaves tendrils that grip and let go and grip again, learning tension by touch. When a predator passes, the city holds its breath; when the School flashes through, the city shivers in harmonics it did not know it possessed. It is crowded with lives that refuse to shout, and in that crowding it finds its order.

Together they make a pocket of becoming: fauna that are almost architecture, architecture that is almost fauna, water that is almost thought. They do not need names because they are still learning how to need. And in the dim, soaked light, they continue their gentle construction—one small, quiet act at a time.
