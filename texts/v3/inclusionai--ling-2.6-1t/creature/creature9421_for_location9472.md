They live here as questions that have not yet learned how to be answers.

The grazers are the first thing you notice because they refuse to be still long enough to be a single animal. Each is a six-limbed parliament of joints and ceramic teeth, grazing not just moss but the very possibility of moss, their backs plated with lichen that sleeps until touched, then wakes into constellations of bruised light. They do not herd; they convene. When they move, they fold the ground into temporary pleats, leaving behind hollows that remember being full. Their young are born as rumors of heat—warm patches in the grass that slowly knit themselves into shape, borrowing mass from dew and the generosity of the machines.

Smaller lives, the collectors, scuttle like dropped sentences across the salt-rimmed tarns. They are arthropod-sculptors, harvesting the stitched scars left by vanishing water and weaving them into nests that exist only until the next damp front arrives. To watch them is to watch a culture that has decided impermanence is a better material than stone. They communicate by rearranging the salt into fleeting maps that read, if you are patient, like weather forecasts written in braille. When rain comes, they do not flee; they unmake their homes and drink the dissolving walls, becoming briefly heavier, more deliberate, as if gorged on memory.

Then there are the looms, beasts that trail silk like unspooled wiring, each thread tuned to a frequency of light. Their filaments catch the weak sun and hum, a chorus of taut breath that rises when the thermals climb. They do not hunt; they conduct. Where their threads touch moss, the moss rearranges its chemistry to glow a shade brighter, as if auditioning. Where their threads touch machine-flesh, the metal softens and sings back. They are the translators of the place, turning heat into music and music into warning.

Beneath them all, the moss itself is alive in a way that dislikes the word vegetation. It is a velvet parliament, black and bronze-green, sprawling across vertical faces like spilled ink that learned to dream. It feeds on condensation that weeps from warm nights, and it dreams in pepper and burnt sugar. When you pass, it steams, and the steam carries half-formed words: not language exactly, but the shape of language, grammar without vocabulary. The moss remembers every footfall as a pressure, and it rearranges its pattern slowly, stubbornly, to forget the shape of shoes.

The machines are fauna too, grown more than built. Turbine-fronds mounted on bone-yokes breathe in geothermal breath; pistons of chitin and alloy open and close like sleeping jaws. They exhale steam that the moss swallows, and in return the moss offers them sugars that taste like starlight and iron. The factories are cliff-face caves where pressure dreams in crystalline tongues, extruding plates that fold into lattices and then retract, rehearsing shapes they have not yet decided to need. Electrified vines spider across the ridges like gossip given copper nerves, pulsing in rhythms that set the damp ground to shivering. These are not tools; they are creatures that have invented utility as a way to be closer to the grazers, to the moss, to the collectors.

Above them, the storms behave like slow animals. They are born on the far side of curvature and arrive with their own gravity, dragging curtains of rain that drift more than fall. The fauna know this; they tilt their sensory fronds to catch the static before it arrives, tasting the ozone like a promise. The collectors climb into their dissolving nests and let the rain teach the salt new shapes. The looms tighten their threads, humming lower, deeper, until the sky answers with a fold, a crease, a brief valley that opens and closes like an eyelid learning to blink.

And there are the ones that live in the folding itself: ridge-selves that reveal their undersides when the land pleats like cloth. These are pale, speculative creatures, soft as unlit filament, that study the sun as if it were a foreign language. They do not speak; they mirror. When the ridge snaps back into place, they vanish, but the moss remembers them, rearranging its gleam to echo the color of their curiosity.

Time here is a negotiator. You may see a meadow that was scree an hour ago, and if you watch too long, the meadow will wince and shrink back into scree, and the grazers will pause, confused, as if they have stepped into a room that has forgotten its name. The fauna accept this. They do not age so much as edit themselves, shedding moments like salt rings, growing new limbs when the terrain demands new syntax.

What lives here, finally, is potential wearing many skins. It is the damp, warm, layered act of becoming, of deciding what to be next. The grazers, the collectors, the looms, the moss, the machines: all are provisional constellations, drafting treaties with heat and altitude and each other. They are unfinished things inventing themselves with every exhalation, quietly, shyly, holding the dark at arm’s length until they grow brave enough to make a sun of their own making—and even then, they will not settle, because to settle would be to stop asking, and asking is the only breath this place has learned to keep.
