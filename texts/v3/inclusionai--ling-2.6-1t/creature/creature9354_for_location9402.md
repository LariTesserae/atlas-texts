They live in gradients—rats in the steepest ones, beetles in the middle, birds along the rooflines where heat and wind negotiate. The rats do not scurry so much as flow, braided currents that follow warm conduits the way trout hold to seams in a river. Their fur carries a metallic sheen from the air, and their whiskers have learned to read vibration: the difference between a cooling tower’s idle hum and the thump of a valve about to open. They nest where moisture gathers but heat lingers, lining chambers behind grates with lint, copper wool shaved from cable glands, the soft gray fuzz of oxidized zinc. At night they climb into switch boxes, not to gnaw the vital lines but to bask on the transformers’ bellies, pressed to metal that remembers the day’s load. They have grown accustomed to the city’s pulse, and their hearts beat in time with it, a second, quieter rhythm beneath the thump of bulk valves.

Beetles sort themselves by temperature like guests at a banquet choosing seats by candle warmth. On transformer banks, oil-slicked carapaces glint in halos of browned light, and their antennae twitch to the infrared leakage of machinery. Some are matte black, drinking heat; others carry a chalky bloom of salt that flakes off as they walk, leaving tiny maps of mineral migration. They smell of oil and ammonia, a sharp signature that lingers in the throat. At dawn they retreat into crevices where expansion joints weep, clustering where metal and concrete fret against each other, as if the friction itself keeps them alive. Their larvae thrive in the damp seams, feeding on the slow sweat of rust, on the sweet slime that grows where water meets hot iron.

Birds treat the basin as a cage with the door open, stitching across the sky and descending to peck at ledges where condensation pearls. Their feathers are soot-soft at the edges, darkened by the oily rain that falls when the city exhales. Ravens wedge themselves into ductwork, building nests of wire offcuts and glass slag that ring like wind chimes when the fans turn. Swallows carve arcs through the visible breath of cooling towers, catching droplets that taste of copper and old rain. At twilight, their wings beat in unison, a clapping that answers the stutter of geckos on warm concrete and the click of beetles dropping from a heated flange to a cooler one below.

Geckos are the city’s punctuation, small commas clinging to walls that taste of minerals and drying oil. Their feet have learned the grip of oxidized metal; their eyes, slit into the evening, track the slow drift of infrared ghosts rising from vents. They hunt the beetles that stray too far from the warmest banks, and when they leap, the sound is a dry snap, like a seed pod breaking on pavement. At day they nap in sun-warmed hollows behind louvers, bodies pressed flat to stone that holds the memory of long afternoons.

Predators know the thermal map as a territory. Stray cats with ears like torn tickets patrol the seams where heat leaks steadily, their whiskers brushing the warm metal, their bellies lowered to catch the updraft from sumps. They hunt by patience, sitting for hours at the mouth of a drain where condensation drips, listening for the skitter of rats in the walls. Raptors nest where updrafts are strongest, in the bellies of cooling towers or the crowns of conveyor arches, their feathers edged with limescale. They dive through the visible breath of the towers, emerging with beetles or small birds, and carry their prizes to ledges where the wind polishes their kills to a dull sheen.

Even the city’s moss-people are animals of gradient. They move along paths of least resistance, through service alleys and catwalks, pausing where a grate breathes warm air, skirting sumps that gurgle like old throats. Their hands are maps of minor burns and oil stains; their shoes wear the sheen of long flow. They know which expansion joints give a particular note when the temperature drops, which inspection covers hum with the pressure of underground water. They listen for the city’s held note between machines, and when they stand together on a bridge at dusk, they look like another species of moss, sparse but patterned, taking in the metallic-sweet air with lungs that have learned to love the furnace and call it home.

All of them live by exchange. The rats trade vigilance for warmth; beetles trade their sharp scent for safe harbor in the seams; birds trade flight for the city’s condensation; geckos trade silence for the quick heat of a fleeing beetle. Predators trade patience for fat; people trade attention for passage. The fauna does not conquer the mineral city; it becomes part of its metabolism, a million small lives tuned to the city’s rhythms, nesting in switch boxes, raising broods inside hollow balusters, scavenging in the damp seams where heat leaks. They do not need wilderness to be alive. They have made their own wildness from heat, water, metal, and the insistence of wanting to live, and in that slow metabolism of exchange, the city breathes, and they breathe with it.
