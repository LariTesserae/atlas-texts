They live in the margins the way rust lives in iron: not as invaders but as inevitabilities.

The blind fish are the first to notice you, though not with eyes. Their skins are oiled leather stitched with faint phosphor along the lateral lines, and they wear their darkness like fitted gloves. They do not dart; they translate, slipping from one pressure to the next, reading the architecture by the way it thrums against their scales. Where the water pools thickest, they gather in congress, slow revolutions that braid into a single, many-bodied rope. When the pumps sigh, they shiver all at once, a school become tuning fork, and the vibration lifts silt from the floor in thin golden curtains. They are patient. They know that hunger is only a rumor until the current decides to remember it.

Closer to the metal, the beetles walk in shifts. Their bodies are lacquered plates scavenged from nameless gauges, hinges polished by generations of thorax. They travel in files so tight their shadows overlap, and where they pass, a trail of fine oxide blooms like breath on glass. They do not eat in the way you understand it; they season. They sip from condensate lenses, from the sweat of pipes, from the slow bleed of iron that weeps at every rivet. Their antennae are feathery things, calibrated to the smallest tremor, and they use them to argue. A disagreement sounds like a zipper, a consent like a struck match. When a new seam opens, they are already there, laying down a carpet of crushed rust so fine it feels like soil.

Eels occupy the transparent places, where walls have been cut away to confess their interiors. Their bellies are windows, milky with tiny organs that shift like fish in a bowl, each one a clockwork lantern. They do not swim so much as peristalsis, contracting the corridor into a series of soft chambers, as if the tunnel itself were a throat they are helping to swallow. They are sensitive to voltage the way some creatures are sensitive to scent; they taste the difference between copper and fear. When the generators growl, their skins brighten, and the light that spills from them is not illumination but confession: they know which parts of the wall are tired, which parts are lying.

Higher still, in the half-dry creases where pipe meets stone, the felt-mosses congregate. They are not plant so much as pact, a symbiosis of fungus and rust and something older that refuses a name. They grow in velvet plaques, and if you press your ear to them, you hear a sound like wool rubbing against wool, a friction that keeps the cold at bay. They hoard heat, these mosses, and they share it grudgingly, by degree. Their spores are tiny lenses that magnify the dim light, so that in patches the wall looks starry, a sky that has forgotten how to be high. They are territorial, but their borders shift weekly, negotiated by the drip of water and the patience of shade.

There are others, half-seen and more rumor than body: eel-things that have grown into the insulation, their scales turned to wire; beetles that have split their shells to let vines through, becoming jointed gardens; fish that have fused with the grate beneath them, their mouths now doors. They are not hybrids so much as agreements, settlements reached over decades of living close enough to share pulse. They do not apologize for their density. They wear it like a second skin.

The warmth that steeps them is a kind of listening. It invites their sweat and turns it into a language. The darkness does not empty when they pass; it repacks itself, tighter, as if their bodies were keys. The noise does not scatter; it gathers around them, a crowd of sound that teaches the young how to move without collision, how to yield by millimeters, by breath, by the timing of a valve’s intake.

They do not want to leave. The thought does not occur to them, any more than a rivet wants to be elsewhere. They will fill the next gap and the next, learning the shape of fullness until there is no gap left, until the below becomes the middle, and the middle becomes the edge, and every edge is lined with breathing, with rust, with the slow, patient certainty that packed is better than empty, and that to be warm and wet and roaring is to be alive in the only way that lasts.
