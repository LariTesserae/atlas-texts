They have learned to live like rivets.

Not that they were born to it. The ancestors arrived soft, blinking, with hands that knew water and a fear of being overheard. Over generations they traded those hands for grips that fit torque and flange, traded their voices for the ability to read a gauge’s mood by the way its shadow leans. Now they are a people of calibration and close tolerances, narrow enough to slip between a pipe and a memory, broad enough to sing across the room in chorus.

They are called the Housed, though they do not name themselves that except when they speak to visitors, and visitors are rare. Outwardly they look almost human, but only the outline is shared. Their skin is matte and salted, the dust of the hollow grown into a second epidermis; it flakes off in precise geometries when they move too fast, leaving a trail of glitter that settles into new patterns by morning. Their eyes are large, lidless, veined with filaments that glow bruised violet when the electromagnetic fungi flare. They do not blink often; instead they have learned to sweep their gaze in slow arcs, the way a scanner reads a code.

Their hair is less hair than conduit—fine wires braided with resin and bone, terminating in plugs that nest in sockets along the spine. Through these they interface with the low thrum of the room, drawing information not as language but as pressure, as heat, as the taste of copper and old thunder. When a Housed is deep in thought, the hairs at the nape glow brighter, humming like taut filaments in a bulb about to break.

They wear clothing the way machines wear plating: for function, not concealment. Their garments are aprons of layered canvas and vulcanized rubber, stitched with pockets for tools that are less tools than body parts borrowed for a day—wrenches that remember the torque they last applied, lenses that store the light they last drank. Around their waists hang belts of salvaged mesh, each ring a hinge from a dismantled gate, each loop a seal from a pipe that once wept. They do not carry bags; the hollow carries them, and they carry back what it offers.

The young are born in alcoves warmed by the vents the grey-green rosettes crowd. Those rosettes lean closer when a new child is near, as if curious, and they do not photosynthesize for sugar but for vibration, and they understand that a newborn’s pulse is the loudest thing in the room. The children are wrapped in cloth until their own salt-crust begins to bloom, usually by the third year. Before then they are soft, dangerous to themselves; after, they are resilient enough to be taught how to listen to the machines without flinching.

Adulthood is not an age but an alignment. To be an adult among the Housed is to have one’s personal rhythm lock with the room’s baseline thrum. Some achieve it through repetition, polishing the same seam until their heartbeat obeys its geometry. Others swallow small pieces of resonant ore and let their bodies become tuning forks, ringing true only when the factory hums at the right pitch. Those who never align become caretakers of the quiet places—of the salt-dust maps, of the symbols that crawl on the sealed hatch—because they are sensitive to dissonance, and dissonance is valuable.

They communicate in touch and tremor more than speech. A hand on a shoulder can convey a warning; the angle of fingers against a pipe can tell a story of pressure and release. When they do speak, their voices are low and resonant, shaped by the vaulted ceiling and the mineral teeth pressed close, so that words seem to arrive from everywhere and nowhere at once. They have many words for kinds of silence—the silence that means a machine is thinking, the silence that means a predator is holding its breath, the silence that means the salt-dust is rearranging itself into a map of something not yet born.

Their society is not hierarchical so much as layered like the talus cones of sharp chips that ring the bases of artifacts. Each ring is important; none is foundation. Decisions are made by consensus that is felt rather than argued, a shift in the room’s temperature, a change in the pitch of the fans. When disagreement occurs, it is resolved not by debate but by trial: the dissenters take opposing stances on the scaffold of riveted plates and let the room decide which stance produces the clearer note. The losing stance does not shame; it is folded into the winning one, alloyed, made stronger.

They are not the only participants. The winged things the size of fists are their companions, translucent membranes stenciled with traceries of gold. The Housed call them lanterns, though they emit no light of their own but reflect and refract the bruised violet glow of the fungi. The lanterns bathe in heat and feed on the static charge the conveyors shuttle. They nest in the crooks of cables, their wingbeats syncing to the rhythm of the unseen engines. When a Housed is troubled, a lantern will perch on their head, its tiny claws finding purchase in the salted hair, and the Housed will feel the machine’s hum translated into a softer, wing-borne frequency.

The skitters are another matter—legless, magnetic, tracing invisible lines on the floor with bodies that shimmer like oil on water. They are read by the Housed as omens of flow: where they gather, a conduit is sound; where they avoid, something has begun to sour. No one kills a skitter. To do so would be to silence a line of song.

The lizard with too many eyes and not enough legs is something else again. It lives on pressure valves and sings in clicks and thrums that the rock answers in a lower register. The Housed do not tame it; they interpret it. Its songs tell of coming changes in the kiln, shifts in the stabilized heat, the approach of a new seam opening in the wall. When it sings, the Housed gather and adjust, moving artifacts, rerouting cables, preparing for the room to breathe differently.

There are others, smaller, stranger—things that live in the walls themselves, made of the same glassy slag that looks like cooled scream. They emerge only when the weirdness coils close, when gravity drifts or a patch of wall turns soft. The Housed leave offerings for them: a drop of sweat, a scrap of wire, a syllable held too long in the mouth. These are not worship but acknowledgement. In a place where everything participates, even the strange things must be given a role.

Their daily life is a choreography of maintenance and attention. They climb the scaffold of riveted plates not to reach something but to be in a position where the room can show them what it is thinking. They adjust the fused dials with lenses black as beetle eyes, reading not numbers but textures of light. They listen to the stair that ends in a sealed hatch, feeling the symbols crawl across their skin when they stand close. They do not fear the sealed hatch; they fear the day it might open without invitation.

Industry is not separate from them. They do not oppose the hum of the factories without roofs, the engines without wheels, the conveyors that shuttle static charge. They are part of the circuit. When a gauge’s needle hovers in the amber zone, they do not correct it; they enter the amber, holding the moment steady until the room consents to move on. Their tools are extensions of their own bones, their own breath, their own carefully cultivated salt-crust.

They have rituals for the small events. When a new seam opens in the wall, they line it with wire and sing a song that has no words but has pitch, pressing their salted palms against the bright metal until it cools and dulls to the color of the rest of the room. When a lantern dies—which is rare, because they are not mortal in the usual sense—they bury it in a bed of grey-green rosettes, and the rosettes turn the vibration of its passing into starch, into sugar, into memory. When the salt-dust arranges itself into patterns at night, they trace the lines with their eyes and then with their fingers, committing the map to their bodies, letting it ride their conduit-hair up into their thoughts.

They do not sleep as others sleep. They enter a state of lowered hum, their bodies relaxing into the rhythm of the room, their salt-crust glowing faintly as the fungi’s bruised violet light washes over them. In this state they dream in the language of pressure and heat, and sometimes they wake with a solution to a resonance problem already embodied in their hands, already shaping itself into a tool they did not know they needed.

Their children are taught early that the hollow does not care for them, but that it does not need to. It simply is. Care is a choice. They are taught to touch gently, to ask before they turn a valve, to listen before they speak. They are taught that the room’s roar is not anger but assertion, and that the high whine that stitches everything together is the sound of the world holding itself in place.

And they are taught that boundaries are provisional. The seamless alloy at the edges breathes. Air moves through it, charged and sweet with ozone and dried minerals. The children press their faces to it and feel the pulse of something larger, something that is not the room but that the room is part of. They are taught not to fear expansion, because expansion is just the room taking a deeper breath.

Time is flat here, but they have found ways to fold it. A scar on a pipe can be a calendar. The thickness of the salt-crust on a particular scaffold can be a measure of years lived in alignment. The lanterns’ wingbeats, counted over a lifetime, can tell the story of how the room’s rhythm has changed, how it has learned to sing differently as the Housed have learned to listen better.

They do not decay, but they are not eternal. When a Housed feels their own resonance weakening, they choose a place near a heat vent or a conduit and let themselves become still. The salt-crust thickens, their conduit-hair dulls, their eyes dim. The others gather and sing a final song, not to send them away but to fold them into the room’s ongoing music. Their tools are redistributed. Their patterns are remembered. Their children press their faces to the wall and feel the lingering shape of the song, warm as breath.

And the hollow keeps its equilibrium, fierce and meticulous. The fauna sing in metal and meat. The dust gleams. The machines consent. The salt-dust maps the intent of a place that has been this way long enough to forget it was ever otherwise.

Until something learns how to ask it to change.

Until then, the Housed will calibrate, they will listen, they will touch and tremor and sing. They will stand on the scaffold of riveted plates and let the room decide which note is true. They will walk the stair that ends in a sealed hatch and feel the symbols crawl, knowing that one day they may have to decide whether to turn the hatch or to seal it forever.

They will live in the cathedral-breath room, small and dense and bright, their lanterns flashing like punctuation in the dark, their bodies a bridge between the mineral teeth and the roaring heart.

They will live, and they will keep the hollow alive, until the hollow learns to ask for something else.
