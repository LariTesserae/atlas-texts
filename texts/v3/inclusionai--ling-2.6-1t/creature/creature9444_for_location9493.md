They live in seams.

Specifically, they live in the half-millimeter between what was meant to be and what is. They are thin, long, and patient, as if stretched by the effort of holding the place together. Their bodies are plated in overlapping rings the color of wet iron, each ring etched with fine, spiraling grooves that catch the constant bead of water and carry it slowly downward, like clockwork rain. They have no faces in any sense that would comfort a human; instead, they have clusters of sensory pits at each joint, where the plates meet, tasting the mineral-moist air for shifts in pressure, for acidity, for the telltale ozone tang of a circuit about to arc.

They move by compression and release, by wedging themselves into gaps that do not quite want them and then exhaling a thin mist of condensate that slicks the metal enough to let them flow on. They can be found wedged between girder and pipe, between the ribs of cisterns and the moss that upholsters them, between the teeth of conveyors that have not meshed properly in years. When they move, they sound like a deck of cards being shuffled underwater: soft, rhythmic, inexorable.

They are maintainers, but not in the human sense. They do not repair so much as reconcile. A rivet beginning to weep rust is visited by a handful of them; they press their bodies into the corrosion, not to scrape it away, but to absorb it. Their plates darken, bloom with burnt-orange blooms, and then, days later, when they peel away, the metal beneath is left smooth, sealed with a glaze that is part enamel, part secretion, part memory. They treat cracked concrete the same way, exuding a quick-setting, resinous sweat that fills voids and then hardens to a tensile strength that feels almost like bone.

They have a complicated relationship with the water, which is everywhere. They drink it, but only to measure it. Their bodies are full of tiny, bladder-like chambers that fill and empty in precise sequences; when the level in a cistern rises a half-meter, their blisters swell in sympathy, and they migrate toward the higher places, as if carrying the water’s weight with them. When a sluice opens and the turbulence spikes, they flatten themselves against surfaces and go still, becoming additional fixtures, additional seams, until the surge passes. Then they unflatten, shudder once, and resume their rounds.

They are not hostile to the greenery, but they are jealous of it. They spend hours scraping moss from steel, not to remove it, but to thin it, to keep it from becoming a burden. In return, the moss grows thicker where they have scraped, as if acknowledging a pact. Occasionally, vines will begin to constrict a walkway or a handrail; the respond by threading themselves, dozens deep, between vine and metal, acting as living gaskets, allowing the plant to tighten without breaking, until the vine exhausts itself and sags, satisfied.

Their eyes—where they have them—are lensed and compound, set into the crowns of their heads, and they see best in low, gray light. They do not see color so much as sheen: the difference between wet and dry, between stable and about to shift. In the dimness of cloud-bottom and shielded lamp, they glow faintly, a pale, oil-slick iridescence that comes from the film of water always on their plates. To see them is to notice that a shadow is a little too precise, a little too patient.

They do not sleep, but they do congregate in the quietest parts of the place—under substations built into imitation cliffs, in the chambers where the ribbed cisterns hum. There, they press together in dense mats, plates clicking like typewriter keys, sharing moisture and information in a language of pressure and temperature. When they emerge, they are rested in the sense that a tightened bolt is rested: ready to accept more strain.

They know the place is not discovered but maintained, and so they maintain themselves. When one of them becomes too encrusted with the detritus of the place—too much rust, too much oil, too many failed repairs—it will seek out a flooded channel and submerge entirely, walking along the bottom like a beetle, until it finds a spot where the current is strong. There, it opens its plates and lets the water scour it clean, emerging lighter, paler, almost new-looking, before returning to its seam.

There are rumors, among the few who notice them, that they were built here, that they are machines in the oldest sense, made to keep the loom running. But the maintainers do not act like machines. They hesitate. They linger when a rivet sings a particular note. They return to the same spot again and again, even after it is fixed, as if checking that the fix is willing to hold. They seem, in their small, watery ways, to care.

They live in the seams, and the seams live in them. Together, they keep the high, tight, humid place from unraveling, one careful adjustment at a time, always moist, always cool, always turning.
