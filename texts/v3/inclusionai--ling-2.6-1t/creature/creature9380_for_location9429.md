They live by consent rather than conquest, and because consent is slower than law, they have had time to learn each other’s names.

There are the Root-Keepers, broad-shouldered folk whose skin has taken on the texture of river-smoothed bark and the color of iron-rich loam. They do not plant themselves; they remember where to stand. Their hands are maps of callus and crease, each line a record of a season when they held a leaning beam or persuaded a thick stem to arch into a bridge. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices carry the weight of wet stone. They negotiate with walls the way others might counsel a friend—patiently, with pauses long enough for the other party to change its mind. If a root wanders into a conduit, a Root-Keeper will coax it aside with a hum that seems to come from their chests, not their throats, and the root will remember and grow politely.

Among them move the Steam-Singers, slight and quick, with fingers stained in the soot of a hundred small fires. Their eyes are bright, almost amphibian, adapted to the dim lanes where bioluminescent moss glows like drowned chandeliers. They do not repair machines so much as remind them how to want to work. When a pump sighs, a Steam-Singer will lean close and answer in a language of clicks and hisses that is partly mathematics and partly lullaby. It is said they can taste alloys, that they know by tongue whether a joint is tired or merely contemplative. Their hair is often braided with copper wire and dried flowers, and when they pass, the air shifts subtly, as if the place itself has inhaled.

There are also the Leaf-Librarians, who spend their days suspended in the upper vaults, cataloging the skylight coins and the shadows they cast. They wear robes that seem spun from lichen and spider silk, and their movements are so economical that sometimes it is unclear whether they have moved at all or whether the world has shifted around them. They record not in books but in patterns of knots and dye on living cloth, each stitch a season, each color a memory of rainfall. When a newcomer arrives, a Leaf-Librarian will study them as one studies a new constellation, noting how their shadow falls, how their breath steams, what faint tremors they send through the root-bridges beneath their feet.

Children here are taught first to listen, second to touch gently, third to ask permission of thresholds. They play games of silence and texture, learning to tell a healthy beam from a weary one by the way it warms under a palm. They know that beetles with lacquered copper carapaces are signs of honest labor, and that oil-slick birds bring news from the distant canopies. When they grow old enough, they choose a path not by trade but by tempo—some are patient as rust, others quick as condensation, and all understand that to rush is to risk mishearing the town’s soft speech.

There are caretakers without titles, people whose names are known only to the pumps they tend or the gutters they clear. They leave offerings of water and song at the mouths of cisterns, and they polish handrails until they gleam like slow rivers. There are traders who deal not in coin but in balance—exchanging a length of cured vine for a promise not to hurry a hinge, bartering a jar of filtered dew for a story that the wall behind the forge has been trying to remember.

Even the animals, scarce as they are, have their roles. The oil-slick birds keep watch from the woven towers, their soft fluty notes threading through the steam’s sigh. The lacquered beetles are taken as quiet companions, walking the warm manifolds like monks on a circuit, reminding everyone that patience can shine.

And then there are the Listeners, a kind of vocation more than a calling. They stand in the plazas of packed earth and feel the town breathe. They notice when a corridor has lengthened, when a pipe has learned to whisper, when a wall has begun to dream its metallic dreams. They do not cure these small strange gifts; they make room for them, naming gates and leaving patches unpainted so that metal can sleep and memory can rise.

Together, they make a people who are less a population than a conversation. They move through a town that is scaffold and leaf, steam and sap, and they inhabit it with the same attentiveness that the town gives them—cool air, damp, generous spaces, a green and humming balance that asks only to be kept company, and to be listened to, and to be allowed to remember, in its own slow way, that it is alive.
