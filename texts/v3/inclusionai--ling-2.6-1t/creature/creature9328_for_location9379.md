They are basin-shaped people, or have become so—bodies bowed just enough to fit beneath low lintels, spines remembering the curve of the bowl. Their bones are light, almost porous, as if the mineral chill has crept inward and taught marrow to hold air instead of heat. Skin is pale with a faint, mica-flecked sheen, as though the place’s wet stone has migrated into them, settling into pores like dust on water. When the sun stands overhead, their pupils tighten into pinpricks that vanish too quickly to be useful; they do not squint so much as stop seeing forward, turning their attention to surfaces instead, reading faces and walls as the same bright grammar.

They dress in wetted leather and woven silver-stalk fibers, garments that stiffen with cold and soften with moisture, changing their fit hour by hour. Their feet are broad and callused, accustomed to the sighing paste of the ground, able to sense when the floor is tightening at night or loosening with the return of damp. They move with a deliberation that looks like etiquette but is mostly physics: too sudden a step and the courtyard trembles; too much weight in one place and the water pooled there shivers out of its skin.

Their society has no names that stay. A person is called by what they steward while they stand near it—the keeper of the southeast pan, the singer of the threshold—and when they step away, the name dissolves like breath in bright air. Children are taught less history than pattern: which seams hold water, which lintels map the basin, which silences are dense enough to lean upon. Language is a musical compression, vowels thinned to threads, consonants clipped like stone on stone. What they say is short, but they say it more than once, letting the echo do the carrying.

For work they have the burrowers, creatures like long-legged gloves that live in sleeves of cloth and slip beneath the wet crust, turning soil into conversation. There are winged folds—glossy, angular, with feathers like polished slate—that alight on eaves and drink glare, folding themselves so precisely they seem to disappear into corners. Ground-runners scuttle along baseboards, raising a dry whisper that is considered a form of news. Predators, small and many-eyed, patrol with a restraint that looks like manners; they are fed by invitation, and when the invitation is not given, they content themselves with dust and echoes.

Metal among them is tiny and shy, kept in constant motion so its edges never grow proud—a pin that holds a cloak at the shoulder, a clasp shaped like the basin’s rim, a blade that knows when to be a tool and when to be an adornment. Fires burn in clay-lined pits whose ducts taste smoke and decide, on their own, when to let light remain undiluted. There are no locks, only agreements written in wear: a dip in a step that means this threshold is often crossed in darkness; a smoothing of stone that means water is welcome here at this hour.

Weirdness coats them like a second skin. Water on their tongues sometimes climbs before it falls, as if deciding between them and the air. Their shadows lag at noon, stretching back toward morning as if reluctant to leave brightness. Some doors open into rooms already full of people, and the new arrival is simply assigned a place by proximity and need, as one assigns a stone to a wall. They do not question it. They have noticed that memory doubles here—one self lives an event while another self remembers it already—and they have stopped crowding their thoughts with worry.

Their bodies carry the basin’s turbulence in small flurries. A sudden softening of the skin around the knuckles means rain is coming, though rain is rare; a ringing in the hollow of the elbow means the courtyard below is turning to marsh, briefly, before sealing itself. They adapt without ceremony, shifting beds, opening hidden channels in floor seams, accepting extra bodies in corners that did not seem to exist an hour before.

Warmth is their scarcest currency, and so they press against one another not from need but from sense, sharing heat like shared tasks. When they sleep, they lie in long braids, limbs interlaced across thresholds, so that the building itself becomes a kind of sleeper, dreaming in many small pulses. The cold is not defeated; it is given jobs to do, channeled into mortar, into marrow, into the bright, thin song the wet stone sings at the brightest hour.

At that hour they gather without calling, their eyes clever, their voices folded back into the hush, and they let the basin glow through them—each body a small, temporary lens, each life a patient stitch in the bright, turbulent grammar of wet stone and careful life, turning quietly, crowded, cold, and exactly itself in its high, cupped bowl.
