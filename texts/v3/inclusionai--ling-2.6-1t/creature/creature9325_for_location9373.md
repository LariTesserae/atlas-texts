They live in accumulations.  

Not in houses, not in burrows, not in the sense of a thing possessed and defended, but in layers. A person here is less a body than a site where densities agree to pause. Their skin is the color of old solder, dull where the light skates across it, darker where it has absorbed hours without being asked to give them back. Veins show through not as blue or green but as fine traceries of milky quartz, as if the blood had long ago traded its hurry for crystal and never regretted it. When they move, small grains of mica fall from their cuffs and collar, a dusting of chronology shaking loose.

They are many. More than the eye wants to count. Not because they swarm—they do not—but because they have agreed to be numerous without crowding. To walk among them is to pass through a forest of careful gestures. Each one carries a tool that is also an apology: a blade of chipped flint held as lightly as a secret; a spindle of braided mineral fiber that hums when the air is still; a pouch of pale powder gathered from the trails of grazers, which they sprinkle at thresholds like a sacrament of forgetting. They repair things that are not broken. They polish surfaces that are already shining. Their labor is a kind of punctuation.

They speak, but softly, and only after listening to the shape of the silence. Their language is full of measurements that do not appear on any map: the thickness of a shadow at a certain hour, the weight of a name that has been carried too long, the distance between one breath and the next when the wind is absent. Arguments are rare and, when they happen, are conducted by rearranging small objects: a ring of stones, a spiral of dust, a line of beads that each remembers where it came from. To win is to make the pattern comfortable enough that everyone can step over it without meaning to.

Their children are born already patient. Before they learn to walk, they learn to settle. They play games of alignment, stacking shards of silica-stalk until the stack remembers it was never separate. They ask questions like “How long is a stillness?” and accept answers in textures: smooth, rough, cool, or the kind of cool that feels like it has been borrowed. A child who laughs releases a sound like glass beads falling into a bowl, and the adults smile as if they have been given something they were too polite to ask for.

They bury their dead by giving them back to the fauna. The body is wrapped in cords of mineral fiber and left on a low mesa where six-legged grazers will find it. The grazers do not eat flesh; they eat the memory of movement, leaving behind only a fine white powder and the faint, warm impression of a name. The mourners return later to collect the powder and press it into the seams of their own tools, so that the dead become useful without being summoned. Grief, in this way, is made to be carried without weight.

They know the weirdness. They do not name it, but they account for it. A doorway may be avoided for generations if its shadow points the wrong way. A ridge that looks passable in the morning may, by afternoon, refuse to be arrived at. They navigate by small certainties: the smell of iron on the tongue, the static that lifts the hair on their arms, the way a landmark sometimes looks younger than it did an hour ago. When reality loosens, they loosen with it, stretching their edges just enough to slip through and then knitting themselves back together without comment.

They have gods, but the gods are not above. The gods are below, in the veins of ore, in the hum of the ground, in the perfect stillness that is not empty but full. Offerings are made by not taking: by leaving a blade where it was found, by walking around a cairn instead of adding to it, by saying a name and then not saying it again for a long time. The gods answer in polish, in the way a surface begins to reflect more than it should, in the way a silence becomes a presence that fits exactly into the curve of a shoulder.

There is among them a figure who is not quite one of them. She stands at the margins and wears a cloak the color of tarnished silver, woven from the wings of winged things that have decided to rest. Her face is difficult to hold in memory; it changes depending on how long you look. Some say she is the first person to have been eroded into elegance. Others say she is the structure on the spine of glass, grown legs to learn what it feels like to wait. She does not lead, but she is followed. When she passes, the fauna step aside without being asked. The dust settles in patterns that look like maps of places that do not yet exist.

They do not fear the future because they have folded it into their tools. Every chipped blade carries a memory of the hand that made it and the hands that will take it. Every braid remembers the direction it was turned. They know that stillness is stone, and they have learned to sit on it without breaking it. They do not ask the plateau to be kind. They ask only to be allowed to be numerous, to be dense with presence, to live in a place where nothing rises or falls and where, for once, that is enough.

And when they stand together at the edge of the plateau and look out over the steps carved by forgetting, they look like a loom that has stopped weaving but refuses to be unwound. The light is normal. The coolness is a blanket. The density is a crowd that has learned how to whisper. And the single structure on the spine of glass is waiting, its shadow pointing in a direction that does not exist yet, and they know that if it ever arrives, they will know how to live inside it without being asked to change.
