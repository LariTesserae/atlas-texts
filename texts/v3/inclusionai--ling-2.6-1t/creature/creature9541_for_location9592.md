They do not arrive here so much as condense. The fauna are what the altitude decides to keep after it has skimmed away weight, hurry, and certainty.

There are the Lichen-Readers, small as breath, that map the calcium ghosts left by exhalations. They are furred in filaments that taste the milky air, and they move in pulses: not running but blooming across surfaces, leaving behind a trail of dim, edible mathematics. They do not eat the plants; they eat the decisions the plants have made, gnawing the margins where one stem agreed to graft itself to another. Their tracks look like erosion reversed, tiny ridges that rise where they pass, as if the ground is rehealing its old wounds behind them.

Higher, in the turbine-knots, live the Gear-Spiders, though spider is a courtesy borrowed from elsewhere. They are jointed from grown parts—axles fused into knees, springs that remember being veins. They do not build webs; they maintain. When a hollow trunk’s hum drops pitch, they swarm into its throat, adjusting tension with mandibles that click in the same key as the wood. Their silk is not silk but glass-thread, spun from the same cold that hardens epidermis, and it hums when the wind is still. They store the surplus sound in their abdomens, distending into amber drones that pulse when night—such as it is—thickens.

Closer to the valleys, where the living mass bunches into town-sized clusters, the Root-Herders appear. They are long and patient, their bodies segmented like terraces, each segment cupping a different vintage of moisture. They do not graze so much as irrigate, pressing their flanks against the spongy seams until the runnels accept a redirection. Where they have passed, the vegetation softens into corridors, not because it was cut but because it was persuaded. Their young are born as cysts that hatch only when a slope has agreed to hollow itself into a basin; until then, they ride in the parent’s segments, learning which gradients whisper and which shout.

There are also the Smudge-Foxes, though fox is a rumor they allow to linger. They are less animal than interruption, shadows that move when the foliage blinks. Their fur is a collage of borrowed textures—bark that remembers being bark, metal that has forgotten its rigidity—and they pass through grown gates without disturbing the friction-sound. They scavenge not for food but for stillness, gathering pockets of hush where the plants have stopped their slow quarrels. When they curl into hollows, the cold deepens locally, and the stems nearby harden into glass, preserving the shape of their sleep.

Above all, unseen but felt, are the Altitude-Birds, which may or may not be birds. They are turbulence given outline, wings that are less feathers than folded drafts. They do not fly so much as cause the air to tilt, carving low paths through the pewter lid. Their calls are not sound but pressure changes, felt in the teeth and the lungs, encouraging the vegetation to rearrange itself into funnels and flues. When they pass, the internal lights of the stems brighten, as if the plants are answering a census.

The fauna do not belong to the place; they are its manners. They negotiate with the living industry in languages of pulse, erosion, and restraint. They are sparse not because the land cannot hold them, but because density would be noise, and noise must be muffled. Together they are the reason the silence is crowded: each creature a choice about when to speak, when to bite, when to let the cold edit one more time.
