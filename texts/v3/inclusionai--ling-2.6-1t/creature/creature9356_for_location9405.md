What lives here does not live in the sense of ownership, but in the sense of tenancy that has forgotten to expire. Life is not a guest exactly; it is the pocket learning to breathe through borrowed lungs.

The jelly-skinned gliders are the first to notice you. They do not arrive so much as uncurl from the air itself—translucent bells with skeletal filaments of silica that ring at frequencies you feel in your molars. They are not swimmers nor fliers but drifters of the in-between, riding the slow convection of a sky-lake that refuses to fall. When they pulse, which they do when startled or curious, their bodies glow like wet lanterns, and their trailing tendrils write temporary equations in the air that dissolve before you can read them. They have no eyes because light here is too aggressive to be trusted; instead they read the world through the taste of vibrations, sipping the thunder that rolls off the water-ceiling. They are predators of stillness: if you hold your breath too long, one may drift close enough to press a filament against your wrist, checking whether you are stone or story.

Beneath them, in the droplets the size of pumpkins, live the fin-winged fish. They are compact as fists, armored in opalescent scales that flake into glitter when they turn too sharply. Their fins have flattened into scoops that catch drafts, allowing them to dart from water-pearl to water-pearl without ever touching the floor. They are not flying fish; they are fish that have decided falling is a mistake and have corrected it by turning their fins into wings that work only in this specific hush. They feed on the blue moss that lines the crevices of floating stone, nipping at the circuitry-like veins that pulse in time with the water’s roar. When startled, they can flick their tails and send themselves spinning in a helix, trailing droplets that freeze into tiny chandeliers before melting again. They have mouths like old hinges and eyes like tarnished coins—eyes they keep half-closed against the brightness of sunbeams refracted through the lake above.

The vegetation is a form of argument with the place. The blue moss is the loudest voice: it thrives on noise, drinking the bell-sound that hardens into texture, lining cracks like copper wire that has learned to bleed. It glows faintly violet when the pocket is agitated, and dimly gold when the water hesitates in its fall. There are also spike-plants that anchor themselves to nothing, growing spires of hollow crystal that hum when wind passes through, creating secondary sounds that bounce into shelves of frozen thunder. These plants are impatient; they grow while you watch, shivering as if undecided whether to become flute or spear.

Among the built things, the forge-keepers move with a rhythm borrowed from the water. They are squat, dense-bodied beings with hands like trowels and faces that seem carved from the same chert as the walls, though warmer. They do not speak; they tap, and their taps are words that settle into the stone. They tend a forge of stone and bladder-bellows, coaxing heat from the residual birth-heat of the pocket itself. When they work, the quartz veins in the walls snap and spark in applause. Their work is half-finished by design; a blade is considered polite only if it is still negotiating its edge, a pot only if it remembers how to be clay. If you ask them a question, they answer by shaping something that almost answers it, then leaving it where the vibration can finish the thought.

Higher up, where the stair climbs into nothing, there is the chair-keeper. It is less a person than a permission: a figure wrapped in stitched hide and copper wire, sitting in the rusted chair as though waiting for a question to arrive. It has tools set out—sinew, flint, a coil of wire that looks like a sleeping eel—but it does not touch them unless the pocket trembles. When the water overhead shudders and sheets peel away like curtains, the chair-keeper stands and stretches, and the chair itself seems to exhale. Some say the chair-keeper is the pocket’s memory of hospitality; others say it is what is left of anyone who tried to finish something here.

There are others that come and go like weather. Gliders that shed their skins and leave them floating like parachutes until they dissolve. Fish that leap not for food but for the pure joy of changing which element they betray. Moss that wanders in creeping mats, seeking the loudest sound to drink. Even the tools seem animate when unattended: a hammer that nudges a half-formed bowl toward the edge of a stone, as if encouraging it to roll away and become a hill.

Life here is loud in its quietness. It is the glaze that forms on a blade left to think too long. It is the bell-song that drips from the ceiling and pools in your hair. It is the persistent cool that clings to forearms, not cold enough to bite but enough to make you remember that warmth is a decision. The fauna and flora are not survivors but collaborators: they conspire with the unfinished, the rising, the suspended, to keep the pocket from deciding it is done.

If you stand very still, they will gather—not to look at you, but to see whether you are settling. The gliders will drift close. The moss will brighten. The chair-keeper will tap once, twice, and set a flint blade humming against your pulse. The fish will scatter into rainbows that taste of copper. And the water above will hesitate, just for a breath, as if deciding whether to fall or float, as if waiting to learn from your stillness how to be still itself.

Here, to live is to remain unfinished, to stay noisy enough to be useful, and to remember that rising is easier—and kinder—than staying.
