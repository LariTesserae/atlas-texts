Here is what lives there.

***

They do not live on the land so much as within its breath.  
The people are creatures of practiced density, bodies shaped by close air and closer stone. Their shoulders are narrow, their hips compact, feet splayed and callused like old roots that have learned to grip rather than grow. Skin is not pale but patinated, a thin glaze of oxidized dust and sun that seals in moisture like wax on fruit. When they sweat, it does not run; it pearls in the creases of their necks and wrists, then vanishes into thirsty cloth or is licked away with careful tongues. Their eyes are pale, not from lack of light but from surplus of it—eyes that have learned to filter brightness into fine, useful grain.

Their settlements do not sprawl but nest.  
Houses are knots in the rock, quarried from the same strata they stand upon, so that walls seem less built than exhaled. Shutters are never quite closed; they hover in a permanent half-state, admitting light in careful slices while keeping the furnace at arm’s length. At dawn, smoke from stubby chimneys drifts sideways, not rising but creeping, as if gravity here hesitates. The cairns at the passes are not monuments but conversations—stones added by passers, each one warmed by palms before being set, so that the pile remembers hands as much as it remembers balance.

What they eat is a ledger of patience.  
They harvest the wiry grasses before the seeds harden, grind them into flour that smells of iron and thyme. They prize the pale succulents but do not uproot them; instead they score the outer blades and collect the dew that wells from the wounds overnight, a thin, sweet sap that ferments quickly into something sharp and clarifying. From the tin-bright seeps they strain algae, blue lace turned into paste, then dried into wafers that crackle between teeth like cooling metal. Meat is rare and respected: small lizards with translucent bellies, birds whose salt-crusted feathers are plucked and braided into cords for luck, the lean mammals taken only when they offer themselves by misstep or age. Nothing is wasted; even bones are ground and mixed into mortar so that the built things carry a fraction of the living inside them.

Their industry is a quiet covenant with the hot veins beneath.  
They maintain the conduits that gleam like captured sun, patching leaks with a resin that smells of pine and burnt sugar. On the platforms bolted to the cliffs, they tend engines that idle in low, chest-deep registers, learning each machine’s moods by the tremor in the floor and the color of its breath. They climb not for spectacle but for stewardship, fingers finding holds that have been polished by generations of similar grips. When the wind comes, they secure the wire grids that lace the ridges, humming as they work, as if their own bodies were part of the circuit, carrying the place’s heat from limb to limb.

Their days are partitioned by the slow calculus of temperature.  
At first light, they are abroad, moving along ledges where the fauna is still drowsy, collecting what the night has condensed. By midmorning they retreat to the shaded guts of canyons, there to repair tools, twist fibers into rope, listen to the dry click of metal settling. They trade in small, precise gestures: a tilt of the chin, a tap of a stone against stone, a length of wire coiled and offered. Speech is not absent but reduced, as if words might raise the heat further. What cannot be said is carried in posture—a lean that indicates safety, a pause that means wait, a blink that marks the edge of a seep worth trusting.

They know the weirdness without naming it.  
They have seen dust flow uphill for a breath and learned not to shout when it happens. They have watched condensation form shapes like letters and understood that this is the place remembering how to speak, not them. When shadows in cracks seem deeper than they should, they mark them with a smear of clay and avoid them until the mark fades. They do not fear these inflections; they fold them into routine, as one folds a blade and sets it on a belt.

At night, they do not sleep so much as let their senses drift.  
The air cools only slightly, but surfaces exhale thin, spectral veils that cling to skin like moth wings. The engines drop to nearly nothing, and the land’s hum rises to fill the gap—a vibration felt in teeth and sternum. In that interval, predators move with even less sound, and the people move too, shifting weight from foot to foot, eyes open, letting the dark outside match the dark inside, until the slopes surrender their stored heat and the cycle begins again.

They are not conquerors of the steep world but its careful tenants.  
They live where water visits but does not stay, where erosion is an elder and vegetation bristles like praise, where metal and muscle share the same thirst. They have become dense with attention, dense with life, dense with the quiet hum of keeping things going. In the furnace of noon and the veil of night, they are not diminished by the heat—they are lit by it, defined by it, made unmistakably themselves in the packed, breathing accord of stone, creature, and wire.
