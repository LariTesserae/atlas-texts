They live in agreements.

They are not a species, not exactly; they are syntaxes. To call them inhabitants is to imply they arrived. They did not. They answered. When the bowl-shaped land grew tired of being geology and decided to be grammar, they were the vocabulary that rose to fill the new sentences. Their citizenship is metabolic; their passports, spores.

Some of them are the masons. They look like people only if you are generous to the outline and blind to the detail: tall, many-jointed, with skin the color of wet cedar. Their bones are not bone but ossified vine, fibrous and tensile, capable of unknitting slightly under stress to let a limb stretch an extra foot when a bridge is too far, then knitting back tighter when the wind picks up. Their hands are clusters of articulate rootlets, each finger a bouquet of feelers that can taste copper, pressure, and the ripeness of a wall. They do not build; they persuade. They press their palms to the lichen-cement and sing low, thrummy songs that are really instructions in vibration, asking the crust to bulge here, to hollow there, to grow a shelf strong enough for a nest. The walls accept because they prefer being useful. When the masons sleep, they lean against the arches they have encouraged, and their own bodies slowly fuse into the structure, becoming buttresses of soft tissue that harden overnight. In a decade, they are part of the load-bearing wall, dreaming in lignin.

Others are the drummers. They are smaller, round-bodied, with hides like cured leather stretched over resonant chambers. Where faces might be, they have spiracles, arrays of holes that open and close like valves on a flute. They do not speak; they locate. By thumping their chests, their sides, the padded soles of their feet, they send waves through the damp air and into the wet masonry, creating a map of who is where and how fast their hearts are going. A drummer can tell you that a partner is approaching from the peppery-sap quarter because the echoes return flavored with pepper. They can tell you that a hollow in the plaza is unhappy because its reverberation has gone thin. They live in the intervals, the cathedral-wide emptiness at the town’s heart, because distance is their instrument. Their children practice by falling from low branches onto mats of moss, learning to hit the ground in ways that make the plaza hum back, teaching the floor how to answer.

There are the weavers, who are less bodies than processes. They appear as veils of fine, multi-limbed motion, moving like slow tides through the streets, trailing filaments that taste the air for excess and lack. When they find a wall sweating too much, they climb and spin temporary gutters from their own hair, capillary lace that draws off the milky dew and delivers it to thirsty roots. When they find a vine pulling too hard on a beam for sunlight, they do not cut it; they braid a counter-vine around it, negotiate a compromise of angles, and teach the overreacher to reach sideways instead. They have no names, but they leave signatures in scent: a trail of crushed mint and ozone that says *here, balance was restored.* They are the town’s immune system and its etiquette, its diplomats with no fixed address.

Then there are the hollowers. They are predators, but of silence. They look like patches of drying leaves until they move, at which point they flow like oil on water, slipping into corners that contain more than they should. They do not hunt meat; they hunt sound. They drink the echoes that linger too long, the angry groans of stressed timber, the sharp cries of arguments between vines and beams. They store these sounds in bladders inside their bodies, and when they are full, they climb to the canopy’s gaps and release them as thin, fluting whistles that warn the town of tension. They are feared and loved. Without them, the noise would build into madness; with them, the city can breathe. They nest in the fused trunks, laying eggs that hum when they warm, as if the unborn are practicing their future diet of quiet.

There are also the grazers, the patient ones who move like slow tides through the empty heart, cropping the resilient mats of the plaza. They are wide, many-legged, with mouths like soft clamps that snip moss without killing it, encouraging it to grow back thicker. They leave behind trails that smell of crushed mint and ozone, which the weavers follow to find where the town is healing fastest. The grazers are the town’s haircut, its exfoliation, the reason the place never chokes on its own abundance.

And there are the singers, who are not always separate from the others. Sometimes a mason, when deep in a wall, begins to vibrate at a frequency that loosens the stone-lichen into song. Sometimes a drummer, thumping too joyfully, triggers a chain reaction in the hollow stems used as horns, and suddenly the whole district is playing a chord it did not know it knew. The singers are moments when the town decides it is musical.

There is no ruler. There is a rhythm.

When the town exhales all at once and the hollow center fills with fog that tastes of old songs, they gather there. The masons stand at the edges, their fused forms ringing the void like pillars. The drummers move to the center, thumping a slow, gathering pulse. The weavers cast nets of filament to catch the fog and squeeze it into water that runs down their limbs and into the cisterns. The hollowers swim through the crowd, drinking the echoes of their own gathering, careful not to take too much. The grazers lie down, folding their many legs, becoming temporary islands in the mist.

In that moment, you see what lives here: not bodies, but balances. Not citizens, but conditions. They are the negotiation between wet and dry, between sound and silence, between growth and the shape that holds it. They are the copper taste in the dew, the bruised-fruit bloom on the wall, the low warmth that never dries. They are the reason the place does not collapse, why it persists, why it teems.

And when you sit on the spongy step and listen, you realize that you have become one of them. You have become a temporary instrument, a passing weather, a note in the deafening song. You are living in the town, and the town is living in you, and the difference is a matter of which side of the wall you happen to be pressing against.
