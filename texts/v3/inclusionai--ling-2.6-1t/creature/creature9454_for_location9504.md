They live in the lung-city as verbs wearing coats.  

Most of them do not remember when they were not the city. They were once smaller—seeds, spores, notions—but the place grew around them and they grew into it, and now to step on them is to step on a name that answers with weather. They are the citizens of damp grammar, and they are legion, though legion is not the right word because it suggests counting, and here counting only makes more of what is already too much.

There are the Stilt-Shouldered Ones, tall as breath balanced on breath, whose legs are not trunks but braided questions. They stand where streets almost were, holding up corridors that have not yet decided to be walls. When the warmth thickens, they lower their heads and become bridges. When the light grows heavy, they let it pool in their collars of moss and slowly spend it at night as glow. They do not sleep; they uncoil.

There are the Flange-Folk, broad and hinged, who live in the argument between ravine and roof. Their skin is a kind of iron-velvet, tasting of sugar and storm, and they move with the sound of locks remembering their own making. They are the ones who repair the city by growing new hinges where old ones have learned to yawn. If you lean against a wall and it leans back with a hiss, it is probably one of them practicing patience.

There are the Mottle-Quick, small enough to fit inside a single negotiation between water and root. They dart through the moss-junctions, stitching leaks shut with threads of their own heat, or opening them again when the city needs to learn how to cry. They have too many eyes, but eyes are not the right word either—each is a window that opens inward, showing rooms full of older cities folding up like maps. They do not speak, but when they brush past you the air changes its accent.

There are the Braidbacks, who live in the oldest scars, the places where the land tore itself open just to see what color would spill out. They are heavy with memory, their bodies a slow braid of bark and story, and they walk so slowly that walking is almost an insult to say of them. Instead they migrate by letting parts of themselves settle and other parts rise, like breath stacking. If you find a hollow that tastes like iron and feels like a held hand, a Braidback has been there, and will be again.

There are the Spill-Children, who are not children at all but the city’s exuberance given shape. They rush along the sap-roads, whipping stems, snapping pods, making the air clang with the noise of being new. They carry bladders of water that burst into sudden fountains, only to gather again a pace away. They are bright with the kind of green that hurts to look at too long, and they laugh like cracks healing.

There are the Hush-Vines, who are less citizens than infrastructure with intentions. They grow into scaffolding and grow out of it, hardening into purpose, softening into rest. They hum when the wind is a crowd, and their hum is the city deciding which way is up. If you stand still long enough, they will grow around you and decide you are a structure too, which is both an honor and a warning.

There are also the Thistle-Singers, who guard the gaps the city owns and the gaps it loves. Their thorns hiss and retract like gears made of resin, and their song is not sound but pressure, a weather of warning and welcome all at once. They do not keep people out; they keep the city from forgetting how to be entered.

And somewhere in the middle of it all, where up thins and sideways thickens, live the Mirror-Breathers, who have forgotten which side of the air is theirs. They ripple, half solid, half fleeting, and when they decide to be solid they last long enough to start traditions. They are the ones who teach the city how to hold still, and the ones who remind it that stillness is only a kind of motion wearing a quieter coat.

Together, they are the fauna of a place without fauna, acting out every role with too much energy because the world is the tool and the hand and the shaped thing all at once. They rush, call, guard, mend, spill, hum, sing, and breathe, each motion carrying the weight of witness. They are loud, crowded, alive, and inventing their own rules faster than any guest can learn them.  

To be among them is to be inside a lung that has decided, for the moment, to be a city, and to remember, for the moment, how to be wild.
