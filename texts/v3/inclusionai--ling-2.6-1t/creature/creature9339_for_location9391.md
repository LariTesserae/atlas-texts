They live in the grammar of the slope.

Not a species, exactly, but a citizenship. A thousand minor lineages that have agreed, over long millennia, to be altitude’s method of holding water without becoming lake. They are the tenants of a city built by patience, and they wear its architecture like clothing woven from mist and mineral.

There are the Slick-People, for whom skin is a brief weather-event. They are low and many-jointed, their bodies matte with a dew that is not dew but exhalation, a constant sweating of salts that taste of iron and old mica. They creep along the stone faces in the early mornings when the cold is still pooled in the hollows, their footsteps soft as absorbed rain. Their legs are short and numerous, arranged less for walking than for anchoring; they do not conquer the slope, they marry it. When they pause, their bodies dim, becoming almost indistinguishable from the lichen-continents that map the rocks in gray-green. They live on the thin films that rise through the bedrock’s capillaries, drinking the mountain from below, and in return they leave behind tiny, polished depressions—cisterns for the city, each one a perfect cup.

Above them, in the widely spaced ranks of pale grass, dwell the Stem-Spires. They are not animals, but they move. Slowly. Over years, decades, they flex and straighten, learning how to stand in the open air without crowding. Their blades end in sharp, crystalline dew-tips that catch light before falling, and these tips are their eyes—facets that see not shape but moisture, not predator but passage. They communicate by thinning or thickening, by making more room or less, teaching the eye how to measure distance by the fading of their ranks. When the wind moves in sheets, they bow in unison, a quiet hush that sounds like paper settling. They are the city’s planners, arranging space so that weather can flow through it like a population assembling and dissolving.

In the vents where warmth seeps up, the Huddle-Cushions cluster. Evergreen, tight as fists, they are the keepers of heat. They remember warmth in their cores and release it in small, rhythmic pulses, like slow heartbeats. Around them, the ground is softer, a high-saturation of information: mats of roots, fallen needles, peat-skins layered like palimpsests. The Huddles do not grow upward; they grow inward, compressing time into density. They are the archive of the high place, storing the memory of every frost and thaw in the geometry of their rings. To rest beside them is to feel the slope decide how to hold you, giving without yielding.

There are also the Drift-Cloth, beings of almost no substance, who live in the low cloud-ceiling. They are the wet made semi-solid, the air turned into a second ground. They pool in hollows at night, dark syrups of chill, and by noon they have been lifted by the slow boil of the sun into wavering columns. From a distance they look like stains that darken, then gleam, then finally move—the true streams of the place, threading through tussock and stone like stitches that come undone only to be retied downstream. They are the messengers, carrying the smell of wet stone and distant lichen from one ridge to the next, arranging the horizon not as a line but as a gradient, a region where land softens into haze.

And threading through all of them, rare and deliberate, are the Margin-Walkers. Small, unobtrusive, they use the architecture of stems and stone to appear and disappear, their tracks faint and quickly softened by moisture. They are not predators; they are editors. They trim the edges, nudge a stone, test a wall built by generations of frost and thaw, and in doing so they keep the city from becoming finished. They know better than to insist. Their rarity makes the place feel like a shared interior, where the dominant inhabitants are the processes themselves: exchange, shelter, passage, patience.

Altogether, they form a single organism of slope and wet and cool air. They are the fauna of a city built without tools, governed by biology and gravity and time. They do not fight; they align. They do not consume; they clarify. And when you stand among them, you feel the vastness arrange you into its long-settled stack, thin and temporary, another layer in the high place’s learned habit of holding water without ever becoming lake.
