They are the patient ones, the ones who have learned to breathe in spite of the air, or perhaps because of it.

Down on the raw stone, where the water laps with a deliberation that feels like measuring, the fauna move as if choreographed by the memory of gravity. The lobster-things are the first you notice, because they break the smoothness of the flood with geometry. Their bodies are casings of riveted iron, segmented like medieval armor that has forgotten chivalry and kept only the mathematics of defense. Each step is a negotiation: a piston exhales, a joint locks, and the foot settles with a click that is swallowed almost instantly by the larger roar. Their clockwork hearts are not engines so much as disciplined habits, wound once and trusted to keep time. They do not seek food; they seem to harvest momentum itself, converting the resistance of the thickened water into a slow, gliding progress across the trachyte spines. When they pause, their claws open to reveal not pincers but lenses of polished brass, still and dark, catching the sourceless light and tossing it into the gloom like thrown coins.

Above them, the jellyfish bloom. They are constellations made local, drifts of glass and filament that pulse with a rhythm suspiciously like listening. Each bell is a struck note held in suspension; the sound is thin and bright, a chime that fractures against the water and reforms downstream. They do not sting, but they are not gentle. To touch one is to touch a circuit closed, a momentary completion that hums in your teeth. They move in swarms that trace invisible currents, mapping the hidden architecture of the flood. When a machine coughs steam somewhere below, the jellyfish recoil and then reorganize, their choir shifting key as if translating the vent’s complaint into music. They are abundant because stillness would be a kind of death for them; their persistence is a performance repeated so many times it has become indistinguishable from existence.

Deeper, where the water darkens into a blue that looks almost solid, the leviathans cruise. They might be submarines that grew tired of purpose and decided to become landscape. Their hulls are mapped with accretions of glass-barnacle and rust-flower, each patch a history too slow to read. They move with a certainty that suggests the world has been carved to fit their passage, channels worn not by erosion but by the simple insistence of their presence. Their eyes, when they surface to regard the edge of the basin, are not eyes but sensor arrays grown patient, blinking only when the pressure shifts. They sing, though it is a sound felt more than heard, a vibration that travels up through the water and into the plates of your ribs, a reminder that you are inside something that is itself inside something larger.

Among these, the smaller oddities persist. Fish-shaped things of sheet metal and solder dart between the pipes, their movements twitchy and electric, as if powered by jokes they refuse to explain. Crab-things with legs like calipers pick at the fungal mats, not eating but dismantling, reducing the pale ash to geometries that settle into the stone and are forgotten. There are birds, or things that substitute for birds, wheeling above the waterline where the air is thin enough to scrape. They have wings of canvas and wire, and they do not fly so much as climb, converting altitude into a kind of speech, a cawing that is really the sound of cables tightening.

The weirdest of all are the patches that behave like water but refuse to be water, the zones where the flood thickens into syrup and conducts light in spirals. In these moments, the fauna change. The lobster-things slow, their gears weeping thin lacquer that hardens into amber. The jellyfish sink, their bells expanding into shapes that look like question marks half-erased. For minutes or years, they inhabit a state that is neither motion nor rest, but a holding of breath. When the zone fades, they resume, and no one speaks of the interruption, because interruption implies a norm, and here the norm is simply the accumulation of moments.

You cannot call them machines or animals with any confidence. They are both, and neither, and something else that has been polite enough not to announce itself. They thrive because the world never demands they become other than what they are. They are the inhabitants of a cathedral built from flood and frost, and their song is the same as the place’s: a deafening insistence that to exist is to keep existing, note by note, wave by wave, until the idea of ending feels like a rumor from somewhere else entirely.
