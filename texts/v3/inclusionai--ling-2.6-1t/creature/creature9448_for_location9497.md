They did not evolve so much as *settle*, and in settling they learned how to be heavy, how to be architecture.

The first to claim the hollow were the **Ribs**, not trees but the memory of trees grown vast and soft. They rise in colonnades that are less trunk than tunnel, bark like cured leather over muscle-fiber, green-black with chlorophyll so dense it looks like stained glass left out in the dark. They breathe with a rhythm that is almost tidal, expanding when the subterranean warmth rises, contracting when the brightness of the cold presses in. Their branches do not reach—they brace. They become arches, vaults, buttresses, and doorways. To walk among them is to walk through the lung of something that has forgotten it needs to surface.

Between the Ribs live the **Loomvines**, plants that behave like weavers. They do not climb so much as *tie*, braiding themselves into bridges, lattices, and hammocks that hold water in trembling lenses. Their stems are clear, ridged tubes that conduct not sap but light, channeling the glow from one end of the cavern to the other like optical nerves. When the convection-wind stirs, the Loomvines thrum, and the sound is less music than calibration, tuning the forest to its own temperature.

The ground, where it can be found, is mostly idea rather than substance. It gives way underfoot like the inside of a loaf, soft and resilient, composed of generations of moss stitched into felt. This moss is not passive. It moves overnight, knitting itself into spongy staircases, reclaiming footprints, smoothing paths that were never meant to last. It drinks the 0.9 of water that is citizenship here, storing it in pearl-blisters that burst only when the pressure of growth behind them becomes polite insistence.

Water itself is governed by the **Spillways**, rivers that behave like children who have just learned their names. They do not flow; they *prattle*, chuckling over stones that are still learning to be smooth, carving channels that taste of iron and newness. Their banks are transient, held together by roots that tighten like fists whenever the current laughs too hard. The water never settles because to settle would be to forget it is moving, and forgetting is not permitted here.

Light is produced by the **Glow-caps**, fungal structures that have nationalized luminescence. They do not burn; they *seep*, a cold fire that stains everything it touches with a pale, persistent brightness. Because the cavern is immense, the light has room to misbehave. It forms fronts and halos, luminous weather that drifts like slow storms above the canopy. Shadows, when they occur, are not absences but bruises—purple afterthoughts that pool in hollows like condensation on glass.

Above, suspended in the height of the cavern, live the **Drifters**, six-limbed grazers whose antlers are chandeliers of bone and crystal, grown to catch the light and ferment it into warmth they can carry. They move rarely, as if conserving momentum, and when they do, their footfalls ring like coins dropped in a vast, green cathedral. They are fed upon by the **Gliders**, furred creatures that ride sheets of cold convection, their membranes lined with veins of bioluminescence that pulse in time with the forest’s own hum. They are rare, and their rarity gives them gravity; when a Glider passes, the Loomvines lean away in respect.

Scattered among them are the **Hearth-keepers**, not a species so much as a lineage of behavior. They are recognizable by the carefulness of their hands, by the hearths they carve into living roots, by the bridges they lash from timber and bone. They leave behind tools that seem to molt, appearing and disappearing as if made of decisions rather than metal. Their altars are stacks of slate that accumulate moss beards, offerings that outlive the need to offer. They do not rule the forest; they annotate it, correcting its grammar with patience.

Among the quieter interstices live the **Singing Spores**, plants that do not grow so much as *unwind*. They shed dust that chimes as it falls, creating thin rain of sound that maps the temperature of the air. There are **Memory Pools**, waters that retain the shapes of animals that drank there long ago and replay them as ripples—ghosts of gills, ghosts of snouts, brief holograms of a biology that learned to leave. And threading through it all are the **Thought-roots**, slow currents of pressure and pause, plant-time that does not hurry because it does not need to.

The fauna here is defined by its punctuation. A beak clicks. A foot falls on moss that brightens beneath it. A Glider banks, and the Loomvines tighten their grip. These are not events so much as acknowledgments, moments when the forest remembers it is occupied.

When turbulence rises, the Ribs exhale together, and the entire cavern registers the sound as vibration more than noise, a low roar that feels like the world shifting its weight in sleep. In these moments, bridges collapse into new shapes, corridors seal, and new ones rupture into being with the soft violence of growth. Destruction here is not failure but invitation, a way of saying *there is room*.

And there is always room. The vastness is cavernous, almost empty the way a bell is empty except for its ringing. This emptiness allows the rare to feel monumental, allows a single glowing spore to seem like a star fallen close enough to touch.

They live by a small set of generous rules: growth is authority, cold is clarity, light is a form of soil. Motion is inevitable, stillness is temporary, and change is a kindness. To exist here is to be rewritten slowly, to have one’s edges gentled by damp and bright, to become part of a system too deep to ignore and too alive to ever settle.

In the end, the hollow is not a place but a collaboration—a green-and-black continent that breathes, glows, and rearranges itself with careful turbulence, where every footfall is a conversation, and the only true landmark is the moment the forest decides to be elsewhere.
