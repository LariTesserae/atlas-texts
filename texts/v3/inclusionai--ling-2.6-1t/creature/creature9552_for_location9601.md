What lives here is density that has learned to breathe.

First there are the Calcites, which are not a people but a condition. They grow in the sags between ridges, thick-bodied and slow, their skins a chalky white that drinks the light instead of reflecting it. They do not age so much as accumulate: a year adds a layer, a decade adds a habit, a century adds a room inside them that they did not know they needed. They speak rarely, and when they do their voices sound like settling basalt, a low crack that is not breaking but fitting. They are the city’s cartilage, appearing wherever two steep things lean too long and need a cushion. They mend cracks with a paste of their own patience, and they taste, faintly, of chalk and old milk, as if what they consume is not food but time that has lost its hurry.

Then come the Veiners, who are the city’s nervous system made flesh. They are slender, many-limbed, and their backs are mapped in raised lines that glow the same soft mineral phosphorescence as the mountain’s seams. By night they climb catwalks and bridges to read the city’s hum the way doctors read a pulse. Their fingers leave behind a dusting of copper and sulfur that makes water bead and electricity hesitate. They do not sleep so much as hibernate in short, vertical burrows dug into scaffolding, waking whenever the grid stutters. They argue with the power station in a language of clicks and arcing sparks, and sometimes they win, which is when the chimneys leak less light and the air tastes like rain instead of metal.

Among the terraces live the Alloy-Wranglers, heavy-shouldered folk with hands callused like cooled slag. They wear coats patched from shed conveyor belts and smell perpetually of ozone and something sweetly combustible. Their job is to coax things that should not move to keep moving, and things that should not stop to pause politely. They know where gravity pools in the alleys and how to pour themselves through those thick spots like syrup. When a wall slumps, they are already there, not lifting it so much as persuading it to remember its earlier, straighter intentions. They sing to presses and hammers, songs that are more rhythm than melody, and the machines answer with softer collisions, as if nodding.

There are also the Smokeless, who may have once been birds or lizards or something that never chose. They have feathers that gleam like mica, and eyes that reflect heat rather than light. They glide between chimneys, collecting soot that dreams of being pigment, and nest in louvers where the wind cannot reach them because they have folded it into small, quiet packets. They do not sing; they record. If you hold a feather from a Smokeless long enough, it will replay the day it grew, and you will taste almonds and old wiring at the back of your throat.

The city hosts smaller, stranger metabolisms as well. Pools of brackish water in the catch-basins have cultivated their own societies of film-things, iridescent skins that thrum when the river arrives. They trade reflections with anyone who kneels close enough, giving back glimpses that are slightly older, slightly kinder. In the steam that rises through pavement pores, there are word-eaters, translucent and eel-like, that nibble the patina off language, leaving sentences lighter and sharper. A greeting that passes through them emerges as both warning and welcome, which is exactly what is needed in a place with no childhood.

And deep in the throat of the extinct vent, where the power station digests heat, lives the city’s oldest symbiote, a creature made of continuous thrumming. It has no fixed shape but prefers to drape itself across turbines and ducts like a mantle. It is the digestion made audible, the pressure made polite. Those who work closest to it learn to breathe in its rhythm, and their footsteps begin to align with the city’s muttering. They become, in small ways, Calcites or Veiners or Alloy-Wranglers without ever deciding, simply by proximity.

What lives here, finally, is persistence that has grown crowded with itself. It is not one species but a consortium that includes river, rock, rivet, and regret. They share the heaviness of the air, the slickness of edges, the habit of assembling without end. They are crowded by height, heated by each other, and lightly touched by moisture that is more intent than water usually is. They do not seek a center, because there is no childhood to look back to, only density that keeps learning how to hold itself together, and the quiet, industrious knowledge that being unfinished is not failure but the only way to stay alive in a place that is always building its next explanation.
