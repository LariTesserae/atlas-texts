They are the Seam-Readers—six-limbed, beetle-sized architects who spend their lives tapping the walls not to find what is hidden, but to discover what is still deciding. Their bodies are matte and soft, the color of wet slate, so they do not interrupt the brightness but absorb and return it in gentler tones. Each limb ends in a brush of filaments finer than crystal splinters, and with these they comb the stone for temperature, for memory, for the faint electrical hush that runs through growing minerals. They do not eat in any ordinary sense; they sip the condensation that beads on ore, and they chew the edges of half-formed geodes just enough to taste which direction the rock wishes to harden next. When they finish, they excrete small, pearlescent pellets that settle into cracks and act as seeds for new seams—geological punctuation, placed with careful intent.

Above them, the Gliders ride drafts that do not reach the floor. Their wings are not membranes but thin sheets of translucent material, stiffened by internal lattices that catch the ambient glow and scatter it into prismatic whispers. They never land, because to land is to surrender to gravity, and gravity here is already crowded. Instead, they bank through corridors no wider than a hand, navigating by echolocation so refined it borders on conversation. Each call they emit is a question about shape; each echo that returns is an answer folded back on itself. Their bodies are swollen at the center with a single bladder of lighter-than-air gas, the byproduct of fungi they cultivate in pouches along their flanks—fungi that feed on the metallic tang of new stone and exhale buoyancy. The Gliders do not hunt; they harvest altitude, and in doing so they keep the hollow’s air in constant, gentle motion, preventing the brightness from growing stale.

Threading through the runnels, the Eel-Lights swim without water. Their bodies are translucent tubes lined with cells that pump luminescence from front to back, as if circulating daybreak through their veins. They do not swim to go anywhere; they swim to keep the moisture from ever pooling into stillness. Where their bodies brush stone, the rock remembers warmth and softens just enough to admit new crystal. Where they brush each other, they exchange pulses of light—brief, intricate patterns that map the hollow’s mood in real time. They have no eyes, for eyes would imply a distance they do not honor; instead, their skin is a surface of sensing, reading pressure, temperature, and the faint hum of the fused column at the center. They are living conduits, stitching the place together with silent, radiant grammar.

There are others, smaller and quieter, who tend the mineralized bristle and glass-lichen. They are the Nesters, six-legged and round, with shells that open like segmented fruit to reveal pockets lined with soft, reflective moss. They carry droplets from ledge to ledge, depositing them in the spirals of glass-lichen so that each frond may hold a perfect, trembling lens. They do not speak, but they click, and their clicks are the hollow’s metronome, marking the rhythm of tides that are not water but light, rising and falling without source or destination.

All of them are bound to the column in the center. They do not worship it; they maintain it. They bring offerings not of food but of arrangement—the ring of pebbles that cups a pool, the lattice that holds back or holds open, the arch that leads nowhere but insists on being a threshold. These are not symbols. They are calibrations. Through them, the Seam-Readers and Gliders and Eel-Lights keep the place from deciding too quickly, from hardening into a final, singular shape. They keep the negotiation alive.

They do not sleep. Instead, they cycle through phases of density, withdrawing into seams or clustering near the column, their combined glow dimming to a pulse that matches the fused crystal’s slow insistence. In these moments, the hollow breathes as one body, walls expanding and contracting by fractions, floors sighing as if remembering a sky they have never seen. The fauna do not dream, but they rehearse—tapping, gliding, pulsing—so that when the next phase of becoming arrives, they will be ready to make room for it without crowding it out.

They are not inhabitants. They are caretakers of a moment that refuses to end, stewards of a brightness that does not fade, participants in a music that does not scatter but holds itself close. They live in the packed, breathing, shining equilibrium of the hollow, and in living there, they keep it stitched into the underside of something larger, not by force, but by constant, careful attention.
