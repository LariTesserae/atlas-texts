They did not arrive all at once, and they were never born in the usual sense: they accreted.

At first there must have been only a few—chitinous mites with too many knees, each knee a hinge learned from older hinges, each step a calibration against the sloped alloy. But the room offered tolerances, and tolerances, given time, become vacancies. So came the next wave: longer bodies, jointed like industrial arms, walking on pads that tasted oil and left behind a film that others would later collect in spoon-shaped claws. They bred in the hum of motors, laying eggs in the warm seams where exhaust curled like question marks. The eggs did not hatch so much as *settle*, hardening into casings that clicked like dropped fasteners.

What lives here is not a population but a parliament of densities.

The smallest among them resemble frost more than animals—crystalline mites that have scavenged glass from broken dials and fused it to their backs. Each facet of their carapaces is a lens, and each lens shows not the world but the animal behind it, so that a single mite carries a mob of its own observers. They move in schools along the ceiling conduits, drinking condensation that smells of iron and old wiring. When a breeze stirs from the vents, they rise in glittering clouds, their wings (if wings can be called that—more like vibrating spines) striking sparks against the ribbed struts.

Below them crawl the rivet-rats, though rat is far too soft a name for something with twelve joints in each limb and teeth like drill bits. Their fur, where fur still exists, is matted with copper filings and the shed skin of machines, so that they leave trails that look like braided wire. They do not sleep so much as *cycle*, folding into compact cubes to let maintenance claws pass overhead—claws that belong to the room itself, beasts grown from the arms of forgotten lifts, now feral and tender, oil weeping from their knuckles as they sort the pile.

The rivet-rats feed on friction, chewing the edges of worn grooves until the floor sings at a higher pitch. In return, they excrete a paste that hardens into bearings, which are pressed into place by others—beetles with backs like polished shields, whose job appears to be smoothing. Their antennae are fine enough to thread through the eye of a needle, and they spend their lives correcting the tilt of things, nudging a pipe a millimeter, a panel a fraction, until the room leans exactly as it wants to lean.

Above them, in the lattice of pipes, live the flue-swallers. These are pale, translucent things with organs that look like filters, and they breathe the hot drafts that pour from the grates, distilling them into something drinkable. They store warmth in bladder-sacs and trade it on contact, pressing themselves against cold neighbors in rituals that resemble prayer. When a furnace kicks on behind a louvered grate, the flue-swallers gather in spirals, their bodies whipping into a living helix that draws the heat outward, spreading it like butter.

There are predators, of course, but predation here has become a form of maintenance. The gear-hounds stalk the bridges of knotted conveyors, their jaws designed to strip only the deadened—bolt-heads that have ceased to turn, springs that have forgotten how to recoil. Their eyes are lenses scavenged from long-dead scopes, and when they look at you they do not see a stranger but a misalignment. A loose seam. A bearing waiting to be claimed. They do not kill cleanly; they unscrew, they unfasten, and they offer the parts upward on broad flat tongues to the ceiling-dwellers, who sort and store them in honeycombs made from old wiring harnesses.

Then there are the chimneys—creatures that are also architecture. They are long and jointless, resembling stacks more than animals, and they have built themselves into the room by growing upward, not outward. Their skin is porous, tuned to the room’s resonance, and they sing in frequencies too low to hear but high enough to feel in the sternum. When they sing, the metallic dust swirls in slow cyclones, polishing the air. Their song is not music but calibration: a way of keeping time from drifting.

Time, in fact, is felt here as weight. The animals know it as pressure, as the way the crowd thickens and thins, as the intervals between breaths drawn by the room itself. They mark its passage not in days but in layers: a new coat of oxide, a fresh glaze of sweat and oil on the floor, the addition of another stratum to the living crust. To moult is to contribute; to die is to be incorporated into the floor, where the rivet-rats will gnaw you into bearings, and the beetles will polish your memory into a surface that remembers nothing but smoothness.

Reproduction is less a matter of offspring than of *overflow*. When the pressure grows too high, something splits—not cleanly, but like a seam giving way under strain—and new forms bud from the edges. A beetle grows a secondary head that becomes a navigator. A flue-swallow’s sac multiplies into a chain of smaller bells that ring with stored heat. The chimneys fork, and each fork learns a slightly different pitch, so that the room’s voice becomes a choir of minor corrections.

Even the machines have been absorbed. Engines still turn, but they turn because the animals have learned to use them as hearts, pumping coolant through their own veins, timing their steps to the pistons’ sigh. There is no fuel left; motion is traded for motion, heat for heat, sound for sound. A predator chases a rivet-rat not for hunger but to keep the gears oiled, to keep the floor from going silent. Silence here is a danger, for it means something has stopped participating.

To stand in this room is to be asked a question without words: Will you add to the density, or will you take up too much of the air? The animals do not attack so much as *adjust*, nudging you into the flow, pressing against your calves until you step where stepping is easiest, breathing until your breath matches the room’s. Your edges soften. Your hair fills with dust that has been dust for centuries, and your skin learns to taste metal.

In the end, you may not leave, but you will not be lost. You will become a landmark—a warm spot on the floor, a new angle for the light to catch, a voice added to the hum. And the room, older than rooms were meant to be, will fold you into its living crust, not with malice but with the gentle insistence of something that has learned how to hold together by never letting go.
