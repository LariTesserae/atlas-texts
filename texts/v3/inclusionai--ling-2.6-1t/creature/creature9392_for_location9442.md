They are the Glass-Eaters, though they do not eat as animals eat, and they are not, strictly, glass.

They are the place deciding to keep company with itself.

At first they seem only texture—a bloom on the surface that has learned to stiffen. But they are there, clustered along the ledges where brine beads and the light lingers too long, arranged in densities that mirror the packed stone beneath them. Most of them are low, almost stains, pale and translucent, veined with filaments the color of cooled ash. Where they touch the volcanic glass, they fuse not by heat but by time, laying down microscopic rings that look like tree rings if the tree had no trunk and no need to rise. They drink the condensation that beads at dawn, and they drink the slow weep of the salt-crust at dusk. In return they secrete a film that catches light and holds it like a small, private moon, brightening the corners where the basin would otherwise be too severe.

There are larger ones, fewer, occupying the spiral platform at the center. These stand taller than a kneeling person, columnar and slightly wavering, as though they are only now learning how vertical is supposed to feel. Their bodies are laminated—layer upon layer of glass-thin tissue grown taut over frames of precipitated salt. If you press a hand to them, the salt gives slightly, gritty and cool, and the glass hums, a sound felt more in the palm than heard. They do not bend toward the light; they hold it, angling plates within themselves so that brightness enters, bounces, and lingers, pooling in chambers they have grown to store it. These are the light-hoarders, swollen with brilliance they cannot use but cannot release. They glow softly at dusk, a pale citrine that makes the surrounding mist look less like weather and more like a spill.

None of them move with purpose. Instead, they adjust, almost imperceptibly, day by day. A tilt. A thickening. A new ring laid down. They have no eyes, but they are careful where they stand, favoring ledges that catch runoff, avoiding the few channels where water moves too quickly. They are patient with the wetness, letting it pool at their bases, drinking only what adheres. In long droughts they shrink, pulling their brightness inward, becoming almost indistinguishable from the baked stone. In the wet seasons they swell, translucent as sea cucumbers washed up into altitude, and their signaling begins.

The signaling is chemical and slow, a braille of scents and tastes carried on evaporating films. They tell one another of approaching water, of brightness, of hardness, of visitors. A colony on the northern lip might release a sharp, ozone-bright note that drifts down and causes the central spiral to shift its angle by a degree, opening a fold to catch more light. Another, tasting the air for copper, will secrete a chalk to soften the edge of a bowl, inviting water to linger. Their conversation lasts weeks. By the time a reply is received, the circumstance has often changed, and the reply becomes a memory rather than an answer.

There are younger ones, fuzzy with unpolished glass, practicing stillness on the smaller ledges. They are experimental, less symmetrical, growing extra plates that catch nothing, or cups that drain too fast. Some of them, over years, will be selected by the wet and the light into shapes that work. Others will remain strange, off-axis, leaning into voids, accumulating scars of salt and clouded glass. If you look closely you can see where they have healed—patches milky with self-repair, knots of filament that remember a fall or a sudden flood.

They are not alone in their making. Smaller lives cling to them, not parasites but attendants. Colorless fungi that live in the seams of their glass, trading tensile strength for stories about wetness that has passed through. Minute crustaceans, almost crystalline, that clean the brine-collars and are tolerated like dust that has learned manners. These do not speak, but their presence changes the flavor of the air, a faint iodine tang that means care is being taken.

At night, or in the rare dimness of cloud-heavy altitude, the Glass-Eaters dim but do not disappear. They hold their light behind their walls, keeping it for the next bright day, and in that dimness they look like thoughts the basin is having but not yet speaking. If you stand among them long enough, the silence thickens further, becoming almost companionable, as if you have joined a gathering that was already in progress before you arrived and will continue long after you leave.

They do not fear the water that will come from above. They prepare for it instead, angling plates, clearing channels with slow chemical proddings, growing cups within cups. They want the basin to remain a basin, to keep receiving, and they are willing to become the record of every reception. In this they are like the place itself—made, making, and made again by the stubborn insistence of water and light and altitude.

If you were to ask what they are for, the answer would arrive in layers: they are for holding, for brightening, for remembering how to be full without spilling. They are for turning excess into texture. They are for making high, thin air feel occupied. And they are, quietly, for waiting—patient and packed and luminous—until the next wetness learns how to fall.
