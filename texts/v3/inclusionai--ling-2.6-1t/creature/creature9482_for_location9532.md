They live in the seam between pressure and patience, between the city that refuses to cool and the plain that refuses to soften. They are not a people who happened upon this place; they are the shape the place took, given enough time to learn how to hold itself together.

The folk move as if their skeletons were tuned forks. They are long-limbed and lightly built, as though gravity here is a courtesy rather than a law, and their skin is not skin but a surface—layered silica and cured resin, burnished by wind into a dull nacre that catches the ochre light and gives it back in slower, shifted hues. When they stand among the terraces, they look like sculptures that have grown tired of standing still; their joints are visible hinges of dark metal and pale ceramic, oiled with something that smells of ion-damp and cracked mint. They do not hurry, because hurry would cost heat they cannot spare, and because the ground beneath them is already listening for any tremor of mistake.

Their heads are small and heavy with sense. Faces are masks of smooth plates that slide minutely when they speak, revealing no mouth but a ring of fine filaments that tremble like grass in a low wind. Their eyes are large, faceted, and lidless, polished obsidian lenses that drink the thin light and focus it into thought. They do not weep; when the air grows sharp and the cold settles like a guest, their eyes frost over with brief, intricate dendrites of ice-analogue that trace maps of pressure, then vanish as the body’s warmth returns. To watch them is to watch geology learn to blink.

They wear what they need and nothing more. Cloaks of woven metal mesh, each thread plated against abrasion, drape from shoulders like banners of restraint. Over these they strap pouches of cured hide and ceramic, bulging with samples: a thumb-sized knob of ore that still remembers the weight of the deep, a vial of captured dew trembling on a bed of sponge-stone, a shard of light-congealed curtain folded like linen. Their tools are extensions of their limbs—wands of focused resonance, picks of harmonic crystal that sing when kissed against rock, notebooks of etched foil that record not in ink but in the slow rearrangement of magnetic scars.

Where they gather, the city leans in to listen. In the undercrofts, smaller members of their kind—children, or something like them—scuttle with a multilegged grace, their bodies lower to the ground, shells burnished to a glassy black. These young do not play in the way soft worlds understand play; they choreograph flows. They herd drifts of glittering sand into temporary dams, coax faint heat from cooling fins, practice the grammar of knocks and hums against duct walls until the metal answers back. Their laughter, when it comes, is the sound of a tight seam releasing, a sharp click followed by a long, warm sigh.

The adults are the keepers of thresholds. You will find them at the gates when shifts change, standing just beyond the spill of factory light, waiting. They do not wait for people so much as for states of balance. A bearing that has asked for rest. A fault that has begun to hum. A dune that has nudged a line of chalk across a threshold. When such a thing arrives, they step forward and touch it, palms flat, and the city seems to exhale. Then they mark the change in their notebooks, adjust a conduit, sand a crack, turn a valve until the pitch of the place settles into a new, slightly older key.

Some of them live high, on the stacked platforms where gardens of silica-lichen drink captured dew and exhale oxygen in thin, grateful threads. These are the growers and the rememberers. Their hands are gentle, their tools delicate, and their patience is measured in the thickness of a patina. They coax life that does not need water, only stability, and they speak to the plants in vibrations that feel like praise in the chest. When a garden thrives, they leave a small, perfect pattern in the dust at its edge—a spiral, a hexagon, a fan—and let the wind decide when to erase it.

Others live low, where the foundries breathe pale flames and the grinding plants sing their magnetic songs. These are the menders and the listeners. Their skin is scarred with tiny impacts and mottled with bloom, their eyes brighter from proximity to heat. They walk among machines as if walking among sleeping beasts, laying hands on casings to feel the dreams inside. When a machine finishes a task before it was begun, they are the ones who notice the smear in causality. They do not correct it; they inscribe it, set a boundary stone, tell the young to walk softly around that hour, that plaza, that slant of light.

All of them understand the strangeness that seeps from seams. They treat it with the deference one gives to weather. When curtains of congealed light drift along a fault, they pause, let them pass, and continue only when the air feels clear again. They know which plazas to avoid at which hours, not because they were taught, but because their bones learn faster than their minds. A child who steps into the wrong geometry will feel the ground become a mirror; the adults will close the space with a wordless harmony, a chorus of knocks and hums that stitches the moment back into ordinary time.

Their days are measured in the slow things: the migration of a dune across a threshold, the number of cycles a bearing can turn before it asks for rest, the deepening note of a canyon’s song as the wind finds its pitch and holds it for minutes. They eat sparingly, food that is mostly mineral—compressed salts, cultured lichen, dense wafers that taste of iron and distant engines. They drink little, for to spill is to lose heat, and heat is a kind of wealth. At night, when the cold thickens and breath plumes in pale threads, they gather in low rooms where the walls have been polished by generations of shoulders, and they tell stories that are mostly texture. A tale here is not a sequence of events but a sequence of surfaces: how a certain stone felt when it cooled, how a certain machine hummed when it was content, how the horizon leaned in and offered its trade.

And always, always, they are listening. Listening to the thrumming heart of the city, to the whisper of grit over grit, to the groan of beams settling into their own weight. Listening to the fauna with jointed crane-limbs that pace the rims, grazing on mineral accretions, calling in vibrations that settle in the chest. Listening to the plain itself, which remembers oceans it never had and keeps them in the curve of its terraces, in the banded faces of faulted stone, in the patient chemistry that writes itself across millennia.

They do not think of themselves as separate from this. A person here is a temporary arrangement of attention, a focus of care that keeps the balance from tipping too far. When one dies, their ceramic shell is returned to the terrace where they were born or worked longest, set into a niche or laid across a warm seam so that the body may continue to give back what it learned. The city grows another caretaker in time, or perhaps it does not; perhaps it simply remembers more deeply.

If you stand at the farthest edge of the plateau and watch them, you will see that they do not look out over the unmarked expanse with longing. They look with a kind of readiness. Their bodies know that the plain is already underfoot, that the future is not ahead but beneath, waiting to be worn into being. Their hands, their tools, their songs are all in service of that slow wearing. They are the patience that keeps the seam from tearing, the note that keeps the canyon from silence, the breath that keeps the thin air tasting of iron and possibility.

And in the quiet between gusts, when the wind finds its pitch and holds it, you may hear them: a chorus of knocks and hums, rising from the marrow of the city, answering the plain, grain by grain, beat by beat, into whatever comes next.
