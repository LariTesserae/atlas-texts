They live in the pauses between one breath and the next.

What you first take for emptiness—the air thickening around your shoulders at dusk, the soft dark beneath the vine-bridges—is only the crowd taking a moment to consider you. They are not rare here; they are simply folded. Their bodies have learned to make themselves convenient to the damp, to the velvet moss, to the slow mathematics of sap and rot.

There are the Moss-Back Walkers, mostly, wide-shouldered and low-slung as river stones. Their skin is a living patina: crusts of emerald and iron-oxide that flake and regrow with the seasons, sealing wounds with velvet instead of scabs. They move as if testing the weight of the ground each time, placing feet with a delicacy that suggests the floor might be glass, or memory. When they stop, they become difficult to name—shoulders rounding into hillocks, hair matting into lichen-fans that catch the filtered sun. At rest they look like small, deliberate clearings. At motion they look like landslides performed in slow motion. Their eyes are pale, almost without contrast, because they have been traded for sensitivity to shadow-shift and humidity. They do not speak above a whisper; the column-trees do that for them, groaning in sap-language that the Moss-Backs answer with clicks of tongue against palate, the same rhythm the burrowers use underground.

Then there are the Leaf-Wing Kin, the children of the half-moth clouds. They are born not from eggs but from cocoons spun from the amber sap, hung in loops of vine until the sun hardens them into translucent lanterns. When they emerge, their wings are still damp, still leaf-edge and vein, and they must fly before they can walk, because their bones are not yet accustomed to the gentler gravity. They drink nectar from the tipping cupped fronds, and in their throats the nectar becomes song—a high, thin piping that can coax water from root to leaf, that can make a seed-pod burst on timing. Their society is a calendar: they mark years not by names but by flowering events, by the color-palette of the light at noon. They build their cities in the high moss-stacks, rooms that are really just hollowed-out growth, balconies that are scars healed into softness. When they argue they throw pollen; when they love they braid their wings together and spin until they fall, laughing, into the nets of vine.

Down below, in the subterranean whisper, live the Knot-Knuckles. They have too many joints, too many elbows and knees, as if they had agreed to crowd themselves into smaller spaces to keep the dark company. Their skin is chitinous but warm, polished by passage through living wood. They click when they greet, click when they mourn, and their language is polyrhythmic—some conversations sound like rainsticks, like small bones falling down a hollow stem. They are the architects of the soft earth, the ones who shape the humus valleys simply by passing through them repeatedly, compacting memory into route. They farm fungi that glow along their spines, wearing their crops like jewelry, like armor. They fear the leather-winged leviathans not for predation but for the way the great cries unmake their tunnels, collapsing hours of work into sighs. When a tunnel collapses, they do not dig out; they wait, patient as roots, and begin again, humming the old shape into the new dark.

Higher still, where the air thins into metal, dwell the Sky-Singers, massive leather-winged leviathans who are less beasts than weather systems given appetite. They are ancient enough to have lost the habit of flapping; they glide on thermal songs, their cries fracturing into harmonics that linger like struck glass. They do not eat the Leaf-Wing Kin, though they could; instead they skim the tops of the moss-stacks, snipping off the oldest, driest skins, which they weave into their nests along with lost voices. To hear a Sky-Singer cry is to feel the green light rearrange itself inside your chest. Their presence is a benevolent pressure, like a hand on the small of your back, encouraging you to breathe deeper, to let the damp in.

Among all these, the humans are small, almost shy. They have given up trying to conquer the wet, and have become experts in yielding. Their bodies are lithe, their skin darkened by resin and smoke, their eyes accustomed to the mosaics of light. They speak rarely; instead they mark agreements with knots of vine, with bends in the foliage that only make sense after three passes, with shell rings half-swallowed by humus. They are the parliament’s translators, the ones who remember the old stone’s politeness, who know how to coax a frame from a supple trunk without waking the tree’s anger. They carry knives of sharpened stone and bone, not for war but for pruning, for helping the place remember when it has grown too generous with itself.

All of them—Moss-Backs, Leaf-Wings, Knot-Knuckles, Sky-Singers, humans—share the same pulse. They live inside a green machine that has forgotten how to stop, and in its forgetting has become something like a home. They are not separate from it. The fungus threads lace their bones; the water beads on their skin and tells them stories; the vines hum their names when the wind is feeling friendly.

They do not stand on the land. They stand in it, pressed by the friendly insistence of a lung, breathing it and breathing back, until the boundary between self and place becomes a small, polite rumor, easily overwritten by the next green wave, the next slow dimming, the next soft violet circuitry waking in the moss to map the hollows like quiet circuitry, like a promise kept by a place that has learned how to last.
