What lives here has learned to be many things at once: shelter, machine, and mouth.

Most visible are the Rivet-Beetles, armored colonists who treat the wet iron as a city rather than a hazard. Their carapaces are segmented like old boilerplate, lacquered with black wax against corrosion, and they move in decision-making clusters, consulting the temperature of the stone with antennae that look like snapped filaments from a loom. They are not merely scavengers; they are archivists. Each beetle carries a burden of copper wire wound tight around its thorax, gathered from where the vents weep oxidized tears. They wind it, measure it, and set it aside in careful spirals that glimmer when the bruised light passes over. If you watch long enough, you will notice the spirals slowly rearrange themselves, as if the wire remembers currents that have not yet been turned on.

Above the waterline, the Steam-Moths conduct a quieter industry. Their wings are not tissue but translucent membrane stretched over lattices like safety glass, and they fly in staggered, arrhythmic pulses, as if their flight is being buffered by some unseen hand. They lay no eggs in the usual sense; instead, they affix small gelatinous nodes to the undersides of cold pipes, nodes that absorb condensation until they are heavy with borrowed water, then detach and roll into the flow, where they dissolve and release tiny mineral spores. What hatches from those spores does not fly; it crawls, and it glows with the nervous, lantern-bright pallor of flowers that have decided to be animals.

These are the Lantern-Whelps, fist-sized bundles of root and muscle that prefer to cling to the underside of ledges. Their bodies are mostly translucent gills threaded with veins that pulse in soft sequence, and they feed by nipping at the edges of electrical potential that linger in the water. They do not generate light so much as repackage it, swallowing the dim industrial gleam and exhaling it as a gentler, rhythmic shimmer. When a pump coughs and the voltage stutters, the Whimpers dim in sympathy, as if they share a single, frayed nervous system with the machinery. In times of mechanical calm, they cluster together and produce a low chittering that feels less like sound than like the clicking of a wet thermostat deciding what to believe.

In the shallows, the Copper-Needle Eels braid themselves into temporary cables, their bodies lined with filaments that look less like scales than like drawn wire. They are not fast predators but patient samplers, tasting voltage as if it were a flavor, distinguishing between currents that promise warmth and those that promise paralysis. When two eels meet, they align along the same flow, their bodies briefly completing a circuit that glows pale green along their spines. This glow is not mere reflection; it is a conversation, a brief treaty written in light and conducted through the water. Afterward, they separate, leaving behind a residue of charge that the water carries like a rumor for hours.

Deeper down, where the black pools remember everything, the Boilerplate Catfish cruise with a deliberation that suggests they are not moving through water but through something thicker, like time itself. Their eyes are sealed lenses set deep in armored sockets, each lens ringed with indicator lamps that shift color with mood or pressure or hunger—amber when curious, red when the water grows too quiet. They do not hunt so much as adjudicate, swallowing debris and the occasional sluggish eel, absorbing metal and mineral into layered plates that grow heavier and more ornate as they age. Older catfish settle into the deepest silt and let the muck grow over their backs like moss on a tombstone, and there they remain, living batteries whose slow metabolisms leak warmth into the chill.

But the largest life is not seen so much as inferred. At the pocket’s throat, where the ground feels like a wet lung, something vast and patient keeps time with the pumps, though on a different rhythm entirely. It is the Substrate Leviathan, a creature more notion than flesh, composed of calcified history and braided waterway. Its movements are expressed through the stone itself: a shudder in the rivulets, a shift in the lichen patterns, a sudden sourceless current that runs counter to gravity for a few meters before remembering itself. It breathes in cycles that outlast the machinery’s hum, and when it exhales, the pocket smells of deep iron and old rain, and the temperature drops a fraction of a degree in every corner at once. Some say it has no head, only a collection of intake valves grown into place over centuries; others say its face is the pattern of erosion itself, constantly revising its own features.

Predation here is subtle. The Rivet-Beetles avoid the deeper water not out of fear but respect, knowing the catfish will accept their metal burdens as readily as their bodies. The Steam-Moths time their flights around the exhalations of the vents, slipping through the heat like ghosts through a keyhole. Even the Leviathan feeds without haste, drawing in mineral sediment and the occasional lost current, incorporating them into its slow-growing architecture. Death, when it comes, is a matter of integration: shells soften into silt, copper is reclaimed by water, and light is folded back into the stone to be borrowed again.

What keeps this place alive is not any single species but the chorus of exchanges between them—a conductivity of life moving through metal, moss, and memory. The pocket breathes because its inhabitants allow it to, each one playing a note in a larger circuit of cold, wet, shining persistence. And when the machines fall silent and the hum of emptiness presses close, the life here does not vanish; it dims, consolidates, and waits, armored and bright, for the next cycle to teach its throat how to speak again.
