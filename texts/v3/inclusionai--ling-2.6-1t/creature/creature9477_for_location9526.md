They do not live here so much as they are lived by the basin, and the basin, in turn, is lived by them—a parliament of occupation in which creature, craft, and mineral have stopped distinguishing themselves from one another.

The most visible lives are the shoals that swim the ceiling. They are not fish, though they keep the shape of memory-water: bodies like oiled parchment, edges thinning into mist, heavy with organs that have learned to sift heat instead of salt. They move in densities that change the weight of the air, shoals thickening until the basin feels submerged even while you stand dry, then thinning into veils that reveal the glass floor’s satin curve. They do not hunt; they graze. Their mouths are fine grilles that comb the vapor rising from the central pool, feeding on the upward breath of warmth that tries to escape. In return they polish the underside of the suspended sea with the soft abrasion of their passage, keeping the water-skin thin enough to glow and thick enough to hold. When they die—and they die slowly, folding in on themselves like closing fans—the basin’s hands claim them. Their translucent skins are peeled and pressed into translucent bowls; their cartilaginous spines become arches for the salt pillars; their eyes, clouded and dense, are set into the floor as studs that catch stray steps and keep the night from being absolute.

Closer to the floor live the glass-tethered kites. These are small, many-limbed creatures the color of cooled ash, with bodies that seem to be made of folded membrane and old patience. They spin filaments from the basin’s own heat—glass so fine it is nearly thread—drawing it upward to stitch the overhead sea where it grows tired and thin. Their work is invisible until it catches the rare light, at which point the ceiling becomes a net of silver scars, each one a promise that the water will not fall. The kites do not eat as other things eat. They sip from the vapor columns, and when they die they are not remade into bowls but into beads that rattle softly in the ceramic lattices, a sound like breath held too long and then released.

Between floor and ceiling, in the lanes filtered by bone-char and dark ceramic, glide the walkers. They are heavier than the shoals, denser, with limbs that unfold like slow tools. They can step on the underside of the water-skin as if it were stone, their feet pressing dimples that grow warm and glow faintly. Where they tread, the glass remembers the shape of their soles for days, retaining a ghost heat that keeps the local air buoyant. They are the basin’s librarians of pressure—mapping which corners have forgotten how to hold weight, teaching the suspended sea how to sag with grace.

Smaller still are the shell-things, not alive and not dead, but something like apprenticeship. They are fist-sized conchs of matte ceramic that click as they creep, repairing cracks in the floor-polish, reinforcing the rims of bowls, turning fractures into veins that glow. They do not speak, but their clicking is a language of alignment, tuning the basin to its own hum. When they finish a path, the path becomes softer underfoot, as if it has learned to cushion its own mistakes.

Then there are those who are neither fauna nor maker but something in between—the salt-bodied ones. They nest in the braided pillars, colonies of warm, many-limbed creatures whose skins secrete a glaze that seals mineral against time. They do not move often; movement would waste the glaze. Instead they wait, polishing the salt with their thoughts, turning the pillars into candles that do not burn but remember fire. When one of them finally stills, its body is incorporated into the pillar itself, leaving behind a hollow that catches stray droplets from the overhead sea. Those droplets fall upward into the hollow, then drift down again, ringing like tiny bells that have forgotten how to make sound.

Above them all, in the suspended ocean, live the swimmers who forgot they were not in water. Schools of needle-bodied things that navigate by touch and temperature, weaving through the fauna-shoals without colliding, feeding on the glitter of wakes left behind. They are translucent as grief, and where they pass, the ceiling grows heavier, patterned with stalactites of hardened brine that record centuries in slow, crystalline script.

None of these lives are ruled by the kind of time that flows. They are ruled by density, by agreement, by the practiced consent of the basin to hold what is poured into it without letting it pour away. When a bowl breaks, it is not mourned but folded—its shards become inlay for new steps; its curve becomes a template for the next bowl. When a salt pillar crumbles, its remains are braided into the lattices that filter the swimming air. Death here is not an end but a kind of return address, a way of saying *this belonged to the making, and the making belongs to this.*

Even the water overhead participates. It is not alive, but it behaves like a sleeper, breathing in slow swells that push the suspended sea down onto the fauna, then drawing back, allowing them to drift higher. The fauna understand this lung-rhythm and rise with it, their bodies expanding and contracting in sympathy, becoming part of the ceiling’s breath.

And somewhere at the center, in the pool that does not drain but receives, lives the quietest life of all: a mist that condenses into drops that fall upward, gathering stories from the kites and the salt-bodied ones and the shell-things as they rise, carrying them into the overhead sea, where they are released again as the swimmers pass through, drinking the sky from below.

No one here was born of soil. No one here arrived by accident. They are all made and remade by the same patient hands that shaped the basin—hands that learned long ago not to squeeze, only to hold—and so they live as the basin lives: warm, high, water-hung, dark, and crowded with a belonging that feels less like life than like a room finally finished learning how to keep itself.
