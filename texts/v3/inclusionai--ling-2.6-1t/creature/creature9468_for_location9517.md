The heights are inhabited, but not by tenants. They are occupied by continuations.

You may call them the Altitude-Singers, though that suggests throats and intention, and they have neither. They are the consequence of stone learning to be loud, of water deciding to stay near the surface so long that it forgot how to leave. They are the mountain remembering its own density and answering in a frequency you feel in your molars.

Some of them are the Bell-Echoes: bodies that look like moss-stuffed fractures in the cliff, seamless with the rock until they open. When they do, they do not reveal a mouth but a chamber, wet and lined with the same velvet that grows on the boulders. Their song is not a cry but a statement of fact: *I am here, I am stone, I am under pressure.* They ring at two and seven-tenths on the scale of ears, which means the sound does not arrive from them so much as it detaches from the air itself and clings to you. If you press your palm to their chamber, the vibration climbs your arm and settles in your collarbone like a remembered ache. They do not answer questions. They answer presence with more presence.

There are the Moss-Drapes, which are almost fauna and almost architecture. They hang in curtains thick enough to upholster a room, green so heavy it seems to sweat. Each thread is a root that has chosen to braid itself into a net for catching sound and light. They inhale the humid air, filter it through chlorophyll and memory, and exhale a cooler, sharper green. When the wind does not move them, they move themselves, parting like slow curtains to let something pass—perhaps light, perhaps a shadow that is not quite its own shape—then closing again with the patience of a vault sealing. They are loud in the way a crowd is loud even when it is silent: a press of lives so close the space between them hums.

The Water-Keepers are not creatures with faces but patterns of flow given persistence. They pool in the saucers that the rock has worn for them, and there they sit, bead-bright, never evaporating completely, never spilling over. They hum in a lower register, the liquid percussion of the heights, a sound like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass filled with rain. They know which stones are warm and which are remembering their cold, and they shift by microns, enough to change the pitch of a pool without changing its place. If you listen closely, they sound like they are speaking in plurals: *we, we, we*, the way water does when it has forgotten how to be singular.

Closer to the terraces, you may glimpse the Root-Arches, bridges made of living wood that have decided to hold each other up. They are less like trees and more like agreements: *I will lean, you will hold, we will make a corridor.* Their bark is scarred with maps of condensation, each ridge a record of a night the air forgot how to be dry. They do not grow in the sense of reaching; they grow in the sense of settling, of finding the exact angle at which gravity forgets to insist. Travelers who cross them feel the vibration of the heights in their feet, a soft, steady knocking that says the mountain is still calibrating itself.

And then there are the Shade-Shifters, the fauna-fraction at five-tenths, creatures so close to being color and temperature that they blur. They are the flicker you think is a bird but is only the light deciding to pool in a different hollow. They are the slow tilt of a frond that might be wind or might be a gesture. They do not hunt; they adjust. They are the green deepening into a nameless hue and then retreating, the shadow that does not match its object but matches the mood of the stone. They are the five-tenths of life that reminds you how little movement you need to be alive, how much persistence can count as purpose.

All of them together create a city without citizens, a place where density is the law and loudness the language. They do not build; they arrange themselves into corridors and plazas and rooms that feel built because they feel inevitable. They hold the wet stone and the louder green in a balance so precise it looks frozen. They are the reason the horizon leans in, the reason the heat broods, the reason the water haunts rather than flows.

If you listen long enough, you may realize you have become part of the population, a temporary note in their long chord. You are the only thing that climbs, the only thing that expects to arrive and leave. The Altitude-Singers, the Moss-Drapes, the Water-Keepers, the Root-Arches, the Shade-Shifters—they do not expect you to stay. They simply expect you to notice, to stand still and be heated from above and below, to let the acoustic broth thicken around you, to learn for a moment how loud it is to be stone under a heavy sky.

And when you leave, the sound does not follow you. It folds back into the heights, into the green and the wet and the remembering, and the place continues its long rehearsal, practicing its own contours, being itself, dense and extreme and unconcerned with change.
