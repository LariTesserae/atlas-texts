They do not arrive; they condense.

At first they are only decisions made by moss—tight, bright commas that insist on green wherever stone remembers how to weep. They appear at the seams of uplift, where the fractures still taste new, drinking the altitude and turning dampness into a kind of spine. The plants are small and exact: cushions like held breath, ferns that unfurl only as far as the light allows, grasses with hairs so fine they seem to be made of the air itself. They are a cartography of patience, mapping the places where wetness pools and where stone has not yet decided to be hard again. In their shadows, color learns its boundaries—bold where moisture lingers, absent where the rock wants to be bare.

Higher, where the terraces step back into themselves, the builders live. They are not a people of numbers but of hands, of fingers that have memorized the weight of a stone before lifting it. They move through the town as if they are another kind of moss, patient and adhering, shaping walls by listening to the lines of the outcrop. Their tools are simple—other stone, wood, time—and their language is the soft clack of tapping and the hush of settling dust. They do not cut the land so much as ask it for permission, fitting dry-laid courses into cracks that already existed, as if the rock had grown a ledge for them to use. On the roofs, where thatch and thin slabs overlap, they leave space for wind to pass, as if they know that breath is also a tenant here.

The town holds them lightly. Families are small constellations—three or four people, perhaps, and a shared cistern—each household a node in a network of channels and benches. Their days are threaded with quiet tasks: coaxing condensation into stillness, clearing a furrow so water will rise to its given height, adding a course to a wall so slowly that it seems the wall is growing up around them rather than being built. Children learn to read damp patches like a weathervane, to know where color will arrive and where it will wait. There is no hurry in their learning, and no shame in not knowing; the land offers its lessons in glints and seepage, and the people practice answering in kind.

Among them move the tenders, who speak more to water than to other people. They know which fractures exhale most freely, which lips of cliff hold the shyest threads. They trace the paths of leakage with their eyes and sometimes with their hands, guiding moisture into pans and basins set askew like mirrors that have learned humility. They teach the cisterns how to be patient, how to hold only what is given, and how to let the rest fall softly back onto the slopes. In return, the wetness gifts them slick surfaces and cool air and the sense that altitude has its own pulse. To watch a tender work is to see someone interpreting a language written in glisten and trickle, translating it into stillness.

Above the town, the watchers live. They are not separate from the others—at times they are builders, at times tenders, at times simply people who have climbed high to think—but they have learned to hold the whole expanse in one glance. They stand at the edges where paths fray into ramps of stone and let the town settle into their vision like a scatter of purpose in a high, bright bowl. They notice which terraces are likely to warm first, which benches will catch the light, which new dampnesses have appeared overnight. They return with wordless knowledge: a tilt of the head, a pointing finger, a silence held a moment longer than usual. In this way, the town learns to read itself.

The fauna are absent, but something takes their place. It is not a creature so much as a habit of attention. A flicker of motion along a chute where water pools; a quick, careful breath held when stone slides just enough to settle. There is no clatter, only adjustment—the kind of life that prefers to be mistaken for the land itself, that wears green or gray or the soft tan of dry earth and lets the eye pass over it. It keeps the town from being lonely without ever promising to be company.

Even the structures seem to breathe with them. Walls remember the hands that set them; roofs remember the weight of wind; benches remember the warmth of bodies and give it back slowly as the sun moves. The cisterns remember every fall of moisture, each one a small history written in level and clarity. The town is a collection of memories that refuse to erode, held in a stability that is less a wall than a kind of agreement between stone, water, and care.

And in the spaces between—those long runs of emptiness where rock meets sky—lives the high, open span itself, an active presence that holds everything in tension. It is a tenant, too, demanding only that nothing rush, that nothing force a curve onto a line that wants to stay sharp. It watches over the residents the way altitude watches over scent: cleanly, without clinging.

All of them together: moss, mason, tender, watcher, the careful life that is almost not there, and the span that cradles them. They are not a crowd. They are a set of balances, a living calibration of sharpness and dampness, of light that does not flinch and time that refrains. They are what the land has made room for, and what the land has not yet decided to outgrow. For now, they are enough—each one a note held softly in the chord of a newly remembered earth, waiting to see what will come next, and willing to let the waiting be its own kind of fullness.
