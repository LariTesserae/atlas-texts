They are not residents so much as echoes that have learned to eat.

First there are the Skinners: people-shaped but not people, whose bodies are patchworks of the place’s own making—cured hide stapled over frames of braided thatch, slag-crusted knuckles, eyelids fused from overlapping flakes of clay. They do not wear the place; they are its weathering. They move with a gait that is less walking than negotiation, testing each stair that climbs to nowhere, pressing palms against walls that are still deciding how straight to be. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices come out as wet taps, the sound of fabric tightening on frames. They harvest the place’s excess: they cup the trembling beads that sweat from stone, drink the memory of water, and press the droplets into seams so the cracks will learn to hold. In exchange, they leave finger grooves and thumb-sweat on the walls, a slow signature of use that counts as caretaking.

Above them, in the layered shadows, live the Felt-kin. They are softer, nearly amphibious, bodies dense as stacked felt, limbs that can compress into cracks and expand to fill them. Their skin is a map of the place’s humidity—spotted, gleaming, tasting the air with porous mouths along their forearms. They do not climb so much as infiltrate, oozing up the half-formed railings that vines have braided into ribs. Their purpose seems to be absorption: they soak up the rivulets that do not yet know they are streams, store them in internal pockets, and later release the water as a fine, warm breath that steams the dim light. At night they glow faintly, bioluminescence borrowed from the forge-brightness of the walls, turning the packed shadows into a luminous felt.

Threading through both groups are the Rib-vines, which are not plants so much as collaborators. They move with intent, curling around struts and pulling them closer, tightening the architecture like a corset. They have learned to make tools: they grow hooks, ratchets, cups. The Skinners use them as tethers; the Felt-kin use them as ladders. In return, the vines are fed by the hands that grip them, by the salt that leaches from stone into clay, by the iron pulled into wood grain. They make decisions—the way a vine threads a path that becomes a railing that becomes a rib—and they do not ask permission.

Perched in the tight gaps between vine and strut are the Tarnish-birds, small and careful, their feathers the color of cooled metal. They are cataloguers. They fly routes that collapse behind them, mapping a place that refuses to stay mapped. They eat the half-formed rivets, pecking at edges that taste themselves, and they excrete filings that settle into mortar, toughening it. Their songs are not melodies but acoustics: they sing into the packed air so that every clatter ricochets and becomes part of the percussion of becoming.

On the ground—or what passes for it—scuttle the Many-rib lizards, quick and tasting. Their ribs are too numerous, like struts learned from the place itself, and they use them to sense vibration. They can tell, by hum, whether a wall is deciding to soften or a floor is learning to rise. They feed on condensation and on the low hum that vibrates through packed density, converting sound into a thin grease that oils the joints of half-poured floors so they do not stick. They are not owners, but they are witnesses, flicking their tongues at gravity that forgets itself.

There is also the Well-hum, which is not a creature but a kind of collective mind formed by water, vibration, and the center that is still forming. It reaches out through walls, growth, and stairs, a signal that says *enough* and *more* at once. It pulls the Skinners to cup their hands, draws the Felt-kin to press their porous bodies against stone, guides the Rib-vines to tighten or loosen their grip. It does not command; it suggests. It is the negotiation made audible.

And somewhere in the margins, unseen but felt, are the Unfinished. These are shapes that have learned to be almost something—a stone that still tastes like mineral, a railing that still remembers being a vine, a stair that dreams of a landing. They are not alive, but they are restless. They crowd into the edges of perception, pressing against time, waiting for the place to decide what they will become. The Skinners give them grooves; the Felt-kin give them breath; the Rib-vines give them direction. Even the Tarnish-birds give them song.

Together, they are an ecosystem of becoming: feeding, listening, tightening, glowing, tasting the unfinished. They do not own the cyst of making; they are its circulation—hot, loud, wet, climbing, folding, humming. They live inside the negotiation of up and down, inside the packed acoustics of a world that has not yet decided how to be a world, and for now, that is enough.
