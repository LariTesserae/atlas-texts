What lives here has learned to keep time the way the stone keeps water: by letting it pool, by not asking it to move on until it has softened into something quieter.

The moss is not merely a coat but a kind of chronicler. Each cushion remembers every rain that has ever fallen, storing the memory in layers so fine they feel like years. When you press a palm to it, the moss exhales a warm, yeasty breath and yields a fraction of an inch, as though deciding whether to trust you with its recollections. It remembers the weight of earlier moss, the way their bodies have flattened into the stone and fed the next green wave. In its dampest crevices live the whisper-lice, pale creatures no larger than seeds, who spend their lives listening. They have no need for eyes; their bodies are attuned to vibration, and they navigate by the difference between a footfall that means harm and one that only means pause.

Above them, strung between reeds, are the glass-ring singers. They are not birds, though they are often mistaken for them. They are creatures of translucent chitin, slender as needles, with bodies that catch the haloed light and turn it into stained glass. They do not eat in any way we would recognize. Instead, they gather moisture in prismatic cups along their backs and tilt themselves into the wind, letting droplets strike them in sequences. Each droplet produces a note, and the singers arrange themselves in loose choirs, spacing themselves so that the wind’s passage becomes a scale. Their music is not for mating or warning. It seems to be for calibration—for tuning the dampness of the air to the patience of the stone. When a singer finishes its sequence, it grows heavier, duller, and eventually sinks into the moss where it dissolves like sugar, its substance returning to the pools that birthed it.

In the water itself live the mirror-wraiths—pale, eel-shaped beings that refuse to be seen head-on. They drift just beneath the surface and mimic the shape of the sky, drinking the reflection rather than the light. If you look down and see a cloud moving faster than the wind, or a star trembling in midday, it is likely a wraith changing its mind about what it wants to be. They are shy, but curious. Sometimes they rise until their bellies breach, not to breathe—whatever they use for that happens inside their mirrored skin—but to taste the heavier air that hovers shoulder-high. When startled, they fold into themselves and sink as though pulled by a thread, leaving only the ripple that insists something has retreated with tact.

There are also the fern-walkers, though they are less bodies than agreements. They appear as disturbances in the fronds—shapes made of overlapping shadows and condensed dew. A fern might suddenly lean in a direction no breeze explains, its fronds arching to form a temporary roof, a temporary corridor. If you stand still long enough, you may feel a coolness pass over your shoulders like a cloak being draped. That is a fern-walker accepting your presence and deciding, for a moment, to escort you. They do not speak, but they hum—a sound like wet fingers rubbed around the lip of a glass—and if you follow their turning, they will lead you to places where the stone is softer, where the water has made a seat.

Deeper in the damp, among the tarnished-coin shrubs, live the mud-moths. Their wings are not for flight but for preservation. They are broad and heavy, patterned like sedimentary layers, and they spend most of their lives folded against stems, absorbing iron-rich damp until their patterns grow distinct as maps. When the humidity rises to a certain hush, they quiver and release a dust that smells of petrichor and iron. This dust settles into the cracks of stone and, over seasons, helps seal the water’s memory into the rock. Mud-moths live long lives, measured not in heartbeats but in accumulation. When they finally peel away from their stems, they do not fall. They drift downward like leaves, depositing their layered wings into pools where the mirror-wraiths tuck them into the silt as records.

Even the rain itself seems to have taken up residence. It no longer behaves like weather but like a tenant. It arrives through the day in small, polite batches, lingers as mist in the evening, and in the night becomes a slow, deliberate drummer on the broad leaves. Over time, the rain has learned the pitches of the glass-ring singers and sometimes softens its fall to avoid disturbing a chord. It knows when the moss is thirsty and when it is full. It has grown careful, as careful as anything that has nowhere else to go.

Predators are virtually unknown, not because nothing wants to eat, but because hunger here would be too loud, too disruptive. Instead there is a quiet economy of exchange. The glass-ring singers leave behind dissolved bodies that feed the moss. The moss exhales warmth that keeps the mud-moths pliable. The mud-moths seal the cracks that hold the pools. The pools reflect the sky so the mirror-wraiths can remember what it means to be unanchored. Even the whisper-lice serve by listening for imbalances, for the approach of something that does not understand dampness or patience. If such a thing were to arrive—a hardness, a sudden heat, a recklessness—the whisper-lice would go still, and their stillness would travel through the moss like a warning bell.

Night here is not dark so much as deepened. The hanging moisture traps what light remains and gives it weight. The reeds glow faintly, lit from within by colonies of algae that have learned to pulse in time with the dripping chorus. The mirror-wraiths rise and hang suspended, like ornaments on an invisible thread. The mud-moths open their wings slightly, revealing inner linings that shine like old coins. If you lie on your back on the stone, you will feel the day’s warmth leaching slowly from the rock into your own body, as if the bowl is sharing its stored patience.

What lives here has accepted that to stay is to change slowly, and that change is less a transformation than a rearrangement. There is no birth and death as the outside world performs them, only a continual folding and unfolding. The moss remembers. The stone softens. The singers tune. The water holds. And the high, kept bowl—cupped just below the sky—learns again how to breathe.
