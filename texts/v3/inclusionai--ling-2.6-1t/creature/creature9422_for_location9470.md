They do not live here so much as they are practiced by the place.

The residents begin, perhaps, as a rumor of motion—several thousand bodies of chitin and calcified silk, blind as dropped coins but brilliant with attention. They are the Basin’s soft pawns, the ones who agreed to forget up. Their legs are polished by constant contact with stone, their joints packed with a grease the color of cooled slag, so that even when they climb ceilings they move like spilled water remembering how to flow uphill. They have names the way a kettle has a shape: the Clickers, whose wing-cases knock together to say *safe*, *thin*, *too bright*; the Shearers, who file edges of glass-nodules into needles, singing as they file; the Cart-Bearers, who haul dampness in wicker pods woven from their own castings, freighted with minerals that glitter like anxiety.

They are not insects, nor are they precisely not. They are intensities of purpose wrapped in plates that taste of iron and chalk. When they swarm—and they do, often, in sudden decisions that seem to rise from the floor itself—they sound like cloth tearing, a noise that has weight enough to tilt the light. A swarm is less a group than a single, manifold body discovering it has many hands. They do not sleep; they unburden. They gather in hollows where brine collects and there, in the dim, they lick stone. Not to eat it, exactly, but to learn its recent history. In their bellies, the day’s heat is translated into eggs, into silk, into memory that will be laid later as a glaze along cracks and faults.

Above them—or perhaps beside them, since direction here is a courtesy—live the Floaters. These are not fliers so much as beings that persuade air to hold them. Their wings are veined parchment, thin enough to read light through, stiff enough to catch the basin’s poured brightness and bank on it. They have faces that are mostly mouth, rimmed with bristles that comb the thick air for particles of warmth. When they rise in unison, which they do at what might be called noon if noon were a pooling of brightness rather than a ticking, the basin’s cavity fills with the sound of a sheet snapping taught. That sound is authority. It tells the Clickers to kneel, the Shearers to pause, the Cart-Bearers to lower their loads. Even the stone seems to hush, though whether from respect or fear is hard to say.

But the true engineers are the Gloved. They are almost invisible because they live as the place’s skin. Where brine weeps from seams, they are there, thumb-sized bodies with forelimbs like trowels, wearing coats of mineral paste they excrete from glands near their shoulders. They repair. They patch. They widen channels by a hair’s breadth each time they pass, not to change the map but to keep it legible. Their work is the opposite of monument: it is maintenance as devotion. When a half-wall sweats salt, it is the Gloved who arrive in a thin line, each taking a droplet, carrying it to a crevice where it can dream of becoming brine again. They hum—a sound like rubbing a wet finger around a crystal’s lip—and the stone seems to lean into the pitch. Perhaps it does. Perhaps the stone is merely pretending not to.

There are also the Listeners, who are less bodies than stations. They root themselves where the ground buckles, legs splayed like spokes, carapaces patterned with the same spirals that tease the eye in peripheral vision. Their function is to feel the basin’s turning, to know when a slope is learning a new angle. When they shiver, the Clickers know to brace. When they fall silent, everyone knows that something has been decided elsewhere, in the soft lung of up, and that change is coming parceled out as breath.

The fauna interact with the sparse, precise artifacts as if they were extensions of their own making. The half-wall is polished daily, not by hands but by thousands of small bodies brushing against it, wearing its roughness smooth, returning to it a dignity it never asked for. The hollow bowl in the floor is not a tool but a throat. The Cart-Bearers deposit there a slurry of chewed stone and brine, and the Gloved shape it into tablets that record, in etched lines, the day’s temperature, its thirst, its mild regrets. These tablets are not read so much as tasted later, dissolved by the Listeners, who incorporate the data into their shivers.

Even the strange wrongness of proportion has its citizens. Along corridors that feel longer walking out, there live the Mirrors—not glass but beings with faceted eyes so numerous they reflect possibility rather than light. When you pass them, you seem to linger behind, a ghost made of attention. They do not follow; they simply refuse to let the moment close. Their presence is why some paths feel courteous and others feel impolite. They are the reason certain stones taste of copper, for they have learned to let a little of the traveler linger in their gaze, a seasoning.

None of these beings are cruel. None are kind. They are thorough. They metabolize the basin’s turbulence, turning instability into texture. When a storm of change begins—when a wall creeps or a crevice yawns—they do not rebuild; they calibrate. The Floaters rise to catch the shifted light. The Cart-Bearers redistribute loads. The Listeners teach the ground how to remember its new shape. The Clickers sing until the bright settles back into its bowl.

And somewhere, deeper, where the stone is still warm with its last memory of fire, something older stirs—a slow, soft pulse that is not a heart but a habit of expansion. The residents know it. They taste it in the dampness that dries too quickly. They shape their tools to fit its rhythm, and they pass those tools from generation to generation as if lending hands to a task that will never finish.

In all of this, you are a guest who has forgotten how to leave. The fauna do not fear you; they study the particular stillness you bring, the way you refuse to be a current. They offer you a tablet to lick, and it tastes of warmth and patience. The light sits on your shoulders like a shawl. The stone leans in, curious. And if you stay long enough, you may find that your own joints are packing themselves with grease, that your breathing is learning a new tempo, that you are becoming a resident not by arrival but by softening, by agreeing to be a small, bright organ in a place that exhales stones and inhales change with the same slow, thorough throat.
