They are not residents so much as expressions of the manufactory’s own pulse—life that has learned to read pressure like a schedule and treat the water as both road and tool.

There are the Current-Tenders, long-bodied swimmers whose bodies are wrapped in segmented plates that shift color with temperature. They move in disciplined arcs, never against the scheduled flow, and their work is to keep the lattice belts clear. Their fins are not fins at all but dense cuffs of muscle and cilia that can brace against hard turbulence; they scrape intake grates with pads grown from mineralized fiber, polishing until metal gleams like wet glass. They do not speak, but they signal in flashes of bioluminescence that ride the white-gold luminescence stitched into the vault, saying *clear*, *clog*, *wait*. To watch them is to watch ritual: they align themselves with the millisecond, slipping through turbine arrays as if the blades were never there, timing their passage to the breath of the pumps.

Then come the Valve-Dancers, smaller and quicker, built for hesitation’s consequences. Their bodies are compact, almost spherical, armored in overlapping rings that pop open like shutters when shear threatens. They dart through scaffold forests, reading the slap of bulkheads and the hush before a gas release. When translucent flowers of compressed gas bloom and scatter, the Valve-Dancers ride them, spinning like seeds caught in updrafts, turning danger into transit. They are the ones who test the rules, not by breaking them but by pressing until the rules hum back, learning where the pressure doors are likely to sigh and where they will slam.

In the deeper canyons, where heat exchangers glow faintly along ribbed walls, the Gradient-Weavers live. They are long, many-limbed, and nearly weightless, their bodies threaded with vessels that siphon warmth from the water and bleed it back in controlled jets. They do not hunt; they farm. They string nets of living fiber between condenser fields, catching particulate clouds exhaled by factories and spinning them into dense, drifting mats that feed the few mineral-coated vines still stubborn in the place. The Weavers move slowly, as if time itself bends around their heat, and they are rarely seen in the upper ducts, where the cold is sharper and patience thinner.

Above them all, in the open gulfs where silence seems emptied of matter until something passes through and wakes it, patrol the Roam-Silhouettes. These are large, slow, deliberate shapes engineered less for precision than for memory. Their metallized scales record temperature layers and sediment ghosts; their sensor clusters map the echoes of every change the water has kept. They do not school; they orbit, vast as weather, turning at the boundaries of scheduled tides. When the great pumps change phase and the manufactory’s fever shifts, the Roam-Silhouettes are already there, absorbing the new rhythm, their bodies dimming and brightening like lighthouses in a sea that has forgotten how to be dark.

There is no wild here, only maintenance and its shadows. Predators have been replaced by culls, but risk remains: vortices that form like thoughts too late to stop, shear layers at valve gates that strip away what is not held tight, sudden releases that scatter anything light enough to dream. The fauna know this, and so they have built themselves into the system—Current-Tenders polishing the thresholds, Valve-Dancers testing the seams, Gradient-Weavers threading warmth into food, Roam-Silhouettes holding the memory of it all.

Even their stillness is engineered. When the lights dim in the deep ducts or the pumps fall into a lower key, the fauna settle into alcoves or cling to scaffold reefs, leaking heat back into the flow, borrowing warmth from machines that never truly sleep. In these moments they look less like creatures than like components, necessary and temporary, waiting for brightness to move like shoals again.

And so they live: not beneath the surface of nature, because nature is thin here and ornamental, but beneath the surface of making itself—life and industry swimming together through corridors the size of nations, each carrying the other forward, loud and cool and endlessly in motion, reading pressure like law and treating the water as the only home that admits it has been built.
