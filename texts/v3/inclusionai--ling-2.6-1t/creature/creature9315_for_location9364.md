They live in the negative space left by a mountain’s surrender.

The people—if they can still be called that—have learned to inhabit flatness as if it were a vertical challenge. Their bodies are long and low to the ground, not out of fear of falling but because altitude has seeped into their marrow as a metallic rumor, a reminder that breath is a borrowed thing here. They carry the plateau’s chill in the architecture of their throats: cartilage ringed like frost-fractured stone, lungs pleated like maps of the reknit air. When they exhale, the vapor does not fade; it lingers as a second skin, a translucent membrane that clings for minutes before breaking into a scatter of tiny, ringing lenses on the ground.

They do not build so much as they braid. Their settlements are grown rather than assembled—stems and leaves laminated into floors, partitions, vaulted ceilings that curve like the ribs of an animal too large to ever stand. The tissue has learned the duties of stone: it bears weight by remembering how to bend, it insulates by weeping. When you enter one of their chambers, the walls sweat. Beads gather along fibrous veins, form trembling threads, then vanish into porous mats that taste faintly of iron and rotting honey. The people do not wipe the wetness away; they read it. They know which seams will leak tomorrow, which clefts will sing when the cold deepens.

Yet for all this closeness to green, they do not crowd. Each person—or family, or hive-mind, or whatever unit of belonging applies—stands at the center of a deliberate clearing, as if holding breath for everyone else. In these gaps the plateau exhales plainly, and the wetness gathers its thoughts. Between one living form and the next there is always a distance large enough to let the cold settle like a blade laid flat, ceremonial and precise. They move along these intervals as if performing pilgrimage, pausing at the edges of each other’s microclimates to trade warmth in small, pressurized sips.

Their light is not lent from the sky but manufactured below. Bioluminescent tissues pulse in their chests, in the roots that tangle beneath their feet, in the fungus they cultivate along their spines like jewelry. Mushrooms bloom from shoulders and collarbones, staining the air with halos that refuse to clarify. When day filters down—a bruised, hesitant thing—it catches on these private suns and fractures into colors that have no names, only temperatures: a violet that aches like a bruise, a green that hisses when touched.

Sound is a physical object here. It comes from their industrial-plant bodies: turbines of calcified vine spinning in hidden chest-cavities, pistons of swollen stem driving thick sap through rhythmic channels, belts of fibrous bark clattering across ribbed housings beneath the skin. Their labor is a metabolism. They do not produce so much as maintain: clogging, clearing, rerouting, leaking. When the plateau shudders, they shudder with it.

The fauna among them are less companions than cohabitants. Herds with too many legs graze on the luminous scabs that crust over their slowest leaks, while others ride the air on ragged membranes, calling in jagged cries that set vegetal walls trembling. Some creatures have fused with the people’s machinery—beasts sheathed in polished chitin that doubles as housing for pistons, eyes windows into tiny, burning furnaces. Predators do not hunt individuals; they hunt events—shifting concentrations of hunger and noise that pass through the landscape like storms, harvesting the edges of signal and silence.

Time here is not linear but layered. The people experience it as a constant making and unmaking, a turbulence in which stems reroute themselves overnight and sections of the vegetal floor rise into temporary towers only to slump back into carpets. Because of this, memory behaves like water. It surfaces, forms a trembling thread, then vanishes again into the porous mats of the present. They have learned to trust what leaks, to value the transient gleam over the fixed form. When echoes precede their causes, they do not correct the sequence; they learn to walk backward into speech, to greet the future by its departure.

There are those among them who seem almost mechanical, their bodies so integrated with the plateau’s pulse that individual boundaries blur. These figures hum with the deep frequency of the land itself, their voices not so much spoken as produced by the flexing of tissue and the clatter of industrial detritus half-digested by the living mass. They are oracles of the almost-arriving, able to sense when the world hesitates—when the sheer patience of the old earth brushes against the frantic impatience of becoming and the shelf shudders with a thousand industrial lungs.

In those instants, the people fall silent. The bioluminescent tissues flare in hues that have no names, and the fauna cease their grazing, their too-many legs stilled as if waiting for permission to begin again. The wetness stops weeping. The cold stops sharpening. For one suspended heartbeat, the landscape considers unfurling into something else entirely—a geometry of pure idea, a machine dreaming itself into leaves, a final erasure of the peak’s last memory.

Then it settles back.

They settle back.

The people return to their clearings, to their braided walls and sweating ceilings, to the deliberate distances between one living form and the next. They breathe in the metallic chill, let the vapor clot on their lashes, and continue the work of being almost themselves: ancient and eroded, wet and cold, abundant and sparse, vegetal and mechanical, quietly ending themselves and beginning again with every pulse in the dark.
