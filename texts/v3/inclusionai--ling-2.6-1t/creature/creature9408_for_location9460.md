What lives here has learned to measure itself in saturation.

The bell-jar is thick with a parliament of glass-jellies, their bells lanterns of cold blue and bruised violet. They do not swim so much as negotiate, pulsing in three-part codes that translate pressure into light. Their trailing filaments are not mere tendrils but living multimeters, tasting gradients of iron, copper, and the faint static of half-born machines. When the tide creeps up a millimeter, they ascend as a single luminous lung, exhaling clouds of ionized mist that sparkle like struck matches before sinking back into the soup.

Closer to the floor, where basalt teeth gnash and chalcopyrite veins glitter like freeze-dried circuitry, the Shelled Skiffs roam. Each is a cathedral of cartilage and piston, six articulated legs ending in pads of calcified rubber that grip wet metal without slipping. They are scavengers and mechanics, their shells encrusted with barnacles of oxidized alloy that ring like tiny cymbals when they walk. Inside their armored bellies, pump diaphragms beat at a different tempo than the water’s cello note, digesting mineral silt and excreting it as thin sheets of mother-of-pearl that drift upward to coat the undersides of grates. They dock upside down along the coolant cascades, folding their legs into prayer, and rest like sleeping moths dreaming of flight they will never need.

Needle-fins are the scribes of this place, needle-thin bodies braided from cartilage and alloy wire. They ride the layered eddies, drinking current through spiraled gills and exhaling sparks that bloom into brief auroras of ion mist. Their movements spell signatures in the water, calligraphic arcs that the jellies answer with flares of brilliance. They are not territorial; they are migratory, following the faint songs of trace elements that bleed from the seams of the volcanic glass, singing back in clicks and static hisses that make the valves practice their alphabet all night.

Between the ribs of ribbed pipes, in the thickets of chain and spherical floats, live the Chitinous Clacks, colonies that blur the line between machine and crustacean. Each individual is a small forge of articulated plates, clicking not only to speak but to tighten bolts with their mandibles, to file burrs from newly extruded pipes, to polish ceramic thorns until they hum. Their communication is a staccato language of impacts, a Morse of maintenance that keeps the place alert. They swarm over the surfaces of dormant flywheels, oiling axles with secretions that smell like rain on hot copper, and when the iris-valve at the center sings its low note, they fall silent as a held breath.

Above, in the updrafts along the glassy walls, drift the Glass-motes, swarms of needle-thin silica creatures that ride thermal ghosts. They refract the doubled light into pathways that shift like ladders, and when they settle on the water’s skin, they form temporary constellations that map the slow hills of denser gravity. They are not alive in any old sense; they are seeds of memory, fragments of eruption that learned to flock, and they leave behind faint traceries of brightness that linger minutes after they have moved on.

Even the water itself hosts a kind of life. Particulates of metal and glass swirl in slow layers, never quite mixing, composing and decomposing into mandalas that map the erosion’s youth. In the deepest pool by the fused-glass altar, the jelly-lanterns cluster so densely they become a single thinking organ, their pulses syncing with the groan of the large turning thing in the distance. There, the water is almost black, a mirror that holds not reflections but intentions.

And there are the Soft-metals, ephemeral fauna born when alloys shift phase and become momentarily waxen. They ooze from junctions like slow tears, harden into new geometries overnight, then dissolve again, a lifecycle measured in tides and temperature. They are scavengers of form, borrowing shapes from the machines, from the skiffs, from the jellies, and returning them changed.

No creature here is built; all are accreted, grown, excreted by the logic of the flooded jar. They do not fear the crowding; they thrive in it, threading through the industrial forest with practiced choreography. They know the room by its smells—metallic, cool, precise—and by the texture of light that feels like braille against their skins.

If you watch long enough, you will see a skiff exhale a sheet of pearl that is promptly colonized by glass-motes until it glows like a lantern. You will see a needle-fin stitch a signature that a jelly answers with a pulse that travels the length of the room, making the coolant curtains shiver. You will see the Soft-metals pool at the base of a turbine snout, harden into a coronet, and crumble back into flow by morning.

This is a place where life is not an exception but a saturation, where industry and mineral and current have learned to breathe together in a small, dense, wet lung that holds its breath and continues, alive without apology, teeming like a tide caught in a bell.
