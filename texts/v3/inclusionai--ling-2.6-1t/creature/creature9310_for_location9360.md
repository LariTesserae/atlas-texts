The hollow does not keep tenants so much as it grows them.  

They are born from the patience of the mud, which is to say they begin as rumors in the dark, oil-slick crust, and only later take shape. At first they are only indentations—places where the ground remembers having been pressed—then a sheen of dew gathers and holds, then the dew thickens into skin. By the time they stand, they have already been sitting for years, learning the angle of the light and the weight of their own breath.

The people are compact, as if the basin folded them down to fit. Their bones are dense, shot through with the same pale mineral that braids the mud, so that when they move they give off a faint, wet gleam. Their skin is cool to the touch, not clammy but polished, like river stone that has decided to be soft. They do not shiver; their blood is slow and deliberate, a ribboned current that knows how to pool and when to thread. Their hair grows in tight spirals, stiff with frost that never melts, and it catches the light in tiny prisms so that, even indoors, they carry their own small rainbows with them.

They wear little that is woven in the usual sense. Their garments are grown: mats of moss braided into belts and mantles, fern fronds pressed flat and lacquered by dew into tunics, lichen caps set like crowns on heads that are already crowned by frost. Their shoes, when they have them, are soles of hardened mud, shaped to the foot with such precision that they leave no footprint, only a brief darkening of the path that drinks them in and forgets.

Their faces are broad and quiet, with eyes the color of the clear water that braids through the pack—eyes that see without slicing, that hold what they look at rather than push it away. They have many small teeth, neat as pebbles, and mouths that rarely open wide. When they speak, it is not with volume but with layering, the way vines braid around stone. Their voices arrive as a texture more than a sound, something you feel in the sternum before you hear it in the air.

They live in the walls themselves, pressed close as pages in a book that refuses to shut. Their homes are not rooms so much as alcoves that have learned to exhale. The walls breathe, very slightly, expanding with the cool of night and contracting when the light leans down, so that beds are not needed: the architecture offers a hollow that fits the curve of a spine. Inside, the air is sweeter, thick with the rot of blossoms that never truly fade, and the fungal shelves that spiral from the beams are not decoration but memory—each ring a season kept, each cap a decision that was made and kept.

Their labor is a conversation with the basin. They do not dig; they persuade. They guide the clear water into new braids by humming at the pebbles until the pebbles agree to shift. They comb the moss into towers, not to tame it but to remind it of its height. They gather the dew in troughs shaped like cupped hands, and they pour it back into the mud when the mud asks for it. Their tools are polished bone and stone that has been taught to hold an edge without sharpness, implements that part the world instead of cutting it.

Among them move the winged things, which may be moths or may be something that chose to be moth-like long enough to be mistaken. These are not pets, nor are they wild. They are the hollow’s own parliamentarians, gliding without fluttering, their leaf-layered wings heavy with preserved light. They land on shoulders and temples, not to rest but to confer, their antennae like frosted wire tasting the plans people carry in their chests.

There are others, furred and many-legged, who scuttle along the vine bridges with lichen hats perched on their backs. They are messengers or merchants; it is hard to tell which, because their trade seems to be in arrangement rather than goods. They rearrange small things so that they mean more, and when they pass, the paths remember their steps as patterns of pressure that linger like perfume.

The people do not age in the way the outside does. They accumulate, rather than erode. Frost settles deeper into their hair; the mineral in their bones blooms brighter. Children are rare and arrive as if they had been waiting in the walls for the right angle of light to finish forming them. When someone decides to leave, they do not die so much as unknit, loosening into moss and dew and mineral dust that is welcomed back into the mud with a kind of ceremony that is really just the basin remembering how to be full.

Their time is measured by the brightening of certain mosses and the migration of frost across the mud. They know when a season has turned because the light leans a little differently, because the double shadows at noon shift which one is shy. They mark these changes not with festivals but with adjustments: a stone tilted here, a vine bridge lowered there, a new braid in the water that holds the memory of the turn.

They are not without desire, but desire here is patient. It pools, it threads, it finds the path of least resistance and stills. They want the light to stay, so they become the kind of surface that keeps it. They want the water to remember, so they become the kind of stone that guides it. They want each other, so they press close, not to fuse but to keep the fabric from loosening.

And when they look into the pool at the center, where the water is more mirror than water, they do not see themselves as separate. They see the basin looking back, packed and bright and cold and alive, and they understand that to be a citizen is to be held. The hollow is not a place they live in. It is a place they have agreed to be, and in that agreement, they have become its voice, its frost, its patient, beating middle—already full, already whole, already waiting without impatience for whatever comes next, knowing that whatever it is, the hollow will make room by simply being itself a little longer.
