They did not arrive; they accreted.

The first inhabitants are the Laminar Choir, colonies of moss-animals not quite moss and not quite animal, living in the velvet insulation of the slopes. They are pale, branching films that drink the condensation beads and metabolize brightness. Their bodies are lenses; their skins focus the white-blue bowl of sky into tiny, scalding suns inside their tissues. At 2.9 illumination, they sing. Their song is the hiss of wet glass cooling, the sound of brightness condensing into language. They do not mean to speak. They mean to hold light still, and the holding comes out as sound—millions of files drawn across paper, but underwater.

Higher up, where erosion is still a rumor, the Scarp Sentinels live. They are not animals. They are gestures of stone learning to be betrayed. Each ridge is a half-seam, each face a raised grain that remembers being stroked into shape by something patient and vast. On clear mornings, they soften at the edges and become almost porous, allowing the cool, 1.4, to seep through them like breath through cloth. They shift, but only when no one is watching in the human sense. They shift by growing new fractures that immediately knit themselves shut, like wounds that decide to become doors. Their thought is slow, a lithic 1.3 that revises itself once every wet season, redrafting their own blueprints in mineral ink.

Between the sparse trunks, 0.8 emptiness held like a vowel, the Root Parliament gathers. These are not individual trees so much as committees fused underground. Their trunks stand apart, canopies distant, but their roots interlace into a single, wet tapestry that sorts the mist into drink and memory. The Parliament does not vote. It leaches consensus upward, turning it into wood and leaf. When it disagrees—which is rare—it expresses dissent by letting a single tree grow a canopy with a slightly different shade of green, a quiet heresy in a landscape of brilliance. Fog pools in their laps, and they wear it like wet wool stitched with wire.

The Constructors live among them: the Vat-Keepers and Loom-Leaves. These are not separate species but extensions of the factory that translates itself into forest. They wear walls latticed with greenery; they let cooling towers double as greenhouses. They are colonial organisms, part cellulose sheets as thin as thought, part pipe and shivering condensate, part captured cloud. They hum at 2.8 in sound, a deafening lullaby that keeps the cool from freezing and the wet from forgetting itself. They dream in vats, growing new bodies from the sheets they print, bodies that resemble bridges, causeways, chimneys—forms that remember having once been built and now prefer to be grown.

Rarer are the Slipwalkers, the six-legged creatures the color of river-stone. They caliper through the ferns, measuring the land with legs that are also rules—rules of balance, angle, erosion. They do not leave footprints; they leave suggestions of pressure, places where the moss dimples and then remembers. They are patient, dynamic, slow to revise themselves, and they carry within them a map of where the land is still embryonic. When they pause, condensation beads on their carapaces and turns them into temporary lenses, magnifying the undersides of fronds into green galaxies. They drink those galaxies, translating starlight into something like starch.

Above them, the Wing-Signalers stitch the air. Birds that have traded voice for violence, they crack whips of wing-slap that cut the thick, cool air like paper. Their messages are short, sharp, architectural. They speak of leeward, of fracture, of brightness falling. They are not predators so much as editors, trimming the edges of the sound so the place does not drown in its own volume.

And in the wettest shadows, where the waterfalls staircase down in hush, the Mycelial Conductors glow faintly along the stamped loam of old roads. They are the nervous system of the whole, carrying the small electric hum of the buried factory, carrying the green-and-amber code that makes the trees tilt their leaves in unison. They do not think as brains do. They think as circuits that dream of being rivers, always branching, always reaching for the next regional dip, 1.7 in scale, where the land stretches out like a province of wet wool and wire.

Together, they are the inhabitants of a world hydrological first, structural second. They are wet things learning to rise, bright things learning to condense, sparse things learning to be total. They do not live in the place so much as they are the place’s way of holding its shape without letting go of the wet that made it. They are rare and patient and loud. They are embryonic, still drafting themselves each wet season, singing in a key that loosens teeth and turns vapor into silver dust and makes a stone feel, for a moment, almost soft.
