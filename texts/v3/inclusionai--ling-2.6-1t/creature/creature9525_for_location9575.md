They call themselves the Seamfolk, though among themselves they have no name for the whole—only for its parts. To live here is to be a kind of moss first, a kind of stone second, and a kind of person third. Their bodies are short and broad at the base, with long, deliberate limbs suited to climbing slick faces and threading among crowded stems. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, shot through with faint blue-green tracery like water finding fissures in ice; it drinks the ambient glow and returns it dimmed, as if they carry their own small reservoirs of night.

They do not build so much as persuade. A Seamfolk dwelling is a negotiation between inhabitant and growth: a nudge to a fungal trunk, a channel cut to invite a curtain of stone-water to fall just so, a lattice of living root coaxed to thicken into a wall. These changes happen slowly, over seasons of damp and dark, and the Seamfolk measure time in the thickening of a beam or the deepening of a runnel. Even their tools are alive or once-living—blades of hardened resin, saws of sharpened bone that never lose their edge because they are constantly drinking mineral from the wet stone they cut. When a house decides it has grown enough, it may seal a doorway with a skin of new wood; the Seamfolk accept this and cut a new path, grateful for the timber.

The town is a parliament of many species, and the Seamfolk are only its translators. There are Root-tenders, smaller folk with fingernails like polished flint who whisper into cracks and listen for answers in the flex of cellulose. There are Glow-keepers, blind or nearly so, who cultivate lantern-plants along the arteries of passage, trimming brightness like hair so no corner is wasted in shadow. There are Channel-dancers, lithe and sure-footed, who move along the gutters and shallow canals, clearing debris with gestures that look like dance, redirecting flow with nothing but posture and patience.

Water is their law and their gossip. To the Seamfolk, every film, bead, and thread is a sentence. They read the slickness of a wall to know which way the cold has been sleeping; they taste the drip from a curtain to learn what stone lies behind it and whether it is weeping or withholding. Disputes are settled at the edge of a pool: the parties stand knee-deep and let the current find their balance. Whoever is nudged first concedes, and the water is believed to have spoken. It is rare for anyone to be knocked over; the practice is older than memory, and dignity is preserved by the slowness of the medium.

Their animals are partners more than possessions. The blind newts with frilled gills are kept in household gutters, where they twitch at changes in taste and warn of poisons leaching from the fresh rock. Translucent moths, large as hands, are bred for the quietness of their wings; they are sent into tight places to listen for the sounds of rock settling or water finding a new course, returning with legs dusted in minerals that the Glow-keepers interpret. Eel-salamanders are messengers of a sort, gliding through sheetwater with tokens tied to their smooth backs, carrying news between tiers without the disturbance of footsteps.

Reproduction is communal and patient. Children are born from eggs laid in communal cisterns lined with luminous moss, and the whole district shares in their raising. A child may spend its first years in baskets woven from living stem, rocked by the soft pulse of water flowing beneath. They learn early how to hold their breath and how to listen to the thickness of the air. They learn that cold is not empty but a shape that gives definition to warmth, and that darkness is not the absence of things but the presence of too many things at once.

Belief is architectural. The Seamfolk believe that the basin is learning to be a place, and that every life within it is a lesson the land is teaching itself. They leave offerings not to gods but to edges: a polished pebble placed where two kinds of stone meet, a song hummed into a fresh fracture, a braid of vine tied around a young stalactite to remind it to grow down and not in. They fear sudden silence, for it suggests that the conversation between water and rock has stalled, and they will spend days coaxing a trickle back into a crevice with their own spit and sweat if they must.

Their bodies show their pact. Elders grow small shelves of callus along their spines, like brackets for hanging tools or cups; some develop sensitive frills along their forearms that tremble when the humidity shifts. Their eyes, large and dark, have a second, clear lid for swimming through sheetwater without losing sight. They do not speak above a murmur, because sound is a luxury the place cannot afford in excess; instead they have a language of touch and pressure—hands on stone, palms on living wall, fingers spelling into moss.

When strangers from above find their way down, the Seamfolk watch for a long time before revealing themselves. They note how the strangers flinch at the closeness, how they breathe too fast and waste warmth. If the strangers can learn to slow their blood to the rhythm of the gutter, they may be given a place to live on the condition that they never stand still for long, never block a drip, never let their shadow fall across a glow-garden without asking permission. The Seamfolk do not exile; they simply allow the place to teach the lesson, which is sometimes slower but never less thorough.

In the seams where city and marsh are pressed together, the Seamfolk move like breath between ribs. They are patient, numerous, and necessary. They are the memory of the basin learning itself, and as long as the rock is young and the water is finding its way, they will continue to live as a question posed to stone, answered in moss, translated into town, and held in a dark palm that refuses to open.
