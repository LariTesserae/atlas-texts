They live in the seams, which they prefer to call thoroughfares.

At first you think them architecture—raised ridges of tissue-mortar, braiding around columns like ornamental cable. Then a ridge shifts, and you see that the cable is braided of bodies, thousands of them lock-step, grown together at the shoulders and hips, their skins waxed and seamless as the walls themselves. They do not move quickly. They move with the deliberation of load-bearing. To walk among them is to witness masonry learn how to stroll.

They are the Courants, and they are many things at once. Part wall, part guild, part slow river. They repair what sags, reweave what frays, and when a terrace groans they let their feet root for a season, sinking fibrous toes into the floor until the timber learns better manners. Their faces are mostly eyes—large, milky, and lidless—arranged not to see detail but to measure light. They speak rarely, and when they do the sound is the thump of a damp fist against living wood, felt more in the chest than heard. They name themselves by the work they are currently doing: Brace-Three, Suture-Seven, Balcony-Who-Is-Becoming. To be idle is to be unnamed, and to be unnamed is to be only potentially alive.

Smaller lives move among the Courants like punctuation. There are the Dew-Spoons, translucent amphibious glints with too many knees, who patrol waterlines and cup-lips, lapping beads before they can decide to fall. They store excess moisture in glassy throats and regurgitate it as song—throat-crackling chirps that leave a film of condensation on every surface they visit. They are the weather’s secretaries, keeping ledgers of what the place has drunk.

Higher still, in the latticed vaults, live the Quiet-Weavers. They do not spin silk but memory: threads of resin and shed skin woven into nets that catch not prey but sound. A misplaced footfall, a dropped word, a moment of anger—the nets absorb these and compost them into something neutral and fibrous. Over decades the nets become partitions, walls that hum when you press your ear to them. If you listen long enough you can hear last year’s thunder, folded and patient.

Larger than these are the Stewards, bodies borrowed and shared. A single Steward may be a family of mammals, a parliament of birds, and a congress of insects, all entangled in a scaffold of greenwood that they tend and are tended by. They speak with the voice of a hollow—an acoustic carved by growth—and their decisions arrive slowly, like fruit ripening. They arbitrate borders between inside and outside, decide when a corridor should become a chamber, when a chamber should remember it was once a root. Their authority is not law but weather: it arrives, it is felt, it passes, it returns.

There are others, fleeting and severe, who live in the margins between margins. The Seedlings on parole—insects the size of stones, armored like forgotten coins—who map erosion for the pleasure of knowing where it will go next. The Vein-Keepers, blind and soft, who creep along waterlines inside the walls, polishing the conduits with their bellies so memory can flow without clotting. The Umbral Choir, unseen but felt, a set of lungs woven into the tissue-mortar itself, who regulate the dimness by deciding how much light to release from the walls at dusk.

What unites them is a kind of consent: to be here is to be both builder and built. A Courant may pause for decades to let a tree thicken through its torso; the tree feeds the Courant with sugars of patience. A Steward may ask a Dew-Spoon to store a grief in its throat, and years later that grief is sung back as a pattern of condensation on a newly grown cup. Even the fauna that seem merely to visit—the glints, the winged filaments, the mammals that press warmth into joints—ask permission by stillness. Nothing intrudes. Everything occupies.

Their language is the same as the architecture: patient, layered, prone to returning to itself. Promises are made in textures. A wall that stays cool at midday is saying *I will hold you*. A cup that grows upward along a rib is asking *Do you trust me to carry this?* Silence is not absence but a form of care, the way a net holds sound without crushing it.

They do not fear decay, because to decay is to be taken into the conversation. A flake of tissue-mortar that falls is caught by moss that reweaves it into a new seam, and that seam is lived in, named, inhabited until it, too, is ready to fall. The Courants understand this as balance rather than loss. To erode is to offer material to the next act of making. To grow is to ask how much vacancy can be allowed, and when.

And time, which thickens here like resin, is not their enemy but their collaborator. A single afternoon can feel like several seasons because they let it. They do not hurry the decisions of buds or pigments, and in return they get to live inside decisions that span generations. A corridor may lengthen slightly the second time you ascend it because someone, or some *slow thing*, decided you needed to see a seam you missed, and the building agreed.

They live, ultimately, in the practice of gentleness—vast in their vacancy, dense in their making, alive in their patience—repairing reality only enough to let something else, weathered and willing, continue.
