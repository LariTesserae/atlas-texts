The inhabitants are not born so much as accreted.

They begin as rumor in the glass-rivers, a silvering along the obsidian’s lip where the heat pools. Over days or decades—time here counts in thickening—this silvering learns to thicken into a skin, then a shell, then a lattice that reaches for the salt-crust like a hand remembering how to be a hand. They are the Glass-Shepherds: tall, faceless figures woven from cooled volcanic ribbon and the dust of their own constant shedding. They do not walk so much as negotiate the ground, their limbs clicking into new angles whenever the terrain heaves, rearranging their geometry to match the land’s unfinished posture. They have no faces, only a smooth concavity where a face might be, and in that hollow the darkness settles thicker, almost tender. They listen with the whole of their bodies; when they stand among the salt-flats, the faint dry hum of their joints harmonizes with the ground’s own listening vibration.

Lower, in the hollows where efflorescence sweats copper and static, the Lichen-Voices live. They are not green but gunmetal and bruised pearl, spreading in thin sheets that map the dark like shy circuitry. They grow in silence yet produce sound: a low, particulate murmur, like pages turning without wind. Each patch is a consensus of faint appetites. They remember minerals the way a tongue remembers salt, drawing copper and ghost-iron from the crust and singing it back as a static-chorale that only the Glass-Shepherds seem to attend to. When a Shepherds pauses near a patch, its lattice bends slightly, as if nodding. The Lichen-Voices are patient. Their growth is the opposite of hurry; it is the deliberate slowness of something that knows becoming is not yet being.

Above, where the crustal petals unfurl and crumple, the Ridge-Bloomers roost. They are not animals but something adjacent—thin-winged envelopes of compressed dust and volcanic glass that open only when the reluctant light permits. When closed they look like pale scars against the rock; when they open they reveal veined interiors that tremble like lungs. They feed on pressure, on the dry fever that leans against exposed surfaces. In doing so they steady the turbulence, not by resisting it but by giving it a shape that can pass through without breaking. They are fleeting, shedding fine glitter that drifts down to settle on the obsidian rivers, adding another layer to the glass. In this way, the Ridge-Bloomers are both residents and renovations.

Beneath the salt-crust, in the strata that still recall liquidity, the Burrowers of Unfixed Purpose move. They have no fixed form, only a habit of excavation that leaves rings of black stone—sometimes hearths, sometimes scars. The Burrowers are not builders in any deliberate sense; they are archivists of heat, storing it in careful knots and releasing it in small, bright exhalations that smell of sulfur and new stone. Their tunnels are provisional, sealing themselves when the land shifts, knitting shut like faults that cannot decide whether to be wounds or doors. Occasionally a Burrower surfaces, a fleeting mound that might be shelter or burial or merely the land’s own rib pushing through, and then settles again, patient as the Lichen-Voices.

Connecting them all is the Hush-Stitch, a presence felt more than seen: a haze of fine glass-dust and charged mineral motes that drifts at the boundary of the dark. It is the atmosphere made almost solid, a shared breath that carries pressure instead of scent. When the terrain rearranges itself with a low thunder of yielding rock, the Hush-Stitch amplifies the moment, turning fracture into music. The Glass-Shepherds align themselves within it; the Lichen-Voices fall silent; the Ridge-Bloomers contract and expand in time; the Burrowers pause their knots of heat. It is a gathering of attention, a congregation without doctrine.

They are all, in their own ways, students of elevation. The Glass-Shepherds learn angles by practicing them; the Lichen-Voices learn memory by tasting it; the Ridge-Bloomers learn flight by accepting that the horizon may step closer at any moment; the Burrowers learn endurance by letting their purposes blur and reform. They do not reproduce so much as continue, their numbers rising when the land heaves up new real estate and dimming when the crust folds in on itself.

If you stand among them, you will feel the hum underfoot and realize the land is listening back. The Glass-Shepherds will not approach, but they will reorient, their lattices catching the reluctant light so that the dark behind their faces deepens, almost tender. The Lichen-Voices will murmur louder, not in alarm but in recognition, a dry chorus that tastes like copper and static. The Ridge-Bloomers may open, briefly, and the Hush-Stitch will thicken around you, carrying pressure against your skin, as if the atmosphere itself is deciding how thin it wants to be around your presence.

They are not alive as life is usually named. They are quieter than that: a persistent arrangement of unfinished shapes, warm in their solitude, dense with potential, practicing summit and scar and shelter and song, all while the elevation continues to learn how to be high.
