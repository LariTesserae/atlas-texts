They are, first of all, the city’s way of remembering that it is not yet done.

Most of them are small enough to fit inside a closed hand, though size is less a fact about them than a permission the city grants: they can grow larger when the ledges widen and the channels deepen, and they can shrink when the stone tightens its seams. They move in sheets and spirals, not swarms, because swarms imply a center and they have many centers, or none. Their bodies are glossed with condensate that never quite drips off, carrying the metallic taste of the air in every step. Their shells are not merely protective but architectural—ribbed, latticed, pocked with micro-valleys that catch the faint, downward-bowing pressure of the dark. When they pause, they look like fixtures; when they move, they look like thoughts the city has just had.

Their legs do not tap. They articulate in damp clicks, each segment resolving a tiny portion of the weight around them, redistributing cold into vibration. Some have too many joints to count, others have almost none, their limbs folded into themselves like tools waiting for a problem to reveal its shape. A few trail filaments of silk that are not silk—stiffened water, mineral thread, memory of flow—which they lay across the black-ice buttresses and glass-bark lattices as if mapping the city’s intentions. The maps change nightly. The city, in its own time, accepts or erases them.

They do not eat in any sense that would comfort a stomach. They harvest patience: they rasp at the embryonic ridges and drink the mineral ghosts that bleed from the seams. In exchange they polish. Their passage leaves surfaces not clean but *finished*—notices given and acknowledged. Where they have been, the stone holds its breath a little longer before continuing its slow becoming. Their nests are less homes than propositions: temporary intensities of shell and damp that settle into faults, then dissolve, returning what they borrowed with interest in the form of aligned grains and tuned resonances.

Some of them hum. Not with wings or throats, but by rubbing internal lattices until the city answers. The hum is not sound alone; it is pressure shaped into rhythm, a tactile thing that moves through wet stone and climbs the transparent tubes, causing the sub-visible light within to brighten by degrees. In these moments the fauna seem less like animals than like maintenance crews made flesh—or chitin—and the city like a machine that has learned to enjoy being maintained.

There are the Scuttlers, low and many-limbed, who work at the capillary level, coaxing water into precise, metronomic drops and teaching it to remember intervals. There are the Glass-Spinners, lighter, almost translucent, who trace fault lines with filaments and persuade the half-formed canyons to hold still long enough to be bridged. There are the Bell-Ringers, rare and heavy, who climb the porous spires and knock until the stone sings back, confirming that thickness and hollowness are still possible here. There are the Thread-Walkers, who do not walk at all but are carried on updrafts of cold vapor along the layered causeways, adjusting shutters and nudging conduits into gentler gradients.

They are tolerated, yes, but also interrogated. The ledges designed for them are not resting places but conversation pits. The channels that guide them are not rivers but questions. The lights that herd them do not command so much as *ask*, repeatedly, whether this is the best way to carry warmth, or memory, or the idea of a wall.

At the center, around the hollow column where water ascends in a skin of glass, they gather in densities that look almost like governance. Their clicking rises to a pitch that is less noise than calibration—each small body contributing a frequency to the column’s silence, asking it what it has learned about rising without falling, about holding a shape made of ascent. The glyphs that shift in the refracted light are not messages but receipts: acknowledgments that the city has been understood, however briefly.

Beyond them, in the damp margins, live things that are harder to recognize because they are less interested in being recognized: patches of motion that travel the underside of overhangs like slow stains; colonies that are not colonies but agreements among droplets to behave like a single, reflective thought; forms that appear only when a section of wall remembers being elsewhere and shift their texture to match the memory. These do not click. They thrum. They do not harvest. They remember.

Together, they compose the loudness of the place—not a chaos but a density, a texture thick enough for darkness to wear. They are the city’s way of knowing it is crowded, and therefore not alone. They are the unfinished world learning how to hold itself together by holding each other, click by click, drop by drop, thought by slow, wet, metallic thought.

And when the terraces rearrange themselves overnight, optimizing without hurry, it is not magic. It is the city listening to its own fauna and deciding, gently, to make their lives a little easier—a courtesy exchanged between beings that are neither fully natural nor fully built, but both, and therefore more.
