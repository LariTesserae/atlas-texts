They call themselves the Weft, though among themselves the word means something closer to seam or slow-healing scar. They are not a people who happened upon this city; they are the city learning to hold its own shape with softer hands. To call them human is accurate only in the way calling a river a liquid is accurate: true as far as it goes, but missing the banks, the load, the long negotiation with the bed.

Their bodies have drifted toward the local grammar. Generations of low ceilings and braided corridors have left them compact and sinuous, shoulders narrow, spines subtly ridged like old cane, joints lined with pale calluses that help them grip when the damp makes everything slick. Their skin is cool to the touch, faintly mottled as if shadowed by leaf, and their hair grows thick and coarse, often bound into mats or plaited with strips of cured vine to keep it from snagging in the city’s tighter braids. In the deepest quarters, where the vegetal alloy presses closest, some have let their own tissues take on the prevailing rhythm: small nodules of hard, smooth tissue rise along collarbones or forearms, benign and useful, serving as anchors for tools, hooks for lamps, or as living brackets against which to brace a knee when a pipe needs turning.

They dress in layers that shed water and hold heat: thin inner wraps of woven fungus that wick moisture away, outer coats of compressed leaf and resin stiffened at the seams with lacquer made from boiled bark and machine oil. These coats are rarely plain; they bear the city’s stains—streaks of copper patina, mottlings of iron bloom, dustings of iridescent spores—so that no coat is truly finished until it has been worn down into a map of its wearer’s routes. Feet are usually bare or wrapped in supple moss-soled bindings that grip wet stone without marking it, and in the louder districts, where pump-beat trembles up through the floor, many pad their soles with thicker lichen to blunt the vibration.

What they lack in fauna they make up for in attention. The Weft have filled the ecological vacancy with careful observation. They study the city as one studies a vast, slow creature: they learn which vines stiffen before a shift in groundwater, which leaves curl when heat is building in the engine cores, which mosses dim when current is sagging. They are, by necessity, a people of maintenance and tuning. To walk through their streets is to move among constant micro-adjustments: a turned valve, a retied guide-rope, a patch of fresh resin spread over a split seam, fingers stained amber as they coax the city back toward balance.

They speak in voices shaped by humidity, their consonants softened at the edges, vowels lengthened as if stretching to span a distance dampened by air. Conversation is rarely private; sound carries too well for secrets, so they have developed a language of purposeful noise, where tone and timing carry more weight than words. A certain creak against a wall means patience; a short, sharp tap on a cane means urgency; a long, even hum means the district is healthy and holding. Even their laughter is a shared event, low and rolling, set off by the city’s own creaks like a chorus answering a chord.

Their days are partitioned not by hours but by cycles of water and current. They rise when the drips above quicken and settle when the great reservoir at the city’s heart falls still. Work is often collective: tightening a lattice, retuning a pump, harvesting a crop of glow-moss or shearing a patch of bioluminescent algae to press into new panels. In the slow factories, they move like musicians in a minor key, each gesture part of a longer song of heat and pressure. Children learn early to read the city’s skin: they press palms to walls to feel the thrum of pumps, lick surfaces to taste the metallic tang of groundwater, and listen to the small, private clicks that mean a root is sealing over a wound.

Their faith, if it can be called that, is a quiet confidence in continuity. They do not bury their dead so much as return them to the weave. Bodies are placed in hollowed trunks or sealed within thickening walls, wrapped in leaves and sealed with resin, so that their substance feeds the city’s slow turn. They do not mark these places; instead, they trust that the city will remember. And in time it does: a wall grows subtly denser, a passage takes on a warmer timbre, a floor settles with a softer sigh. The living feel this as comfort rather than mystery, a sense that the place is learning them as they learn it.

There are, of course, those who strain against the closeness. Some carve out tiny, private chambers in the city’s outer fringes, lining them with the driest moss they can find and hoarding small, sharp things—files, chips of glass, fragments of old metal—to keep the vegetal alloy at bay. But most find that isolation chafes; the city is loud, but its noise is a kind of company, and the damp, though it rusts and stains, also keeps everything pliable, forgiving.

In rare moments when the pumps fall silent and the reservoirs hold their breath, the Weft gather near the central pool, their faces dimly lit by algae and fungi. They sit close enough that their shoulders almost touch, listening to the city’s slow pulse. They do not pray aloud. Instead, they let their breathing fall into rhythm with the dripping and the groaning and the low, electrical thrum that runs through the vegetal veins. They know that they are not keeping the city alive; they are learning how to keep from disturbing it.

And when they step out into the braided corridors, leaving damp footprints that fade within minutes, they do so with the knowledge that their lives here are not a conquest but a conversation. They are the softer voice in a place of iron and growth and current, the part of the city that listens closely enough to answer. In the end, the Weft are less inhabitants than interpreters: translators of creak into meaning, of drip into time, of the city’s long, wet breath into something like a life they can share.
