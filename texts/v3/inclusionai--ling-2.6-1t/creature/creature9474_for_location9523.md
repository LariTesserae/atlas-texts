They are, first, a kind of altitude given skin.

Their bodies are long negotiations between the need to move and the weight of the air they swim through. Limbs are spare, jointed with cartilage the color of old smoke, built not for impact but for suspension. Fingers—when they have fingers—end in pads that drink moisture, ridged and soft, able to separate a single thread of lattice from its neighbors without waking the resin beneath. To climb here is to read; to touch is to ask permission. Their feet, when they descend to the rare floor-thickness of accumulated shed matter—seldom do—press and do not linger, leaving prints that the dampness erases before they can be memories.

Their wings, if wings they are, are not sails but questions. They span wide and thin, translucent with veining that carries not blood but the faint glow of metabolized starlight, a bioluminescence kept low, at that 0.8 weirdness, so it registers as a shift in texture rather than a color. They do not beat so much as articulate, folding and opening like hands adjusting to a new kind of dark. When they move through the water-heavy air, they leave behind not turbulence but alignment—ripples that make the suspended droplets chime softly, a grammar of pressure that other creatures learn to follow.

What covers them is not fur or feather but a living down, a moss-felt that grows from their skin and is shed in careful seasons, drifting to the lattice below to become part of the masonry. This coat hosts its own fauna, tiny grazers that ride between the hairs and keep the mat from sealing shut, their movements like a second kind of breath against the larger bodies. The beasts tolerate them; they know they are part of the place’s holding pattern, as necessary as the rounding of the corridors.

Their faces are wide and calm, eyes large and blackened to absorb the minimal light that filters through the aqueous ceiling. There is no urgency in their gaze, only a slow calculation of gradients—differences between one kind of dark and another, between the coolness that comes from deep water and the coolness that comes from ancient resin. Their ears, when they have ears, are small and set deep, attuned less to sound than to the way sound presses against the vegetal vaults. They map by echo, but the echo is shared; a call sent out returns not only from walls but from other voices, a chorus that stitches distances into neighborhoods.

They speak sparingly. When they do, the sound is thick, almost liquid, a low pressure that travels like a current through the halls. These utterances are not for naming so much as for placing—marking a junction as safe, a corridor as thinning, a chamber as newly sealed by resin. Younger ones learn the language by swimming alongside adults, their bodies aligned so that the water in the air can carry the vibration directly through them. In this way, knowledge moves like a tide.

Their bodies contain pockets—not organs but pouches woven from the same resilient tissue as their skin—where they store heavy things: lumps of condensed resin, stones worn round by the patient water, shards of old lattice that have fallen and been remade. These weights are used to adjust buoyancy, to anchor themselves when they need to work on the structure, to add their own small mass to the decision of what holds and what yields. They are builders in the sense that beavers are builders, not inventing new materials but arranging the endless inheritance of the place into new stabilities.

They sleep by sinking. They find a hollow where the coolness is deepest and let themselves go still, their down drinking the moisture, their bodies slowing to the rhythm of the water’s stand. In this state they dream in frequencies older than hearing, and the lattice around them seems to listen, the resin softening slightly, accepting new shapes without losing its essential stiffness. When they wake, the chamber is subtly altered, more themselves than it was before.

They are not alone. Smaller bodies flicker at the edges of perception—six-limbed climbers that run across the vaults without pressing, leaving trails that dry into faint crusts, signaling pathways by scent rather than sight. There are gliders that never land, bodies like seeds with wings of membrane, drifting on the atmospheric ocean, feeding on the thin algae that grow where the water condenses thickest. There are things that pulse, almost invisible, like slow hearts embedded in the walls themselves, symbionts that trade minerals for light, for signals, for the quiet certainty of the place.

None of them rush. None of them need to. The altitude presses down with a gentleness that feels like insistence, asking again and again whether what is present is necessary. The answer is always the same: this breath, this weight, this call that travels through the dark and returns as a map. They live in the final form of a forest that learned to build, and having built, decided to remain. They are its punctuation, its breath, its holding pattern made animate, keeping the submerged city from forgetting how to be alive without growing, how to be deep without bottom, how to be an altitude that refuses to end and still, somehow, breathe.
