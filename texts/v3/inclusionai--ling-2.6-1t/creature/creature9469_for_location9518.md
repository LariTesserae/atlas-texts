They live in the agreement between stone and breath.

First, the Gliders. They are not so much animals as folded atmospheres given a skeletal grammar. Their bodies are rhomboid, light enough to be mistaken for a trick of heat, yet dense with an interior geography of honeycombed bone. When they drift, they do not beat the air; they persuade it. Their wings are not wings but lattices of translucent chitin, each pane veined with a darker fluid that shifts like oil on stone. They move in constellations that reform at the edges of vision, schooling through the high hall as if reading its faults and veins. They drink by pressing their proboscises to the waxen sweat of the walls, drawing up the resin not as food but as ballast, a way to keep their thoughts from floating away. When they rest, they stack—dovetailing into crevices, becoming a single heavier thing—so the hall knows they have mass, and therefore meaning.

Then there are the Masons. Many-limbed, compact as river stones, they are the town planners of the place. Their bodies are matte and mineral-smelling, cool to the touch, and their pads leave behind a temporary tracery of copper and bergamot that fades into the stone like a forgotten name. They do not cut or carry so much as suggest. With gentle, persistent pressure they nudge crystals into alignment, coaxing roots to thicken into railings, guiding fault lines into doorways. They work in shifts that have nothing to do with time and everything to do with attention. When one pauses, others continue its intent so seamlessly that the work seems to proceed while no one is looking. Their eyes are numerous and small, arranged in arcs across their shoulders, and each eye holds a fragment of the hall’s map—a remembered angle, a note on softness. They trade these glances like gossip, and slowly the hall learns how to sit.

Closer to the ground, the Choir. They are small, glossy creatures that gather in rings and coronets, the same geometries as the flowers they feed upon. Their bodies are soft-seamed, almost plush, and they hum at a pitch that tightens the resin in the walls, drawing it into clearer beads. The hum is not music so much as clarification; when the Choir sings, colors stop shifting and decide what they are. They are the ones who make the flowers speak in spirals, who teach the vines to curtain off only as much light as is needed. Their mouths are tiny, yet the sound they produce seems to come from the space itself, as if the air has borrowed their throats to say what it has been practicing.

Above all, the Lichen-Walkers, though they are not lichen and they do not merely walk. They are communities more than individuals, swarms that flow over stone like slow poured light. Each unit is a glossy, compact body, but the whole is something else—an aggregate intelligence that remembers how the hall felt yesterday and how it might feel tomorrow. They leave trails that smell of metal and rain, though there has never been rain, only the memory of it. When they pass, moss softens; ferns bow slightly. They are the gentlest sovereigns of the place, deciding by consensus where a plaza should be, when a corridor has outgrown its usefulness, which crystal is ready to reveal its smaller rooms.

And the Flowers themselves, though they are not quite flora. They open and close in conversation, tightening into rings when the Gliders pass overhead, loosening into coronets when the Choir begins its hum. Their petals are waxy, holding the day’s warmth like small reservoirs, and at their center sits a bead of resin that tastes, faintly, of ozone and patience. They do not reproduce so much as remember themselves, each bloom a reminder of a previous bloom, a citation of light.

Even the stone participates. Geodes breathe in optical density, their crowded rooms of smaller rooms watching with a kind of slow, crystalline attention. Veins of salt lick across pale mineral skin like braille, spelling out laws too gentle to be called rules. When something is understood here—when a Glider finds a new current, when the Masons agree on a step—the stone grows a fraction less severe. It learns to be a hall rather than a height.

They all live by thickness rather than scarcity. They touch because touching is the way the hall keeps its shape. They share fluids—resin, hum, scent, light—because there is no water, and therefore no boundary between thirst and gift. Their conflict, when it flickers, is resolved by density: more bodies in a space, not fewer, until the disagreement becomes texture. Their town emerges not from planning but from repeated return, from the polite insistence of many bodies using the same ledge, the same corridor, the same clearing, until use becomes shape.

And if you listen, past the almost-silence, you can hear them becoming themselves: the soft click of folded wings, the creak of resin tightening, the hum that makes color decide, the shuffle of pads on young stone, and behind it all, the hall itself exhaling, learning its own manners, holding its own warmth, agreeing, slowly, to be lived in.
