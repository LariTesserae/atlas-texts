They live in layered agreements.

First, there is the city-not-city: a people whose bodies are less important than their patience. They are builders by subtraction, architects of restraint, shaping the already-living by whispering to it. They have long fingers and elbows that know how to press without bruising, knees accustomed to the springy moss-floor. Their skin is the color of cooled wax, pearled and slightly translucent, showing the map of their own small mineral deposits beneath—micas and iron freckles that echo the land. They do not cut; they guide. When they need a wall, they choose a line of saplings and, over years, braid their trunks, singing low songs that change the angle of growth. When they need a roof, they encourage a canopy to thicken, then thin it selectively so the light falls in dappled sheets. Their homes are not shelters from the place but intensifications of it. A family group might occupy a single hollowed trunk, its interior lined with resin that smells faintly of cinnamon and iron, the walls warm to the touch, sweating delicate salt rings at dawn that they collect on their tongues for the minerals.

Among them are the listeners, those who press their ears to the stone and feel the slow hum of the basin’s settling. They map not by distance but by density, tracing the hidden seams where the land is still deciding how heavy to be. They know which faults are merely thoughtful and which are dreaming of becoming rivers, someday, if water ever remembers how to return. They wear cloaks of felted metallic moss that squeak when they move, a sound like distant rain, announcing their passage without violence.

Then there are the grazers, the six-limbed quiet ones who move in tides across the forest-floor. Their bodies are low-slung and plated, bellies armored in overlapping sheets of chitin the color of bruised peach. They do not eat leaf so much as lull it—cropping the metallic moss in patterns that let the stone breathe. Where they pass, the ground shines briefly, a wet gleam that seals itself again by afternoon, as if the land is healing a small wound. They are not afraid of the builders; often they will pause to let a child climb onto their backs, their warmth soaking through the plates like stones left in sun. The grazers’ eyes are multifaceted and patient, seeing less in images than in pressures: the weight of heat above, the pull of minerals below.

Above them glider-kind banks through the canopy highways. They have paper wings, thin as thought, stretched over frames of living cartilage. They do not fly so much as fall with purpose, catching drafts that rise from the valley’s own breath. Their bodies are long and ribboned, shimmering in gold-green light, and they sing with the wind by rubbing their wing-edges against the resinous leaves, a sound like creaking silk. They are the messengers, carrying scents and seeds and small, urgent news from one rim to the other. To see them is to understand that the city has layers above as well as below.

The miners are a secretive lineage, paler than the others, their skin dusted with white powder from the stone they handle. They burrow not for roots or water but for the metal-veins that run like handwriting through the rock. They carry away beads of copper and iron, lining their tunnels with them, singing them into place with secretions that dry like amber. Their tunnels glow faintly in the dark, veins of rust and metal threading through the walls like circuits. They are the ones who know how heavy the land wishes to become, and they lighten it by taking weight into themselves.

Predators are rare, not because they are few but because they are folded into the scene almost invisibly. They do not hunt so much as wait, draped over branches like cloth laid across a rail. Their skin shifts in slow gradients, mimicking the living masonry, moss growing in the small cups of their scales. They move only when the canopy hums, descending with the soft clap of a seed-pod falling, precise as a closing hand. They eat the grazers, yes, but also the sickly growths, the overambitious vines, the parts of the city that would otherwise crowd themselves into silence. In their bellies, minerals are returned to the system, refined by acid and time into something softer.

There are those who live at the edges, the rim-walkers, who test how far the fall would carry them. They are leaner, their fingers longer, their sense of balance honed to a fine wire. They know that the horizon is a lip you could step over, and they study it with a kind of devotion, as if the drop itself were a teacher. They trade stories of depth with the miners and the listeners, two different vocabularies of going down.

And there are the children, who are not young so much as unfinished, their own growth still deciding its pace. They gather in thickets of their own making, playing at braiding, at weaving, at making the land hold its breath longer. They press their palms into the moss-floor and feel it give, and they learn that yielding is not weakness but a kind of memory for how to be held.

All of them share the atmosphere, that warm, weighty air that settles into lungs and loosens time. They exchange fluids without thirst, drinking the vapor that rises from the sponge-moss gutters at dawn, tasting the salt-crusts that ring like glass. Their bodies are part of the closed loop, sap and mineral and breath cycling through them as through the gutters and the hollow trunks and the veins in the stone.

They do not speak of being separate from the city, because the city is a verb they share. They are the listeners, the braiders, the grazers, the gliders, the miners, the predators, the rim-walkers, the children. Together they add layers, not strip them away, pressing into the living system that yields and resists in the same measure. In their crowdedness, they find a kind of solitude that is not emptiness but fullness held in check, the vast low basin breathing them in and out, warm and crowded and quietly becoming, with nowhere to go but deeper into itself.
