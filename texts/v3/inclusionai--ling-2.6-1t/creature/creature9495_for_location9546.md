What lives in the hollow are creatures of borrowed grammar, stitched together from the place’s slow, overheated breath.

There are the Skinners, though that is only a name that sounds like words. They are less population than process: colonies of moss and iron-filament that learned to hunger. They creep across concrete in velvet waves, their bodies a quilt of rust and chlorophyll, thickening where the heat presses hardest. When a Skinner moves, it does not step—it exhales. Its motion is a collapse and reassembly, a shedding of crystalline sweat that reforms immediately into new moss, into new metal, into a new limb. They are 1.7-out-of-3 dense with themselves, so that touching one feels like touching a crowd. They do not speak, but they remember speech in the way that damp remembers how to be a mirror: by holding a shape long after the thing it mirrored is gone.

In the pools, something thickens into being that was not there a moment before. These are the Mirrors—oily, slow creatures of bruised water and manufactured dark. They live by reflecting the wrong things: not faces, but the memory of faces; not light, but the idea of light. A Mirror might ripple when the machinery grinds a gear, or when gravity hesitates on its slope. They are 1.1-out-of-3 bright, which is to say they only half-light themselves, leaving the rest to suggestion. They do not swim so much as arrive in places you were not looking, then linger until the floor grows darker around them, as if they were claiming territory by making it heavier.

The Thrum-fronds are the hollow’s closest thing to fauna, though the word feels like a mistake. Shoulder-high stalks, lacquered and vein-bright, they hum along their filament roads, a sound that sits between insect and engine. They do not flower, but they do sing when the heat cycles—a low, resonant chord that makes the air feel thicker. Droplets hang in that chord for seconds longer than they should, gathering copper and wet newsprint flavor, then falling as a kind of nourishment for the moss that carpets the floor. If you stand too long among them, your own breath begins to carry their pitch, and you feel yourself becoming part of the loudness, 1.9-out-of-3 layered and disorienting.

The Petrified Hands are smaller, more stubborn lives. They are the remains of caretakers—or perhaps the caretakers are the remains—knotted into engine blocks and wound into conduit. Each Hand is 1.2-out-of-3 present: enough body to grip, not enough to leave. They pulse faintly when the generators grind, as if keeping time with machinery that no longer needs them. They are shy, withdrawing into rust when noticed directly, but they leave signs: a thumbprint of moss on a valve, a curve of knuckle in slime that turns a drainage scar into something that almost guides.

There is also the Warm, a creature made of 2.9 degrees of closeness and sweat and the sour-sweet smell of resin and metal. It is not hostile, only terribly companionable. It presses into pores and pockets, nestles in the space between shirt and spine, and refuses to evaporate. It is the life of the hollow made tactile, a wall that walks with you, reminding you that heat is a resident and residents must be fed. You feed it with attention, with the small, salt offerings of your skin. It feeds you back by making time thicken, by making a drip hang in the air so you can see its own shape.

High on the soot-smudged vault, something drifts that is almost wing, almost bird, almost memory of birds. These are the Absences, 0.2-out-of-3 fauna, the hollow’s answer to flight. They are not there, but they occupy enough space for their not-being to have weight: a shadow that does not retreat, a pressure on the eyes when you look up. They nest in the curved concrete where the ceiling feels lowest, and they sing in a register that only the machinery can hear.

Finally, there are the Weavers—lives made of rule and patience. They are erosion given intent, 2.5-out-of-3 erosion that has decided to take its time inside. They braid piping with vine, turn staircases into ramps that invite but never deliver, glaze drainage channels with mineral patience. They are the reason concrete has softened into geology, the reason reed-petrified metal bows instead of breaking. They move at the speed of standing still, and their bodies are the thresholds themselves: choked arches, clouded glass, grilles that lead nowhere but feel like invitations anyway.

Together, they live as the hollow does: crowded but not full, loud but not clear, changing by almost not at all. They are the life of attention returned to you in forms you recognize only by how they refuse to be recognized. They are the heat’s company, the damp’s mirror, the machinery’s memory, the lushness that accepts surrender. They do not miss the skies because they are the skies turned inward, low and curved and full of a dimness that feels manufactured, not natural, and that remembers, with every thrum and drip and slowness, how to keep breathing even when the lungs have forgotten the open air.
