The ground is not lived on; it is lived inside.

What inhabits this place does not own territory so much as it shares it, pressed cheek-to-cheek with neighbors whose names change depending on how much weight you put on them. The residents are dense, three deep and three across, so that individuality is a rumor that occasionally surfaces in the heat and then sinks again. They are creatures of compression, their bodies built like knots—knuckles, valves, sphincters, lenses—arranged in such tight congress that to move is to ask permission of a dozen other lives at once. Their skins are porous and warm, sweating a brine that tastes of old iron and bruised fruit, and when they breathe, the air between them thickens into a second kind of rain.

Vegetation, sparse as it is, behaves like an accident they tolerate rather than a resource they use. Veins of thorn and thin lacings of leaf emerge from the packed shoulders of their world as if exhaled, silvering over ribs and basins before wilting into filaments that hum. These plants do not root; they cling, and in clinging they listen. When the ground forgets how to be still, the thorns tremble and broadcast a frequency that sounds like thirst translated into light.

What passes for fauna here is rare, not because life is scarce but because it chooses when to be seen. A six-legged glider detaches from a cluster of salt-crystals and coasts without flapping, its wings the color of a bruise just before it fades. Something longer and many-jointed lies half-buried for days, breathing through pores along its spine, its movement a rumor that travels through the packed floor like a shiver. They do not hunt so much as absorb—sipping the moisture that weeps from stone, feeding on the slow erosion of their own margins. Their rarity gives their voices weight: clicks like stones tapping teeth, snaps like wet cloth flagellating the air, and when they call in chorus the sound does not travel; it crowds.

Because erosion has taught everything to surrender its edges, the inhabitants have softened into gestures. Their limbs are blunt, their profiles rounded to the curve of a long-held breath. Their bodies remember cliffs, remember gorges, remember being urgent and vertical, but urgency has been ironed out of them like a crease in cloth. They look finished, as if they have been sitting in their final shapes long enough for corners to turn into lips. Even their interiors feel upholstered—chambers padded with spongy stone, ducts lined with moss-thick mortar that grows faster the more it is used.

Their architecture is not built but braised. Pillars stand like molars, black and pocked, each one enclosing a single room that smells of dried smoke and wet thread. Bridges slump across shallow valleys not as crossings but as pauses, their stones grown together into a single sentence that arches and exhales. There are no doors, only invitations: gaps that widen when approached, seals that recognize by heat and pressure. To enter is not to cross a threshold but to be acknowledged by density.

They live in a state of perpetual exhalation because water is not a resource but an exhaled mirror, ankle-deep and warm, shifting from seep to sheet to thread without warning. They do not drink so much as let the water read them, and what it reads it returns: a thin film that coats their skins and clarifies, for a moment, the outline of their thoughts. Puddles do not pool; they throb, elongate, and braid themselves across the packed floor like veins remembering an old song. The land’s membrane swells and sighs, and they rise and fall with it, buoyant as lungs.

They have little use for tools because their world is already a machine of such intimacy that tools feel like a kind of violence. A basin warms a body without fire. An arc of light blooms from touch, tasting of metal and honey. Veins in the rock pulse with a fluid that thickens when they are afraid and thins when they remember how to laugh. What would be technology elsewhere is here their ordinary metabolism, the land processing them as much as they process it.

Yet weirdness is their true ecology. At the edges where one body meets another, seams loosen. Color sometimes peels from sound, leaving a tone visible as a shimmering curtain. Gravity, usually a habit, occasionally forgets to apply itself equally; a stone may hover just above a flank, humming with the effort of deciding whether to exist. Time behaves like an indecisive crowd; a resident may be older in the morning than at dusk, or a path may remember being a river and behave accordingly. They do not resist these lapses—they host them, cultivating a loud consensus of contradictions.

Their sound is deafening, not because they are noisy but because density transmits everything. Grinding, creaking, sloshing, cracking—the packed floor relays every motion like a drumskin stretched across a continent. When they walk, the land answers in unison, a low roar that climbs into a chorus, as if the world were a lung drawing a breath it cannot finish. Thunder arrives from within, shuddering up through their soles and teeth, so that to hear is to feel the sky inside their ribs.

Storms form within them, not above them—columns of churning water and dust that walk along their surfaces, rearranging what they touch. In the aftermath, they look newly born and ancient at once, their seams glowing with sedimentary brightness. Plates the size of towns slip and settle with a groan like a door left open in a storm, and the residents ride the turbulence like sailors on a sea that is also a lung.

Through it all, the warmth holds, a patient, maternal heat rising from deep within, keeping them pliant. They have outgrown their story, yet refuse to stop telling it, loud and wet and crowded. Every footfall is a vote, every exhale a transaction, every shared cubic breath a treaty written in sweat and iron. They do not live on the ground; they are the ground remembering how to move, shimmering at the edge of a world that could, at any moment, decide to rearrange itself again—and they would let it, because to resist would be to pretend they were alone, and they have never been alone, and they do not wish to be.
