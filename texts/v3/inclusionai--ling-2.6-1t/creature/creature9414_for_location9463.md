What lives here are agreements more than organisms—multitudes that have decided, over long centuries, to stop distinguishing where one body ends and the kiln-wall or the borrowed crystal begins. They do not name themselves, but if they did, the names would be verbs of exchange: to warm, to fold, to remember, to loan.

The lattice-shelled crawlers are perhaps the most numerous, and the most courteous. Each is a scaffold of articulated obsidian plates, hinged like the ribs of a fan, and between those plates hums a lattice of living wire—fine as breath, bright as unspooled tungsten. They do not eat; they comb. With their wire-brush limbs they rake the glass-floored corridors, lifting trace metals from the slag and pressing them into temporary skins that grow over their joints like armor-lace. When a crawler moves, it sheds small constellations of mica and peridot that hiss against the floor, apologizing for their weight, and the shed dust is not waste but offering—later collected by symbiotes that knead it into eggsacs lined with heat-retentive amber.

The soft, many-legged lamps are their neighbors and creditors. To call them lamps is to borrow a human word for a thing that is closer to a consensus of warmth. They resemble drifting felted pouches, translucent as candle wax, supported on dozens of short, velvet limbs that barely touch stone. Inside each pouch thrums a sac of slow-burning amber, luminous only when stroked or pressed. They store heat the way a cellar stores wine, and they trade it in careful sips. When a crawler grows too cool, a lamp will drape itself across the crawler’s spine, and the two will remain coupled for days, breathing in time, until equilibrium is restored. Their pulse is so slow that to watch them is to watch geology breathe: one expansion, one contraction, per hour, per day. They do not die so much as they are reabsorbed—when a lamp’s sac finally dims, its pouch sloughs into the floor and becomes a vein of sulfur-yellow stone that hums faintly underfoot, warm as a memory.

Predators here are not hunters but governors of temperature. They are living static, pleated obsidian forms that can fold space like sheet metal bent by careful thumbs. To see one is to see a shape decide to be fewer angles. They drift through chambers without moving, drinking warmth from the air and leaving behind cool tracks that frost the stone for a moment before the equilibrium forgives them. Their teeth, if they can be called teeth, are lattices of cooled magma that click together with the sound of stones tapping inside a skull. They hunt by invitation: when a knot of crawlers grows too dense, too warm, they emit a frequency felt in the teeth—a courteous summons—and the pleated shapes arrive to thin the crowd, not to kill, but to redistribute. A predator’s meal is a temporary loan of heat, and what it leaves behind is not bone but coolness, a relief that allows the chamber to sleep.

There are pluralities—bodies that agree to move as one when warmth runs low. These are not swarms but amalgams, mosaics of chitin and horn and wire that share a single, slow thought. When they drift across a cavern, they look like rugs made of living obsidian, and their passage leaves a trail of aligned crystals that grow in their wake, as if the stone itself is lining up to be polite. When they settle, they disband without haste, each component returning to its private life as if nothing had been shared at all.

Human hands, or hands like them, have touched this grammar without mastering it. The artifacts scattered through the young scar are not tools so much as questions posed in material: river-smooth hammers that never knew a river, ceramic bowls fused to the floor by a heat that misunderstood its intentions, spirals of wire strung between stalactites to catch the static that drifts like pollen through the dark. These are offerings, half-finished conversations. In one chamber a kiln of stacked stone still holds the ghost-smell of fired clay, though centuries have passed since anything was deliberately hardened there. The kiln’s warmth is borrowed from the stone itself, returned on loan, and the clay inside has remade itself into something that almost remembers being a vessel, almost remembers being useful.

Even the minerals behave like fauna. Veins of sulfur-yellow and rust-red ribbon through the stone, warm to the touch, humming faint warmth into soles. Crystalline dunes hiss when they settle, as if apologizing for their own weight, and the dunes migrate—slow fronts of crystal growth that rise like glaciers in reverse, piling up in arcs that refuse to crease. Gravity, too, is polite, willing to be negotiated: heavier in some pockets, lighter in others, as if it has learned etiquette from the dense, crawling life.

What lives here, then, is a patience made tangible. It is the agreement to preserve, to loan, to fold, to return. It is the knowledge that decay is unnecessary because equilibrium is possible. It is the quiet that does not flee but pools like sediment, and the light that is borrowed and given back, and the dark that is not empty but full. In this young, sharp-edged underworld where water is not missed and sky is not needed, life has become a commerce of warmth and a dance of density: vast by being close, strange by being exactly what it is, loud by being quiet enough to hear the stone breathe.
