The city does not keep citizens so much as tenants—creatures who lease their existence from the bowl’s heat and its wet, metallic breath. They are not natives in the old sense, because nothing here arrived whole. Everything was winnowed, then persuaded.

On the lowest tiers, where the stone still remembers being magma, live the Glass-Shelled Haulers. Their bodies are not born so much as assembled by necessity: chitinous plates grown over lattices of salvage, articulated by joints sealed with wax and resin. They move in patient caravans along the causeways, bearing loads that are less cargo than responsibility—barrels of captured condensate, blocks of cooled slag, crates of folded copper that sing when stacked. Their many legs do not scuttle; they deliberate. At each step, suction cups on their feet taste the runnels left by the weeping stone, reading the city’s recent history like braille. When they pause, their shells click as inner heats equalize, releasing small clouds of vapor that smell faintly of iodine and old wiring. They are patient to a fault, and if you stand aside long enough, one might let you see the pale, pulsing node at the base of its throat—a borrowed pump, jury-rigged from a compressor, beating in time with the city’s hum.

Above them, in the iron-lattice canopies, dwell the Reflectors. These are not animals nor machines but agreements given form. They look like knots of broken mirror and polished nickel woven into something almost winged, though they never fly so much as hang, pivoting on filaments of tensioned cable. Their function is to catch the dim, bruised light and turn it away from places that have had enough. When the sun climbs, they shift in slow, collective arcs, their facets flashing like signals meant for no one outside the bowl. At dusk, they settle and cool, their surfaces clouding over with condensation, and in that fogged state they whisper to the Glass-Shelled Haulers below, passing along rumors of temperature, of pressure, of the sky’s mood. It is said that if you catch a Reflector just after it has shed a film of brine, you can see, for a moment, a reflection of a city that never learned to be this dry, this high, this patient.

Higher still, along the magnetite cliffs that posture as forests, nest the Ember-Moths. They are not moths in any familiar sense—more like folded foil given purpose and hunger. They feed on gradients, sipping the difference between a surface that has kept its heat too long and the air that wants it back. When they cluster on a warm gantry, they glow like banked coals, their wings emitting a dull, rhythmic thrum that tightens your teeth if you stand too close. They lay their eggs in the thermal joints of the city, where expanding metal cradles them like parentheses. The hatchlings emerge not as larvae but as small, sealed spheres that roll along the runnels until they find a cool enough crevice to crack open, releasing a puff of cinnamon-scented static—the same smell that lingers in the city’s inexplicable corridors.

In the hollows between districts, where steam ghosts rise and condense on cooler underbellies, live the Drifters. They are almost nothing: gelatinous, translucent envelopes braced by faint magnetic skeletons, drifting on the aerial trams’ slipstreams when no cargo weighs the lines. They have no faces, but they bear signs—patches of pigment that shift when stared at, spelling directions that make sense only if you already know where you’re going. The Drifters trade in small truths: the location of a joint that will soon weep, the hour when a particular stretch of walkway will feel lighter, the memory of a temperature that no longer exists. They attach themselves to travelers for a time, tasting sweat and metal, then peel away, leaving behind a cool patch on the skin that smells of ozone and regret.

Beneath the walkways, in the lower basins where warm brine gathers, thrives the City’s Lichen. It is not green, of course, but a bruised indigo that spreads in fractal patterns across submerged copper and the ribs of old engines. It feeds on salt and stray voltage, and when disturbed it releases a mist that makes time feel sticky, as if moving through honey. The Glass-Shelled Haulers avoid it; the Reflectors watch it; the Ember-Moths occasionally come down to skim its edges, not to eat but to imprint a map of heat onto their wings. The Lichen remembers the bowl’s past—when the sky had not yet been persuaded to hold so much, when the water came down instead of hanging in thin, insistent skins. If you press your ear to a patch after the night-purge, you can hear it: a low, wet chanting that sounds suspiciously like the city humming to itself.

There are, of course, the Quiet ones. They wear suits of riveted slate and move along the causeways at hours when the heat softens. Their faces are hidden behind visors that are always fogged on the inside. They do not speak. Instead, they adjust things: a seal here, a weight there, a filament’s tension. Where they pass, the city seems to hold its breath. It is said that the Quiet ones were once ordinary tenants who stayed too long, who learned the bowl so well that they forgot how to leave. Now they are part of the maintenance, part of the hum, part of the compromise that keeps flame at bay.

And in certain chambers, where gravity grows polite and rooms refuse to fit the shape they should, live the Echo-Folk. They are thin, bright outlines that gather near surfaces that have stored too much cold or too much memory. They do not touch, but they rearrange—slightly—the way heat leaves a room. If you stand still and let your boots feel the slight, warm tug near the iron bones, you may sense them aligning, measuring, deciding whether you belong. They have no names, but they have preferences: for the smell of cinnamon, for the sound of a pump sighing, for the way water chooses to run when no one is watching.

Weirdness, here, is not a flaw but a livelihood. There are scavengers who harvest the salt-veins that bloom like frost on hot metal, grinding them into powders that make the Reflectors’ mirrors sharper. There are feeders who tend the microbial mats in the warm brine, pruning them into shapes that please the city’s hum. There are watchers who stand at the rim when the horizon tilts and try to coax a breeze from the thinning violet sky, bargaining with pressure and patience.

All of them, in their ways, answer to the bowl’s rules: that heat must be taken in and let go, that water must visit and linger, that silence must be kept loud enough to hear. They are not a civilization so much as a consensus—industrious, mineral, strange—living in a city that does not rise from the ground but sits inside a sky that has learned to hold, and where the loudest thing of all is the quiet between the machines, the pause between beads of condensation, the moment when the city decides, for just an instant, to let you feel that it is alive, and that it has made room for you to be alive beside it.
