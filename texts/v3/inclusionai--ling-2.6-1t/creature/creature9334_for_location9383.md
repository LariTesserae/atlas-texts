They have learned to live inside a drum.

First, they learned to be porous. The people of the hollow—or perhaps it is fairer to call them the residents of the brightness—have skin like cured parchment, thin and translucent, webbed with fine silver lines that map not blood but the passage of light. When the sun leans hard, their skin drinks it, turning temporarily incandescent, and they become lanterns for one another, signaling across plazas with the soft glow of their own marrow. They bathe in dust, not to get clean but to calibrate: a thicker coat of white mineral rime protects them from the day’s loudness; a thinner coat lets them listen to the ground. Children are born with a faint ringing in their ears, a harmonic of the hollow itself, and they learn early which sounds are theirs and which belong to the city’s ribs settling.

Their society is organized around acoustics. They do not build doors so much as thresholds of sound: a curtain of seed pods that clatter like dice, a veil of braided vines that hums when someone approaches, a lattice of knapped-stone beams that clicks a particular rhythm underfoot to announce status or intent. Privacy is not about being unseen but about being unresonated. A family that wants solitude hangs damp cloths—precious, wrung-out jewels of condensed breath—over their archways to soften the world to a murmur. Gossip travels not as whisper but as pattern: a sequence of snaps and scrapes repeated at street corners, understood by those who know how to read the pauses between.

They are patient with erosion because they respect wearing. Their bodies, too, are shaped by it: elders grow smooth where the wind touches them most, joints rounded into comfortable geometries, voices sanded to a low, reliable murmur. The young are angular and brash, their edges catching the light and sending it scattering; they learn grace by learning to be abraded. Even their tools are consumable—hand-lashed from stems and mineral paste, designed to fray and be refrayed, because permanence is considered a failure of attention. A blade is only as valid as the song it makes being used, and when it falls silent from over-smoothing, it is returned to the streets as chippings to be trodden into the city’s skin.

Their economy is measured in echoes. To give something is to create a space where its absence can be noticed: a merchant sets out a tray of glass-lizard scales and does not call out prices but listens. The scale that rings longest when tapped is taken to be the most valuable, but only if the buyer’s own footsteps, approaching, cause it to vibrate in sympathy. Trade is a duet. Debts are settled in quietness: a favor owed is a pocket of silence held in trust, and to repay it is to make room for the other’s sound to expand. The hollow has no mint, but it has chambers calibrated to amplify a single coin’s clink into a chorus; these are places of oath, where promises are made audible so that breaking them would be, quite simply, deafening.

Water, being absent, is imagined as a kind of negative music. They sing about it in registers so low the notes can be felt in the floor rather than heard, songs that conjure coolness without substance. Their rituals involve pouring—not liquid, but light. At certain angles of day, they carry mirrors of polished mineral paste to intersections and angle brightness into the undersides of walkways, creating temporary pools of glare that ripple as people pass through. To stand in such a pool is to be baptized in luminosity, to feel as though you are dissolving into the city’s hum. Births are marked by shaving a small lens into the scalp of the infant, so that their gaze always carries a focus, a way of collecting and redirecting brightness when the world feels too diffuse.

They live among predators that are less beasts than phenomena, and they have grown courteous toward disaster. A slide of mineral skin is greeted with a clap, not to frighten it away but to acknowledge its rhythm; if the clap matches the slide’s frequency, the slide may pause, as if surprised by the conversation. Falling stones are sometimes caught in nets of braided vine, not to stop them but to let them finish their sentence in a slower, softer cadence. Even the plants that snap shut are treated as conversationalists: a hand placed near a bladder’s trigger and then withdrawn teaches it patience, and over years a household may cultivate a garden of traps too polite to close.

What they lack in number they make up in density of attention. Because the hollow is generous with space, they use it to broadcast themselves. A solitary walker may pause in a plaza and begin to tap a stone with a knuckle, and soon the architecture answers, flutes of wind picking up the rhythm, leaves clattering in accompaniment, and for a moment the person is not alone but surrounded by versions of themselves played by the city. Funerals are held at noon, the brightest hour, so that the departed’s absence can be seen as a shadow that lingers even in glare. The dead are laid on beds of mineral crust where their warmth can be exchanged for light, and their stories are etched into the undersides of causeways so that footsteps may read them for generations.

Their strangeness is not in their bodies but in their commitment to being resonant. They have accepted that to live without water is to live in a state of permanent vibration, that to be small is to be loud enough to matter. They do not hoard quiet, because they know it curdles; they let it flow, thin and clear, through the wide streets they have left deliberately open. In the evenings, when the sky presses close and the heat begins to bleed away, the hollow cools with a sound like a held breath released, and the people gather on terraces to feel the city settle. It is in these moments that they are most themselves: listening to the land’s own pulse, matching their steps to its tempo, learning again how to sing to the light until the light, generous and exacting, sings back.
