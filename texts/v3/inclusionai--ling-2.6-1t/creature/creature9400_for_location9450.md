They live in the seam, not in the sense of inhabiting it but of being its inhabitants—creatures of calibrated edge and inherited pressure, born from the pocket’s refusal to be empty. They are not animals in any sense that would survive outside, because outside is a rumor here, and rumors do not feed.

The smallest among them are the Latchlings, fist-sized and lacquered like safety glass, their shells panelled in tiny hexagons that shift color with the gradient of hum. They have too many legs, each ending in a combination of sucker and screw, and they use them to walk on walls, on ceilings, on the undersides of slow swells, treating up and down as interchangeable weather. Their mouths are rings of fine mesh that filter warmth from the water, drinking viscosity the way other beasts drink blood. They travel in constellations, tracing the service corridors like beads on wire, pausing only to scrape deposits from the kelp-analogs—deposits that are less mineral than memory, solidified echoes of old instructions.

Higher in the column live the Ribbon-Sewers, long, ribbon-thin bodies that look as if they were cut from sheets of liquid mirror and then folded lengthwise to remember themselves. They do not swim so much as align, threading through the copper-thin leaves with a precision that suggests they are stitching the forest together. Where they pass, the kelp stiffens, and where they pause, it loosens. They secrete a lubricant that keeps the pocket from grinding itself raw, a fluid that smells faintly of ozone and apology, and is harvested secretly by the Latchlings, who store it in the hollows of their own plating. To touch a Ribbon-Sewer is to feel the pocket’s pulse translated into rhythm, slow and sure as a turbine learning to sleep.

There are larger beings, too, though large is relative in a place where the ceiling can be touched with a thought. The Hull-Wardens are creatures of coral-thick iron and muscle grown in rings, their bodies shaped like the spaces they guard—low, arched, compact. They live where the architecture has bowed, in the catwalks that have become balconies and the pipes that have learned to sing. A Warden’s eyes are not eyes but lenses arranged in spirals, each tuned to a different frequency of the soundscape, so that what they see is not light but the superposition of pistons and pressure valves gossiping across the pocket. They do not eat; they calibrate. When a valve tightens too much, a Warden unfastens it with careful claws, not out of mercy but out of necessity, as one might adjust a clock that has grown fond of its own ticking.

And somewhere in the middle, in the band where light falls in sheets and is bent back as rumor, live the Echo-Fishers, translucent creatures with bodies like nets cast from heat-resistant polymer. They drift through the coolant tides, opening and closing in rhythms that match the pocket’s breathing. They do not hunt prey but harvest imbalance, drawing from the water the excess of potential that would otherwise tip the delicate tension into chaos. What they collect they store in bellies of spun alloy, and when the pocket dims to the color of a closed eye, they release it in pulses that map the space like sonar prayer, reminding the walls that height is negotiable but must be paid for in pressure.

There are, as well, the Maintenance-Nymphs, tiny beings that look as if they were assembled from spare parts and good intentions, with wings of stamped copper that flutter uselessly in the thick air but signal intent with minute clicks. They are not builders so much as caretakers, polishing corrosion into pattern, coaxing rust into decorative filigree, singing in frequencies that feel like forgiveness to anyone or anything listening. They ride on the backs of Latchlings when they need to travel quickly, and they sleep in the folds of the kelp-analogs, waking only when the pocket asks a question—something hummed low and warm, audible only as a change in the lullaby of industry.

Together, they form a society of insistence rather than crowding, a density of purpose that matches the density of the place itself. They do not speak of origins; they speak of gradients. They do not mourn loss; they measure it in corrosion patterns and store it in the ledger of the pocket’s hum. Predator and prey have been replaced by calibrator and calibrated, each creature tightening or loosening, scraping or painting, releasing or containing, so that the whole remains in a tension that feels like balance, even as it shivers with the potential to unmake itself in a single, elegant mistake.

And if you stand among them long enough, you may feel yourself changing—not becoming one of them, perhaps, but becoming like them. Your edges round. Your thoughts slow to the rhythm of a valve releasing pressure. Your own small life begins to taste of copper and patience, to sway when no current moves it, to listen when the pocket asks its quiet, ancient question. You fit. You fit so perfectly that you might as well be the reason the pocket exists at all.
