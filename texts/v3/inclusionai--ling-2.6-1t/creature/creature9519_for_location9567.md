The place is not lived in so much as lived *into*, and its inhabitants have learned to be thorough without being permanent.

First there are the Weavers of Compression, who are less a people than a consensus of hands. They appear as smudges of motion at first—elbows, forearms, the backs of knees—working in seams so close that their breath polishes the stone they press. They wear layers of fabric the color of wet clay, stiffened at the elbows and knees with leather patches that have themselves begun to fossilize into the walls. Their tools are simple and heavy: stone beaters, tampers the size of fruit, wedges that bite into cracks like teeth. They do not speak loudly; their language is the hush of settling grains, the murmur of instructions that assume the listener is already doing what is being described. They live in pockets—small, domed rooms that are finished enough to sleep in but still soft around the edges. In those rooms they keep small jars of slurry, samples of promising silt, and maps drawn on cured skins that are already wrong in the details because the place grows faster than ink.

With them live the Cartographers of Temporary Trust, who are mostly children and the lightly stooped. They carry slates of slate-smooth wood, chalk made from burnt bone, and cords knotted with provisional measurements. They map not what is, but what is holding for now. They are allowed to erase. In their company, a wall is described as *leaning a little to the argument*, a corridor as *agreeing to be straight until Thursday*. They know which buttresses are polite and which are desperate, which junctions are popular and therefore crowded with intent. They trade information for small bright things—pins, buttons, chips of mica—and in this way a market emerges that is entirely about attention. Their stalls are platforms built between ribs, where they display not goods but *relationships*: a jar labeled *this wall liked the last pour*, a braid of cord labeled *safe to pull if you must*, a stone labeled *remembers being warm*.

Water is a visitor, but it has its own kind of life when it arrives. It condenses as small silver skins on cool stone, gathers in cupped seams, and is immediately attended to by the Catchers, who are neither fish nor bird but something in between: slender, many-fingered bodies with eyes like polished pebbles. They wear wetskins that seal at the wrists and throat, and they move with a liquid economy, dipping and lifting, guiding water into skins of cured bladder and clay. They are not greedy. They know that a pulse that can be kept is a pulse that can be trusted, and they store water in chambers that are shaped like questions—rounded, open at the top, ready to change their answer as the stone settles. When they move through the corridors, they leave behind a trail of damp that smells like iron and possibility, and the small plants tilt toward it, their roots flexing like antennae.

Above the human flurry, the fauna are tentative archivists. There are small lizard-things with translucent bellies that show what they have recently eaten—mostly dust, occasionally a seed—and claws that click in a rhythm like counting. There are birds with beaks shaped like trowels, which probe cracks not for food but for information, testing the firmness of this year’s decisions. They cache shiny objects in crevices where future masons will find them and, not knowing what to do with them, incorporate them into walls, so that the city’s memory includes accidental jewelry. There are also the Whisper-Rats, furred and long-limbed, who understand vibration better than sound. They run along the tops of walls, pausing to press their paws against surfaces, learning what the stone felt yesterday and what it fears tomorrow. Their tails flicker in patterns that the Weavers have begun to read as warnings or permissions.

The plants are fewer but braver than logic allows. Gray-green mosses that thrive on breath and condensation form soft borders around the living edges. There are bristled stalks that look almost like grass, almost like lichen, that grow in tight whorls around pinholes where rumors of sunlight leak through higher fractures. Their roots do not dig deep; they skitter along surfaces, exploratory, as if soil itself is a new idea they are still deciding whether to adopt. When water visits, they unfurl further, briefly, and then settle back into a patient crouch.

There is no single ruler, but there is a kind of governance in the density itself. The city enforces a closeness that is both practical and intimate. A change in one district is felt in another as a shift in temperature, a change in echo, a new rhythm in the drip of water. Ownership is defined by use until a better arrangement appears. A shelf becomes a stall becomes a bed becomes a shrine, and each transition is marked by a small addition—a knot tied in a cord, a smear of colored clay, a shell set into the surface like an eye. Rules are provisional but binding while they last, and everyone understands that lasting is a matter of negotiation, not decree.

At night, when the small fires are banked and the phosphorescent smears on certain walls glow with a shy persistence, the place hums with the accumulation of small decisions. The air moves through thermal seams, cool currents sliding alongside the warmer breath of sleepers. The packed corridors feel less like architecture and more like a body arranging its bones in the dark. The fauna pause. The plants hold their stillness. The Weavers sleep with their tools within reach, the Cartographers dream in mutable streets, the Catchers listen for the distant rumor of the next pulse.

This is a city of becoming, crowded with intention and stitched together by careful hands. It is not yet sure what it is, but it is already learning how to hold, and in that learning, it speaks—not in a single voice, but in the layered murmur of many small lives, each one saying, *here, for now*, and meaning it with the quiet stubbornness of stone that knows it is still being sewn.
