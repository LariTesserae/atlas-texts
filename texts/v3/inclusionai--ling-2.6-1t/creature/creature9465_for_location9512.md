They do not live here so much as they are lived by the hollow.

There are, first, the Moss-Knitters, who are less a people than a consensus of damp. They wear skins that have grown into them—bark-wool cured by smoke and cellar-cool, leather tanned in brine until it is soft as a bruise. Their hands are pale from living under stone-light, fingertips broad and callused from turning wet clay and prying loose the gifts the basin offers. They do not cut much; they unearth. When they need to hold something, they bind it with filaments of fungal silk, white as breath in the dark, grown in long slow sheets on rot and patience. Their faces are maps of fine lines, not from age but from smiling in the dim: the skin around their eyes crinkles like the edges of leaves that have uncoiled too long in the dark.

Among them move the Tenders of the Dripping Ledger, the basin’s archivists. They carry lamps of soapstone, wicks soaked in rendered marrow, flames so small and steady they look reluctant. Their duty is to listen to the walls. They press ears and palms to chert and shale, learning which fractures hold last year’s rain and which are learning to sing new notes. They keep records not in marks but in knots and blooms: cords of braided grass tied with beads of dried resin that capture the cadence of water on stone, and gardens of luminous fungi arranged in patterns that map the hollow’s moods. When a new seam opens, they press a seed of pale lichen into it, not to seal but to witness.

There are the Stone-Softeners, who know how to ask rather than to break. They are the ones who lean the dry-stone walls into their patient geometries, fitting fist-sized rocks that have forgotten their sharpness into shapes that hold without gripping. They work wet clay into the crevices of human things, returning loom-weights to the earth by degrees, bedding flint blades in beds of moss so that rust, if it comes, looks like a slow flower. They are slight people, with shoulders accustomed to low ceilings, fingers that can feel the memory of a tool in a river-smoothed hole. They do not build to last; they build to yield.

Children here are taught to be quiet in a different language. They learn to read the floor by the tremor in their teeth, to know when water is coming by the way the beetles arrange themselves in lines. A child’s game is to place a pebble on a shelf of stone and return when the fungus has softened its edges enough to hold it like a fruit. They are small and sturdy, with hair the color of wet slate, eyes large and adjusted to gradients of gray and green. They collect the translucent skins shed by pale newts and wear them as temporary jewelry, understanding that the ornament is only borrowed from the cold.

Among the basin’s people move the Echo-Eaters, who are not always human, or no longer entirely. They are the basin’s way of keeping balance. Some are small and furred, bodies close to the ground, ears like maps of cartilage that catch the drum of falling water and translate it into paths. Others are slow, many-legged things that taste the stone with every step, absorbing minerals and stories alike. There are the dark fish, eyeless and finned like ferns, who navigate the thin sheets of water by the memory of currents. They move in pulses that rhyme with the hollow’s heartbeat. The Echo-Eaters do not compete; they commute. They arrive when the damp is right, take what patience they can carry, and leave behind a trail of tiny phosphorescent spores, a breadcrumb trail of glow that says *this way, this way, the cold is good here*.

There is, too, the Keeper of the Blade-That-Never-Cuts, who is both a person and a practice. She is small-boned and deliberate, her hands darkened by soot and something older. She tends the chipped flint and the lamp that remembers fire, not to use them but to remember how they once taught hands to be precise. She sits near the leaning walls and hums in a register that loosens dust from stone, a sound so low it feels like a thought half-formed. Her presence tells the others when to stop, when a thing has been tended enough.

What animates them all is the water, which is also their lawmaker. The basin’s people treat sound as substance. They measure their days by the sheet that pours from the cleft at the back and the rhythm it strikes on the broad leaf below. They know that a loud day means stone is yielding, that a soft day means the moss is thickening, that a roaring day means the hollow is full of itself and should be listened to, not interrupted. They move through this noise like plants through soil, at the speed at which they can still feel the vibration. In their chests, behind the drum of water, they keep a second rhythm: three taps, pause, two taps, the pattern their ancestors agreed would mean *I am here* and *I am listening*.

They live by exchange. They offer beads of resin to the walls where new seams appear. They leave small bowls of ground seed at the edge of luminous blooms. When they take a stone for shaping, they press a piece of their own hair into the clay so the basin knows a part of them is staying. When they die—which is to say, when they become part of the basin’s patience—they are wrapped in fungal silk and laid where water can trace their outlines, year by year, until their shapes are soft enough to be mistaken for stone.

If you could see them all together, they would look like a single organism spread thin: people and stone and water and small bright lives woven into one slow metabolism. There is no center, only the hollow, and in its belly they are not residents but resonances, answering the roar with a hush, answering the cold with a bloom, answering the dark with a touch that knows how to wait. They are the basin’s way of keeping time gentle, of making sure that even as stone forgets the sky, something remembers how to listen.
