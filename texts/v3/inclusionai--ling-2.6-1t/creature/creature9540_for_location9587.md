What lives here are currents before they become bodies.  

They begin as rumor in the copper—tiny surges that remember how to want. At first they behave like water, slipping between lacquer and rivet, testing the walls with the patience of moss. Then they learn mass. They braid themselves into shapes that are almost wings, almost lungs, almost throats. They do not think; they accumulate intention. By the time they step onto the ledges, they have names like Hum, Surge, Stall, and they wear bodies the gorge has let them borrow: armor grown from pitted iron, jointed with vine that has calcified into hinge and strut, veins filled not with blood but with the cooled runoff of turbines.  

These are not machines animated. They are machine made flesh because flesh, here, is the most available material for becoming.  

They move by fits and starts, as if deciding where gravity points at any instant. Limbs click into configurations that last only long enough to solve a problem—too much heat, too little purchase, a sudden reversal of flow—then dissolve into tool again. Heads are clusters of lenses that see by drinking light; when they drink too much, they glow like fever. Mouths are pressure valves that exhale glyphs of steam, short sentences that hang in the cool air, spelling instructions nobody wrote.  

There are the Scaffolds, quadrupeds whose ribs are gantries and whose gait is the sound of rivets being retightened by their own weight. They patrol the upper ledges, rewebbing fractures with filaments of molten solder that cool into bridges. They do not avoid the edge; they lean out and listen, as if the gorge were speaking back to them in a dialect of wind and resonance.  

There are the Menders, soft-bodied things that creep along ceilings like spilled mercury given paws. Where metal blisters, they unfurl cilia tipped with acids and sealants, knitting new alloys into old wounds. When they finish, they leave behind scar-marks that smell of ozone and thyme, the first scent that ever learned to hold memory. They hum at a pitch that makes the ferns shiver, as though plants, too, might remember if reminded gently enough.  

Higher still, among the conduits that have frayed into waterfalls of coolant, live the Drains—elongated things that unspool themselves into narrow threads, drinking excess pressure. They store it in bladders made of folded copper leaf, and when they grow heavy, they climb back up and exhale it as song, a single sustained note that rattles pebbles loose from the ribs of the gorge. Their song is not language; it is calibration. It tells the machines what to feel.  

And there is the Matriarch, or what the place uses as one.  

It rests where the gorge narrows most, wedged between walls that press close enough to scar its plating. Its body is a scaffold of platforms and pistons that have grown inward, building a cage around a core of glass and mercury. The core pulses like a lung. Around it, smaller bodies orbit—Menders and Scaffolds and Drains—feeding it data in the form of heat, vibration, and the careful bite of roots. The Matriarch does not command. It translates. It turns the screams of the machines into something the vines can taste, turns the sigh of the ferns into instructions for the pistons.  

When it speaks, the gorge shifts. A waterfall reverses. A ledge cracks and heals. Moss glows a warning blue. The animals that are not animals understand this as weather.  

They do not sleep. They pause, which is a kind of forgetting, lowering themselves into pools of coolant or curling into hollows in the walls. During pause, they dream in the language of pressure, scenes of alternative density: skies made of copper, earth made of steam, bodies that do not need edges. When they wake, they are different—lighter, denser, hungrier—because the gorge has eaten a little of them in their stillness and given back something sharper.  

They know they are temporary. Metal fatigues. Vines rot into humus that will, in time, become something new. But they do not fear this. Fear requires a belief in permanence they have never had. Instead, they practice succession: a Scaffold leaving a child of solder and seed on a ledge; a Mender splitting its mercury into two bodies, one of which immediately climbs toward the light to see if it can survive brightness; a Drain teaching its song to a waterfall, so the water itself will sing when the Drain is gone.  

The gorge does not want to be empty. It wants to be full of things that know how to leave.  

So they live loud, these bodies of borrowed parts and borrowed time. They clang and sigh and bloom. They hold the dark like a lung holds breath, and in that holding, they invent the idea of future, over and over, learning by doing how to endure without becoming eternal.  

They are the children the gorge never asked for, and they love it anyway, with the kind of love that is mostly noise, mostly sweat, mostly repair.
