In the pocket, what lives wears altitude like a second, thinner skin. They are not residents so much as arrangements that have learned how not to settle. Because the city is young and the mountains still ache from the violence that lifted them, everything that persists here has had to invent a way to be unfinished and durable at once.

There are the Glasswings. They are small, the length of a finger and no thicker than a shard of splintered light, bodies faceted from material that rings like glass but flexes like cartilage. They do not fly so much as suspend, skating across vapor layers where the wetness beads and slides. Their wings harvest static from the city’s exhale—tiny coronas that bloom like halos around their edges—and drink the mineral film that settles on warm cables like syrup on snow. They do not eat in any older sense. They metabolize charge, converting cold and friction into a light, ionized waste that drifts upward to be caught by the city’s skin. In this way, they power the micro-lifts that carry messages between tiers, becoming both courier and current. When they cluster, the air smells faintly of ozone and struck flint. When one dies, it does not fall so much as dim, its body cooling into a translucent pebble that is taken up by the gill-streets and recycled.

Then there are the Moiré-Stalkers, longer and quieter, creatures of tension and insulation. They look almost like diagrams of bridges given flesh, four-limbed and jointed with braided alloys that creak like new rope. Their skin is matte, the color of basalt under cloud, and patterned with veins that glow faintly when they hold a charge. They do not hunt the Glasswings so much as eclipse them. Predation here is subtraction: the Stalkers wait on cantilevered ledges, bodies coiled like springs, and let the cold pool around them. When a Glasswing passes too close, the Stalkers unclench, creating a sudden sink of warmth that bleeds from the prey into the air. The Glasswings stall, their lights guttering, and the Stalkers gather them in hands that are more like clamps than claws, careful not to shatter what they mean to keep. What is consumed is not body but charge; what is left behind is a husk of glass that rings softly when tapped and is later used in the city’s tuning systems, a note in the infrastructure’s hum.

There are also the Sieve-Rats, though to call them rats is to be generous with resemblance. They are small, furred with spun mineral wool that repels the wet, and quick enough to seem like afterimages. They live in the gaps between tiers, in the 0.6 quiet where the city listens to itself. Their teeth are edged with volcanic glass, and they gnaw at the gill-streets to keep the tessellations from seizing, nibbling away mineral buildup and spitting it out as neat pellets that harden into tiny lenses. These lenses are harvested by the Glasswings, who carry them to ridge-faces to help focus the slanted light. The Sieve-Rats, in turn, feed on the cooled water that drips from those same faces, lapping up the drizzle that hisses when it meets warmth. They are the city’s maintenance crew, invisible and indispensable, their small metabolisms tuned to the 0.6 register of mostly quiet.

Above them all, skimming the rim where the young ridges still shed gravel, are the Echo-Hawks. They are not feathered but plated, wide-bodied fliers built to ride the thin air without ever truly committing to it. Their wings are membranes strung over frames of tensile alloy, and they move with the deliberation of weather fronts. They do not hunt animals. They hunt sound. The pocket is cupped, and sound bounces within it, catching on ice and mirror. The Echo-Hawks listen for patterns—expansion joints clinking, bridges unspooling, the soft bell-arrival of distant shifts—and memorize them. They return to the city’s central plaza and reproduce what they have heard, their bodies vibrating in frequencies that the infrastructure recognizes as maintenance signals. In this way, the city learns its own condition through them, a flock of living diagnostics.

At the plaza itself, where the black ice is kept from freezing solid by warmed veins, there gathers something harder to name: the Process-Kin. They are not creatures in any familiar sense but temporary assemblies—human and more-than-human, machine and mineral and weather—who convene when the load map changes. They stand in sparse constellations, breath-clouding in the 2.2 light, hands on panels and knees on ice, listening to the wet air carry distant sounds. They adjust mirrors, unspool bridges, and tilt facets to redirect brightness into shaded streets. They speak rarely, and when they do, it is in gradients, in the language of pressure and condensation. They are the city’s deliberation made flesh, its activity given voice, and they disperse as soon as the negotiation is complete, returning to cantilever and tier, leaving only the faint ozone scent of Glasswings and the soft clink of gravel shed by the remembering mountains.

All of them—wings and stalkers, rats and hawks, assemblies of breath and alloy—live by the pocket’s rules. Gravity is respected but edited; cold is a medium, not merely a temperature; wetness climbs and is routed; time is measured in cycles of drainage and annealing. They are sparse as the city is sparse, generous with their emptiness, holding the altitude close and letting it shape them. They do not grow like plants or march like armies. They persist like weather, like engineering, like a quiet that continues only because everyone is listening. And in the high, young bowl cupped by stone, that is enough—more than enough—for a place that is still becoming, a cold machine that breathes, a city that is also a conversation, a pocket that lives by learning how to keep what passes through it.
