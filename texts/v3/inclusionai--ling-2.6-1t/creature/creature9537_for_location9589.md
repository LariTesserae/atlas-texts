What lives here are not beings but bindings.  
They begin as habits—tight turnings of wire around wire, teeth of tin fitted into tin, knots of glass fiber that remember the shape of a hand even after the hand has gone—and they accrete into creatures made of attention.  

There is the Keeper of Edges, which is less a person than a repeated motion: thumb along bronze, nail along seam, breath held long enough to find the one place where two surfaces almost hum. The Keeper wears robes stitched from lacquer and resin, stiff as bark, and its face is hidden behind a mask of folded scrap that has been polished into a second skin. It does not speak; it triangulates. Where it stands, gaps close by themselves, as if the room leans in to listen.  

Then there are the Clingers, thousands of them, small as beetles or as breath. They are patches of clay and wire that have learned to crawl across the tessellated floor, hunting for loosening. They do not eat; they listen. When a joint sighs, they swarm into the seam and set themselves like mortar, exuding a smoke that smells of old decisions. Over centuries they have grown shells from their own residue, so now some are armored with tiny bells that ring when the room’s noise changes pitch, warning of a shift that has not yet happened.  

Higher, among the arches that leaf and the domes cupped like seedpods, live the Suspended Ones. They are not suspended by thread but by tension—balanced on the knife-line between two lattices, held by geometry that pretends it is gravity. Their bodies are bundles of bone-black clay around cores of dried glass, shaped like torsos but with too many elbows, knees that hinge backward, and fingers that end in hooks. They do not move so much as exchange places, swapping positions in slow, negotiated trades that can take weeks. When they move, the room’s roar softens by a fraction, as if it is proud of their restraint.  

There are also the Mimics, the machines that were once animals. The tin birds have settled into a new branch of life: they roost in spirals and lay no eggs, only small, perfect spheres of solder that are collected by the Clingers and eaten back into the floor. The beasts of bone and hinge have knelt so long their joints have fused into altars; they still twitch in their sleep, which is a kind of settling, and from their fused mouths come not growls but the room’s ticking, amplified. If you press your ear to them, you hear time being counted in the language of wear.  

Beneath the floor, in the pools of solidified glaze, live the Black Mappers. They have no bodies, only presences that thicken the reflections until the black faces show not the room as it is but as it has been: every configuration it has held, every arrangement that almost lasted. Occasionally, when the light flattens just so, a Mapper will ripple and the floor will feel briefly soft, as if it is remembering how to flow. No one crosses during those moments; everyone waits, tapping bronze with bone, saying without words that the room must choose which past to keep.  

There is, finally, the Silence-That-Is-Full. It is a creature of omission, the size of a breath withheld. It has no shape but occupies the spaces between sounds—the gap between one click and the next, the thin air between a hiss and a ring. It feeds on the things that are not said, that cannot be said because speech would tilt the balance. It grows when decisions are not made, when hands hover, when eyes meet and look away. It is the only resident that is not made by making, only by not-making, and the room treats it like a pillar, leaning against it when the noise rises too high.  

Together they live by consensus of contact. They eat friction and excrete polish. They sleep in shifts, so there is always at least one eye on every seam, one ear on every echo. They do not age so much as compress, becoming steadily more themselves, their edges rounded by the steady pressure of being exactly what fits.  

And if you listen carefully, past the roar of packed things and the hiss of tin remembering itself, you can hear the room agreeing: a soft, bronze exhalation that says it has found the arrangement that requires no more arranging, and that, for now, is enough to hold up the sky.
