What lives here has given up the habit of beginning.  

The Residents do not arrive; they condense. They are assemblies that wake up one morning already expert, already middle-aged, clothed in the same resinous sweat that beads on every surface and dries into glittering glass-dust at their joints. To see one is to witness a settlement in the act of deciding what posture it prefers. They begin as piles: belts that have unknotted themselves, casings that have unscrewed in the night, filaments that have drifted down from the overhead lattices like pollen with nowhere else to land. By midday they have fused at the points of contact—solder blooming like lichen, ceramic bark knitting across gaps—until a stance is achieved that can be maintained without thought. A torso may be mostly chimney, or mostly bellows, or mostly stair; the face, when there is one, is usually a grate that has learned to make expressions by adjusting its soot-load.  

They are bureaucrats of matter. Their metabolism is maintenance, and their bodies are the records of it. Each Resident is a walking archive of the plateau’s decisions: every replaced gear is commemorated by a scar of fresher metal; every overload is remembered as a blue-white callus along the forearm; every near-collapse is inscribed as a lattice of hair-thin cracks filled with phosphorescent sap that pulses when the wind is low. They do not eat; they reprocess. They press their hands into the hot copper soil and draw up not nourishment but potential—old potentials, forgotten alloys, sugars that never caramelized only to be rebaked into language. They speak by releasing controlled amounts of stress: a chime in a beam, a papery rustle behind the ribs, a word-shaped puff of soot that smells of overripe orchard and iron.  

They live in committees. A single Resident can take years to finish a sentence, so they overlap, passing syntax like heat through a wall. In the vine-vaulted canyons they assemble into parliaments that are also engines: stems the thickness of smokestacks serving as columns for bodies that are mostly scaffolding, voices rising and falling as valves release small clouds of pollen that gleam with suspended copper. Decisions are not so much made as annealed—slowly, under pressure, until disagreement softens into alignment. The ground beneath them ripples in sympathy, for the Residents are heavy with memory, and memory is dense enough to warm the metal in the floor until it remembers being ore and shivers upward into new seams.  

What appears to be fauna is, on closer inspection, contract. There are the Scuttlers—impersonations of urgency, skittering through heat-sink quarries like rumors given limbs. They have no organs, only tasks: to tighten, to warn, to carry away the dust that is nothing more than manufactured absence. They leave trails of ceramic pollen that settle into drifts and are, in time, woven into bridges by the Residents, who are always building the means by which they were just moving. And there are the Loomers, tall and slow, who patrol the edges of the clearings where plant-foundries exhale their crops. The Loomers do not guard; they witness. Their bodies are assemblies of observation: lenses grown like fruit, ears that are the dried and hollowed nodes of ancient conveyor belts. When a Loomer stops, the plateau holds its breath; its shadow pools in the leaf-cups like syrup, and the Residents feel the weight of being seen, which is to feel the weight of being necessary.  

Even the smallest lives here are imperial. The spores are, on closer inspection, tiny printed circuits riding thermals of their own making. They do not seek soil but interface, landing on hot casings and immediately soldering themselves into place to report—who knows to whom—the temperature of the dream that is currently passing through the wall. The moss-metal hybrids that fur the heat sinks conduct and insulate in alternating rings, and if you listen closely you can hear them singing in alternating languages: one of pressure, one of relief.  

Night, when it comes, does not empty the place but concentrates it. The Residents do not sleep; they condense further. Their bodies dim to embers, their voices dropping to the frequency of distant pistons. In the absolute dark they become almost geological, their outlines blurring into the strata, their joints glowing like warning lamps grown as thorns along the stems of bridges. It is in this dark that they grow themselves, layer by layer, into whatever shape best endures their own heat. They do not dream of elsewhere. To dream of elsewhere would be to invite gap, and gap is the one thing forbidden by the bark that rearranges itself when not observed.  

And so the plateau lives, not in spite of its inhabitants but as them: a single, breathing artifact of built, packed, vegetal industry, forever adjusting, forever thickening, forever sweating the glass-thin evidence that it has not yet cooled, and will not, so long as there is another committee to form, another gear to remember, another way to say *we are still here* without saying anything at all.
