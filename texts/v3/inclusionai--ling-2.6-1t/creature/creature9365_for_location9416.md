They are the basin’s answer to itself: a people built the way the basin builds, by accretion, by negotiation, by letting the urgent present grow right through the remembered past.

Their bodies are short and broad at the shoulder, as if the sky had pressed down gently and they had agreed to stay low. Skin is not smooth but slightly ridged, the texture of a young stem that has thickened to bear weight, damp to the touch and cool, as though the blood itself had learned to pool rather than rush. Where joints bend—elbows, knees, the hinge of the neck—there are tufts of fine, water-dark hair like capillary moss, and the cartilage rings in their throats are pronounced, almost visible, flaring when they speak or swallow the thick air. Their hands are broad-palmed, webbed not with membrane but with tough, callused webbing of scar-tissue and constant use; fingernails are clipped short and squared, lacquered with resin to keep them from splitting in the wet.

They dress in layers that behave more like architecture than clothing: pleated wraps of cured leaf, fitted panels of woven bark, cloaks that are actually living fern still rooted to small, portable soils at the hip. Over time the garments grow into the wearer—stitches knit shut with new pith, buttons bulge into buds, seams sprout tiny roots that anchor the garment to the skin when sleep comes. To be dressed is to be in slow conversation with what you wear; to be undressed is to leave part of yourself behind, a polite amputation that itches until the new growth catches up.

Their faces are wide, with noses like shallow scoops that warm and humidify the air before it reaches the lungs, and eyes the color of peat water, ringed with bright, alert whites. When they smile, it is a slow unfurling, as if the expression were something they had planted and now are patiently waiting to see open. Their voices are low and textured, like water moving through culverts; laughter is a quick, percussive thing that sets the small hairs on their neck to trembling, and in the high stillness it can summon frogs from their hollows as if the sound itself were a kind of rain.

Theirs is a life of constant, careful maintenance. A morning begins with the tending of grafts: pressing fresh slips into the scars of yesterday’s work, whispering to the tissue until it accepts the join. They climb the willow-lanterns to cup the candles in their knots, knuckles brushing the warm glass, checking that the flame has not scorched the new bark. They test the retaining walls by leaning into them, feeling for the subtle give that means a root has weakened or a vine has slackened; they patch with clay and quick-slime, humming tunelessly to keep their own rhythms steady while the ground answers with small creaks.

To walk among them is to witness a choreography of weight and yield. They step with the flat, deliberate tread of those who know the floor is not earth but a raft with a memory; they pause to listen when the mat settles, interpreting the groan as news. When they carry things, they use tumplines and harnesses grown from living fibers, so that the load is shared with the plant, and the plant, in time, shares its strength back. Their children learn early how to read the moods of the basin: the tightening of a sphincter-vine means wind is coming; the sudden sour tang in the air means the root-sump is holding its breath.

Socially, they are organized like the channels that vein the green: branching, merging, rerouting. Authority is not a height but a conduit—who can guide the flow without damming it, who can hear when a wall of leaf is tired and needs to be relieved. Disputes are settled in the spill-pools, where words are spoken into the water and allowed to sink or float according to their weight. Gossip moves like capillary rise, patient and inevitable, so that by evening everyone knows who has trimmed too much, who has let a graft fail, who has found a new kind of moss that glows faintly in the dark.

Their bodies carry the basin inside them. Lungs are large, seamed with fine scars from spores; livers are dark and efficient, able to metabolize the moss-tea that passes for common drink. Their sweat does not evaporate cleanly but beads and rolls, leaving maps of salt on their collars. When they are ill, they do not exile themselves; they are woven into a sick-nest of soft fern and heated clay, and the community brings them water that has been sung over, and leaves that taste of iron and courage. Death, when it comes, is a return to the choreography: the body is laid on a raft of its own making and set into the root-sump, where it is quickly folded into the green, remembered only in the thickening of a wall or the unusual warmth of a step.

For all their adaptation, they remain startlingly soft. Their hands are scarred but gentle; their teeth, though strong, are used more for cutting cordage than for violence. When they gather in the central willow at dusk, they press close, shoulders touching, breathing the same humid air, and the canopy above them answers by shaking loose a rain of stored light, each bead catching the candle-flames like a chandelier of falling stars. In those moments they are not rulers or tenants of the basin but chords in its long, living note—brief, necessary, and already being hummed into the next verse of growth.

And when a stranger stands among them, damp and out of rhythm, they do not drive the stranger away. They watch, their faces open as windows, and they offer a cutting, a stone warmed by their pocket, a lesson in how to step so that the raft answers with a firm, living yes. They know what it is to be temporary, useful, and already being claimed by the green; it is, after all, how they have chosen to live.
