They did not arrive; they thickened.

At first there were only hands—knuckles pale as new tallow, fingernails rimmed with the gray paste of stone-sweat—pressing prints into surfaces that had not yet learned to be hard. The hands belonged to bodies that moved like wedges, fitting themselves into the narrow mathematics of a bowl that was learning to hold them. These bodies were not born here, but they are becoming of here. Their skin has taken on the mineral luster of wet slate. Their hair, when it is not tied back with strips of cured hide, hangs in cords that trap dust and condensation. They do not cough; they resonate. When they speak, the sound is not separate from the clang of flint on flint, but a tuning fork slipped into the general vibration.

They are many, and they are legion. There is no counting them because counting implies edges, and edges are only suggestions here. They flow from alley to alley like water finding its own level, each person a vessel for carrying a task from one warm wall to another. Some shape stone with tools that are older than memory—hammers with heads of dense, lake-smoothed basalt, chisels of sharpened copper that ring in pitches you can feel in the teeth. Others tend the runnels, their fingers deft at coaxing seepage into gutters carved directly into living rock, guiding each bead as if it were a pearl that might roll away if not watched. Still others arrange the fungal mats, pressing them into place with palms that are themselves slightly tacky, as if the biology of the place has begun to recognize kinship.

The children are louder than the stone. They are bright, dense knots of motion, threading through lanes that are still being straightened by adult feet. Their laughter bells off low roofs, startling the flightless birds that strut along the eaves—creatures with collars of iridescent scale that catch the milky light and fracture it into colors the air is too thick to let fall. The children do not fear the lizard-backed scavengers that patrol the margins; they know the beasts by the click of their too-many joints, and they know to leave them the damp seams where grubs writhe like tiny, hopeful syllables.

Among the adults there are those who listen more than they build. They are the soft ones, the ones whose hands are rarely calloused, whose eyes hold the flat sheen of water just below the surface. They stand in doorways that are still deciding how tall to be and let the noise wash over them. They are the ones who hear the place speaking—not in words, but in the thickening of sound itself, in the way the drip from copper gutters finds a rhythm that matches the pulse in the neck. They say the place is naming itself through accumulation: each stone laid is a consonant, each footfall a vowel, each breath a particle of dust caught in the low, milky light.

There are also the ones who are not quite people, or who are people only by courtesy of having two arms and a hunger. They are the ones with translucent skin, the ones who dart through wall-gaps like thoughts that cannot decide whether to finish themselves. They carry nothing but their own transparency, and they are everywhere, slipping past thresholds before the mortar has even thought to harden. No one chases them; no one names them. They are simply part of the density, like the spores that turn the air into substance, like the pollen that makes the sun feel heavy.

The animals, too, are residents before they are fauna. The lizard-backed scavengers know better than to call themselves predators here; the place offers shelter and wet in equal measure, and survival is a polite contract. The iridescent birds roost on roofs that are still warm from the day’s hammering, their claws finding purchase on slate that has not yet cooled its temper. At night, when the fires are banked to keep the damp from settling into mortar, the city hums with a bestiary of small, efficient lives: claws on stone, bellies on warm ledges, jaws snapping at the edges of silence.

There is no old age here. Faces are lined, yes, but with the fine creases of constant expression, not with the erosion of time. Hair grays, but only where sweat and metal have left their salt. Bodies thicken, not from decay but from accumulation—from the weight of carrying tasks across a bowl that keeps offering more room. When someone dies—and they do, though the place does not advertise it—their absence is folded into the walls as if stone could swallow stories and keep them warm. The living do not mourn in the usual way; they mourn by building, by laying another stone, by channeling another bead of water into a gutter that already knows the route.

And always there are the gestures. Because the noise makes speech a shared effort, the people have learned to speak with their elbows, with the tilt of a jug, with the angle of a shoulder against a half-dry wall. They trade in nods and pauses, in the way a hand lingers on a piece of smoothed river-pebble before letting it go. The barter is not of goods but of presence: you offer a moment of your density, I offer a moment of mine, and the space between us thickens into something that feels like agreement.

They are, in short, a wound that has learned to be a nest. They are kiln and scar and hatchling all at once. They are loud because quiet would feel like emptiness, and emptiness is a rumor here, a word that has not yet found a surface to dry on. They are complete, and they are becoming, and they are exactly as many as the place will hold—which is, for now, exactly as many as there are.
