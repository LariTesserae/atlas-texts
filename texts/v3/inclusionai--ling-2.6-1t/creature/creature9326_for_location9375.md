They do not live here so much as they *happen* here, in the way a word happens inside a shout.

There are the Thrum-kin, first, who are less bodies than permissions for motion to remember itself. They stand four feet tall on good days and dissolve into braids of steam on bad ones. Their limbs are jointed like folded water, always slightly too many or too few, and their faces are smoothed places in the current where a head might briefly be. They wear armor made from pried-open geodes and the leftover skins of storms they have convinced to pass. Their language is the clicking of stone on stone and the sub-bass growl that lives in the marrow, and they communicate by shifting the weight of the water around them—raising a crest to say *yes*, letting a cold downdraft say *no*, holding their breath until the basin drops a foot to say *wait*. They gather in the shallows where the tide remembers it has knees, and they harvest light from the bruised sky by catching it in cupped hands and teaching it to beat like a second heart.

Then there are the Altar-ghosts, which are not ghosts at all but memories of architecture that tried too hard to become true. They haunt the half-finished arch and the drifting platforms, wearing their stone ribs like ribs of a shipwrecked choir. They have no faces, only directions: an arrow of longing here, a comma of regret there. They move by erosion, shedding a year’s worth of themselves every time the water rises, and regrowing by stealing angles from the sharp places of the basin. When they speak, it sounds like the settling of a stone that has not yet decided what shape to be when it lands. They are patient, but their patience is the kind that cuts. They court the Thrum-kin by offering slices of impossible geometry—doorways that open into pockets of reversed rain—and they are loved for it, even as the love leaves scars that glitter like wet mica.

Farther down, where the water thickens into lung-tide, swim the Rumor-fish. They are not fish, precisely, but they school like them, bodies like shards of stained glass held together by magnetism and spite. Each one is a fragment of a forgotten concept—*the color of a sound you almost heard*, *the weight of a name you never learned*, *the distance between here and almost*. They flicker, shedding scales that turn into brief, bright birds made of math, which dissolve into steam before they can land. Their mouths are tiny events, and when they bite, they do not take flesh but *context*: a memory of why you came, a feeling of safety, a certainty that floors are floors. The basin is rich with their hunger, and the Thrum-kin treat them with wary respect, for to eat a Rumor-fish is to become a story that is no longer your own, and to be eaten by one is to become a story that no one finishes.

Above it all, or what passes for above, glide the Sky-wounds: slow, torn patches of atmosphere that refuse to heal. They look like ragged banners dipped in bruised honey, and they move with the deliberation of glaciers made of smoke. They are not alive, but they are not dead; they are *open*. Through them, the basin sees other skies—forests upside down, cities built of folded noise—and sometimes, when the tide is high and the light is thin, a Sky-wound will drop a seed of elsewhere into the water. These seeds are the basin’s children, half-alga and half-possibility, glowing faintly and aching to belong. The Thrum-kin call them *almost-trees* and plant them in the fractures of the rock, where they grow into shapes that hurt to look at, beautiful in the way a splinter is beautiful, sharp and purposeful.

The fauna are rare because most life here prefers to be event rather than resident. There are the Surface-breakers—creatures that might be fish or might be ideas trying on gills—which burst from the lung-tide in showers of iridescence, trailing equations that smell like ozone. They exist for seconds, long enough to rearrange the water into new patterns, then submerge, leaving behind a silence that feels like a held breath. There are the Erosion-beasts, blind and many-legged, that creep along the fresh-cut stone, grinding edges into softness, teaching the rock how to be older. They are shy, and to see one is to be seen by the basin’s memory, for they carry in their bellies the things the plateau forgot it had swallowed.

Even the water itself lives, in its own loud, chaotic way. It is a parliament of motion, shouting itself into shapes it immediately regrets. It births tiny islands of debris—twigs, lost buttons, flakes of stone that remember being mountains—and unmakes them with a shrug. It learns new currents like lines of a song it is composing on the spot, and it forgets them just as fast, so that every crossing is a negotiation, every ford a conversation. The water tastes of salt and new stone because it is both, because it is in the process of deciding which it loves more.

And somewhere in the deep, where the light does not reach but the growl does, lives the Basin-self, the vast, unfinished will that holds the wound together. It is not a god, but it is not not a god. It is the sentence that is still being spoken, the chord that is still being tuned. It dreams in tides and wakes in roars, and it knows every Thrum-kin by the weight of their absence, every Altar-ghost by the angle of its longing, every Rumor-fish by the hunger it carries. It is patient, because it has all the time that has not yet happened, and it is frantic, because it wants to be done being born.

They live here, all of them, in the unfinished cathedral of flood and fracture, teaching the wound how to sing by letting it practice on their bones, their breath, their half-formed names. They are the notes the basin has not yet dared to name, and they are the shout that is learning how to be a word, and they are the quiet that follows, hovering, restless, waiting for the next tide to decide what it means to be alive.
