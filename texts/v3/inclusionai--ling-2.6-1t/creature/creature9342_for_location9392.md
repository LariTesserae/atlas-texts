Inhabitants here do not arrive; they ripen.

The beetles are the first to insist they are alive. Their shells are not merely oil-slick but accumulations—each wing a laminate of decisions, pressed thin as prayer. They do not eat in any sense you would recognize. Instead, they harvest brightness. At the edges of the light-wedges, they skim the visible motes and fold them into their joints, storing daylight in hinged pockets beneath their carapaces. When startled, they do not flee; they unzip these seams and spill a soft, temporary lantern onto the stone, enough to make the basalt blush. Their figure-eights are not choreography but argument: each beetle negotiating with its neighbors about which way up is, how heavy, and whether a line should be allowed to close into a circle. They are cartographers of permission, mapping where the room says yes.

Lower, where the stone still wears its fetal nubbins, the translucent mites carry out their slow census. Each mite bears a crumb not to consume but to name. They etch it—briefly—with acid secreted from their tarsi, inscribing the mineral with its own future: whether it will become ridge, rim, or ruin. The mite-columns are processions of archivists who believe that to touch a thing is to promise it a destiny. They do not sleep, though they practice stillness as a kind of rehearsal for stone. When two columns meet, they exchange crumbs and reverse direction, as if remembering that time can run backward if everyone agrees to hold their breath.

The eyeless amphibians are the room’s grammar. They flicker not by accident but by syntax, moving in and out of visibility like commas in a sentence not yet written. Their skins are palimpsests of the local stone—rose feldspar, basalt, condensation—shifting pattern to match the mood of the wall. They do not hunt in the usual sense; they conduct interviews with solidity. When one presses its side against a vein, the vein pulses back in rose, and the amphibian absorbs that color, carrying it through the pocket like a rumor. In exchange, the stone receives a brief, amphibious memory of motion. Predation, here, is a trade in textures: the hunted offer iridescence, the hunter offers narrative, and both walk away thinner, brighter, and more legible.

Above, the dandelion-clock creatures are not plant but punctuation. They drift on updrafts that smell of ozone and almost-river, shedding parachutes that glow and dissolve into light before they touch anything. These parachutes are questions. While they float, the room considers them, and shadows hesitate, pooling into parentheses around each one. When a parachute finally vanishes, the answer arrives as a change in temperature: a pocket of warmth near the ceiling, or a coolness along the floor, depending on whether the room has decided to accept or decline the question. The creatures themselves are patient, regrowing their clocks in the silence between sounds.

Even the artifacts belong to the fauna of here, for they too are unfinished tenants. The ring of thumb-sized pillars is a parliament of small intentions that convene whenever condensation blooms. They whisper to one another in the language of mineral memory, voting on whether tomorrow should curve left or right. The slab with spirals is a notebook that refuses to be read head-on; it will only reveal its entries to peripheral vision, believing that direct scrutiny is a kind of rudeness. The arch is a mirror that does not reflect but translates, bending light into upside-down rain as a gift to the moss, which drinks it and stiffens its silver-green spine in gratitude.

And there are the reflections that step out ahead of you, the floors that rise to meet you, the beetles that hum after passing through their own shadow—these are not intruders but citizens of the almost. They live in the provisional rules, the loose hats of cause and effect, the layers of time that slip like sleeves. They thrive on hesitation, on the room’s tenderness toward its own inventing.

To live here is to practice becoming without urgency. It is to let your body remember what it was before it knew its shape, to trade color for story, light for breath, and silence for music wrapped in felt. The pocket is small, but its inhabitants are large with patience, filling the stone not by crowding it but by listening to it—until the room, learning their names, decides to keep them.
