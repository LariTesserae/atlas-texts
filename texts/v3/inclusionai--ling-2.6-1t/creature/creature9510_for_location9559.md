They do not live here so much as they are lived by the place.

There are the Root-Lords, first of all, though title implies hierarchy where none exists. They are the architects who never build, the surgeons who never cut. They appear as columns of knotted muscle and bark, taller than houses, their bodies braided from multiple trunks that have fused into a single intent. Their faces are not faces but weathers: openings in the wood that exhale spores in rhythmic clouds, lids of petal and plate that blink moisture across their surfaces. They do not walk; they shift, settling their weight into new alignments, grinding foundations deeper into the loam. When they dream—and they dream in slow seismic events—the vault shudders, and the streets tilt to accommodate their thoughts.

Above them, in the canopy-cathedral, live the Lantern-Spine. These are not animals but living chandeliers, colonies of insect and flora fused into winged chandeliers. Each spine is a lattice of hollow bone-vine filled with bioluminescent algae and stung into brightness by captive electric eels that have forgotten how to be alone. They drift in slow orbits, shedding light like dandruff, feeding the lower city with brightness that tastes faintly of copper. When one dies, it does not fall; it dims gradually, its passengers evacuating into cracks to begin again.

The merchants are the Mycelium-Tongues, sprawling across the market floors as mats of felt and film. They do not speak, but they translate: chemical to electrical, pressure to promise. Their bodies are thin, white fans that ripple when approached, tasting the air for intent. They accept payment in vibration, in heat, in the careful placement of heavy objects that changes the resonance of their mats. A good trade is one that leaves the mat singing in harmony with the vault’s baseline hum. They remember every bargain in the rings of their growth, and in lean times they can recite past exchanges in flavors released into the air—bitter for debts, sweet for surplus.

Then there are the Gear-Keepers, the maintenance caste, small and frantic and indispensable. They resemble armored lice the size of terriers, with jaws like wire-cutters and backs plated in chitin polished by constant oiling. They swarm the ligneous engines, chewing away calcified buildup, spitting resin into failing joints, laying eggs in the warm seams of pistons so their young may feed on friction and grow into new tools. They understand pressure and tolerance better than affection, and they will die in place before allowing a bearing to seize. In death, their shells are harvested and ground into paste to seal leaks in the cistern-bladders.

Higher still, in the districts where gravity forgets itself, dwell the Drifters. These are creatures of minimal substance—jelly-thin membranes stretched over frames of cartilage and helium sacs. They float between the tilted walls, grazing on condensation and the occasional small thought that detaches from the Root-Lords and drifts upward. They reproduce by budding off fragments of themselves that catch on updrafts and grow into new Drifters if they find purchase. They have no predators, only accidents, and the place feels a subtle guilt each time one is torn apart on a hidden gear.

The predators are not hidden, but the Watch-Center is something else again. This is not a single creature but a position, maintained by the Centipede-Tram, an arthropod the length of an avenue. Its body is segmented into compartments that house tenants: beetles that polish its armor, mosses that soften its stride, small birds that nest in the warm valley of its underbelly. It moves with the inevitability of a closing door, and its passage clears the streets, resets the market, crushes the inefficient. Along its back travel the Drifters and the desperate, seeking to be elsewhere when it passes. It does not hunt; it administers.

Beneath the industrial hum, in the wet black where the cistern-bladders dream, live the Silt-Singers. These are eyeless, many-limbed things that maintain the undercity, sorting nutrients by taste, routing decay to the furnaces and memory to the walls. They are soft and deliberate, leaving grooves in the metal-wood that channel future flows. They never surface, and their song is a subsonic pressure that makes teeth ache and marrow settle. They are the archivists of rot, and it is said that if you lie still in the damp long enough, they will translate your history into a flavor that rises through the floor as vapor.

There are also the Brief-Rulers, those who seize control of a corridor or a valve for a season: swarms of wasp-things that take over a turbine and spin it too fast, fungi that colonize a gate and demand passage in spores, beetles that plug a conduit to hoard pressure and sell it drop by drop. They rise and collapse within weeks, absorbed into the greater metabolisms, their ambitions reduced to heat.

And through it all, the place itself persists as a kind of sovereign plural. The walls remember conversations in mineral deposits. The floors learn the weight of habits and grow softer or harder accordingly. The ceilings bloom with fruit that open into small, temporary storms when the pressure is right, as if the vault is practicing weather.

To live here is to be incorporated, slowly and with ceremony. The Root-Lords will notice you first, or not at all. The Mycelium-Tongues will taste your purpose and offer a price. The Gear-Keepers will keep you moving. The Silt-Singers, in the end, will sing you into the dark, where you will join the ongoing work of keeping everything warm, wet, and turning.
