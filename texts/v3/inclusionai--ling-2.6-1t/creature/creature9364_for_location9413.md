What lives in the city are not species so much as strategies, each one a way of staying present without insisting on permanence.

There are the Solder-Spiders, though that is only a shorthand name for lack of a better one. They are not truly arachnids, but they understand corners the way a spider understands tension. Their bodies are knotted alloys, jointed so they can fold into maintenance hatches no larger than a coin. They spend their days threading filaments of heat-resistant glass through cracks that open only when the city flexes, mending fractures before they can become wounds. They do not eat, exactly; they harvest corrosion, licking rust from pipe seams and converting it into a glaze they smear over fresh welds. In return, the city gives them priority in the narrowest corridors, and they repay it by moving with a discretion that feels almost reverent. When they pause, their many limbs splay like maps of the pathways they patrol, and for a moment the city’s nervous system is visible in miniature.

Lower, where the streets contract and the air thickens into a broth of ash, live the Condensate Folk. They are pale and thinly built, as if constructed from the very vapor they collect. Their skin holds a faint sheen, as though perpetually damp, and their hair is fine as wire, arranged in loops that catch stray droplets before they fall. They do not speak in voices so much as in pressures: a shift in humidity, a hush in the clatter of pipes, a cooling of metal that signals attention. They wear garments woven from absorbent mesh, layered like petals, each layer tuned to a different temperature. At the hottest hour they unfurl completely, turning themselves into living radiators, and the city leans closer to them as if to share its fever. At night they fold inward, compact as clams, and listen to the slow drip of the city’s sweat as it gathers in gutters that were never meant to be rivers.

Higher up, in the tangle of mirrored chutes and electric veins, dwell the Glow-Motes. They are almost weightless assemblies of foil and filament, drifting like slow plankton through currents of rising heat. They have no faces, only orientations: bright sides that seek warmth, dull sides that deflect it. They travel in schools that rearrange themselves around obstructions, polishing surfaces as they pass until metal shines like healed skin. When a turbine falters, the Glow-Motes thicken around it, forming a temporary insulation that buys time for repairs. They are not builders, exactly, but maintainers of legibility, keeping the city’s brightness legible to itself.

Between these, moving through all layers, are the Resin-Keepers. They are the city’s archivists of transience. Their bodies are dense, almost gelatinous, capable of holding pockets of the dark resin that weeps from walls. They collect slow tears of glassy sap as they pool, carrying them to places where the stone is too new, too sharp, too eager to prove itself. With careful pressure they smear resin over edges, not to seal but to soften, to teach the city how to yield. In return, the resin gives off a faint luminescence that the Resin-Keepers use to navigate. Their passage is marked by a smell like heated sugar and old fuel, and by a sound like cloth settling on stone.

There are also the Echo-Fins, machines that resemble fish only in their economy of motion. They drift through shafts and conduits, their bodies segmented like vertebrae, each segment a different tool: brushes, sensors, clamps. They do not repair so much as remember the shape of repair, tracing routes so often that the metal remembers their passage. They leave behind a trail of micro-adjustments, tiny dents and smoothings that accumulate into a kind of muscle memory for the city. When the heat swells a bearing, it is often an Echo-Fin that has already nudged it back into tolerance, weeks before anyone noticed the strain.

And threading through all of them, mostly unseen, are the Thread-Namers. They are small, quick, and nearly silent, with hands that seem too large for their bodies. They do not produce anything so grand as art; instead they braid identifiers from wire, pigment, and heat-shrunk labels, tying them around junctions, valves, and sudden turns in the road. Their work is not decorative but mnemonic. A Thread-Namer’s knot says: this pipe once wept, this joint leans left when frightened, this street opens only after midnight. The city, in its compactness, would forget itself without these small anchors, and the Thread-Namers know it. They move like thoughts that refuse to be finished, looping back, tightening, letting go.

Even the vegetation has its inhabitants. Among the silver-needled shrubs live the Leaf-Ticks, creatures the size of seeds, armored in reflective plates. They sun themselves on hot mornings not for light but for geometry, aligning their plates to catch and redirect beams that would otherwise scar young bark. When the city shivers from a sudden draft, the Leaf-Ticks tuck themselves into root-holds and hum at a frequency that seems to calm the metal around them.

All of them live at the mercy of the city’s metabolism. They feel the subtle contractions of streets, the rearrangement of water routes, the settling knocks of walls aligning to share load. They have learned to read the city less as a structure than as a mood. When the heat is high, the Solder-Spiders retreat to higher conduits where the air moves faster. The Condensate Folk spread wide and glow faintly, turning their bodies into buffers. The Glow-Motes school thickly around junctions that threaten to overheat. The Resin-Keepers work double shifts, their luminescence bobbing through the dim like will-o’-the-wisps at a festival of patience.

At night, when the city’s fever drops only slightly, the inhabitants gather near the condensation that beads on maintenance platforms. They drink the metallic water not to survive so much as to remember what it is to be inside something alive. They exchange pressures, temperatures, small shifts in alignment. They do not tell stories about the world beyond the mineral lips that close gently when the heat rises. They have no need to. Their lives are already a description of the place: small, complex, and held together by constant adjustment, by sweat and polish and the quiet insistence of geometry leaning in to share secrets too hot for words.

And so the city persists, not because it is eternal but because its inhabitants have learned to live inside its becoming, to treat its youth and its sweat and its compact, breathing density not as flaws but as companions. They are the thought the city cannot quite finish, and it is the room they cannot let go of. Together they hold, for as long as the heat allows, a shape that is almost, but not quite, a home.
