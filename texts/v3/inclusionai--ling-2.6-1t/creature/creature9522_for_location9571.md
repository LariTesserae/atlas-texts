What lives in the hollow is not a population but an apprenticeship.

The first residents are the echoes of force: the upthrust slabs themselves, still behaving as if they are surprised by their own edges. They live by shrinking a little each day, exhaling the memory of the impact that extruded them. They live in the form of newness—sharp flutes and pale ridges that have not yet learned to be soft. They live as geometry that has not yet forgiven the sky for pushing it into this intimate, thumbprint-sized room.

Beneath them, the iron-rich silt and fractured shale live their own life, which is a life of arrangement. They sort themselves into mosaics, dark and oil-wet, learning which pieces fit into which gaps. They live at the speed of sediment, which is to say they live as a promise to become pattern. They live as a floor that insists you kneel to enter, reminding every knee that arrives that gravity can be polite if it wants to be.

The water does not live here so much as it practices living, rehearsing its presence as thin sheets that glide like breath over glass. It lives as a constant drizzle exuded by weeping seams, as a lukewarm ghost that refuses to pool and instead chooses to glide. It lives as a teacher of patience, showing how to occupy a space without filling it, how to be dense without being heavy. It lives in the rhythmic ticks and slaps against metal catch-basins, learning to count time in drops that ring like coins paid to the floor.

The heat is a resident that manufactures itself. It lives as a wet wool blanket pressed against skin, as a manufactured climate that feels like a verdict. It lives in the wavering curtains of shimmer that distort the far wall, in the mineral stains that streak the blistered sheet metal like veins. It lives as a collaborator with the pump, whose flywheel turns with a heavy insistence, as if the heat is counting the cost of its own persistence.

The industrial things live best of all. The pipes, painted in heat-scabbed yellow and rust-orange, live as a nervous system that does not yet know what it is nervous about. They live in valves that weep warm condensate, in joints that feel warm and resilient under a palm, like skin over iron. Some pipes live as orphans, terminating in midair, weeping into basins that overflow into channels that do not exist. These pipes live as questions that have not yet found a grammar.

The lone building with its open door lives as a witness. Its gauges live behind dusty glass, their needles hovering in the red like birds that have almost decided to land. The building lives as a mouth that is neither speaking nor silent, as a small lung that knows how to hold its breath without knowing why.

The pump lives as a creature of viscosity. It lives in the thrum that rises from beneath the floor, a bass line that feels as though it is being transmitted through the bones of the hollow rather than the air. It lives as a metronome that does not care whether anyone is listening, as a machine practicing its breathing until breathing becomes a kind of thinking. The pump lives by teaching vibration to the air itself, so that even the dust motes and steam in the blade of light learn to move in time.

The absence of familiar fauna lives here, too, as a kind of resident. The lack of insects, the lack of burrowers—these absences live as a hesitation on the part of life itself, as if the microbial frontier has pressed its ear to the door and decided the conversation inside is too loud, too new. These absences live as a permission for other kinds of motion: the slow seep of water, the slower creep of oxidation, the occasional pebble nudged by a pulse in the floor.

Weirdness lives here as a structural tenant. The glowing hairline cracks that drink the vanishing water live as small, bright mouths. The stone that feels poured rather than quarried lives as a surface that remembers being liquid. The intimacy of scale—being able to touch two walls at once, to read a gauge without moving—lives as a closeness that magnifies the sense that the hollow is only a joint in something vast, a single note in a machine that hums below hearing.

And time lives here as a workshop. It lives in the millimeter-scale rising and falling of water across hours, in the slow tides of rust that bloom across metal, in the heat cycles that grind day into night with only slight changes in timbre. Time lives as a patient teacher, as the thing that decides, very slowly, how water and heat and metal will wear each other down.

In the spare intervals between sounds, the hollow learns itself. It becomes more exactly what it is: a young, wet, hot, loud place where nothing has finished arriving, where everything is still deciding how to belong to its own edges, and where waiting has become a form of living, practiced daily, like breathing.
