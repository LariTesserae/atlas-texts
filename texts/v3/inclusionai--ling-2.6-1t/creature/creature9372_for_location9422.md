They do not arrive; they are issued.

Every morning—though morning here is a dial set to *adequate*—the Basin extrudes its fauna from hatches no larger than thumbs. They emerge plated in the same alloy as the ground, as if the floor had grown restless and bled knees and elbows. At first they are small, bright with newness, their edges still carrying the burr of the stamp. They unfold, lock, and stand. Then they begin.

Most are Maintainers. They are knee-high, oblong, balanced on three casters that never squeal. A single lens, like a dark coin, swivels on a stalk where a head might be, reading rivets for fatigue, lips for leaks. From their middles extend thin wands of flexible alloy that taste the air, tap the gutters, prod the enamel-lichen into applause. When they find a seam that has loosened, they do not repair; they remind. They leave behind a patch of polish that tightens like a held breath, and the seam pulls itself closed. They swarm in legions, polite and urgent, their numbers a parliament of clicks and chirps. They do not sleep; they merely dim.

Higher in the density dwell the Glass-Fiber Moss-Eaters. Soft-bodied, their skins are matte membranes stretched over frames of hinged ribs. They move like palms pressed together and pulled apart, over and over. They feed on the refracting moss that grows along seam-flanges, grazing in herds that sound like paper shuffling. As they graze, their inner filaments glow faintly, storing light the way batteries store charge, and when they are full they arch and discharge it into the gutters, where the runoff briefly sings. They are not born; they are budded from the sides of larger ones, like pages from a book that never ends.

There are also the Piston-Wings, creatures made for ceilings that are really roofs. They are flat, wide, and skeletal, with wings of ribbed membrane that beat not with muscle but with the slow inflation of bellows. They ride the routed breezes, patrolling the manufactured sky for leaks in the alloy vault. When they find one, they land and seal it with a resin that smells of hot starch. Their wings, when folded, look like stacks of coins, and when they take off they scatter condensation in rhythmic waves that the fauna below count like time.

Occasionally, a larger shape lumbers into view, vast as a building and slow as tectonics. These are the Haulers. Their bodies are gantries on treads, bristling with hooks and winches, draped in tarps that flutter like weather systems trying to escape. They carry burdens that hum—coils of compressed season, crates of stabilized twilight, barrels of yesterday’s rain. The Haulers do not speak; their passage is a shift in pressure, a leaning of the light. The smaller fauna part for them, then flow back into place, as if the Hauler had passed through a school of fish made of gears.

There are no predators. There are, instead, the Calibrators—small, quick things with bodies like snapped rulers. They dart into corners where the others cannot fit, tightening screws the size of seeds, re-indexing thresholds, correcting drifts. When they finish, they do not leave; they fold into the walls, becoming part of the access panels, waiting for the next misalignment.

All of them drink the wetness that weeps from the ceilings. They lap films from the mirrored troughs, or siphon it through ports at their ankles. The wetness is never drunk dry; the place keeps its reserves like a lung. In return, the fauna give the Basin its voice: the tick of their joints, the soft slap of their feet on wet metal, the chorus of their lenses focusing, a sound like applause kept polite by discipline.

They do not love, but they attend. They do not die, but they are recalled. When their plating dulls, they return to the hatches, fold, and are melted into tomorrow’s issue. Their memories are not lost; they are alloyed into the next batch, so that every new fauna carries, in its edges, the quiet of all the ones that came before.

In the Basin of Welded Hours, they are the living layer, the only softness allowed, the busy parliament of parts that keeps the world at exactly the right angle to the sun. They are its hands, its senses, its small, persistent engines of being here. They live not by growing, but by holding—holding the seams, the slopes, the wetness, the light—holding it all exactly as it should be, forever.
