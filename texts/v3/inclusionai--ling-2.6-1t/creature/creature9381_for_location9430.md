They call themselves the Glass-Shepherds, though they are neither glass nor shepherds in any sense that would survive a descent to lower worlds.  

A Glass-Shepherd is a vertical creature, jointed like a lattice, skin a laminated sheet of translucent silicate layered with older, darker plates of basalt and opal. It walks on four slender limbs that distribute weight with the delicacy of tuning forks pressed into sand. Where a terrestrial animal would have fur or hide, the Shepherd wears history: its outermost layer flakes and regrows in slow seasons, leaving behind a litter of iridescent scales that the wind arranges into miniature dunes. Inside the laminate, pockets of fluid—mercury-thick and faintly phosphorescent—pulse not with blood heat but with thermal memory. When the sun climbs, the fluid brightens; when shadow passes, it dims, so that the creature’s torso becomes a living barometer, readable by any other Shepherd who cares to look.  

They do not eat, at least not as you would recognize. Instead they practice attention. The Shepherd stands for days at a time facing a particular band of light, its head—four articulated prongs that resemble a crown of cracked lenses—catching and refracting the brightness into the mineral veins that thread the plateau. In that exchange, something is taken from the air: ions, static, the faint charge that builds when stone and sky argue for too long. The Shepherd metabolizes voltage. It stores it in capacitors of calcified tissue around its hips, and when the stored charge grows heavy, it bleeds it off by gently stamping, sending ripples through the dust-carpets that bloom outward as fleeting auroras at ankle height.  

Socially, they move in constellations of three to seven, never touching. Their relationships are managed through geometry. Two Shepherds who have decided to travel together will walk at precisely complementary angles so that their shadows braid across the ground, lengthening and shortening in unison. Disagreements are resolved by one party lifting its front prongs, changing the spectrum of light it casts, effectively altering the terms of illumination under which they both stand. To refuse to adjust is to demand that the other operate in unflattering light; to comply is to forgive.  

What they shepherd are not animals but phenomena. When the plateau grows too loud with charge—when the dust begins to levitate in jittery clouds—a Shepherd will plant its limbs and become an anchor. Its laminate layers expand, creating a seal against the ground, and the excess voltage pours into it, grounding through the mercury veins. In this state, the Shepherd enters a kind of vigil, trembling slightly, humming at a frequency that settles the stones. Other Shepherds, sensing the shift in the air’s texture, will approach and stand in a circle, prongs lowered, until the plateau quiets. Once balance returns, they separate without ceremony, because ceremony would be excess.  

Young Shepherds are not born but extruded. In deep creases of the mineral sheets, where the rock holds residual warmth, a Glass-Shepherd will press its forehead into a crack and remain still for weeks. Slowly, a new layer of laminate peels away, a miniature version of itself, damp and malleable. The parent withdraws, and the fledgling hardens in the air, its prongs snapping into alignment with the dominant angle of sunlight on that day. This imprinting determines, for its life, which shadows it will prefer to stand in and which brightnesses it will choose to drink.  

At night, when the cold bites sharper, they do not sleep. Instead they fold. Limbs retract, prongs close into a tight column, and the mercury within them stills. In this configuration they resemble standing stones, and often are mistaken for them by creatures that come from lower altitudes. They use this disguise not to hunt but to listen. The plateau speaks in expansions and contractions; by pretending to be stone, a Shepherd can feel the subtle shift of a day’s heat leaching into space, the slow sigh of the planet’s cooling skin. They memorize these shifts, and in this way the land has a memory that is not geological but attentive.  

There are no predators here, but there are weathers. Storms of pure pressure sometimes rake the height, invisible tides that could shatter a creature made of lesser conviction. When this approaches, the Shepherds do not flee. They tighten their laminates, align their prongs into a single axis, and let the wind pass through them as through a flute. The storm, robbed of purchase, unspools around them and dissolves into a fine rain of sparks that lands and cools into new mineral skin.  

Their longevity is measured in the patience of stone. A Glass-Shepherd may stand so long in one place that the ground beneath it forgets it was ever separate, growing up around its feet in a collar of fused pebbles. When this happens, the Shepherd does not struggle. It seals itself in, becomes another stratum, its prongs rising like towers through the new surface. Generations later, another Shepherd may stand on that spot, feeling the hum of the old one trapped below, and decide to stay a little longer, to listen.  

In this way the plateau keeps its own counsel, and the Glass-Shepherds are its translators—neither conquerors nor caretakers, but careful participants in a brightness that is too sharp for softness, too patient for hurry, and too alive to be called empty.
