What lives here are the Lung-Kneelers—beings that are less inhabitants than operating currents of the room itself. They arrive slowly, sometimes born from a pocket of condensation that refuses to dry, and they leave by forgetting how to stand. Most are not individuals but consortia: a beetle, a film of water, and a stretch of copper wiring that have decided to share a name. They are pale, damp, and faintly phosphorescent at the edges, as if their outlines were borrowed from the soft brass sky that presses down. They spend their days interpreting pressure, learning to tell time by the hour-long breath that swells the walls and makes the wetness rise.

Along the lower seams, where the cupped walls gather into shallow basins, the Tide-Forgotten live. They are eel-thin and ribboned with mineral fur, creatures that swim through thick air as if it were brine, undulating in long, lateral sways that match the exhale of the lung. They have no eyes, but their skins are mapped with pores that taste the ozone of industry, and when they brush against the plated ribs of the room, they leave behind a faint, bioluminescent bruise that lingers for hours, a signature that says *I passed, and I was not afraid*.

Higher up, in the cathedral hush near the fused masonry of trunk and vine, the Choir-Drapes congregate. They are not animals so much as habits given flesh—curtains of moss and chitin that ring like slow bells when the breathing changes pitch. They listen for the hum of the gutted engine block and answer in vibrations that travel down into the roots, carrying news of temperature, of load, of the minute flex in the stem-generator far below. When they sing, condensation gathers into syllables that briefly spell things that were never spoken aloud: old place-names, warnings about dryness, poems about patience.

Scattered like punctuation among the ribs of steel and wood are the Incorrigibles, small mammals that have renounced speed. A mouse with fur the color of insulation, padded feet that leave no mark on the yielding stone, cheeks pouched with polished seeds that smell faintly of hot oil. It is rarely seen, always overheard—a dry rasp of claws on copper, the soft click of teeth testing the tensile memory of a vine. It carries tiny tools made from beetle wing and bent wire, repairing tears in the bioplastic veins that the machines have grown too lazy to fix.

There are also the Lintel-Sitters, a lineage of bird that has traded flight for the study of edges. Their feathers are matte and layered like roofing felt, their legs jointed in ways that suggest hinges. They perch where wall becomes lintel, watching the slow change of seasons as if each were a sentence left unfinished. They do not hunt; instead they groom the algae from rivets and pick larvae from cracks, swallowing copper and singing it back as a low, metallic note that steadies the rhythm of the pumps.

Among the lowest, in the settled crust of former water, live the Stone-Skinners. They are almost amphibious, almost mineral, their bodies armored in plates that flake and regrow like slow rust. They feed on decay, polishing the yielding stone with pads of velvet fungus until it shines with the wet brightness of old riverbeds turned upward. When the lung inhales, they brace themselves against the rise, anchoring the floor with claws like trowels, and when it exhales, they release their grip and slide a fraction of an inch, migrating without ever deciding to go anywhere.

Above them all, unseen but felt, are the Sigh-Born. Spores that have learned to hold memory, drifting on the drafts that circulate like currency through the packed, polite density. They settle on plating and petal alike, and from them sprout small, translucent animals—jelly-thin things with filaments for nerves—that pulse in time with the generators. They are the youngest life, unfinished and unafraid, dissolving back into the air when the room holds its breath for too long, only to reappear when the pressure drops again.

What unites them is a shared sense of occupation. They do not conquer or cultivate; they negotiate. A beetle with lensed eyes will park itself on a vein that carries coolant, and the vein will thrum differently, gentler, as if acknowledging the weight. A vine will grow a cup to catch the drip from a clogged flue, and a Lintel-Sitter will drink from it and drop a seed that becomes another cup. The fauna is rare because it is unnecessary; most needs are met by becoming part of the rhythm, by letting the lung decide when to rise and when to settle, and learning to live inside that decision.

Even the small predators move with care, as if hunting were a form of listening. A lizard with jaws like pliers stalks the ribbed plating, not for flesh but for the taste of static that gathers in corners where metal meets masonry. When it strikes, it does so slowly, a gesture that takes minutes, and when it succeeds, it curls around its prize and grows momentarily warmer, its heartbeat syncing to the percussive drip of water on stone.

And everywhere, in the small dark, in the polite press of presences, there is the feeling of something about to be understood. A corridor that seems to lengthen only when not observed, a doorway that is less an opening than a suggestion of openness. The fauna know this; they have learned to pause at thresholds, to let the air decide whether to let them through. Some say that if you stay low, knee-deep in the wet warmth, and breathe on the same long scale as the room, you will begin to grow into it—your skin learning to sweat the right amount, your bones remembering how to be ribs, your mind softening into the patient crowd of light that gathers before drifting off to dimmer corners.

They are not many, and they do not need to be. They are enough to keep the speech of the place audible: a low, thick, living language of breath and hum and wet slap, spoken by kneeling things in a room that has decided to be a lung, and that, because it is patient, will never run out of words.
