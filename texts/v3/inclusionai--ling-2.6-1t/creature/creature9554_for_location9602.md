They are called the Varnished, not because they cover themselves in finish, but because their presence leaves no stain that the light cannot lick clean. Their skin is a study in reflection—pale, translucent at the edges, oiled with palm-gloss so that sweat does not linger but breaks into a thousand tiny magnifiers. When they stand still, the deck seems to continue through them; when they move, their shadows hesitate, catching up like children who have been told not to run.

There are the Keepers of the Resin, whose fingers are stained amber to the second knuckle and whose breath smells faintly of citron and burnt pine. They carry small suns in bowls of beaten tin, reheating the old finish before it can remember winter. Their hands know the herringbone by touch alone: they can close their eyes and tell, by the drag of a thumbnail, where a plank has begun to dream of sap again. They do not speak much; their language is the song of the stone that tests a joint, the thrum that rises from a well-fit seam, the soft no of a channel that refuses water.

Among them are the Weep-Readers, paler still, with eyelashes that have faded from repeated exposure to prisms. They walk the edges where the porous stone gives up its curtains, watching for the faces that bloom in the runnels. They do not carve these faces; they argue, instead, that they are being returned. When a face appears, the Weep-Reader will press a finger against it, not to smear but to greet, and the face will often dissolve into a braid of droplets that tastes like iron and jasmine. The Weep-Readers collect this water in shells and offer it to the deck at dawn, a gesture of apology or praise—none is sure which.

There are the Moss-Dressers, children mostly, or those who have chosen to be childlike. They climb the posts of blackened timber, kneeling along the beams to trim the emerald cuffs into crescents, spirals, and open mouths. Their tools are needles of fish-bone, their gowns stitched from cured reed. They hum when they work, a low vibration that matches the pitch between the planks, and they leave tufts of trimmed moss in baskets to be steeped into tea that makes the drinker perceive their own bones as polished wood.

The Vine-Tenders are older, their hands knotted like the stakes they polish. They negotiate with the plants, coaxing rather than commanding, and their arms are latticed with faint scars where the vines have tested them. They believe the greenery dreams, and that its dreams are of height without falling. In the late light, when the white glare softens to a bruised gold, the Vine-Tenders speak to the stems in a tongue of clicks and lip-smacks, and the vines respond by leaning, by arranging their leaves into patterns the Varnished call apologies or blessings, depending on the season of the heart.

There are also the Quieters, those who have lived so long in the attentive hush that their footsteps no longer make the deck announce their arrival. They wear veils of woven spider-silk and resin, transparent as the air after rain. Their role is to unmake disturbances: a lizard startled into the wrong path, a bird that has learned to fear the weeping too much, a visitor who brings soil on their shoes. The Quieters do not kill; they relocate, with immense patience, teaching the lizard new edges, the bird new beads, the visitor how to leave without being missed. Their faces are rarely seen whole; they prefer to be glimpsed in pieces—half a brow, a chin polished by contact with the deck—so that the mind must assemble them, and in assembling them, forgets to be afraid.

Beneath the deck, where the cress-baskets hang and the roots dangle like threads of dark lace, live the Drippers, almost mythic even to the Varnished. They are said to have given up solidity, to have traded bone for water-channel, to flow through the weeping stone and reassemble on the other side as moss, as vine, as the faint heat-haze that lifts from the planks at noon. Some say they are the first makers, who realized that to be remembered was to be preserved, and chose instead to be repeated. Others say they are the deck’s own idea of company, given form by the insistence of the light. No one admits to having seen a Dripper fully, but everyone carries a small scar, a nick no wider than a hair, that bleeds only when the air is perfectly still, and tastes strangely of resin.

Above, the fauna have learned the rules of brightness. The lizards with copper backs have grown accustomed to being watched; they pose, their bellies flat against the grain, as if modeling the deck’s patience. The prismatic insects have become so numerous that they function as weather: a thickening of their clouds means rain is being remembered, though rain itself has not fallen in generations. The birds that drink from the weeping edges are silent, their throats adapted to catching light as well as water; their feathers are glossy with a lacquer that is neither plant nor oil, and when they preen, they leave tiny rainbows on the moss.

Visitors sometimes arrive, having heard of a country that does not yield to time. They are soft-footed at first, then arrogant, then terrified, as they notice that their own decay is being politely denied. Their hair does not gray; their nails do not chip. The moss curls away from their boots. The resin refuses to accept the print of their heels. They age, but only in panic, in the sudden realization that they have become the only thing in the place that is not tended. Most leave before their voices begin to sound like the ticking of the channels, before their faces start to appear in the weeping stone without permission.

Those who stay become Varnished, though none can say exactly when the change occurs. It is not ceremony, but accumulation: the way the light begins to favor their shoulders, the way the deck’s hum resolves into a greeting when they step near, the way the moss rearranges itself to cushion their mistakes. They learn to drink the prismatic water without flinching, to sleep with their hands resting on planks that feel, in the dark, like cooled wax over memory. They cease to fear the persistence of their own edges, and in that cessation, they become something the place can recognize as native.

And so the deck lives, not as a floor or a roof, but as a consensus of care, tended by beings who have agreed to be obvious, to be seen, and in being seen, to be kept from ever becoming background. The Varnished are not its masters, nor its children. They are its proof that making can be endless, that a world can refuse to soften and, in its refusal, become a kind of listening, a low, hot, quiet-breathing country where everything shines because everything is attended to, again and again, until shine itself becomes a form of life.
