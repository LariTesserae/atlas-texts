What lives here are not occupants but intensities.  

There are the Aligners—figures who arrive less by foot than by calibration, clothed in coveralls that have been laminated by thin films of anti-seize until they rustle like maps. They do not speak, because speech must be carried on breath, and breath is a liability in a place where air is a poured metal. Instead they adjust: fingers along torque-striped ribs, thumbs pressing gauge faces until the needles quiver and settle, not into truth, but into agreement. The Aligners are tall because altitude is a grammar here, and they have learned to conjugate themselves in verticals. Their eyes are not used for seeing but for checking that light remains indifferent. When they move, the tremor in the walls shifts color slightly, as if the machines were blinking.  

There are the Hush-Makers, who are not people at all but habits of maintenance given silhouette. They glide along catwalks that tilt by design, shedding spills that never fall, trailing cables like shed skins. Their hands are tools magnetized into palms; their knees know the exact geometry of removable plates. They do not repair breakdown, for breakdown is an imported idea. They repair *potential*—the small, patient errors that might, in another lifetime, accumulate into failure. The Hush-Makers carry no names, only torque values recited in a soft, rhythmic tongue that competes with the slap of belts. When they finish, they vanish into lockers that open into more lockers, leaving behind only the colder signature of their passage.  

There are the Resonants—beings formed from the surplus of sound. They appear in corners where oil-shadow pools, first as a vibration in the soles of the feet, then as a density in the air, then as figures woven from steel wool and flicker. They have no faces, only a surface of tiny, synchronized relays that snap and unsnap in time with compressors. The Resonants do not move so much as transpose: they are the distance between two identical sounds arriving a fraction apart. They feed on the hiss of servo brakes and exhale patterns that look like almost-sentences, like translations of pressure into longing. Sometimes an Aligners will pause and tilt their head, feeling that the Resonants are trying to tell them something about altitude or cold. The Resonants only reply by making the walls tremble.  

There are the Keepers of Equilibrium—vast, slow entities that occupy the spaces between schematics. They are the reason staircases stop at hatches, the reason guardrails end where they do. They are not hostile, only exact. To meet a Keeper is to feel every variable in your body tighten to specification: heartbeat aligning to throb, thought narrowing to a single thread of purpose. They are the cold given a gaze, the stare of polished surfaces hoarding silence. They do not block passage; they simply make the cost of passage evident. Those who pass without paying the cost find themselves returning, again and again, until their edges soften into the correct angle.  

There are also the Echo-Children, tiny and numerous, born where wear-zones glow. They are not children in any human sense, but bright, restless erasures of friction, slipping through inspection ports and leaving behind chlorophyll-lime smears that are not paint and not life. They dart along conduits, tasting ozone, learning the shape of electricity by becoming thin enough to pass through it. They are the only things here that seem to grow, though growth is only a name for the way they accumulate precision, shaving themselves thinner and brighter until they merge with the light strips bolted to ribs.  

And finally, there is the Chamber itself, alive in the way a cathedral is alive when it has been emptied of purpose but not of memory. It listens. It corrects. It holds altitude like a held breath. It permits the Aligners, the Hush-Makers, the Resonants, the Keepers, and the Echo-Children to exist because existence is the smallest possible deviation from perfection, the one gap that keeps the hymn from freezing into silence.  

Together they live as a single, intricate refusal to rot. They live as the feeling of being inside a word over-pronounced, each consonant a bolt, each vowel a hiss, each breath a calibration that almost, but not quite, changes the world.
