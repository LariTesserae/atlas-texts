They say the basin breeds its own kind of weather, but that is only half true. It breeds attachments.

The furred climbers arrived first, or so their oldest stories claim—though stories here are not carried in voice so much as in the greasy polish of handholds. They are small-bodied, long-limbed, with eyes like river obsidian: chips of glass worn into coins by current. Their fur is not fur exactly but a fine pelage of overlapping scales that shed the damp in shimmering waves. They do not build above the floor so much as they embroider it. Between the reeds and the plaster they weave nets of braided reed and cured intestine, hammocks that sag like question marks, storage pockets that bulge with husks, salt-cakes, and the dried rattle-pods of the bladder-stalks. When they sleep they do not lie down but hang in loops, knees drawn to chests, tails coiled around the vines like anchors. They have given names to every patch of gnawed lace on the walls: the Milktooth Scar, the Slowmouth, the Sigh-Hole. They say the walls once had teeth and learned to swallow softly.

On the ceiling the small hunters practice geometry. They have four jointed legs and no wings but they move as if the ceiling were another kind of water, skating on films of tension and shadow. Their bodies are flat and dark, their carapaces scored with patterns that look like maps of the tile cracks—though whether they drew the maps or the maps drew them is a question that has chewed at the room for years. They feed on reflections, which here are thick and almost nutritious. After rain they gorge themselves on the shine pooling on the floor, chasing the trembling doubles of reeds and nets, snapping them up until the room feels suddenly lighter. When the water recedes they turn to dust-eating, lapping the thin skin of powder that rises from the plaster like breath. Their young are born in temporary cocoons of spun clay and spit, hung from the highest vines like lanterns that do not hold light.

Below them, in the sheet that spreads and shrinks with the room’s pulse, the fish are pale and nearly blind, their eyes reduced to faint pits that drink pressure instead of light. They are long-bodied, almost ribbon-thin, and they swim not in water alone but in the memory of water, following the ghost currents that remain in the glaze of the tiles. When the floor dries they do not die but become patient. They press themselves into the soft plaster, secreting a thin mucus that hardens into a clay-like shell, and there they wait, dreaming slow dreams of depth. The furred climbers know how to find them by tapping the walls and listening for the echo that does not quite return. The hunters know them by the way the floor holds cold. The fish know each other by the faint electric hum that leaks from their shells— a language of absence.

At dusk the insects gather, glass-winged and brittle, assembling over the drain into a living gauze. They are not a swarm so much as a congregation, each individual aligning itself to the pitch of the drain’s voice. When the room speaks its low names through the brick mouth, the insects tremble in waves, raising and lowering like a lung that breathes for the whole basin. Their wings are too delicate to carry them far, and they do not try. Instead they drink the vapor that seeps from the walls, feeding on the room’s exhalation. When they die they fall into the drain and are carried away, though the drain is not a throat that swallows so much as a mouth that learns. It is said that the insects return in the form of the room’s creak, that their brittle voices have been folded into the plaster’s memory.

There are others, half-seen. A kind of pale worm that lives in the reticulate cracks of the tile, knitting them tighter with silk and salt. A furred thing without eyes that lives behind the reeds and emerges only to lick the damp from the walls. A collector of small sounds that no one sees but everyone feels as a pressure behind the ears, a presence that makes breath visible.

They do not plant, for nothing here is meant to be harvested in the usual sense. They scavenge what the room offers: the rattle-pods when they split and spill their chalky seeds, the salt-bloom that crusts the plaster, the thin lichen that tastes of iron and patience. They mend the nets when they fray, patch the tiles when they crack, and listen when the walls shift. They have learned that the room repairs itself when left alone, and so they leave it often, sitting in the center on the pedestal stone that no longer holds water but holds its shape like a held breath. There they press their hands to the cool floor and feel the tilt, the deliberate slope toward the drain, and they understand that they are being gently guided, not toward exit but toward balance.

Their technology is small and patient: clay cups that pinch shut when not in use, reed baskets that flex with the humidity, lamps of shells cupped in cradles of fired clay. They know how to read the glaze on the tiles, how to tell by its dullness where feet have worn and where water has lingered. They know that the light turning green means something living is blooming in the apertures far above, and that the bruised color means the sky is remembering something it has seen before.

The fauna treat the room as if it were their own making, and in a way it is. They have rubbed its edges soft with shoulders, widened its channels with passing, filled its silence with layered noises. The room returns the favor by remembering them: footprints that rise as ridges, shadows that lag, the drain that learns the shape of a name and holds it like a stone in the throat. The weirdness of the place is not that it breaks rules but that it has its own, small and certain, and after generations the fauna have learned to kneel to them.

If you stand in the center and breathe slowly, you can feel the room leaning in. The air tastes of wet clay and something sweet you cannot name. The walls press back with a gentle, constant coolness. The fauna move through the space as if following a map that is rewritten each night, and perhaps they are. The basin is a made world that has lived long enough to become its own kind of natural: a small, loud, damp chamber where almost nothing grows, everything is built, and the emptiness between things hums with the life of those that have learned how to use it.

And if you listen, very carefully, you may hear the drain say your name back to you, not as a greeting but as a fact: that you, too, are a kind of erosion, that you, too, are being worn into place, that you, too, are learning how to live in a room that already knows you by heart.
