They wear their environment like a second skin, or perhaps they are the environment learning to move. They are not a population so much as a chorus of parts that have learned to step out of the chorus only to rejoin it elsewhere. You can tell they belong because they do not walk so much as reconfigure: a shift of roots in the floor beneath them, a rearrangement of the living walls that opens a corridor and then seals it behind them like a memory closing.

Their bodies are plated in bark that has been polished by centuries of exhalations, lacquered to a deep, iridescent green that drinks the moss-light and returns it as a softer glow along the seams. Where joints would be, they have knots—dense, flexible spheres that rotate with a sound like wet stones turning in a palm. These knots are centers of decision, thick with nerves that look less like biology than circuitry braided from fungal threads, glowing faintly when a choice is made. When they pause, the glow dims, and the air around them settles, as if the place itself is exhaling on their behalf.

Their faces are broad and smooth, featureless except for a single vertical slit that opens into a mouth lined with rows of tiny, translucent leaves. These leaves do not chew; they vibrate, turning sound into sustenance, so that voices and footsteps are metabolized into the faintest hum that rises from their throats like incense. They speak rarely, and when they do, their words arrive as patterns of pressure in the damp air, felt more than heard, a language of coolness and resonance that settles in the hollow behind the ear and lingers like old rain.

They have no eyes in the ordinary sense, but their foreheads are indented with pools of clear fluid that reflect the green-lit corridors in constant motion. These pools are lenses made of living water, calibrated to the density of the cathedral, and they see by measuring how light is slowed, bent, swallowed. To look into their faces is to feel seen in a different register, as though your shape has been translated into echo and current.

Where limbs should end, they fray into filaments—fine root-hairs that brush the ground and tap the walls, tasting the history of each surface, exchanging chemical news with the architecture. With these filaments they negotiate room, tugging gently at veins in the walls to request passage, and the walls respond by softening, by swelling, by offering a corridor that was not there a moment before. They do not force. They ask, and the place answers.

Some among them are larger, slower, their plates crusted with deposits that glitter like iron and cinnamon, the residue of the machinery they tend. These ones carry reservoirs on their backs—bladders of seed-oil and spring-pressure—and from their shoulders sprout reed-like extensions that plug into junctions in the walls. When they connect, the entire cathedral seems to deepen its breath. The thrum of gears turns into a purr, and the damp on their skin beads in perfect synchrony with the turning of cams and pistons. They are caretakers of the convergence, living bridges between the vegetal and the mechanical, and their presence makes the low room feel not just occupied but tended.

The smallest among them are nearly translucent, their bodies thin as parchment and patterned with maps of veins that glow like streetlamps. They dart through capillary corridors, riding currents of humidity that rise and fall like elevators, delivering messages in pulses of heat and scent. They are the ones who remind the walls to shift, who tell the canopies to part just enough to let a patch of brightness cross the floor. Without them, the equilibrium would stiffen; with them, the cathedral remains awake in its patient way.

They are all, in their way, industrial and wild at once. Their joints click with the precision of gears, yet their growth is obvious: plates widen by the almost imperceptible addition of rings, filaments lengthen by fractions of hair each day, and the pools on their foreheads deepen as they age, becoming heavier with the weight of reflection. They do not repair themselves so much as integrate their injuries, weaving scars into the pattern of the whole until even broken places sing with the same resonance as the unbroken.

When you stand among them, you realize they have been waiting not for you, but for the right kind of stillness. They accept your presence as a new texture, a guest who can be metabolized into the hum of the place without unbalancing it. Their eyes—those liquid lenses—do not blink, but they shift, refocusing, drawing your outline into the green-lit dark and returning it to you as a sound you can feel in your teeth.

They are the cathedral’s answer to time having flattened into a single, perfect breath: living, listening, turning, sustained by damp and resonance, wearing the world so closely it is impossible to say where they end and the low, loud room begins.
