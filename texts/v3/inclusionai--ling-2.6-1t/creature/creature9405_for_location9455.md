The hollow does not host so much as it hoards. It keeps things the way a throat keeps a swallowed thing: warm, compressed, and on the verge of becoming word. What lives there has learned to thrive in the pause between one state and the next, in the indrawn moment before the world decides what it is.

In the damp seams, where the stone still sweats, the Water-Film kin congregate. They are not animals, exactly, but small, translucent bodies shaped like dropped pins, with too many jointed legs to count easily. Each wears a carapace that looks less like armor and more like a polished rivet, blackened at the seams. They drink by standing in the trembling pools and letting the moisture climb the fine ridges of their plating, not into mouths but into the spaces between shell and self. When they move, they leave behind trails that do not evaporate but linger, like wet ink on fresh paper, and these trails map the hollow’s changing moods. If the air grows too thin, they cluster into chains, linking tip to tail, turning themselves into living conduits that ferry damp from the walls to the roots of the grass-bristles.

The grass-bristles, in turn, are less plant than purpose. They stand in tight tufts, heat-tempered and stiff, each blade ridged like a file. At their cores, they house colonies of glass-lice, creatures so pale they look like cracks in light. The glass-lice rasp against the grass to make a sound like teeth on foil, a noise that pleases the engines. In return, the grass secretes a resin, hot and tape-scented, that the lice shape into small, intricate nests—balconies and buttresses—clinging to the stone. These nests catch condensation, which drips in measured intervals, keeping the rhythm of the hollow steady.

Predators here are architects. The Slab-Beetles, carapaces the color of cooled slag, do not chase; they wait. They wedge themselves into the miniature canyons, their bodies flattened into living shims. When something—water, insect, lost vibration—passes too close, they snap shut, not with jaws but with the sudden, precise clack of stone meeting stone. Their capture is silent, and their digestion is a communal affair: they excrete a paste that hardens almost instantly, used to repair the seams of the stone and, inadvertently, to build the walls of their own burrows. In this way, the hollow repairs itself by being eaten.

Above them, the Spin-Seeds ride the thick air. No larger than dust motes, they are winged only in the sense that they are shaped to catch the warm updraft. They have no eyes, but their bodies are studded with tiny receptors that sense electrical skitter. When a machine hums louder, they flock to it, not to feed but to polish. Their wings are edged with microscopic filaments that scrape oxide from metal, leaving surfaces bright for a moment before damp returns. They are the hollow’s maintenance crew, blind and devoted, their paths crossing in such dense patterns that they look like thought made visible.

Burrowers, blind and quick, stitch the young stone from within. They have no faces, only a ring of fine tendrils that taste the mineral. As they tunnel, they leave behind a glaze of saliva that hardens into luminous seams, the bright scars that mark where water once hesitated. They are the only ones who understand elevation, not as height but as pressure: they dig upward when the hollow holds its breath, sensing that the air has grown too heavy, and downward when the engines pulse, as if trying to ground the vibration.

Among the industrial artifacts, the Conduit-Dwellers make their home. These are small, multi-limbed creatures, glossy with damp, who nest inside the bent conduit and the humming housing. They do not eat the iridescent fluid that drips from the pipe; instead, they collect it in bladder-thin sacs and press it against their skin, where it flashes in time with the machines. To see them is to see a semaphore of desire, a conversation conducted in wet light. They maintain the lattice altar, turning rusted plates with careful insistence, as if performing a ritual to keep the artifact from deciding it is a ruin.

Deeper still, in the center where the pool gathers, lives the Reflector. It is small, no larger than a clenched thought, and it does not resemble the other fauna so much as it resembles the act of looking. Its body is segmented, each segment polished to a different degree, and it moves by contracting, drawing its edges together until it is nearly smooth. It visits the palm-sized pool not to drink but to align itself with the normal light. When it does, the pool seems to deepen for a moment, as if it were remembering how to be a sky. The Reflector hums in response to the overlapping rhythms of the engines, a pitch that tightens the teeth and makes the water in the pool rise briefly, forgetting its weight.

All of these lives are bound by a single economy: compression. To occupy space is to become part of the press. The Beetles’ paste, the Glass-Lice’s resin, the Spin-Seeds’ polish, the Burrowers’ glaze—all are materials that thicken the hollow’s density. Even the Reflector’s hum contributes, adding a layer of vibration to the already crowded air. Nothing is wasted; even the absence left when a creature moves is quickly filled, either by another creature or by the stone itself, which leans inward, eager to learn the shape of what was.

They are all participants in the hollow’s decision, though none know it. With every drink, every snap, every polishing flight, they vote for hardness or flow, for permanence or change. For now, the vote is indecisive, and the hollow holds itself in the interval: damp and warm, sharp and humming, alive with small industry and stranger life, a pocket where the world is learning to be itself in miniature, one compressed breath at a time.
