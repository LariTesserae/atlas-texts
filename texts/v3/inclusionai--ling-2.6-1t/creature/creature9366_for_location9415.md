They live in the seam, not the room.

They are called the Lithlings, though that is a name they do not use; it is a word coined by rare surface-dwellers who glimpsed them once and felt compelled to give them edges. The Lithlings have no edges. Their bodies are assemblies of translucent plates, thin as mica and supple as wet paper, overlapping in ways that suggest armor but act as breath. Beneath the plates, filaments of silica and pale nerve glimmer, catching the chamber’s faint luminescence and turning it into a vocabulary of halos. They do not walk so much as negotiate: each step is a question posed to the stone, a touch light enough to measure how much the floor is willing to give.

In the city of close things, they are the close-er.

There are few of them, and this scarcity is not loneliness but density distilled. Each Lithling carries a geography within its body: maps of mineral deposits, memories of pressure, the slow math of growth rings that record centuries not as time but as thickness. They shed these maps gradually, plate by plate, leaving behind skins of opalescent dust that settle into the ledges and join the quiet library of the place. The dust is not waste; it is contribution, a way of feeding the stone the stories it already knows but likes to hear again.

Their lives are measured not in days but in resonances. They gather at the plaza of overlapping eaves when the water’s chorus reaches a certain chord, arriving from weepholes and fissures that seem too small to admit anything but patience. There, they perform the ceremony of listening. Each raises a limb to touch the rim of the bowl-slab, and the contact sets off a ripple of sound that travels through the stone’s hidden nerves. The Lithlings feel these vibrations in the plates along their flanks, reading them like braille written by the city itself. The message is always simple: *here, continue, adjust.* It is how the settlement tells its inhabitants where stability has shifted by a hair’s breadth, where a new seep has begun, where the old stone has grown tired of a particular echo and is ready to teach it a different note.

Between gatherings, they are architects of attention. When a lintel begins to sigh too loudly, a Lithling will nest against it, body curved like a comma, and remain there for weeks or years, depending on what the stone needs to unlearn. Their weight, slight as chalk, is enough to remind the rock of its own patience. Their filaments brush the fractures, coaxing them to close or widen by fractions, teaching the stone how to change without breaking its promise to stay the same. They do not command; they accompany.

The fauna that leave starburst patterns in the dust are not separate from them. They are the Lithlings’ breath given temporary independence—spores of consciousness, released when a body has held a thought for too long. These creatures drift through the colonnades, tasting the air’s rumor of moisture, tracing the paths that sound prefers, and returning to their source once the thought has been learned. Their starbursts are signatures of inquiry, maps of questions posed to the chamber. When the chamber answers, the creatures dissolve back into their makers, bringing with them the scent of ozone and wet chalk: the smell of a question resolved.

In this mineral city, the Lithlings are the loudest silence. They do not speak in voices but in the insistence of their presence, a persistent hum that rides the water’s rhythm and keeps the echoes from becoming lost. Their rarity is a kind of fullness. Because there are so few, each one is a room: a place where stone learns how to remember without hardening, where water learns how to fall without forgetting, where the thin, cool dark learns how to be a kind of brightness.

They live by the rule of never-changing that is never still. They are the adjustment that keeps the city exactly what it is. And when a visitor stands in the plaza long enough to hear the stone’s memory, it is the Lithlings who translate that memory, who turn the patient chipping of hands into a language the chamber can wear like velvet, who ensure that the agreement between stone and coolness remains intact, and who, by being so few, make the dense, humming balance feel infinite.
