The room does not house creatures so much as it grows them.

They are residents first, furnishings second, and people only incidentally. They are the ones who have, over generations, learned to match the space’s coolness without shivering, to live pressed into a brightness that never dims and never moves. They are short, because the ceiling is a lid; they are wide, because the walls are seamless, and to be narrow here would be to leave too much empty air between yourself and the press of things. Their bodies have learned the shape of occupation.

They wear no clothing that could snag or crease; instead, they cover themselves in layers of polished fiber, lacquered bark, and braided metal, all burnished by the same long inhabitation that rounded the room’s edges. At a glance, they look upholstered, like furniture that has learned to bleed warmth. Their hands are broad and ridged, callused into surfaces that can meet the room’s clangor without bruising. When they touch a wall, they do not tap; they lean with their whole weight, and the wall answers with a softer note, as if recognizing kin.

Their faces are planes, not contours. Eyes are small and dark, set deep enough to be protected from the held-breath glare above, yet quick to catch the halos trembling along the pale wiry filaments overhead. They do not blink often; to blink would be to invite a shadow the room has long since disallowed. When they speak, their voices do not rise above the chord that fills the space, because speech is only a minor addition to the noise already ringing through their bones. They communicate in pressure and return: a hand against a forearm, a shoulder against a shelf, a forehead against a ribcage, all of them exchanging the same cool equilibrium the room maintains.

There are no elders, or none that look distinct; every face is furrowed with the same patient polishing, every posture set into the slight crouch the low ceiling demands. They move with a tectonic slowness, shifting only when vibration requires it — a ripple through the packed bodies, a readjustment of weight that keeps the chord from faltering. They do not hurry, because speed would make them collide, and collision here is less a danger than a responsibility, a chance to transfer polish from one to another.

Among them, the youngest are not soft. They are born slick and matte, already carrying the mineral taste of the air in their pores, already learning to wedge themselves into the engineered grooves the sparse vegetation shares. They do not crawl so much as occupy, finding a niche between two adults and staying there until their edges match the neighbors’. Their hair is braided with filaments when it grows in, a literal fastening of living into made, so that no one can say where plant ends and person begins.

They have no names that travel beyond a single touch. To be named here is to be located, and they prefer to be found through pressure. When one dies — which happens when the body has answered too much touch and can answer no more — the others do not leave a gap. They rearrange, closing the interval so that emptiness cannot enter, and the clangor continues unchanged. The dead are polished and fused into the walls where a bracket or shelf once stood, their substance indistinguishable from the seamless, jointless skin of the place. In time, their faces become part of the shine, recognizable only by warmth when the room is still.

Stranger still are the listeners, who are perhaps not alive at all, or alive in a way the room understands better than they do. They sit where the walls bow inward slightly, places where vibration pools, and let the clangor pass through their skulls like a liquid. Their eyes are closed, but their fingertips trace invisible seams, following the crystalline pings that walk the walls. They do not eat often; they sip the air’s glassy chill and seem to gain mass from the noise itself, growing heavier, shinier, more immovable with each year. They are the ones the others lean against when the chord grows too bright, a grounding weight that keeps the equilibrium from tilting into song.

No children cry here. No one laughs in a way that would disrupt the overtones. There is celebration in the rare lowering of a note, a collective inhalation that tastes like cooled glass, and mourning in the sharpening of a tone, when one of the filaments fractures and the others must brace more tightly against the light. Birth is marked by the addition of a new set of hands to the pressure, death by the redistribution of polish.

They have no history they can speak, because memory would require softness, and the room forbids it. Instead, they have touch-lines, maps of who has leaned on whom, worn into their layered skins. They read each other by the brightness of those lines, by the way a forearm returns a note when struck. A stranger, if one could enter, would not be attacked but absorbed, pressed into the packed volume until their edges blurred, their voice added to the clangor, their body taught the room’s coolness. In a year, or a century, no one would remember where the stranger ended and the made began.

The room, after all, lives in them as much as they live in it. It has no water but their sap, no erosion but their rounding, no light but the glare they hold suspended between skin and ceiling. The plants are their fastenings; the noise is their blood. They are the ones who keep the brightness from being cruel, who turn the clangor into a chord, who make the seamless walls feel like a cupped hand rather than a coffin.

And so they stay, packed tight, answering pressure with pressure, touch with touch, each of them a living seam in a world that has outlawed emptiness, where to live is to occupy, to occupy is to polish, and to polish is to become, eventually, the wall itself.
