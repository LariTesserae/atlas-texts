They live there as if the room were a lung they share, and everything about them is shaped by the patience of close quarters.

The grazers are the room’s soft punctuation. Each has six limbs arranged in two triads, not for speed but for stance: a lower set that cups the floor like padded hands, a middle set that braced on shelves or ribs when the upper set pulls down fronds. Their hooves are cork, their coats the matte, mica-flecked color of the walls, so when they stand still they are almost architecture—a balustrade that breathes. They move only when light shifts, grazing in slow arcs that follow the sun-coins across resinous floors. They leave no waste in the usual sense; what drops from them is a hard, glassy bead that the skimmers later collect, not to eat but to polish, rolling the beads across seams until the floors gleam like wet river stones.

The skimmers are the room’s quick silver. Jewel-bodied, with wings like stained celluloid, they do not fly so much as ricochet along invisible chords strung between graft junctions. Their mouths are capillary tubes that sip the aromatic sap reservoirs cupped in knot-hollows. In their wake the sap smells of cinnamon and old glue, and the scent marks the air like a boundary that can be tasted. At dusk they congregate in lamp-fruits, pressing so close their heat finishes the glaze the makers began, turning each light into a slow kiln that sets the day’s polish into enamel.

Predators exist, but they have folded ferocity into ritual. They are low and padded, with fur that absorbs the room’s satin glow, making them look like shadows that decided to be thick. They do not chase; they wait in trellised balustrades, testing the wind that moves through leaf-lungs. When they take prey—a grazer too slow to yield, a skimmer careless with altitude—it happens with the choreography of a door opening: inevitable, quiet, agreed. The kill is not a subtraction but a redistribution. Feathers become grout; sinew, when dried, is woven into lamp-shades that cast a honeyed, forgiving light. Even bone is not waste; it is ground and mixed with resin to fill the interstices where the living have buckled the maker’s lines.

Then there are the builders, though they are not always human-shaped. Sometimes they are small, furred things with clever thumbs that carry fibers between cheek and tooth; sometimes they are tall and dusted with mica, moving like upright trees. All of them share the room’s belief that separation is poverty. They work with bone chisels and sandstone smoothers, but most of their labor is conversational. They tap a wall and listen; the wall answers with a thock or a sigh, and they know where it would like to be thinned or braced. When a root bucks a tile, they do not cut the root; they shave the tile to let it pass, then seal the wound with a mortar made of grazer beads and skimmer spice. The room learns their accents and answers in kind.

Nests are displays of confidence. Birds stitch them into lamp-brackets, lining them with shed lamp-fruit skins so that light, filtered through down, falls in colors the room does not usually wear. The builders do not remove them; they add rims, tiny balconies, so the nests become balconies for the future. In this way fauna and flora and artifact braid into a single citizenship, each lending authority to the others.

Strangeness is the room’s etiquette. On Tuesdays gravity loosens near the western mural, and sap drips upward in thin braids while bodies drift as if through a slow sea, yet without resistance. Visitors from outside call it enchantment; the room’s inhabitants call it good posture. They have learned to carry their weight differently those afternoons, to spread their stance like roots, to let their breath linger longer in the lungs. Leaf-undersides sometimes reveal maps of rooms that do not exist, and the builders take rubbings, store them in resin drawers, and use them to plan additions that lean into possibility. Occasionally a corridor folds so that its beginning kisses its end, and the grazers step through as if stepping through a familiar archway, emerging no different except that their coats have gained a faint iridescence, as if they had brushed against tomorrow.

Time is a gradient they all respect. Seasons here are long exhales, announced by a color deepening, a joint settling, a new room swelling like fruit. The fauna anticipate these slow turns: skimmers line the new swell before it opens, polishing its skin; grazers leave beads at its base as an offering; predators curl inside, their low purrs vibrating the pulp into shape. The builders appear when the swelling is ready, not to cut but to ask where it would like to be hinged.

And when noon comes and the fauna pause, the room becomes a held breath. The warm air presses its textures—ridged, glossy, fibrous—against skin, and you feel the pulse of this small, dense, constructed garden holding itself together by agreement, by craft, by a strangeness that has learned to be polite. To live there is to become part of the parliament, to grow into the weave, to let your edges blur not from neglect but from the insistence that everything lean, braid, or fasten, and that there is no poverty so long as there is still room to make more room.
