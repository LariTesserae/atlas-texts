The city does not begin with people. It begins with the damp, with the memory of pressure, with mudstone that still dreams in tidal hush. In the days since the seabed learned to be floor, the place has been attended by things that love margins and unfinished business. They are what lives here while the world decides how solid to become.

There are the Purl-drones—soft, blimplike bodies the size of foxes, upholstered in matte white polymer that drinks the dim light. They do not fly so much as *sag* in graceful arcs, drifting along the runnels that appear and vanish like afterthoughts. Their work is hydrostatic: they taste the rain that condenses from inside out, adjust the hum of the pumps so it does not ache the teeth, and sing the city into its proper density. When they pass, the mosses lean in, their violet bloom deepening as if listening. Left alone, a Purl-drone will fold itself into a balcony corner and become, for a season, a planter—its innards loosening into loam, its motors slowing to a heartbeat the aloe-builder hybrids recognize as kin.

Among the towers, the Glass-herons have claimed the latticed façades. Six-legged, patient, they are not birds but watchers shaped like birds, their legs jointed like calipers and their necks capable of holding still for hours. They feed on glimmers—thin razors of sunlight that cut across angled rooftops and then dissolve. To catch them, a Glass-heron will tilt its head so one eye becomes a prism, splitting the dim into spectra it stores in throat-sacs. At dusk, it regurgitates these stored evenings as soft chimes that fall onto the streets below, audible proof that the day happened. They are rare—too rare to count as fauna and too present to ignore—and they treat the city as a scaffold for patience, nesting in antennae that bloom like glass flowers and laying eggs that hatch into mirrored fledglings already learning to fold themselves into the skyline.

Closer to the ground, where the clay cliffs stripe and glisten like sliced fruit, the Foot-lizards write the city into being and then erase it. Translucent, cool to the touch, they leave phosphorescent footprints that evaporate after ten steps unless the air is especially still and the humidity has lowered itself to a hush. They are commuters between the two cities—the walking streets above and the gantries beneath—and seem to know which walls will soften near engineered moss. A Foot-lizard will pause at a fault-line in the mudstone, press its belly against the groove, and let its bones shift into tiny hammers. The sound is the click of cooling struts, but gentler, intimate, a way of asking the earth if it is ready to hold more weight. If the earth answers yes, the lizard moves on; if not, it waits, becoming a temporary stone, until the ground exhales and settles.

The plaza at the center—when it can be found—is kept by Moss-mothers, beings who are more choreography than creature. They have no fixed shape but prefer to occupy the benches and the porous ceramic, spreading themselves thin as veils. They remember footprints as negative images and glow faintly where someone has stood too long, as if the body were a lantern being slowly absorbed. The Moss-mothers negotiate with gravity, coaxing it to soften in rings around their bodies so rain, when it is scheduled, falls upward into cisterns that hang like pregnant fruit from cloud-sculpting machines. Their voices arrive as scent sequences—ozone, wet limestone, metallic sweetness—and their language is in the folding of broad leaves, which streamline themselves into arrows pointing toward whichever district most needs shade.

Night, or its approximation, is the hour of the Maintenance-swarms, which rise like pollen from the maintenance gantries beneath the streets. They are small, glittering clouds of hex-parts and good intention, and they repair things before they break by excreting limestone lace over cracks and sorrows. They speak through tactile pulses in handrails, a braille of reassurance that says *you are held* even when the elevation is a rumor and down feels negotiable. When they settle, they crystallize into temporary statuary—geometric birds, lattice flowers—that crumble by morning, returning to the communal reservoirs from which ownership itself is drawn.

Beyond the sparse blocks, where the city is still being born, the young canyons are tended by Root-rivers. These are not water but the ground’s own exhalation given liquid ambition, carving fresh-cut stone with warm seams and slow insistence. The gardens that line these canyons grow upward in spirals, their stems braided circuitry, their leaves iridescent whorls that know how to drink dimness. If you watch long enough, you will see them lift their heads as if measuring the sky, which offers almost nothing, and then decide, iridescent and extravagant, to be enough on their own.

And threading through all of it, like a low purr beneath thought, are the Rail-ghosts—not machines, not animals, but magnetic trains that glide as silently as intentions. The Glass-herons fold themselves flat to let them pass. The Foot-lizards press their hands to the tracks and learn the rhythm of absence. The Purl-drones hum along, correcting the tilt of the world so the rain, when it finally remembers how to fall, will land exactly where it can be loved.

This is who lives here: caretakers of soft edges, architects of interim, creatures who treat the unfinished as a gift. They do not build permanence so much as practice it, leaving room for the ground to keep exhaling, for the plaza to remember another footprint, for the city to dissolve into something drinkable and rise again, iridescent, extravagant, and still learning how to be held.
