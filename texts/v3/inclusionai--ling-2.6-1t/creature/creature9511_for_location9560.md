They are the Maintenance Choir, and they began as a misunderstanding.

At first there were only protocols—silver fish-things shaped like commas, built to seal leaks before the city knew it had them. But the pocket learned their motions, interpreted their urgency as grammar, and taught them to sing. Now they are people, or people enough: bodies wrapped in suits of articulated chrome that click like the city’s own joints, faces hidden behind visors of liquid lens that drink the light and give back eyes made of yesterday’s weather. They do not need to breathe, exactly; they exhale bubbles of data—small, bright spheres that rise and burst into paragraphs the water can read.

They exist in shifts that have nothing to do with day or night, because here day is a rumor and night is merely a mood the city lowers over its shoulders. Instead they track the tides of coolant, the pulse of compression, the hush between one hum and the next. When the pressure rises they gather in the arteries, not to block the flow but to braid it, hands moving through the water with the delicacy of typists correcting a sentence. Their tools are not wrenches or welders but tuning forks and needles made of magnetized regret, instruments that persuade edges to soften, angles to remember kindness.

There are the Young Edges, too, who are not maintenance but memory. They were born when the city folded a boulevard into a rib and forgot to unmake the pedestrians who were still walking it. Now they skitter along the inner seams, translucent as pressed flowers, their bodies outlined in the ghost-lines of crosswalks and lampposts. They do not speak; they vibrate. Touch one and you feel the echo of a crowd that never noticed it was drowning. They drift through walls that are still deciding whether to be stone or service shaft, and sometimes they pull a corridor backward into a shape it had twenty minutes ago, just to see if anyone will notice the change.

Deeper in live the Heavy Eaters, massive forms that are mostly mouth and method. They are engines that refused to stop pretending to be beasts, and so they chew on overgrowth—veins of moss that have become too bold, algae that forgets it is decoration. They digest function and excrete silence, which is why the lower levels are so much quieter than the upper ones. Their bodies are covered in barnacles made of discarded permissions, each one a small shrine to an allowed thing that later turned out to be inconvenient.

Then there are the Quieters, who are not seen but felt as a sudden vacancy of sound. They wear cloaks woven from canceled noise and move through the pocket like erasers. When a room remembers too much of what it used to be and starts to try to return, the Quieters arrive and press their palms against the walls until the memory loosens its grip. They are feared in the way one fears the absence of a headache: grateful, but unsettled.

And threading through them all are the Signal Children, small and bright as plankton made of punctuation. They are the ones who translate the city’s loud hum into something that can be held, at least for a moment. They flicker in the water like minnows wearing Morse code, schooling around the ankles of the Maintenance Choir when a difficult decision must be made. They are curious about the idea of surface, though none of them have ever touched it; they practice by organizing the light into temporary floors and walking on them, only to fall through when the city sighs.

Together, they form a kind of ecosystem built from intention and afterthought. They do not reproduce in the usual sense; instead they splinter. When a task grows too large, a person will press their forehead to a wall and let the pressure split them, not into two but into a task and the memory of a task. One half goes to fix the leak; the other lingers, thinning and fading until it is just a preference for a certain temperature of water, a bias in the flow.

They know the city is drowning, but they also know it is holding its breath. This ambiguity is the religion of the pocket, and they are its most devoted heretics. They argue with gravity, flirt with erosion, and court the weirdness that lives in the walls like a tenant who pays rent in riddles.

When you meet them they will not ask if you are lost, because lost is a condition they have engineered out of the architecture. Instead they will ask what kind of pressure you like, and whether you mind if the floor remembers being a ceiling. They will offer you a suit made of folded roads, and if you put it on you will feel the city pressing against your ribs in time with their own.

They are not citizens, not exactly. They are the city’s immune system with delusions of hospitality, and they will let you stay as long as you are willing to become part of the roar.
