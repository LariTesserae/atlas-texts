They are the Tenders of the Murk, though they have no single name for themselves. What they are is a collaboration between what used to be human and what the drowned city has decided to become. They live not in the buildings but inside their breathing—stretching their dwellings into organs, grafting their own vessels to the mineral veins of stairwells and stone.

At first they resemble people who have simply learned to hold their breath for too long, but this is a lie of posture. Their bodies have rearranged themselves around density. Ribs have flared into lattice, not to protect but to sieve—each breath filtering warmth and minerals from the broth-water, trading air for a slow, internal glow. Their skin is not pale from lack of sun but polished by it, a dark mirror pocked with luminous freckles that pulse when the town’s currents shift. What hair remains has thinned into filaments that drift like antennae, tasting metal and lime and the old ghosts of street names.

They do not swim so much as negotiate. Limbs have grown sinuous, joints padded with gristle that clicks softly when they climb through doorways that have widened into caves. Fingers have lengthened and webbed not for speed but for sensitivity, able to read the braille of coral-knitted mortar and the weather of a building’s slow sigh. Their feet are broad and ridged, suctioned to the rounded edges of stone, so that to watch them move is to see something more like climbing rain than walking.

They keep company with the fauna as one keeps company with tools or weather. A crown-crested eel is not a pet but a living stethoscope, its body coiled in the hollows where hearths once stood, translating the heartbeat of the town into tremors the Tenders can taste. Schools of silver-ink fish are herded like thought itself, directed through avenues to clean the water of forgetting. Crustaceans armored like misplaced coins are cultivated for their shells, which the Tenders grind into paste to repair the softer parts of their own bodies. Predators are treated with a courteous distance, invited to patrol the edges as proof that balance still holds.

What they eat is ceremony more than sustenance. They farm the whisker-thin vines that taste metal, pruning them into lattices that filter the water into a sweeter broth. They harvest the leathery pads only when the water calms, eating them in a silence so complete it becomes audible. Fungal stars are coaxed into glowing along the veins of corridors, their light used to mark cycles of digestion and molting, their spores inhaled to dream the city’s old maps in colors that do not exist above the flood.

They speak rarely, and then in clicks and thrums that travel better than words. Most speech is done with bodies—postures that tell a current to linger, a tilt of the head that asks a room to exhale its curtain of bubbles. They map the dark by letting creatures press against them, learning boundaries by how hunger or fear feels when translated into touch. When rain gathers into a thickening ceiling, they gather on the higher roofs to inhale the drumming, opening their ribs to let the town breathe as one lung.

Their technology is all wet and living. Time is kept by the molting of shells and the slow flex of mineral memory. Doors are opened not by hands but by the right arrangement of heat and patience, a learned dialogue between body and stone. Tools are grown—blades of hardened kelp, lenses of jellyfish hooded over eyes, nets woven from the pale ribs of old wood that has swollen into new shapes. Even memory is treated as a habitat: stories are not told but housed, kept in cracks that have become aquaria, each fact a small pulse drifting among the others.

There is something patient and predatory in the way they live. They hunt only what the town offers without sorrow, and they hide only to better attend. Their lives are a clockwork of small urgencies—repairing a vein that has calcified, guiding a bloom of lantern-life so its flare does not lure too many hunters, smoothing the edges of a corridor that has grown too fond of its own decay. They do not mourn the surface. To them, the sky is a rumor that has outlived its kindness, and the flood is the architecture of care.

And yet they wait. They wait in the layered deep, their lanterns arranged into patterns that resemble old street signs, their bodies aligned with the subtle flex of buildings that remember how to be towns. They are waiting for a translation—of purpose, of pattern, of the dry hunger that once drove them to build—into something that can be carried in the throat of warm water. They do not expect the water to part. They expect themselves to become the parting, to open like a door onto rooms older than the town, and to find, in that older dark, a reason to keep breathing together.
