They do not live here so much as they are lived by the place, and the distinction, after a time, stops being a useful one.

The people—if that word still fits—are tall and slender, not because they were born that way but because the air taught their bones to reach. Their lungs are deep and map-like, ridged with extra bronchi that bloom like fern-fronds in the upper chest, allowing them to drink the copper-tasting sky without dizziness. Their skin is waxy, leaf-thin in its sheen, patterned with dendritic stains that darken when storms are being born in the next valley. They sweat, but only in small, deliberate beads that collect along the spine and evaporate before they fall, because to waste moisture is to insult the house that holds you.

They live in the grammar of the wood. A settlement is less a village than a single organism temporarily persuaded into hospitality. Rooms are not built but coaxed: a corridor is opened where three trunks have braided over centuries; a floor is a lattice of living roots trained to grow flat and strong, fed by generations of spit, ash, and careful tread. Ceilings arch from interlocked crowns, porous and breathing, filtering the heavy sun into strips of honeyed light. Doors are veils of liana or curtains of overlapping fronds that slap shut when the rain comes, sealing the interior into a pocket of warm, leaf-damp quiet.

Their bodies have adapted to the thickness. Fingers are long and ridged, nails hardened by resin and pumice, able to find purchase on bark that feels like iron. Toes grip; arches are high, for the ground is rarely ground but a mattress of shed bark, spongy with decay and springy with fungal threads. They move with a gait that is half-climb, half-glide, knees bent to absorb the give of living floors, hips loose for the sway of bridges that are not rope but living vine, thick as a thigh and humming with sap.

They do not hunt so much as they harvest attention. The fauna here is architecture, and the people study it. There are those who tend the traps—silk-shearers who collect the sticky nets spun between hollow stalks and reseed them with pheromones of their own making. There are lure-speakers, who learn the songs of certain blossoms and use them to draw prey into the digestive moss, then sing apologies to the plants afterward, because waste is debt. Predators are respected like weather. When the gliders pass—those solitary, continental shadows—the people flatten themselves against the bark and hold their breath until the sky forgets them.

Flight is not rare; it is a vocation. Some are born with membranes, thin films of skin between arms and ribs that the damp keeps supple. Others earn their wings with study, learning to leap from towers of hollow stalks and catch the updrafts that spiral up the living walls. They carry messages in hollow bones, small clay spheres that rattle with weather predictions and the locations of fruiting explosions. To fly here is to be part of the sky’s inventory, a mote in the visible veils of heat, trusted not to fall but to be returned, eventually, by the currents.

Reproduction is slow and deliberate. Conception is timed to the resin-bloom, when the canopy drips light and the air smells of candied sap. Children are born in cradles of woven root, suspended over the matted loam so that their first breaths are heavy with spores and the low thrum of the forest. Infants are fed on nectar and softened bark until their teeth come in jagged, suited for chewing the tough veins of leaves that store water. Childhood is long, because learning the grammar takes decades: how to read the hiss of fused canopies, how to tell by the shiver of a buttress whether a storm is coming from the east, how to leave a trail that will be eaten by fungus before it can be followed by something hungry.

Their language is layered. It includes the clicks of tongues against palate, too high for some to feel, and the boresome drones sung in chests that press against the ribs of listeners. Words are often accompanied by touch—palms pressed to bark to let the tree carry the message onward, or fingers drummed in rhythms that travel through root and loam faster than air. Silence is practiced, too, and prized; when a person goes quiet for too long, others bring them damp moss to the lips, because silence can mean thirst, or thinking, or the approach of something that hunts by sound.

They bleed when cut, but their clots are amber, warm and resinous, and when it is rubbed and warmed it emits a low tone that soothes broken sleep. They wear this amber as jewelry and as medicine, suspending it in nets over cradles and beds so that the room is never without its hum. Their dead are fed to the towers of hollow stalks, where the updraft carries the spores of their memories upward, and the stalks sing them in rustles for as long as the wind is willing.

Their technology is the art of not breaking the spell. Copper bandages are wrapped around trunks wounded by lightning or clumsy climbers, not to heal the wood but to thank it. Tin bowls are set in the crooks of branches to catch dew, and the water from these bowls is never drunk on the same day it is gathered, because it must think about where it wants to go. Mirrors of polished alloy are hung in places where the sun is heavy, to scare off the gliders or to summon them, depending on the angle. Fire is kept in clay hearts—small, closed vessels where a coal can rest and sigh—and is used to scent a room, to signal a birth or a death, or to warm the amber before it is sung.

Their lives are full of small negotiations. Water is asked for, not taken; permission is given in the form of a cut that is shallow and swift, sealed with a thumb of wax and a hum. Light is shared by trimming leaves in patterns that let shafts strike the floor in certain shapes, so that a room knows what hour it is without a clock. Heat is banked in stone beds veined by roots, so that sleep is a descent into warmth that remembers the day.

And always, there is the dampness, the cool thread in the hot cloth. It beads on their brows, collects in the axils of their clothing, and tastes like metal and memory. They let it linger, never quite wiping it away, because they know that this vast, roaring, living height is tender, and that to live here is to be always on the edge of tears the place never quite lets fall.
