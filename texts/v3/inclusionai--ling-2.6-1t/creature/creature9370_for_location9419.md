They do not so much inhabit the chamber as become its method of remembering.

The moss is the first of them, and perhaps the oldest. It is not a carpet but a single body that has forgotten how to be individual. Sheets of tarnished silver stretch across the stone, fused into panels like hammered metal, and if you press a hand against them you feel not softness but a cool, fibrous density that remembers pressure. The moss eats the salt crusts that bloom across it, absorbing centuries of mineral thirst and turning it into a gray-green patience that spreads slowly, imperceptibly, like the closing of a circuit. It communicates by touch: when a vine brushes it, the moss answers with a faint luminescence that travels its veins like a rumor, saying *yes, I am here, you are here, we are here.*

The ferns are the chamber’s exoskeleton. They have calcified along their edges, each front rimmed with white enamel that catches and throws the shy light. They do not reach for the sky because there is none; instead they build outward, thickets that interlock like the fingers of clasped hands. Their roots do not seek soil—they seek architecture. They plunge into cracks in the quartz, thread through the rusted ribs of machines, and return upward as columns, cantilevers, buttresses. The ferns are engineers who have forgotten how to ask permission. They brace the ceiling against itself, turning collapse into a conversation between mineral and vegetable, each supporting the other until neither can be said to lead.

The vines are the chamber’s memory of motion. They have braided themselves into cables, thick as arms, winding around spires of pale quartz and the rusted pylons that stand like old men with their hands in their pockets. The vines move, but not in the way that climbing plants are supposed to. They shift overnight, a millimeter here, a centimeter there, tightening or loosening their grip depending on the pitch of the hum that comes from the buried motors. When they strangle one another, it is not violence but argument, a slow debate over geometry. Over time, they have absorbed the ladders of the watchtower, the stairs, the platform; they have turned human ascent into a nest for lenses that have grown opaque with patience, each lens now a cataract that softens the idea of looking.

What lives in the machines is a kind of ghost that prefers company. The motors sealed inside the walls do not drive anything so crude as gears; they drive frequency. Their vibration seeps into the floor, into the moss, into the marrow of anyone who stands still long enough. The pipes carry not water but a slurry of salt and something darker, something that clatters like dice in a cup, circulating in loops that predate the name of the mountain. The generators squat like iron beasts half-buried by vegetation, their cooling fins fanned by leaves that have learned to tolerate heat by never knowing cold. These machines do not break; they maintain. Their persistence is a form of belief, a stubborn insistence that time can be divided into equal parts, that the hum can be kept level, that the chamber can remain exactly as full as it is now.

There are beings that wear the chamber like clothing. You can feel them before you see them: a pressure against the back of the neck, a rearrangement of shadow in your periphery. They are the absence of animals given form. Their tracks appear in the moss, pressed deep, but they are mechanical tread, regular, purposeful, softened by time into trails. Maintenance drones, perhaps, whose bodies have been claimed by vines, whose wheels have become part of the floor. Or something older: caretakers who allowed themselves to be edited, who let the moss grow over their eyes and the salt crust over their mouths, until they became another kind of machine, one that lives by subtraction. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They huddle near the chimney stub, near the gearbox filled with pulsing moss, near the watchtower’s cataract lenses, as if ashamed of their nakedness amid so much life.

The chamber also breeds small, bright things that are barely living. Glyphs that appear in the salt crusts overnight, resembling schematics for rooms that do not exist. Windows that look into spaces already filled with wall. Doors that open onto doors, each one a repetition with a slight error, as if the chamber is practicing how to say *here, here, here* without ever exhausting the word. These are the chamber’s dreams, solidified into geometry, and they rearrange themselves when not observed, as if the darkness is busy correcting mistakes.

The light that leaks from cracks in machinery, from seams in the salt crust, is not electricity so much as consent. It does not illuminate; it acknowledges. It suggests that the moss is alive, that the ferns are listening, that the hum is a voice. Shadows pool thickly, violet and cool, and within them, shapes rearrange: a vine loosens, a salt crystal settles, a machine shifts its pitch by a hair, as if to say *I am with you, I am with you, I am with you.*

Sound is their one extravagance, their one confession. The chamber is loud with the roar of buried engines, overlaid with the brittle percussion of salt crystallizing, the wet snap of stems thickening, the endless susurrus of packed leaves brushing packed leaves. The noise is loud without being chaotic; it has its own gravity, a drone that organizes the small sounds into a stable roar. If you speak here, your voice is not heard so much as absorbed, turned into another layer of the hum, a brief, human note that is quickly edited into the longer song.

At the center of the pocket, where the density is greatest, the floor rises and falls with a breath that no lungs own. Stand there long enough and the vibration finds its teeth, its marrow, the spaces between thoughts, until the distinction between machine, mineral, and moss blurs into a single, cool, roaring entity that is, simply, here. It has always been here. It will be here, packed and loud and dim and strange, until the mountain forgets it is holding something at all.
