They live in the margins first—the margins between wet and dry, between built and grown, between the moment a surface begins to soften and the moment it remembers it was never hard. These are not creatures that arrived; they are creatures that allowed themselves to be arrived at by the place.  

Seen up close, they look like concentrations of the same dark, water-smoothed patience that lines the walls. Their bodies are long and low, supple as bent stone, with skin that drinks the feeble light the way the walls do, turning dimness into texture. It gathers in folds like layers of sediment, each fold holding a thin film of moisture that never quite evaporates, so that they leave faint, glistening trails even when they seem to stand still. Their limbs are not so much limbs as continuations—shoulders sliding into arms that taper into fingers that feel more like roots testing the softness of a path. They have too many joints, or perhaps the right number for something that does not insist on being upright all the time. They kneel often. They lean. They press hands into the ground not to rise but to listen.  

Their faces are broad and shallow, as if pressed into shape by constant nearness to the ground. Eyes are small, dark, and set deep, not to see better but to see less—enough to notice shifts in the dark’s thickness, enough to register when a corner softens or a distance changes its mind. They do not blink in the way you would expect; instead, their eyelids lower like slow stones settling, and they keep their gaze fixed for long stretches, letting the heat shimmer across the lashes. When they speak, their voices do not cut through the air; they pool in it, thickened, so that a single word can feel like a weight placed on the tongue of whoever receives it.  

They wear little that could be called clothing, only arrangements: bands of woven grass and thin, flexible bark that hold small, smooth stones against their skin. These stones are not ornaments. They are weights, dampeners, anchors—each one chosen for how it fits the curve of a wrist or collarbone, for how it draws the fever from the air and holds it close to the body so the rest of the world can cool a little. At night, the stones glisten, and the bodies gleam alongside them, a slow constellation of wet dark moving through a darker dark.  

There are more of them than you notice at first. They have learned how to let the landscape fold in front of them, how to step into a lurch of distance and arrive somewhere else without having moved very far. They appear at the edges of vision, not because they are hiding but because the place has agreed to let them be half-present, the way it lets walls sag and corners blur. When they travel, they do not stride. They insinuate, letting their bodies follow the braid of a path the way water follows a seam.  

What they eat is what the place offers without being asked: the pale grasses that taste of iron and something sharper, the beads of sweat from stones after rain, the thin pastes made from roots that have forgotten how to reach upward and instead remember only how to hold on. They do not hunt animals, because animals have long since learned to give this ground a wide patience of their own. Instead, they forage for shifts—pockets of heat that taste metallic, threads of vapor that carry the old memory of thunder, the slight thickening in the air that signals a coming softening of a wall. They gather these things and store them in small clay vessels sealed with wax made from rendered grass, not to keep them fresh but to let them age into something quieter.  

Their young are born with a dampness that does not dry, skin cool and slick as fresh stone. The elders teach them how to lie flat for hours, how to let the ground cool the fever in their blood, how to tell the difference between a wall that is leaning out of weariness and one that is leaning in invitation. Games are not games of chase but of rearrangement: moving a handful of pebbles from one seam to another and seeing whether the corner remembers where they started, stepping onto a known path and seeing whether it steps back. They learn early that distances can be persuaded, and that the cost of persuading them is attention.  

There is no ruler among them, only a kind of consensus that settles like silt. Decisions move through them the way heat lifts from the ground: one person leans a little, then another, and soon the whole group is angling toward a new damp hollow or away from a seam that has begun to weep too insistently. Disagreements are rare, and when they come they are not sharp but slow—a stubbornness that feels like stone refusing to settle. They sit with these stubbornnesses for days, letting the damp work into them, until the disagreement itself becomes porous and lets something else through.  

They have rituals of letting go. On nights when the moisture is thick and the dark feels like a skin pulled taut, they kneel at the thresholds that have no doors and press their foreheads to the moist material of the walls. They do this to remember that they, too, are held open by damp, by heat, by the slow leaning of the thing itself. They offer their own small losses—strands of hair, a drop of blood, a word that has worn thin—to the path, and the path accepts them without changing its mind.  

When the breakdown comes—the softening of corners, the doubling of shapes, the sense that the built has remembered being natural and the natural has remembered being built—they do not panic. They gather closer, their bodies forming a low, breathing ridge, and they let the place rearrange them as it rearranges itself. They know that their shapes, too, can double and un-double, that their edges can blur into the wet seams if they allow it. They do not allow it completely, not for long, because they have learned that to be entirely undone is to lose the ability to feel the fine silver ladders of water as they carve their patient way down a bowed face.  

Instead, they remain fiercely partial—half here, half elsewhere, half solid, half suggestion. They are the place’s way of keeping a memory of motion inside stillness, of keeping a low, living hum inside the held breath of the quiet. They exist in touch and heat and the slow mathematics of accommodation, in the wet logic of never quite leaving and never quite staying, in the dark’s patient rearrangement of what is solid and what is not.  

And when you leave, if you leave, you do not carry an image of them with you so much as a feeling of weight behind your knees, a taste of iron and salt on the back of your tongue, and the sense that somewhere, in a low crouched place where water is everywhere and the dark feels like a skin, they are still leaning, still listening, still letting the ground breathe out and in, and holding their reality together long enough to feel what it would be like to let go.
