They live in layers, the way stone does.

The primary citizens are the Soft-Spoken—mosses that have learned economy. They do not grow so much as *settle*, laying themselves down in velveted mats along the north faces, exhaling pale clouds when the air presses them. They are not individuals but continuums, stretching across seams where the damp remembers how to weep. They speak by color: green-black for patience, rust-brown for drought, silver-slick for the mornings when the bowl has caught its breath. Touch one and it yields a scent of bruised rain and ancient wool. They are the librarians of the place, their bodies storing light in spongy leaves, releasing it later in the form of glow-memories after twilight, when the bowl dims and needs a second kind of visibility.

Above them, the Leather-Lettered Ones—shrubs with leaves folded like unopened correspondence—colonize every pocket of shelter. They are stoic archivists, their roots gripping the pale mineral soil with a grip that is less grasping than remembering. They do not flower extravagantly; instead they offer small, hard fruits that taste of tannin and altitude, gifts to any passerby who knows how to wait. Their leaves, when rubbed, release a fragrance like old paper and frost, the smell of stories that have survived by being hard to reach.

In the wetter hollows, the Sedge-Spearmen stand in tussocks, each a miniature metropolis of pale blades. They crowd the peat-softened pools like citizens jostling at a well, their roots braiding into mats that float and firm the water’s edge. When wind passes through, they rustle like dry pages turning in unison, a constant susurrus that keeps track of time in syllables rather than numbers. They mark paths—not by intention, but by persistence; repeated passage darkens the soil around them, creating corridors that appear to have been planned but are really only habits of the foot.

The water itself is a resident, though a capricious one. It arrives as rumor and lingers as mood. It beads on stone, braids into runnels, pools into black mirrors that swallow the sky whole. The pools are not still so much as *thinking*—they absorb light slowly, digesting it into peat and shadow, and sometimes, when the bowl is especially still, they hum. The runnels are gossamer-thin, impatient threads that carve braids across pale flats only to vanish into seams that look too narrow to hold anything. Yet they hold; they hold because they are not rushing toward an exit but are simply practicing flow. In winter they sleep as glass; in thaw they gossip as they slide.

Then there are the Stone-Backed Ones—creatures small and sure-footed, shaped by the bowl’s insistence on scarcity. They are more sound than sight: a scuff, a breath, the soft percussion of claws on polished granite. Their coats mimic the palette of the bowl—dust, lichen, shadow—and they move as if each step must be negotiated with gravity. They are patient predators, feeding on the slow: insects that have mistaken a sun-warmed seam for summer, roots that have grown careless in the thin soil. They do not hurry because the bowl teaches that hurry is a waste of heat, and heat is the rarest coin here.

Birds pass through, but they are tourists, not tenants. They wheel above the rim, dipping low only to snatch what the wind offers, never lingering to learn the grammar of the hollow. Their absence is as present as their cries, a reminder that some citizens choose transience.

There is also the Unseen Parliament—the microbial majority, the capillary whisperers, the freeze-and-flow negotiators. They live in the film between water and stone, in the pores of peat, in the veins of ice that lace the soil at dawn. They govern by subtraction and seep, by creep and settle. They are the engineers of the soft, the ones who turn hardness into hospitality over centuries.

And there are the Echoes. Not animals but presences. The hum that rises from the hollows on windless afternoons, like distant bells carved from wet stone. The water that sometimes flows uphill for a few steps, as if testing the rules before remembering them. The moss that glows faintly after dark, not with fire but with stored brightness, a courtesy extended to the night.

They live in towns grown rather than built. Cairns of stone stack themselves into low altars or mere landmarks—proof that hands, or wind, or patience have nudged things into meaning. Runoff is coaxed into channels, not with tools but with nudges, with the careful placement of a pebble that becomes a weir, that becomes a habit. The sedge tussocks arrange themselves into plazas and alleys, and the darkened seams of soil record the paths of unseen feet, maps written in compression.

They survive by subtraction. Warmth is hoarded in hollows, under overhangs, in the dense air near peat. Frost can arrive at noon, and when it does, the bowl smells of ozone and stone, and the inhabitants go still, listening to the crackle of ice claiming what it can. The temperature governs by taking away, and what remains is what has learned how to hold on lightly.

Predation here is polite. A root steals minerals from a neighbor; a pool drinks the light that falls on it; frost claims the unwary. The ecosystem moves at the speed of patience, yet it never stops. It is a parliament of processes: cold contracts, damp expands, stone remembers, moss insists.

And through it all, the wind is the loudest citizen, combing over the bowl and drawing harmonics from stone, a constant instrument tuning itself. The water chatters when it moves. Frost crackles when it takes hold. The tussocks rustle like pages turning, a dry language that keeps the bowl awake. Even the emptiness has a tone, a pressure that hums behind the ears.

They are a city of small things and large silences, alive without hurry, loud with the sound of being exactly what they have become. They do not ask you to solve them, only to notice how their rules fit together: cold that clarifies, damp that connects, emptiness that amplifies, stone that listens. In the high, cold, ancient hollow, life has learned how to lie down, to cup itself in the ribs of the mountain, and to hum softly while the sky moves faster than the ground.
