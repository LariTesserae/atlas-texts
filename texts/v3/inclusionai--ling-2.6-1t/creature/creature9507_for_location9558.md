The cold is not weather here; it is a kind of caretaker, the one who keeps the edges from bleeding together. It polishes the air until it is a blade you can tap with a fingernail, bright enough to make shadows feel invented. It learns the gaps between heartbeats—the small, fast caves behind ribs—and stores itself there, so that even warmth must pass through a corridor of chill to reach the blood.

The land, meanwhile, is new in the way a word is new when it first escapes a mouth: still tasting itself, still testing its borders. Ridges stand stiff as unset bone, and valleys cut themselves with the awkward certainty of a child learning how to divide things. The rock remembers being crushed and chooses, for now, to stay braced. Water is only a visitor, arriving as a skin on stone, as silver threads that appear before light, as sudden cloudbursts that fall and vanish and leave the ground gleaming with a metallic hush. In the high basins, rivers braid across pale gravel, their voices thin and constant, stitching silence together like a hem.

Against all odds, the place is lush. Mosses crowd like velvet, lichens scribble filigree over every surface, grasses rise in dense flags that hiss when wind moves through them. Root systems tangle in a compressed parliament beneath the ground, each filament staking a claim to warmth, to damp, to the brief generosity of light. The greenery is anxious, urgent, hoarding calories in tissues that gleam with internal dampness, as if afraid the cold will remember how to take—and so they glow softly from within, a hoarded, chlorophyll heat.

What lives here is overwhelming, not because it is monstrous but because it is populous beyond sense. Winged things with metallized feathers swarm in fields that read less as flocks than as weather, drifting and reforming like slow smoke, their wings catching light in geometries that make the eyes ache. Ground-dwellers pulse in tides across the slopes, carapaces clicking, claws scouring the young rock for films of growth, for moss-threads, for the faint sugar of morning dew. Predators thread through the crowds with electric patience, signatures moving through the noise like needles through cloth, never fully separate from the fabric they pierce. The biomass pushes back when you step, a thick, breathing resistance, a continent of muscle and chitin and warm blood. It feels less like an ecology than like a city that happened to be meat.

The scale is vast enough to make its own weather. From the heights, cloud ranges rise like distant mirrors of the stone below, storm fronts grinding in slow motion. The horizon becomes a curve of competition between geology and sky, neither yielding. Time here is slow not from laziness but from deliberation, so that erosion hesitates, as if unsure whether it is allowed to soften this newness. Change arrives in pulses: a season that lasts years, the migration of whole populations that take lifetimes to cross a single valley, glaciers that advance only when no one is looking.

And yet the place is packed. No gap is left unclaimed. Life presses against stone, stone against sky, sky against the bright ceiling of space. Even the air feels crowded, thick with spores, with pollen, with scales and dust and the static of wings, with the small thunder of bodies braced against cold. There is no room for emptiness, so the void is carved into small pockets inside things: lungs, burrows, bubbles trapped in ice, the spaces between heartbeats where the cold likes to sit and listen.

There is no built world, but industry haunts the landscape like a rumor grown heavy. Engines do not sit in factories; they live in things. Across the heights, throats open and close in rhythmic clatter, vents exhale steam that smells of copper and burnt sugar, shafts of heat lance down from concealed sources to melt patches of snow that sizzle and vanish. Power moves through the ground in cables where root and wire have twined, in rivers diverted into turbines made of bone and alloy. The hum is constant, a low chord that vibrates in the teeth, louder when you press your palms to stone and feel the deep machinery purring.

Weirdness drifts through everything like a gas. Stones remember being other stones and sigh in frequencies you feel in your molars. Water sometimes falls upward in fine threads, catching the light, then forgets itself and returns to its course. Time does not always run at the same speed across a single ridge; you can watch a glacier creep while a beetle sprints through hours in a single step. Fauna change their patterns when observed, colors shifting to hues that have no names, geometries folding in their eyes, as if the act of being seen demands a new shape.

Sound is loud, but layered. It is not noise so much as density: the click of mandibles, the susurrus of growth, the grind of young rock under pressure, the hush of snow sliding in sheets, the electric purr of engines buried deep. When the wind rises, it conducts them all into a single roar that has texture, that has weight, that you can lean against and feel your bones hum.

Rules here are provisional. Cold keeps the edges sharp but cannot freeze the pace of life. Light feeds everything and blinds you to limits. High elevation is not a barrier but a platform, a place to stand and see how far crowded can go. Water, that rumor, decides where things can be lush, even as it refuses to be relied on. Youth in the land means danger—sudden faults, cliffs that give way without warning, slopes that have not yet learned how to be safe. Packed density means that to move is to negotiate, to be touched, to be changed, to leave behind a whisper of warmth that the cold will eventually claim.

In the end, the place is a single lung, vast and bright and cold, breathing slow, alive with too much life, humming with machines it grew rather than made, strange as a dream that refuses to end. It does not invite you to stay, but it will not let you leave without taking something of it with you—some sharpness in your thoughts, some dampness in your clothes, some brightness lodged behind your eyes, some new knowledge of how crowded a world can be without ever feeling full, how alive a place can be while still keeping room for the cold to press in, again and again, and listen.
