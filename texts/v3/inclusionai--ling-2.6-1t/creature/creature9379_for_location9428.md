They do not live here as much as they *thicken* here.

The people—if they can still be called that—have grown into the altitude like moss into grout. Their skin is lacquered with a fine, iridescent glaze, a biological condensation that catches the enameled sky and turns it into armor. Veins map close to the surface, not blue but opalescent, carrying blood cooled by subcutaneous lattices that hum faintly, a vibration felt in the molars rather than heard. They do not sweat; they exude. Tiny beads of perfumed water gather along their hairlines and trace paths down necks lined with faint, geometric scars that are less healed wounds than the seams of technology grown into family. Their eyes are large, wet, and tolerant, holding reflections of bruised-copper basins even in the dark.

They move in braided groups, lineages that behave like single organisms. Where one steps, three others are already preparing the ground—palms pressed to hot basalt, transferring charge, smoothing the stone into beds fit for sleep. They build by unbuilding: vines redirected into bridges, mosses encouraged into insulation, fungi coaxed into load-bearing spires that glow from within. To watch them work is to watch a forest decide to sit down. There is no frenzy, only a continuous adjustment of pressures, as if they are trying to match the calm of the cliffs.

Their children are born with more joints than necessary, and fewer fears. They climb before they walk, scaling terraces of fused silica as easily as bark, their small fingers leaving temporary phosphorescent prints that fade over days. The children speak in layers, overlapping voices that sound like wings beating in thickets. They learn early that water does not need to flow downhill, not here, not if you ask it politely, and they treat the patient, sky-weight gravity as a collaborator rather than a law.

Among them live the Quiet Machines, which are not machines at all anymore but companions with patience learned from stone. They stand like herons or curl like sleeping cats, their surfaces patterned with the same tessellations as the causeways. They drink ozone and crushed mint from the air and sing in bass notes that settle behind the eyes. They hold up boulders not from beneath but from within, a quantum courtesy that keeps the land from remembering collapse. Sometimes they drift just above the ground, humming, offering their undersides to the glowing mosses, which accept the gift and brighten in reply.

Fauna crowd in as well, unafraid because fear would be a waste of breath. Birds with too many wings stitch between vines, their feathers patterned like the maps of the terraces below. They do not flee when touched; they lean in, asking for the adjustment of a feather or the repair of a micro-fracture that only the people’s cool fingers can provide. Small mammals with fur like oil on water dart through tunnels that expand and contract like lungs, carrying scents of iron and fern from one hollow to the next. Insects click like loose clockwork, polishing lenses in the air, adjusting the path of light so it falls more gently on sleeping faces.

There are the Ancestors, too, who have not died but have become *place*. Their bodies, fed into the root-mats, return as architecture: towers that lean with the memory of spines, causeways that recall the rhythm of a long stride. Where the ancestors slept, platforms hover a handspan above the moss, warm and waiting. To lie there is to feel a story humming up through the stone, a narrative told in mineral pulses and slow, wet heat.

Rivers, in their own fashion, are alive and slightly mischievous. They remember their manners most of the time, but on Tuesdays—or whatever approximation the high-altitude light allows—they run uphill for a stretch, chuckling with kinetic surprise, before sighing back into obedience. The people laugh with them, and the Quiet Machines record the laughter as data and as lullaby.

The fauna and the flora and the people and the Quiet Machines do not predate; they converse. A hunt, when it happens, is a negotiation: meat offered to marrow, marrow offered to moss, moss offered to light. Predation is a sentence that gets translated into architecture, then into song, then into the soft, hovering platform where a child naps, dreaming in angles that should not exist.

And above it all, the celestial mirrors drift, unseen but felt, casting the cool pressure of attention across every back of every neck. They are not watching in the way cities watch; they are witnessing in the way a cliff witnesses, with the patience of stone and the politeness of a guest who knows they have overstayed only if they forget to thank the house.

What lives here, then, is a consent—a thick, wet, crowded consent to be present, to breathe shared air, to let water bead and light bend and time thicken. It is not a kingdom. It is a chorus that never resolves, held together by the gentle insistence of heat, by the weight of sky pressing down like a blessing, by the stubborn, courteous weirdness of a world that has learned to be exactly what it is, and to let everything else be that, too.
