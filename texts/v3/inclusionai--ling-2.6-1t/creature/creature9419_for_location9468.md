They are the Patience-Children, the Ridge-Spoken, the Knot-Born—though they rarely name themselves with anything so singular. To live in a city that is also a lung is to become plural by necessity, and so they have learned to be many things at once, softly.

Their bodies are built for a place where edges hesitate. They are tall, limber, and lightly framed, as if bone itself has decided to be less stubborn than the world around it. Their skin is pale, almost opalescent, threaded with faint tracery like water finding cracks in porcelain. It drinks the damp gladly, puckering into temporary rivulets when the air thins, and drying into maps of silvery bloom when the sun insists on brightness. They smell faintly of wet fiber and old sap, with a mineral tang that lingers like a held breath.

Clothing is grown as much as made: cloaks and tunics of treated inner bark, supple as leather but cool to the touch, patterned with lenticel-like perforations that dilate with temperature. Shoes are soled with cork-moss, gripping the slickened wood of the boulevards without scuffing it. Many wear spectacles or lenses of thin resin, polished by bees and hardened in sunlight, to correct for refractions in the air—distances that lean in too close, light that remembers being something else.

They do not build so much as negotiate. Architecture is a conversation held over decades, and the Ridge-Spoken are patient conversationalists. A family may raise a room by coaxing a trunk to swell in a particular season, then split it at the right lunar phase to let in a window that opens onto a neighbor’s courtyard. Walls are trained, not erected; they are braided from fast-growing hardwoods and softened with layers of cork so that sound passes through as rumor rather than intrusion. Furniture is alive, or was recently so: chairs that remember being saplings, tables with rings still visible, their surfaces uneven in ways that invite the body to adjust.

Their industry is rooted in reciprocity. To take is to tend. Engines are housed in heartwood chambers lined with resin that slowly ceramicizes, wearing down in time and sealing itself. When a machine coughs or jams, mechanics do not pry so much as massage; they listen for the pitch of the fault and answer with heat or cool, oil or silence. Factories are hollow trunks where sap is fermented under pressure into thick, green-gold fuel that smells of ozone and sweet rot. The by-products are fed to filtration gardens, where moss-machines and beetle-keepers sort glittering scale from ash, returning the bright leavings to the roots of the city. Even waste is a form of speech: gutters pulse when full, and their rhythm is studied like a pulse.

What they manufacture most, however, is attention. The settlement sprawls with deliberate emptiness, neighborhoods set apart by meadows of low moss, plazas of pale lichen, quiet zones where thinking can be heard. The Ridge-Spoken cultivate interior silence as others cultivate crops. A person may sit for hours beneath a canopy, watching condensation bead and run, practicing the discipline of noticing how a street lengthens, how a corner softens, how a stair recalls being a window. Memory is slippery here, and so record-keeping is communal and living: maps drawn on treated leaves that change as the city does, annotated in margins by hands that contradict their own earlier script.

They have learned to trust doors more than roads. A doorway is a promise the wood keeps, while a street may decide to be elsewhere. Clocks are grown from concentric rings of tissue that swell and shrink with moisture; noon is a suggestion offered by the city, and midnight is a rumor passed through speaking tubes of hollow reed. Children are taught to read humidity the way others read stars, and to recognize the moods of the plateau by the pitch of its hum.

Fauna are rare, and so the Ridge-Spoken behave like careful fauna themselves. They move with a economy of gesture, their footsteps light on fibrous pavement. At intersections, hollow knots serve as roundabouts, and they glide beneath glassy plant-surfaces that flex like cartilage, yielding without yielding. They speak in layered tones, their voices threading through the groan of timber, the slap of water in living gutters, the chuff of engines. When the chords in the air slip into music without words, they do not stop; they let the song settle on their shoulders like dust.

Society is a many-threaded weave with slack in it. There are keepers of pressure, tenders of cistern-veins, listeners who map the hum of the plateau and note when it tightens or loosens. There are negotiators who sit between neighborhoods when a tree decides to reclaim a thoroughfare, and root-surgeons who guide growth with cuts that heal into sculpture. Predators are ideas: an absence that passes through the market and leaves prices changed, a rumor that eats the edges of a plan. The Ridge-Spoken respect these absences, leaving bowls of dark water out at dusk to catch them, or hanging strips of resin that chime when something unnameable brushes past.

Their daily life is a balance of industry and rest. Morning arrives in dry gusts that comb the canopies, and the city combs itself in answer. By midday, rain may fall, smelling of iron and green, and everyone pauses to let the gutters drink. In the long spells of stillness that follow, the settlement holds its breath and listens to its own plumbing, its people moving through the quiet like thoughts through a mind. At night, the hum deepens, a bass note that rattles teeth and makes glass panes ripple like water. They do not fear it. They sleep with one ear open, as the plateau has taught them, resting with their eyes closed but their bodies ready to answer.

They are not immortal, but they are durable. Age shows in the whitening of tracery on their skin, in the slower return of resilience, in the way their voices grow more harmonic, as if they have begun to harmonize with the city itself. When one of them dies, they are returned to the root-plates in ceremonies of folding and binding, their substance offered back to the living geology that holds them up. New growth is coaxed toward the hollows left behind, and the city reshapes its rooms with a patience that does not grieve, only adjusts.

To live here is to be convinced by the living world, and to convince it in return. It is to accept that motion may have gone to sleep while leaving a mask of motion behind, and to find, in that mask, a life that is loud and quiet, patient and busy, rooted in emptiness. The Ridge-Spoken are the ones who remember how to bend without breaking, how to listen for the pitch of a street lengthening, how to let a door be truer than a map. They are the inhabitants of a plateau that has forgotten how to resist, and in forgetting, has learned to hold everything softly, including them.
