What lives here are, first, the listeners.

They have no faces, or perhaps they have too many. They are colonies of resonance housed in bark that never fully decides to be bark, but rather skin that remembers being pith. Their bodies are tuned to the 2.9 roar, and they spend their lives subtracting noise from noise in order to hear the shape of the place. They do not eat in the way animals do; they drink pressure. When the tensile skin of the ground indents under a step, they sip the flex, converting strain into a slow sugar that makes their hearts—small, granular things hidden in knots—thrum at the same frequency as the aquifer’s breath. They are patient. They know that the lung of the world inhales over centuries, and they time their thoughts to match.

There are the bridges, though it would be truer to call them agreements.

They are vegetal in substance, technological in habit, and neither plant nor animal but something closer to a question posed by the forest to itself. They grow when a gap becomes inconvenient, stiffening into lattice with a faint, internal glitter as the unseen lattices awaken. Once the span is learned—once roots on either side have memorized the flex and sway of the bridge—it begins to dissolve, shedding its strength back into the wet loam so that the forest can forget how it once solved that particular problem. They leave behind glassy veins where their structural nerves used to be, and these veins hum when the wind is low, singing the memory of weightlessness.

There are the voters.

Microscopic, many-nucleated, they live in the seams where water hesitates. When a rivet of silver wants to decide which way to flow, the voters congregate, their bodies aligning like tiny magnets or compass needles dipped in consensus. They do not speak, but they vote with their shapes, elongating toward the path that feels most like a future. When the vote is unanimous—which is rare, and therefore precious—the water obeys, carving a new channel that smells faintly of ozone and old milk. Afterward, the voters disperse, becoming part of the moss-thick veneer, waiting for the next indecision.

There are the spore-lanterns.

They are the landscape’s learned habit of listening. No larger than a grain of breath, they drift up from the forest floor on pockets of exhaled air, aligning into temporary constellations that translate root-tip tremors into song. They are not insects, though they move with purpose; not machines, though they compute. They are more like the forest’s daydreams made visible, flickering in the reluctant greens, turning condensation into brief, dying rainbows. Their light is cool, and where it touches skin, it leaves a numbness that feels like remembering something that never happened to you.

There are the time-slippers.

You find them in groves where growth rings contract inward toward a future core. They are small, six-limbed, with bodies segmented like accordions, allowing them to shorten or lengthen their gait depending on whether the moment is expanding or collapsing. They do not walk so much as negotiate with duration, stepping into a footprint they have not yet made and trusting it will be there when they arrive. Their eyes—when they choose to have them—are lenses of hardened dew, faceted to catch multiple phases of light at once. They feed on echoes, clipping them from the air and storing them in bladders that chime when danger is near. Most of the time, they are in no danger, so they spend their days polishing the silence between sounds.

There are the bladders.

Not creatures, exactly, but organs the forest grows when it needs to hold its breath. They are ponds that are really tense sacs of water, suspended in root-cradles, quivering with the pressure of unexhaled weather. When the lung finally breathes out, the bladders release their cargo all at once, and the spray carries pollen like snow, thick and sudden, coating everything in a layer of soft, reproductive hush. Inside each bladder live symbionts no larger than punctuation marks, creatures that stitch themselves to the inner wall and knit it stronger with every beat of the forest’s pulse.

There are the gradients.

Living in the places where plateau bleeds into basin without a line, they are tall, stretching things, half root and half stem, half leaf and half vein. They do not belong to a single tree but to the thickening of stems, to the deepening of hush. They move by unknitting themselves and reknitting farther down, a slow migration that takes seasons. Their sap is mostly rumor, and when you brush against them, you feel a vibration that is less sound and more certainty, as if the forest has just remembered, again, that it is vast.

There are the embryos of rock.

Half-finished, clay-soft, they have not yet decided whether to be ridge or channel, to rise or to furrow. They live in a state of perpetual first draft, reshaping themselves in response to the voters’ choices and the bridges’ agreements. Sometimes they harden for a heartbeat—just long enough to feel proud—before sighing back into potential. They are alive with the stubbornness of things that refuse to be permanent, and they hum with a low, geological laughter that only the listeners can interpret.

Above all, there is the lung.

It is not a creature, but it breathes. It is the sum of moss-thick veneers and canopy-compression, the aquifer that does not pool but breathes, the entire 3.0 vastness exhaling pollen and inhaling silence. It is the throat that is also a forest, the membrane that answers the sky. It does not think in words but in pressure, in moisture, in the slow accumulation of becoming. To live here is to be caught in its breath, to be calibrated by its roar, to be part of the loom that has no final pattern, only the busy, patient work of holding together.

Nothing here is in a hurry.  
Even the weirdness, at 1.1, is gentle, teaching water to run uphill just long enough to prove it can, then letting it rest.  
And all of it—listeners, bridges, voters, lanterns, slippers, bladders, gradients, embryos—sings in the voice of the lung: a ringing, roaring, trembling hum that is the sound of a world learning how to be itself, wet and cool and vast, under a light that is always learning how to squeeze through.
