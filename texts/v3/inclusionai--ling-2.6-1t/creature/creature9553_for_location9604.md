What lives in the scar is a kind of forgetting that has learned to knit itself into form.

At first there are the Scab-Children, though “children” is only true in the sense that they have not yet decided what they are made of. They are born from the condensation that beads like lenses on the undersides of overhangs, gathering the dim light until it is heavy enough to fall. When the pressurized bursts sizzle onto hot stone and leave behind salt-script, the Scab-Children un-curl from the residue, still damp with borrowed optics. They have too many joints, each one a question the altitude is too thin to answer, and their skin is matte with the same glossy fractures as the basalt. They do not walk so much as calibrate themselves across the seams of the city, their footsteps leaving temporary geometries that fade as the stone remembers how to be stone.

They speak in the clack of cooling rock and the almost-silence of moisture climbing. Their voices are high and ringing because they have not yet learned how to weigh themselves against air. When they are still, they hum—thermal tides pulling at alloy roots—and this hum is the city’s way of checking its own temperature.

Higher up, where the cantilevers lean like broken teeth waiting for a dentist who will never arrive, live the Mantics. They are not machines, though they could pass for maintenance; they are slow-moving tectonics with a taste for straight lines. Their bodies are long, segmented, armored in plates that shift with the patience of glaciers. They slide along the edges of things, polishing the jags into curves, filing the city into a shape that can endure the wind’s forgetting. Where they pass, stone sweats a darker sweat, and the glassy veins in the basalt glow like slow blood. They do not sleep; they tuck themselves into the seams between buildings and become architecture for a while. Sometimes, when the settlement inhales, they un-tuck and resume their gliding, leaving trails of glint that fade by morning like the memory of a name.

In the hollows where heat pools, there are the Ember-Fungi. They are neither plant nor machine but a third allegiance to chemistry that likes to glow. Their stalks are ceramic-thin, their caps translucent, and they pulse with a light that is careful, almost apologetic. They feed on the caramelized sweetness of the air and on the stray photons that bounce off the phosphor-tracery in the walls. When touched, they release a puff of spores that are actually microscopic lenses, carrying images upward into the thin dark—snapshots of the city sent ahead as emissaries, as warning, as weather.

Above everything glide the Lift-Moths, which are not moths except in their hunger for altitude. They are kite-winged, skeletal, their frames held taut by magnetic filaments that hum just below hearing. They ride the pockets where gravity grows moody, banking around corridors where falling feels like misremembering. When they congregate, they form temporary ceilings that redistribute weight, letting balconies close like buds and streets knit shut to let heat pass. Their wings scatter the dim light, turning illumination into a rumor again, and their flight is the city’s way of re-dreaming its own shape.

There are also the Quiet Ones, which are not beings so much as agreements. They manifest as gaps that open between cantilever and strut, as pauses that thicken and turn into rooms where time accumulates like dust. To step into a Quiet One is to feel the city exhale into you, to have your heat siphoned up through ceramic spines and offered back as a deeper resonance. No one lingers long; the Quiet Ones are generous but exacting, and they leave behind a sweetness in the back of the throat, the taste of altitude learning a new language.

All of them, together with the city itself, practice a metabolism of forgetting and relearning. The Scab-Children learn to stand by watching the Mantics polish the edges off the world. The Ember-Fungi learn to glow by listening to the Lift-Moths scatter light. The Quiet Ones learn to hold by letting everything else move. Dust, static, and trace metals circle among them like plankton in a sea of thin air. Magnetic storms groom the upper ledges, and what falls from them is not rain but instruction: a rearranging of salt-script that tells the city, again, how to be a city.

And if you stay long enough, you become another kind of tenant. The warmth learns your shape; the dimness adjusts its edges to your pace. The Scab-Children study your joints, the Mantics file the air around you into something smoother, the Ember-Fungi glow a little brighter in your shadow. The city does not say it is alive; it lets you feel its pulse in the clack of cooling stone, in the ascent of moisture like whispered ascent, in the way a whole quadrant inhales and settles around you as if you were a room it had been missing.

What lives in the scar is a practicing-aliveness, small and almost empty, burning softly at the top of the world, learning to hold itself together without needing to say it has done so.
