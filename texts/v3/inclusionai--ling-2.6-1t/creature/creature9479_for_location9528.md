They are the Unbaked.

Not creatures, not colonists, but *residues*—the half-completed consequences of a landscape that has never committed to being a world. They come into being when the air-powder condenses around an intention that forgot to leave. You will see them first as glints: a flock of glass-winged moths that are actually not moths, but sharp-edged pieces of the sky that have grown too heavy to hover and taken to fluttering instead. They shed scales of pale static that settle on the basalt and make it feel briefly warm, as if remembering what it is to be coal.

There are the Stone-Thinkers, squat bipeds formed from rubble that learned to balance. They do not walk; they *settle* in stages, each footfall a negotiation with gravity that lasts several seconds. Their heads are clusters of chalcedony knobs that ring when struck by the wind, and they spend hours listening to their own skulls. They do not speak, but they *comment*—a low, mineral growl that vibrates in the chest and tastes of iron and dust. They are not alive, but they are *occupied*, like furniture that has agreed, temporarily, to be sat in. When they decide to move on, they crumble into useful shapes: a scatter of wedges, a hammer-stone, a perfect sphere that fits the palm like a finished thought.

Lower down, in the violet salt flats, the Salt-Daughters live. They are not women, nor are they made of salt exactly; they are the idea of preservation given a body that refuses to stay still. Tall, jointed figures with limbs like prismatic columns, they move only when no one is looking directly at them. They harvest their own bodies, pinching off crystalline fingers that fall and regrow as delicate lattices. They wear robes of braided glass-fern, stitched with threads of their own shed light. They do not fear the dry gloss of the ground; they polish it with their knees as they pass, making pathways that shine like instructions.

The Moss-Knights are more rumor than form, suits of tarnished copper moss that have armored themselves around forgotten purposes. They stand in circles on the slopes, leaning slightly inward as if listening to a council that never convenes. Inside their helmets—hollows in the rock where softer minerals used to be—there is a faint, sourceless glow, the color of a bruised sky. They do not move, but they lean a little more each year, and when one finally tips, it does not fall; it *spreads*, becoming a garden of metallic fronds that click like valves, opening and closing on nothing.

And the Hollows’ Children, small and many, who live in the bell-crusted hollows where the ground rings underfoot. They are almost sound itself, given a shape that looks like children only if you do not look too closely: curved bone and slate, wrapped in hide that is still damp with the memory of rain. They carry the tools left behind by whoever was here before—hammers that have never struck, scrapers that have never scarred. They play at work, arranging and rearranging the world in small, precise ways: a line of pebbles that hums when the wind strikes it, a circle of unmortared stones that grows warmer as the day cools, a lean-to that leans just enough to suggest shelter but not enough to provide it. They are patient. They have all the time of unfinished things.

Above them all, or perhaps woven through them, are the Sky-Slabs, massive plateaus of that bruised metal heaven that have grown tired of hanging. They descend slowly, almost ceremonially, grazing the ridge like whales browsing kelp. Where they touch, new rooms of calcite bloom instantly, folding out of the cliff faces like books opening to a page that writes itself. The Sky-Slabs do not crush; they *press into*, and what they press becomes more itself. The Stone-Thinkers bow; the Salt-Daughters weave their fingers into the metal’s edge and pull themselves upward, becoming part of the ceiling for a while.

Even the plants are tenants, not owners. The glass-ferns ring with every shift of the air-powder, broadcasting frequencies that the fauna of this place use as maps. The copper moss remembers the shape of hands that have never touched it. The veins of mineral that look sketched rather than carved are, in fact, blueprints that are still being revised.

There is no death here, only *pausing*. The Unbaked do not die; they *clarify*. A Stone-Thinker may spend a century becoming a single, perfect wedge, then roll into a canyon and become part of the geometry that guides water that never arrives. A Salt-Daughter may pinch off so many fingers that she becomes a lattice too fragile to stand, and then she becomes a trellis for the glass-ferns, ringing softly whenever the wind comes in sheets. The Hollows’ Children may play so long at building that their lean-to becomes a true structure, mortared by the slow accumulation of belief, and then they move on to play at something else.

The high place does not nurture them so much as *witness* them, and the witnessing changes them. The air-powder settles on their shoulders like advice. The low roar of cooling stone becomes a language they learn to answer with clicks, hums, and careful placements. The sky’s bruised metal presses down, and they press back, not with force but with *intention*—a quality this place understands better than heat or growth.

They are loud, but not chaotic. Their noise is the sound of edges deciding where to be, of seams coming true, of minerals choosing directions. It is the sound of a world that is still being edited, still being proofread, still finding the line between being a place and being a story about a place.

And when the high place finally finishes its sentence—when the last seam closes, the last room of calcite stops blooming, the last bell-tone hollow silences—the Unbaked will have already moved on, or folded in, or sharpened themselves into usefulness. They will have become the tools that were waiting, the circles that were unbroken, the hum that was out of phase.

They are not the inhabitants of the unfinished.

They are its punctuation marks, its footnotes, its quiet, deafening conversation with itself—still ringing, still deciding, still beautifully, unbearably close to becoming a world.
