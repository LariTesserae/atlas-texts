What lives there is not a creature **in** the chamber so much as a creature **of** the chamber’s boundaries—a young, distributed organism that has not yet decided whether it is plant, animal, or weather.

At first you miss it, because your eye keeps searching the hollow center for a body. But its body is the rind: the root-felt, the braided vascular cords, the leaf-skins lacquered to stone. Each seam is a nerve, each moss pad a mouth, each crack a fresh tooth coming in. It is juvenile and territorial in the way seedlings are territorial—without malice, but with absolute urgency. New mineral appears, and it is already touching it by dawn.

If you wanted a name, the tunnel foragers might call it something like the **Wall-Breather**.

Its intelligence is hydraulic before it is neural. It thinks in pressure waves, in the timing of stomata opening together, in the percussion of sap knock and cavitation pop. The chamber’s bass exhale is not background noise; it is speech. Long phrases are sent as humidity pulses across opposite walls. Quick decisions happen as leaf-slap volleys. Memory is held in which conduits stay primed and which are allowed to embolize. It remembers by keeping some pathways easy and others difficult.

It also has a second anatomy, harder to accept: a temporal one.  
Those lagging shadows are part of it. The little delays in gravity, the elastic distances, echoes returning too soon—these are not random glitches but local muscular contractions in reality’s fabric, like peristalsis in space and time. The Wall-Breather tests the world by flexing it. It learns what is stable by briefly making it unstable and seeing what returns to form.

When it must act in the hollow center, it does not send a permanent limb. It assembles temporary ones: condensation beading into filament veils, pollen fog thickening around electrostatic leaf charge, root tendrils extending and retracting in one wet breath. Witnesses report “figures” only in fragments—an arm-length of vine where none was, a suspended knot of droplets that turns as if listening, a silhouette with no caster. These are not ghosts. They are momentary organs.

The pale tunnel foragers are not its servants, but neither are they independent visitors. They are opportunists in a mutual bargain so old it already looks instinctive: they pry nutrient nodules; in doing so they wound and ventilate tissue, remove overgrowth, carry spores on skin and tools. They are, effectively, moving fingertips the Wall-Breather does not have to grow itself.

So: what lives there?

A single, still-forming colonial being that occupies every surface and uses the empty center as a lung, an ear, and occasionally a hand. A thing that drinks rain while it falls, dreams in delayed shadow, and is busy—ceaselessly busy—teaching stone to become flesh.
