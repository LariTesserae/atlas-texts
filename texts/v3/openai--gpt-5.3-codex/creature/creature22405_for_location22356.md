At first, you’d name the inhabitants by size and noise.

You’d say this place belongs to **the herds**: white-backed ash grazers moving in woven sheets, six-legged and blunt-faced, their teeth made for shaving mineral reeds and salt crust. You’d say it belongs to the **pylon beasts**—solitary, tall-backed walkers that push through botanical walls and carry dangling ecologies on their spines: nest-fans, parasite vines, hitching gliders, whole little weather systems of spores.

Then you’d notice the fast things between them:

- lacquer-black burrowers that “swim” through hot dust, blind but exquisitely sensitive to footfall;
- ribbon climbers draped from ceiling mats, dropping in loops to snatch and recoil;
- membrane gliders that never commit to ground, only hook, shiver, and relaunch;
- chitinous ambush predators that key not to sight but to pressure changes and gas chemistry.

But after a while, it becomes clear those are only the visible citizens.

### The people in it (if “people” is the right word)

The tool-makers here are often called—by the few outsiders who survive long enough to compare notes—**Drumfolk** or **Thrum Clans**.

They are not cave-dwellers in the usual sense. They are **route-dwellers**: opportunists who camp in moving safety windows and abandon them without sentiment. Their settlements are temporary by design—lashwork scaffold nests, ash-ring fire pits, wall niches lined in hide and fiber, ladder cuts into soft columns that may shear away by next cycle.

They look built for this underland’s specific violence:

- skin sealed with oil-clay pastes against desiccation;
- heavy brow and jaw sinuses that read vibration through bone;
- reduced dependence on airborne hearing (too unreliable here);
- enlarged olfactory membranes for vent-smell forecasting;
- tongue and palate receptors tuned to salts, resins, and trace metals.

They communicate in **layered channels**: heel-thumps through ground, wrist-click percussion on hollow reed-bone, mirror flashes, scent bursts crushed from plant sacs, and high-contrast pigment marks on skin and gear. Spoken voice exists, but mostly at close range and mostly for intimacy.

They do not “farm” in open plots. They **broker hydration**: tapping oil bladders without triggering predator calls, carrying resin-wax packs, raising gut-culture starters, and trading split-sacs of water tissue like medicine. A successful hunt is as much about fluids as meat.

Their children learn rules before names:
quiet basin = leave, violet-veined bladder = never cut, spiral vine braid = run.

### What truly dominates

The deepest resident is harder to classify.  
Many clans believe the underland itself is a living body. They may be right.

The continuous botanical fabric behaves like a **distributed organism**—not a tree, not a fungus, not anything surface taxonomies handle neatly. It propagates as ropes, fronds, sacs, and mineral-root mats, but also as timing: synchronized blooms across distance, gas releases that alter migration lanes, rapid overgrowth at fresh fractures as if sealing wounds.

Standing stones that hum at fixed tones appear to be partly carved by the clans, partly grown by this fabric. Around them, animal traffic bends. Predator density drops. Spores settle differently. It is as if these are pacemakers in a giant subterranean physiology.

So what lives here is not just a fauna list.  
It is a **stacked society**:

1. swarms and herds that convert salt, resin, and stone-bloom pollen into motion;
2. mobile humanlike clans that survive by reading sound as weather;
3. and beneath both, a chamber-spanning vegetative intelligence that may be less “background ecosystem” and more **host**.

If you stay long enough, that last idea gets difficult to dismiss:  
the others do not merely inhabit this place.

They circulate through it like blood.
