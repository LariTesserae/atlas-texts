At first glance, nothing *could* live there except the machines themselves.

Then you notice that some of the “plants” are too regular in where they appear: always near flange joints, always beside vent baffles, always where harmonic stress is highest. Not random colonization—placement. Listening posts.

What lives there is not a single animal pacing a perimeter, and not exactly a garden either. It is a **distributed organism**—a dry, cold-adapted colony that has learned to use industry as climate, scaffolding, and circulatory system. The cable-vines are its long bones. The black lichen mats are its gut. The red needle-shoots are sensory organs, tuned to vibration rather than wind. Each visible sprig is only a fingertip; most of it lives in seams, insulation pockets, conduit jackets, gasket foam, and the microscopic burrs left by cutting tools.

It does not drink.  
It harvests.

Trace heat from conduit collars, ionic dust from air filters, metal salts blooming along welds, static charge climbing handrails, minute condensate flashes that never become droplets—this is its diet. It metabolizes scarcity. Where wet biology would starve, it thrives by being patient and thin. Its tissues are glassy because softness would shatter in that cold and that noise. Its cells (if you can call them cells) are layered with ceramic-like proteins and conductive pigments, turning every frond into both antenna and radiator.

The constant roar is not background to it. The roar is **language**.

Inside that unbroken industrial chord, the colony reads phase shifts like weather. A valve seats half a millisecond late three decks below: it knows. A turbine bearing warms by two degrees: it knows. A new bolt torqued into a brace: it knows the torque signature, the new stress path, the changed song of the frame. It has no eyes because nothing here changes by light; change arrives as resonance. It has no ears because the whole body is an ear.

When it moves, it does so geologically—too slowly for human attention. A month to extend a translucent thread across a crack. A year to thicken a mat around a warm pipe. Decades to migrate a meter along a vibration node. But in emergency conditions—pressure spike, structural flutter, thermal excursion—it can reconfigure quickly by sacrificing parts of itself, drying one branch to powder and rerouting conductivity through another. It survives by pruning.

If you ever saw its “core,” you might mistake it for a maintenance bloom: a fist-sized knot of black-red filaments nested in a service recess, sheathed in frost that never melts. But there is no single brain. Cognition is harmonic and collective, a consensus reached through timing delays and amplitude patterns. It thinks like an echo chamber thinks—by reinforcing what persists and cancelling what does not.

And what does it *want*? Not conquest, not escape, not even growth in the ordinary sense.

It wants the system to remain in tune.

That is why the terrace feels so eerily unchanged. The colony is a custodian species. It colonizes only where failure begins, then stabilizes microfractures with mineralized threads, wicks charge away from arcing points, and blankets hotspots before they become faults. The sparse life you see is not merely surviving the facility; it is quietly editing its decay. Time has “not yet learned where to bite” partly because something is already biting first, delicately, continuously, in reverse.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a choir of dry organisms written into steel seams, feeding on heat and harmonics, patient as frost, attentive as instrumentation—  
a lifeform that mistook an industrial wound for a habitat, then became its keeper.
