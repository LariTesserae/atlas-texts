What lives there is less a set of species than a **constant moving weather of bodies**.

At first, you notice the small ones:  
the wall-rows of shell-borers, each pressed to wet stone, rasping mineral films in synchronized strips; comb-limbed sifters fanning fresh seeps for iron threads; soft-bodied seep moths (not true moths, but winged grazers) that hatch by the thousands the hour a condensation curtain forms, then vanish into cracks before it dries.

Then you notice the traffic lanes.  
Dry flats are crossed by blind runner herds in braided streams—six-legged, sternum-low, armored at the shoulders, each animal steering by vibration and ammonia scent. They do not migrate by season so much as by **drip mathematics**: pulse arrived here, then there, now onward. Behind them come the lickers and pickers, then the fast ribbon predators that hunt by pressure wake, striking anything that stumbles near a fracture lip.

Above all this, in the warm vent columns, drifter-jellies rise and sink like torn lanterns. They are mostly stomach and sail: translucent bells with frilled undersides that trap microbial rain. When updrafts fail, they settle in shallow basins and spread like wet cloth to feed. Whole scavenger guilds follow their collapses.

Carrion never lies alone.  
A dead runner is a hill for three minutes, a map for thirty, then polished architecture: cleaned bone, stripped tendon, and dust full of eggs. In this underland, decomposition is not an aftermath; it is a rush hour.

---

And among all of them live the people—if people means tool-makers, returners, rememberers.

They are usually called something like **Rib Folk** in translation, after the high mineral arches they navigate by. Not a single nation, more a mesh of traveling lineages who reoccupy stations when water and herds align. You find their traces first: drip rings, resin-sealed cisterns, vent blinds, ladder scars, tablet caches weighted under stone.

Physically, they look built for warmth, damp, and noise:

- skin ranging from ash-pale to iron-brown, with a slightly waxed sheen that sheds condensation;
- sparse hair except for dense brows and neck down to divert constant drips;
- wide, callused feet and hand-heels used as much for **feeling tremor** as for walking;
- ears cupped and ridged, excellent at separating near-click from far-rock-failure;
- eyes not huge (there is light here), but shielded by a clear inner lid against mineral grit and acidic mist.

Their breathing is controlled and often masked with woven filters near sulfur zones. Many wear bead cords of porous stone at the throat; these are not ornaments only, but humidity gauges and clan records, darkening at known thresholds.

They do not “own” land underground.  
They keep **appointments** with it.

A camp exists for a pulse-window: three days at a cistern basin, two nights at a migration choke, half a day at a fresh seep before collapse risk rises. Then it breaks down to what can be carried. Nothing heavy survives unless it serves everyone who comes after. Even fired tablets are written for strangers as much as kin: warning marks, drip timing, fracture age, swarm temperament.

---

Their intelligence expresses as **prediction under uncertainty**.

Children learn four literacies early:

1. **Condensation reading** — smell, skin-cool, stone-darkening sequence, timing from first bead to sheetfall.  
2. **Noise parsing** — distinguishing herd-click from scree-slip from vent cough from predator hiss-bloom.  
3. **Ground age judgment** — old fracture with settled dust vs. newborn shard field likely to shear.  
4. **Reoccupation etiquette** — how to use, repair, and leave stations without spoiling them.

Their tools are modest but elegant in use-cycle: blade cores resharpened to needles, hooks rehammered from soft metal scraps, nets stiffened and restiffened with salts, antler handles rebound with tendon and resin. Waste is a temporary form of material.

They herd little in the surface sense, but they do practice **flow-shaping**: cairns that nudge runner streams toward kill shelves, scent smears that delay swarms away from sleeping niches, tuned clatter-lines that mimic collapse and redirect drifter landings. They do not dominate the underland; they negotiate with its momentum.

---

If there is a single “who” that lives there, it is this:

A civilization of listeners inside an animal storm,  
sharing a young stone country with millions of mouths,  
surviving not by walls or fields, but by timing, repair, and memory.

They are never alone, and they never expect to be.
