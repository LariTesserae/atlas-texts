What lives there is not a single creature.

It is a **standing crowd**—a population so dense and so rehearsed that, from a distance, it behaves like one animal with a thousand bodies.

At its top edge are the fliers: bat colonies layered like weather along the high vaults, swifts and martins that commute through the light-wells, owls posted in the dim mouths of shafts. They are the visible pulse, the part you hear first: wingcloth shivering, brief impacts of claws, sudden silences that mean something passed overhead.

On the walls and broken slopes are the runners and clingers: cave crickets in moving bands, roaches and beetles in guano seams, pale spiders stretched between cracks, mites and worms turning dung and dead wings into black, rich bands of living grit. These are the country’s true laborers. They take whatever falls—fruit dropped by birds, bat waste, drowned insects, shed skin, old bone—and feed it back into the system. They are why the place can stay loud year after year.

In the water pockets live the still ones: blind fish that feel pressure better than light, thin-limbed amphibians with milk-white bellies, larvae that hang beneath surface film and vanish when shadow crosses. Nothing in those pools wastes motion. A drop, a ripple, a body enters the basin: then all at once, life.

Between all of these move the small predators, then the larger ones. Snakes thread warm fracture lines and wait where routes pinch narrow. Fox-sized mammals—pale, long-whiskered, light-footed—patrol habitual paths along ledges and rubble fans, hunting by smell and echo and memory of traffic. They are clever in the practical way: they crack shells on the same favored stones, wedge food in cracks, nose loose sticks into crevices for grubs. They make no homes that last, but they inherit paths, ambush points, sleeping shelves. Their culture is repetition.

So if you ask *who lives there*, the best answer is:

A **chorus ecosystem** built on imported light and local patience.  
A people of routes more than rooms.  
A civilization of habit without architecture.

No ruler, no monument—just enduring lines of movement in stone: roost, drop, feed, hunt, molt, flood, dry, repeat.

The underplain’s resident is ultimately this:  
**abundance held in place by geometry**.
