What lives there, most truly, is **a timed people**.

Not people in the legal sense—no badges, no language you could transcribe—but a dense, old lineage of whiskered hopper-mammals that have made that wet court their entire world. In maintenance logs they used to be classed as pests. In the court, they are citizens. Everything else—crabs, gliders, ribbon-swimmers, beetle-valves, even the patrol predators—moves around the schedules they read best.

They are small, shoulder-high to a human knee, long-backed and spring-loaded, with wide splayed forepaws that can cling to algae-slick ceramic. Their fur lies flat in the humidity and sheds water in beads; beneath it, the skin is dark and warm, threaded with vessels that dump heat fast when the discharge vents open. Their eyes are vestigial and pearl-filmed. Vision would be wasted there. They map the court through whiskers, paw pads, and jawbones—feeling pump harmonics through metal ribs the way shoreline mammals feel surf through sand.

They do not “listen” for quiet; they listen for **gaps**.  
When turbine pitch dips by half a note, they speak: throat clicks, tooth-chatter, forepaw taps on conduit skin. Family groups exchange route maps this way—where the six-minute dead zone will appear, which intake is swallowing this cycle, where warm condensate is rich with mineral film. To an outsider it sounds like random ticking under thunder. To them it is street traffic, gossip, warning, invitation.

Their nests are precise engineering by instinct. Always above the third flood line, never the second. Glued with bio-resin, lined with stripped cable fiber, armored with discarded crab carapaces that clack when a predator noses too close. They do not defend territory as an area so much as a **time slot**: this ledge from minute 12 to 18; that grate from surge drop to fan spin-up. Two clans can share one crevice peacefully if their clocks do not overlap.

They farm without meaning to call it farming. Their repeated paths keep certain channels clear. Their droppings seed mats near vent warmth. They herd ribbon-swimmers into eddies by drumming on panel skins. They crack valve-beetles for the mineral-rich membrane and leave shell fragments that filter-crabs later rake clean. In this court, waste is just deferred food.

Their young are born during low-pressure windows, when the air is easiest to breathe. The elders—recognizable by broken whiskers and scarred noses—are living tide tables. A very old one can feel a pressure anomaly twenty heartbeats before the first drop moves “the wrong way.” Whole packs turn on that warning and survive flushes that would otherwise strip a ledge bare.

If you asked what single creature lives in that place, this is the closest answer:  
a blind, damp-furred, vibration-reading society that has synchronized its heartbeat to machinery so completely that the court and the colony function like one organism. The structure sheds water; they read it. The pumps change phase; they shift routes. The world roars; they speak in the seams.

They are not surviving in the machine.  
They are part of how it has remained inhabited long enough to feel old.
