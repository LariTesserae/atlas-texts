What lives there is not a single beast but a **compact civilization of heat**—an ecosystem so crowded and synchronized it behaves like one animal.

If you had to name the resident, you might call it **the Basin Choir**.

At the bottom are the soot-mats: black chemo-feeders that look like stains until warmth or touch makes them flinch. They are the pasture, the reef, the bread. Grazers move over them in patient, rasping bands—armored combers with crystal teeth, jointed like little mining tools. Behind them come the crack-fold hunters, pale and blade-thin, living in hinges of rock, unfolding only when vibration tells them a footfall is near. Nothing wastes motion. In a place where every calorie comes from stone chemistry or stolen flesh, speed is rare, precision is common.

Between plates of basalt run the **bead-colonies**: tunnelers made of many linked bodies that can behave like one long organism. They braid through hot seams, seal openings when gas turns toxic, reopen them when the pulse passes. Their burrows are the basin’s temporary architecture. Whole neighborhoods appear and vanish in a day.

Above, in the hottest lanes, drift the gliders—broad, low animals with heat-dark membranes. They do not fly so much as **read thermals** with their skin. They launch when vents surge, coast from ledge to ledge, fold flat against stone when the air goes wrong. Their shadows trigger panic in anything smaller, though even they are hunted when they land to drink dew.

And deeper than all of them—more inferred than seen—are the **pulse-keepers** in the vent throats: thick, blind, anchored organisms that tolerate temperatures nothing else can. Whether they cause the basin’s rhythms or merely survive them, no one can easily tell. But when pressure changes below, they drum it into stone: low-frequency tremors that race through the walls. The mats retract. Grazers flood out. Hunters reposition. Tunnel mouths close. Gliders launch. It looks like instinct, but it feels like choreography.

That is why the place seems coordinated. Most creatures there don’t rely on sight or sound first; they rely on **heat maps and vibration language**. A claw-tap, a belly-rub on obsidian, a timed scrape across clinker—signals that say feed, freeze, scatter, burrow, rise. Silence in the air; traffic in the rock.

Even reproduction follows the basin’s law. Eggs are cemented under mineral lips at exact temperatures. Larvae drift as dust-riders on convection currents. Molts are left in stacked drifts that become shelter for smaller mouths. Death is immediate habitat: carcasses are stripped, then colonized, then mineralized into new ledges.

So who lives there?

A thousand species, yes. But more truly: **a single, old, distributed metabolism** spread across mats, claws, membranes, spores, and burrows. A living network that treats heat as topography, stone as bloodstream, and every opening as temporary.

The old carved alcoves and stacked stones suggest visitors once tried to stay. They left almost nothing because there is no empty ground to claim. In that basin, occupancy is total. If you stand still long enough, you realize the rule is literal:

You are not observing life there.  
You are standing inside it.
