What lives there, most truly, is **the carried life**—animals that do not claim territory so much as borrow it between floods.

If you had to name a resident rather than a passerby, it would be the thing the scavengers avoid and the shell-builders cluster near: a blind, long-bodied predator-detritivore locals (if there were locals) might call a **stonewinder**.

At a distance, a stonewinder looks like a loose cable come alive: a body the length of a causeway span, slate-pale, skin pebbled with mineral scale, no obvious eyes, no fin display, no ornament at all. It moves in patient S-curves through broken stairwells and culverts, never rushing unless something else does. Along its jawline are pressure pits so sensitive it can read a collapse half a district away. Its mouth is narrow and extensible, built less for tearing than for sudden vacuum strikes—small fish shoals vanish into it like filings into a magnet field.

But the stonewinder is not just a hunter. In this basin, nothing can afford to be only one thing. It feeds on carrion pinned in grates, on filter drifters concentrated in upwellings, on crustacean beds scraped from old rivet-lines. It will even nose through fresh fractures for mineral-rich bacterial film, taking thin nutrition where it can. It is a creature of salvage metabolism: part predator, part janitor, part current-reader.

Its young are the most place-shaped of all. Eggs are glued in old bolt-holes and expansion seams, in those chalky shell-builder mounds on vertical walls. The hatchlings do not swim well at first; they cling. They learn the basin by being peeled off it repeatedly—first freshet, first spillway surge, first silt storm—until they can let go on purpose and reattach somewhere better. Survival is not “finding shelter.” Survival is learning which shelters are about to stop being shelters.

They communicate in the language the basin makes available: **impact and pressure**. Tail to hollow slab. Jaw to ceramic plate. A sequence of knocks and low pulses transmitted through stone and water. To anything else it is just infrastructure settling. To another stonewinder it is map, warning, mating call, claim, surrender. Their “songs” are slow because the world is slow: messages about a wall that has been bending for years, a corridor that is now a jet, a feeding ground that will likely exist for one more melt season.

Around them, the rest of the fauna arranges itself like weather around topography. Shoals of blind fish track their slipstream for scraps. Translucent crustaceans clean their scales. Long-jawed ambush hunters shadow juveniles. When an adult dies, it becomes, for a year or two, a moving district of life: shell mounds on bone, drifters in the rib-cage eddies, scavengers nesting in the skull until the whole body is dismantled and carried into channels.

So the answer is not a single beast, not really.  
What lives there is a way of being:

- no roots, only holds  
- no permanent homes, only intervals  
- no conquest of the current, only literacy in it

In that drowned high basin, even the apex animal survives by surrendering early and often—by feeling flow before flow becomes force, and moving before the map is wrong.
