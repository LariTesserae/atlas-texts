What lives there is **not a town** so much as a **managed hunger with a memory**.

At first glance you’d say people. And yes—there are people, unmistakably: tool-making hands, gossip, rules, lullabies, jealousies, apprenticeships, grief. But if you stay, you realize the true resident is a **woven colony** of humans, plants, beasts, fungi, insects, and stored heat, all coupled so tightly they function like one body spread across stone.

The humans call themselves, in their own tongue, something that translates roughly to **“those who keep the seam breathing.”** Outsiders shorten it to seamfolk.

They are compact and tendon-lean, not from deprivation so much as constant climbing. Calves and forearms do the work of pack animals here. Their skin tends toward ash-brown or sun-amber under dust, often rubbed with fat and mineral powder to reduce moisture loss. Most wear layered wraps of woven reed fiber and softened hide: pale on the outside to throw heat, darker inside to hide stains from dung-work, kiln ash, and plant sap. Children go half-feral through the vertical lanes—sure-footed by five, entrusted with hatchlings by seven, airflow chores by ten.

You notice their faces next: slit scarves for dust winds, eyelids darkened with soot-clay against glare, ears hung with little bone tags that clack softly when they move. Those tags aren’t ornament alone; they’re identity and function—water-harvester, pen-warden, crack-feeder, smoke-keeper, night-watch. Here, your trade is less what you *do* than what cycle you keep from failing.

They do not think in ownership the way valley farmers do. They think in **custody windows**:
- this shade screen is yours until hot-wind season turns;
- this root curtain is your care until fruiting ends;
- these three lizards are your burden until they molt;
- this ledge is your sleeping right unless flood-wind shifts and pens must move.

Even kinship is partly vertical. People speak of “upper blood” and “lower blood,” meaning not status but where your line has tended for generations: high seed-dry tiers or warm animal rings. Marriages often cross tiers to keep knowledge braided.

And the animals are not background. They are co-workers, food, alarm system, heat source, and sometimes law.

The pannier lizards—long-backed, patient, bad-tempered in mating season—carry mineral grit, moss mats, eggshell lime, and kiln fuel up impossible inclines. Cliff-hens convert scraps no one else can digest. Throat-runners (all throat and toes and attitude) are living weather vanes: when they cling low and go silent, bad wind is coming. Burrow grazers till the floor by accident and are thanked for it with guarded fungal feed. Even the scavenger beetles are semi-managed; children shake them from compost trays into sorting pans by species.

The lean, big-eared dusk predators at the perimeter are the nearest thing to neighbors. Not tamed. Not hunted to extinction either. The seamfolk leave them a designated offal trench beyond the lower paths. In return—or coincidence dressed as ritual—the predators patrol the outer scree and keep worse things from settling too near. At night, when the rule says stay to tier paths, everyone listens.

If you ask what *kind* of people they are, the best answer is: they are **maintenance specialists turned into a culture**.

Their calendar is not months but wind phases and birthing waves. Their festivals are practical at core: relashing day, first dew wring, hatch count, kiln opening. Their music is signal-born—short reed phrases that began as herding commands and became songs with harmonies shaped by stone echo. Their gods, if those are gods, are verbs: Vent, Hold, Return, Cure, Share.

They distrust waste the way sailors distrust fire. A dropped rind is not litter; it is misplaced feed. A cracked jar is not trash; it is future drainage tile. A dead animal is urgency: skin before flies, fat before heat, bone before scavengers, gut to ferment, sinew to lash, marrow for infants. Their children learn sorting before writing.

And yet they are not grim. Crowded, yes. Loud, always. But full of wit. They nickname stubborn lizards, paint rude cartoons in kiln soot, wager on which throat-runner clutch will survive, flirt over dung-sled routes, compose satirical verses about airflow inspectors. In a basin this tight, humor is ventilation.

So what lives there?

A people, certainly.  
But more exactly: **a breathing system that learned to wear human faces**—and many other faces besides—inside a hidden wound in the mountain, where water is mostly air, shade is currency, and survival is a daily act of choreography.
