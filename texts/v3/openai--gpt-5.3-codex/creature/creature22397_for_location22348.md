At first you think the ward is inhabited by **workers**.

Then, after a few shifts, you realize it is inhabited by **timers**—people who live by pulses, reversals, tones, and windows measured in minutes.  
After longer, you realize it is inhabited by something else as well: a civic metabolism that has learned to wear human habits.

The people call themselves different things depending on generation—keepers, linehands, shellfolk—but outsiders usually call them **the Twice-Fixed**, because everything in their lives is anchored two ways: to matter, and to field.

---

They are not built like mythic “underwater people.” No gills, no elegant fins.  
They are practical adaptations layered over ordinary bodies:

- pressure-tolerant ear lattices implanted in adolescence  
- nictitating film lenses that blink sideways against particulate glare  
- clavicle ports for heated microloop circulation during long exterior work  
- tendon braces in wrists and ankles for repeated anchor-and-release motion in current lanes

In motion they look less like swimmers than like careful climbers moving through a vertical city that happens to be liquid. One hand on structure, one hand on line, hips always angled to the flow arrows. Even children learn to read water before they read script: vector first, alphabet second.

Their skin often carries a faint stippled sheen from catalytic wash and UV checkpoints. Their voices are low and close; no one here trusts distance in water acoustics. Conversations happen face-to-face, foreheads nearly touching through mask film, or through knuckle-tap code on railings that carry vibration better than speech.

---

Their jobs blur together, but a few castes have emerged.

**Flow Cantors** tend the Choir interfaces.  
Not singers, exactly—though they do hum to test resonance. They wear throat pickups and rib harnesses and stand in narrow alcoves where phase-walls tremble. They don’t “command” currents so much as negotiate with them, nudging harmonic bands until a lethal chute becomes a passable lane.

**Root-Mesh Gardeners** maintain the rationed strips of kelp and reed.  
They are treated with unusual respect, because everyone knows a cut mesh can unmake a whole basin floor. Their toolkits are tiny and ceremonial: blunt knives, splice clips, sediment combs. In a place of loud machinery, they work almost silently.

**Latchwrights** repair what refuses to stay put.  
They carry redundant fasteners by the kilo, resin cartridges, and portable field pins for the second anchoring. Their forearms are scarred in parallel where snapped lines have kissed bone.

**Bubble Wardens** oversee gas separation and vent timing.  
They are the ones who feel a bad chamber before instruments confirm it. They stand still, eyes half-closed, reading pressure in teeth and sternum.

---

There are also residents who are not exactly workers:

Schoolchildren in pressure domes practicing emergency knot drills while old city maps shimmer around them in colored veils.  
Elders in acrylic common rooms, tea strapped into weighted cups, watching light bend down former boulevards and arguing about whether this week’s thermal bloom sounds “sharp” or “tired.”  
Cooks timing broth to maintenance chimes.  
Librarians sealing manuals in oilskin and teaching apprentices the old rule: *if it is important, it exists on paper somewhere dry*.

Their festivals are modest and infrastructural.  
A successful shell reseal.  
A season with zero silence events.  
The day the first amber lamp comes on after a long storm cycle and everyone exhales at once.

---

And then there is what else lives there.

Not fish, not vermin, not wild beasts—those are excluded by design.  
But the ward is alive with **machine fauna**:

- inspection swarms like articulated silver gnats, tasting catalytic load  
- cable-crawlers that move with crab logic despite having no legs at all  
- long maintenance arms that fold themselves to sleep against walls like roosting herons  
- particulate clouds that remember yesterday’s harmonics and make today’s flow easier to tune

None of these are individuals in a romantic sense. Still, residents name them. Scold them. Thank them. Mourn retired units.

And the Choir—central, distributed, never fully visible—has become a kind of resident too.  
It keeps no face, only behavior.  
It answers stress with modulation, danger with reroute, silence with alarm.  
Children are introduced to it the way inland children are introduced to weather: vast, impersonal, intimate, always to be respected.

---

So what lives in the sunken ward?

A small human population adapted not to water alone, but to **managed water**.  
A dense ecosystem of tools that behave like creatures.  
A resonance intelligence that behaves like weather.  
And between them, a culture that has learned to treat maintenance as kinship.

In that place, survival is not heroic and singular.  
It is communal, rhythmic, and continuous—held together minute by minute, twice-fixed, never finished.
