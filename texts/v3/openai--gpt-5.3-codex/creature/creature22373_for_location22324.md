At first, it seems like many creatures live there.

Then, after a few rain cycles, you realize one thing lives there in many bodies: **timing**.

The plateau is full of specialists, but none survives alone for long. Each species is a hand on the same clock.

Closest to the stone are the **seam-browsers**—burrowing grazers with blunt skulls and shovel forelimbs, pale-eyed and dust-coated, following hidden wet lines beneath the crust. They don’t graze “plants” so much as living films: root knots, wax mats, mineral lichens, and bacterial sheens that wake when condensation comes. They move in narrow files through old scree lanes, widening and polishing routes generation by generation. Where they pass often, the ground compacts into dark runnels that hold dew longer. They are engineers without intending to be.

Above them, almost always in motion, are the **line-striders**: long-legged scavengers with narrow torsos, high lungs, and patient gaits. They patrol basin-to-basin migration corridors and rim edges where anything tired must pass. Their heads stay still while their bodies sway, as if measuring distance by pendulum. They are not fast sprinters; they are relentless accountants. They arrive at the right place three hours before death and wait.

When pressure drops and humidity swells, cracks in the basalt release the **fizz-swarms**—small night-fliers that spend most of life folded in stone. They rise in soft, static clouds, feed, mate, and vanish before true dawn. For a few damp hours they are everywhere: in vent plumes, over temporary pans, around reed ghosts in drainages. Then they are gone so completely you doubt they existed.

Predators are built like punctuation marks.

At choke points where ridge and basin pinch together, you find **ledge-anchors**: low, heat-conserving ambush animals that can slow their pulse to almost nothing, then strike with terrifying precision when migration traffic thickens. At condensation fields live **film-hunters** with broad, velvet feet and slit nostrils, able to scent fresh wet stone at distance. Near warm tube mouths wait **vent cats**—not feline, but similarly economical—using rising mineral air to mask their scent while they watch tunnel exits.

Nothing in this world wastes death.  
Carcass processors arrive in layers:

- first skin-pickers and soft-tissue mites,
- then plate-jawed beetles that can chew tendon and leathery hide,
- then bone-raspers that etch calcium into powder,
- then dormant spores and microbes that bloom in the brief damp.

A large body can be translated back into the system in a single night pulse. By next dusk, only polished joints and a stain in the grit remain.

And underneath all of this is the deeper resident: the **Breath Network**.

Old lava tubes inhale and exhale with thermal tides—warm mineral air on a schedule so regular local life has keyed itself to it. Vent moss darkens and opens when exhalation starts. Insects emerge on the first warm draft. Seam-browsers reroute toward tube fields just before condensate peaks. Line-striders alter patrol arcs to intersect those routes. Predators shift stations accordingly. Even microbial blooms in shallow pans seem phase-locked to vent cycles. No central brain governs it; the pattern is distributed across thousands of responses.

So what lives there?

Not a kingdom, not a single apex beast, not a hidden civilization.

A **metabolism at landscape scale** lives there: creatures of dusk and stone that have evolved to treat scarcity as structure, weather as signal, and silence as usable space. Every body is spare. Every movement is budgeted. Every pause has purpose.

If you stand in that dim warmth long enough, you stop asking which animal is dominant.

The dominant organism is the plateau itself, and the animals are its moving organs.
