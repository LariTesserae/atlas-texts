What lives there first is not an animal.

It is a **weave**—a living construction layer spread across nearly everything: moss-felt, cork-hard fungal trunks, root curtains, hollow reeds, vine-cables under tension. Not “plants” in the forest sense, and not a single organism either. More like a cooperative occupation force that arrives after every fracture and says: *this surface is ours now*.  

In the basin, stone is scaffolding. The weave is the resident.

It behaves almost like weather:

- In bright cycles, broad leaf fields unfurl and drink light through the thin roof.
- In cold pulses, mats cinch shut, trapping damp heat close to the substrate.
- At night-equivalent cooling, condensate gets caught, wicked, and routed downward through stems and root braid.
- After collapses, pioneer films appear within days—pale green skin on raw mineral, then thicker felts, then load-bearing growth that can hold sediment, then whole hanging gardens from nothing.

So the first answer is: **infrastructure lives there**, and everything else lives in it.

---

Then come the small movers, usually unseen until they aren’t.

Hand-sized gliders with membrane fins skim from fern crowns to moss banks. They are not built for long flight—just efficient falling in a place of vertical options. Blind nibblers nose through root-thatch, following fungal chemistry more than light. Long-legged pool waders arrive at temporary silver basins, feed hard for an hour, vanish before ice skins over.  

There are cold-tolerant insects that hatch in synchronized bursts right after glass rain: one bright window, frantic pollination and mating, then absence again. Their entire visible lives are timed to moisture events, not seasons.

Most animals here are **event specialists**. They don’t defend broad territory. They patrol probabilities.

---

Larger herbivores exist, but in low numbers and great spacing: rangy, narrow-bodied browsers that can stand still for long periods without losing heat, with translucent ear-fans threaded by dark vessels. They feed on frost-burned reeds and mineral-rich leaf margins where seep chemistry is right. You may see one once in a week and then find its tracks in five different light wells—always moving, never herding loudly.

Predation is quiet and close-range. No roaring apexes. Ambush from vine span, sudden drop from root curtains, brief struggle muffled by wet growth. Even violence is absorbed by the basin.

---

And people live there too, but lightly, as if borrowing space from a much older tenant.

Not empire-builders—**timers**. Route readers. Condensation herders.

Some travel in small bands along recurring drip corridors, carrying reed felt, fired-clay catchers, and ladders of split stem. Others keep fixed camps at reliable moisture wells and farm cold moss on scaffold racks, harvesting it for food, insulation, and trade. Their maps are vertical and thermal: they track draft lanes, roof translucence, stone freshness, fog behavior. They count not days but **light cycles** and **water events**, carving both into marker totems.

Their tools stay simple because simplicity survives here: stone edges, antler wedges, plant-fiber line, resin-sealed cisterns, and rare meteoritic iron blades treated like heirlooms or law.

A basin child learns early:
1) fresh rock means recent danger,  
2) silence can mean change,  
3) height is warmth with a price,  
4) thirst is solved by timing, not by walking farther.

---

So if you ask what lives in that underworld, the truest answer is:

A **cold green civil engineering project run by biology**,  
with animals appearing like punctuation,  
and humans living as careful footnotes in the margins of its ongoing draft.
