What lives there is not a “beast” in the storybook sense.  
It is a temperament that has taken bodies.

At the smallest scale, the basin is inhabited by films: microbial sheens on wet stone, fungal lace in peat, moss that behaves like weatherproof fur laid over rock. These are the true permanent citizens. They do not fight the hollow’s noise or cold; they translate it. Mist becomes water reserve. Mineral stain becomes foothold. Weak light becomes slow sugar. Even in stillness they are busy—binding soil, softening shale, storing seasons in millimeters.

Then there are the brief tenants of motion: ledge-nesting seabirds that come in hard weather windows, foxes that pass through like punctuation marks, insects that hatch all at once and vanish, fish that arrive in flood pools as if dropped from another map. None of these define the place. They touch it and move on.

The ones who *belong* in a human way are the line-keepers—small groups who return, not settle. They are people of repeat use: practical, quiet, and deeply literate in terrain. They do not build up; they tuck in. Turf shelters sit low behind boulders, driftwood ribs are tied and retied, cairns are nudged back into true each season. They understand that in the roaring hollow, hearing is ornamental. Sight is law.

So they live by visibility discipline.  
No one walks a path that bends out of line without marking it first.  
No one speaks unless close enough to touch a shoulder.  
Children learn points, not names: *that stone from this stone; that notch from that cairn.*  
Distance is taught as “three clear bodies” or “half a hand of fog,” not as shouted numbers.

Their language, when they use it in the basin, is mostly in the hands and elbows: flat palm for halt, two fingers for split, fist against chest for “I have seen.” At night or in mist, they use tiny directed lights shielded by horn or cupped bark, never open flame unless down in a cut where wind can’t grab it. They peg ropes into old antler holes not because they are weak walkers, but because they plan for the moment the basin decides to erase edges.

Physically, they look ordinary at a distance—thick wool, oilskin, peat-dark boots—but close up their gear reveals what the place has made of them. Hoods are cut wide at the periphery so sightlines stay broad. Gloves have textured pads for rapid touch-signing. Cloaks carry stitched bead-rows that can be counted by thumb in zero visibility: one row means home cairn line, two means bog crossing, three means coastal break. Tools are blunt, repairable, and quiet. Nothing shiny. Nothing that rings.

They do not call themselves owners. At most, they are “those who remember the crossings.” Their work is seasonal and exact: harvest moss in strips without scalping it, cut sedge high so roots hold, lift fish from temporary pools before they foul, gather bird eggs only from certain ledges and only once. They leave markers for their future selves, and for anyone else wise enough to read restraint.

If you asked them what lives in the roaring hollow, they might tap the stone and shrug.  
Not evasive—accurate.

Because the oldest resident is the basin’s pressure-song itself: that ceaseless under-roar from sea channels and wind throat. It enters ribs, gait, timing, sleep. It has selected for patience, low profiles, strong memory, and a culture of never assuming you were heard. Everything that persists there—moss, cairn, person—has learned the same rule:

Stay visible. Stay deliberate. Endure.
