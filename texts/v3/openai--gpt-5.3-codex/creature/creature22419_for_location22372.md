At first you think the residents are the stoneworkers—the quiet, rope-scarred people in layered hides, moving along wet stairs with baskets and hooks.

Then you realize they are only one guild among many.

What lives there is a **compact civilization of listeners**: humans, semi-tamed herds, opportunistic roost-creatures, and fully wild predators, all sharing one unstable bowl of rock by obeying timing, moisture, and line-of-sight more than borders.

### The human inhabitants

They’re often called (by outsiders) basinfolk, but among themselves identity is more specific and practical: *cistern-keepers, wall-menders, mist-netters, root-tenders, ledge-watchers, roost-counters*.  
A person is known less by family name than by what they maintain through one full wet-dry cycle.

Physically, they look shaped by thin air and damp cold: narrow-chested but wiry, strong calves, careful feet. Clothing is soft and layered—felted fiber, stitched hide, woven barkcloth—designed to stay warm when soaked and to dry from body heat alone. Sleeves and collars are often fringed not decoratively but functionally, to catch fog-drip. Hair is usually worn braided close or threaded through absorbent cords so morning condensation can be wrung into cups.

They speak little in open lanes. Not from fear—from ecology. Loudness scatters grazers, startles gliders into wasteful flight, and calls hunters to edges where children climb. So language has developed elbows and shadows: chin-tilt grammar, finger taps on stone, mirror ticks from terrace to terrace, colored strips on lines that shift with the hour’s warning code.

Their temperament reads as patient to outsiders, but it is really **high-frequency attention**. Everyone is always noticing: where grit has newly gathered, where moss has silvered too early, where a familiar herd path has drifted half a body-width upslope. Their memory is topographic and seasonal. Children can recite three generations of collapses by pointing to scars in the wall.

### The nonhuman citizens

The basin’s animals are not background wildlife; they are **co-occupants with predictable rights of way**.

- Pale membrane-eared cliff-goats are treated as living surveyors: if they stop using a stair, people inspect that stair for hidden shear.
- Long-legged moss grazers are followed by compost gatherers, because their dung seeds terrace gardens.
- Six-wing crevice gliders roost in designated wall sections and are never disturbed at first dim; local belief says they “count” the settlement’s breath then. Practically, disturbing them causes panicked swarms and broken necks.
- Sleeve-bodied dusk lizards are tolerated inside granaries because they eat velvet wall-insects before the insects strip bindings.
- Ink-thin vibration hunters are feared but respected; their movement maps dead zones in the substrate better than any tool.

Over time, architecture has become porous on purpose: warm ledges for insects, hollow beam channels for lizards, sacrificial roof strips for night-crossing herds. A “sealed house” is considered both unhealthy and arrogant.

### The social order

Their politics are hydraulic.

No one truly owns land in permanence; people hold **stewardship terms** over moving assets: a fog rack, a retaining wall segment, a seep terrace, a root scaffold. Rights pass by demonstrated maintenance, not inheritance alone. If your wall fails twice in one season and floods lower stairs, your claim is reviewed publicly.

Leadership is situational and rotating:
- In heavy mist, mist-net guilds call traffic.
- After falls, wall-menders gain temporary authority.
- During migration weeks, animal-path wardens overrule market routes.

Law is remembered as proverbs because proverbs can be shouted in one breath when stone starts to move:
- “Water has elder-right.”
- “Count what roosts before you cut.”
- “One ledge unwrought.”

They are practical people who permit myth because myth keeps attention sharp. “The cliff-kin” are spoken of as if ancestral neighbors—sometimes meaning specific species, sometimes the basin itself as a willful entity. Either way, the effect is the same: restraint.

### What they are, in the end

Not pioneers. Not conquerors. Not hermits.

They are a **maintenance culture** nested inside a younger, larger animal metropolis. Their genius is not monumental building but repeatable repair; not domination but negotiated timing. They live by reading condensation the way sailors read wind.

If you stay long enough, the category “wildlife” stops making sense there. There are only residents with different kinds of memory, moving through the same damp, dim hours—some on two legs, some on six wings, some on soft feet that leave no sound at all.
