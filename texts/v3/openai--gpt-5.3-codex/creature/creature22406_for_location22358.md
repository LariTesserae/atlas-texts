What lives there is not one species so much as a **guilded metabolism**.

At first you notice the obvious inhabitants: the workers.  
Not crowds—crews. Pump tenders, valve readers, moss-trainers, membrane stitchers, corrosion painters, heat-shift mechanics. They move like people who learned long ago that haste on wet stone is a kind of arrogance. Their boots are soft-soled and broad. Their clothes are layered for humidity, not rain: mesh under oilcloth, neck wraps full of charcoal fiber, gloves with ridged palms for slick railings. They carry listening rods more often than flashlights. In that district, hearing pressure changes matters more than seeing color.

They don’t talk much while walking. Not from secrecy—from acoustics. The place is too loud for casual speech, so language has migrated into touch and timing: two taps on a pipe means “load rising,” a hand swept low means “steam drift,” a pause at a junction means “let the water choose first.” Their culture is maintenance-shaped. Birthdays are remembered by storm seasons. Trust is measured by who shows up when a pump falls silent.

But the deeper residents are botanical and microbial.  
The reed lots, vine curtains, condenser ferns, root-grate colonies—these aren’t landscaping, they are organs. Biofilms lacquer the channels and do chemical work no machine can do as cheaply. Algae blooms where heat maps say they should. Fungal lace runs in wall cavities, filtering, digesting, binding mineral seep into usable crust. If the human crews are the hands, these mats and roots are the liver, lungs, and skin.

And then there is the resident that is hardest to name: the district itself, as a distributed animal-less intelligence.  
It senses through pressure, flow rate, conductivity, temperature, pH, vibration. It responds without deliberation in the way a body responds—gates adjust, vents purge, overflow paths reassign, shade cloths unfurl, pumps stagger load. No central “mind,” but a continuous self-correction. People who’ve worked there long enough stop saying “the system is running” and say “it’s breathing today.”

Why no animals live there is almost beside the point to locals. They could list likely causes—chemistry, heat cycling, dissolved traces, acoustic saturation, sterilizing steam events—but practically, it’s simply one of the district’s constants, like humidity and rust. The absence has become habitat. Life filled the available niche and became this: **human routine braided with engineered water and disciplined green growth**.

So what lives there?

A wet industrial quarter inhabited by keepers, rooted by plants, skinned by microbes, and animated by machinery so continuous it behaves like a single creature—quiet-biased, loud-bodied, and very much alive.
