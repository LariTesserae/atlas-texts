At first, it seems like nothing *lives there* except the city-forest itself.

Then you realize that’s true, but incomplete.

What lives in that basin is not a population arranged inside a habitat. It is a **stratified metabolism**: one ancient, self-editing organism with many local personalities. Kiln Grove is one mood of it. Lantern Hollows is another. The Old Rack is its scar tissue still doing useful work. “Districts” are just organs that learned names.

The most obvious life is structural: trunk-chimneys that breathe in shifts, canopy exchangers that sweat vapor and salts, root-bridges that tense under load like muscle. But beneath that is a quieter class of resident—what you might call **the tenders**, if you need a human word. They are not wildlife and not quite citizens in the old sense. They are grafted custodial lineages grown for corridor work: tall, heat-thrifty figures with ceramicized skin, conductive filaments threaded through their limbs, and respirators tuned to the basin’s pressure pulses. They move only when the pulse-lines permit it, pausing for long minutes as if listening through their bones. In silhouette they look like burnt saplings walking.

They don’t “build” so much as negotiate. They trim reactive overgrowth, lace cracked buttresses with living cable, seed vacant scaffolds, and mark delamination zones with electroluminescent mold. They carry spore canisters like prayer wheels. Their tools are mostly gradients—temperature, charge, pH, pressure timing. If you watch them at work, it looks less like labor than conducting: a hand raised, a valve opened somewhere out of sight, and three blocks away a bridge-root tightens.

There are other residents that never take a body you can follow.  
Maintenance mycelia thread every surface, deciding what gets fed and what gets dissolved. Static-borne spore swarms patrol bloom edges like weather with intent. Enzyme fog colonies sleep in duct lungs until a failure summons them; then they descend and erase a wall cleanly to template depth. In dead districts, memory lattices persist—electrochemical habits in fossil growth-rings, old routing logic still firing faintly when storms charge the basin. Sometimes a “ruin” answers a current pattern as if it still believes itself alive.

So who lives there?

- The **Basin Organism** itself, oldest and largest, forever pruning and regrowing.
- The **Graft Tenders**, pressure-bonded custodians who move through safe thermals.
- The **Mycelial and Spore Intelligences**, diffuse, distributed, more process than creature.
- The **Residual Minds of Infrastructure**, not ghosts, exactly—more like persistence.

None of them are separate for long. A tender dies and is rendered into feedstock, then returns as insulation skin, then as vascular braid, then as nutrient lacquer in a seed-capsule fired into a new scaffold. Identity is cyclical, not individual. Life there is measured less by heartbeat than by **throughput**: what can keep the hot dry dark in balance for one more growth-cycle.

In that sense, the truest answer is simple:

What lives there is a civilization that gave up the boundary between organism, machine, and city—and survived by becoming all three at once.
