At first, it seems like the obvious residents are the ones you can count in motion: rats on the cable runs, mosquitoes under lamps, frogs at the sump lip, bats in the truss dark.

Then you stay longer, and it starts to look like only **parts** are moving—not separate lives, but one distributed thing.

What lives there is a **quarter-animal** made of routes.

Its whiskers are rat lines: quick brown traffic testing every new conduit within a day of installation.  
Its blood is the shallow warm water, always recirculating, always taking the memory of rust and oil with it.  
Its breath is fanwash and condensation, exhaled through grates in slow wet pulses.  
Its eyes are not eyes at all but lamp halos full of insects, each cloud reporting heat, moisture, disturbance.  
Its reflexes are cats on ledges, sudden and economical where the light is poor and footing worse.  
Its immune system is roach swarms and beetles stripping anything organic down to clean error.  
Its night shift is bats, stitching arcs through turbulence exactly where mosquitoes peak.  
Its gut is the eel-fish channels, turning silt, swallowing what sinks.

It has no center you can point to. The pump house isn’t its heart; the heart can be replaced. The kiosk isn’t its brain; circuits can be rewired. Even the basin walls are temporary, still chalked for future concrete. What persists is the **agreement** among heat, water, machinery, and opportunists: drip here, gather there, hunt there, breed there, retreat when the brown line rises.

It learns fast in the small ways. A capped trench reroutes rat traffic by evening. A failed lamp shifts dragonflies to the next pool by morning. A new warm transformer creates a roach district in a week. A cat discovers it in two nights. Frogs move in after the third rain. By the time human crews call it “normal conditions,” the quarter-animal has already rewritten itself around the change.

If you insist on a single body, you might find one on the upper catwalk at 03:40: a maintenance worker in mineral-stiff coveralls, eating from a foil packet beside a humming panel, boots always above the stain line, knowing which rattles mean harmless slack chain and which mean a pump about to seize. The mosquitoes avoid his fan. The cats don’t. He taps a pipe before stepping past it, checks flow with two fingers, listens more than looks.

But even he isn’t separate. He is another organ: primate hands, tool use, memory over years instead of days. He repaints numbers. He clears intakes. He leaves a crate tilted just enough for cats to den in dry season. He curses, patches, logs, returns. The place has made room for him the way it made room for sedge in seams and moss under a persistent drip.

So what lives there?

Not a monster. Not a myth.  
A **working ecology**—humid, loud, and unsentimental—wearing city infrastructure as a shell.  
A creature with many small hungers and one large instinct:

keep flowing, keep warm, keep just ahead of flood.
