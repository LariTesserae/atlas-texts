At first glance, what lives there seems easy to name: swifts, frogs, macaques, bats, beetles, pigeons, mosses, vines, people on shifts.

But if you stay long enough, you start to feel that these are not separate residents so much as moving parts of a single local thing.

The oldest inhabitant is not an animal in the usual sense. Workers call it different names—**the Bloom**, **the Latch-Garden**, **the Up-Rain Mind**—but they all mean the same presence: a distributed, slow intelligence made of root-mat, wet metal, microbial films, valve timing, nest loyalty, and heat memory. It has no center. If you ask where it “is,” the honest answer is: everywhere moisture lingers and schedules repeat.

It lives in interfaces.

In the insulated seam where a warm pipe crosses a cold beam and a rare beetle always lays eggs.
In the sump where frogs click against the rhythm of pump pistons, and their breeding cycle now matches maintenance pulses.
In vine corridors that thicken where fan turbulence is gentlest, then thin again when turbine load changes for winter winds.
In lichen maps tracing leak histories more accurately than archived diagrams.
In old control boxes where condensation has fused spores, dust, and oxidized copper into conductive skin.

It learns in increments. Not by thought, exactly—by persistence.  
A misting line clogs every third dawn unless opened two seconds early, so eventually it is.  
A rookery shifts from Tower 2 to Tower 5 over twelve years; fruiting schedules quietly follow.  
A dead catwalk becomes a bat highway because warm vent plumes rise there, and then insects gather there, and then predation patterns rewrite there.  
Nothing dramatic. Everything cumulative.

If you tried to draw the creature, you would fail by drawing a body. Better to draw loops:

- air to net to cistern to tray to leaf to vapor  
- heat to plume to bat to guano to slurry to root  
- alarm yelp to startled flock to seed scatter to moss patch to retained water

That is what lives there: feedback with appetite.

The human workers are not outside it. They are a migratory organ. They bring replacement plates, patch fabric throats, clear intakes, curse at overflow alarms, and coin names for patterns they can’t fully model. Then they leave. The place keeps their habits if those habits work. It forgets the rest. In that way, the resident is older than any crew and younger than the last repair.

By dusk, when macaques bark from the cliff and turbine housings breathe out their stored warmth, you can feel the resident most clearly. The deck vibrates through your boots, leaves bead with machine-made dew, swifts carve black arcs through steam, and somewhere a valve opens half a beat before the timer says it should.

No one programmed that correction.  
No single species decided it.  
But the garden-factory did.

That is who lives there:  
an engine that has naturalized into ecosystem,  
an ecosystem that has operational memory,  
a cliff-top organism made of metal, water, heat, and time.
