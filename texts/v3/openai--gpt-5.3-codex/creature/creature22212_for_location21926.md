At first, it seems the only thing that lives there is **maintenance**.

Not metaphorically—literally.  
The pumps cycle whether watched or not. Gates test, valves breathe, turbines wake and sleep on a rhythm older than calendar time. In a place where clocks measure condensation and pressure head, “life” stops meaning motion and starts meaning **regulation**. By that definition, the city is alive before you meet anyone.

Then you begin noticing decisions.

A hand-width section of railing wiped dry in an otherwise beaded corridor.  
A fresh ceramic insulator swapped into a pylon crown.  
A warning chime rerouted three seconds early, exactly when static starts to crawl.  
Someone is here, but they live by intervals and gradients, not by crowds.

---

They are usually called the **Headkeepers** (for hydraulic head, not hierarchy), though outsiders use softer names: fogwardens, boiler monks, cistern folk. They are few, deliberately few, spaced through the districts the way load-bearing columns are spaced through a vaulted hall. You can walk half a day and see none of them, then turn a corner and find one standing ankle-deep in warm runoff, listening to a pipe with their palm.

They are built for this altitude and this wet heat: long-ribbed, narrow-framed, skin permanently sheened, with layered lids that blink sideways against acidic mist. Their clothes are not fashion so much as electrical strategy—matte oilcloth, ceramic-threaded wraps, copper grounding chains at the hems. No bright dyes survive long here; they wear basalt blacks, sulfur yellows gone dim, iron reds that almost match the sky.

They do not speak loudly unless they must. Sound lies in that city.  
They communicate in near-field ways: touch-signals on rail, coded valve turns, pulse lamps through steam, hammer taps into known truss lines. To an outsider it looks ritualistic. It is practical. Their language is designed for a place where echoes misreport distance and porous stone erases footsteps.

---

They don’t really “run” the city. They **tune** it.

A Headkeeper’s work is to keep disputes from becoming failures: heat vs. rain, pressure vs. fracture, mineral growth vs. flow, static vs. flesh. They scrape lace-colonies from condenser fins but leave the bands that stabilize sulfur chemistry. They open floodgates not by schedule alone but by smell, by metal taste, by the pitch of water in throat-grates. They can tell which reservoir is overburdened from the way steam peels off a plaza at dawn-equivalent.

Children, when there are children, are raised in cistern galleries where humidity is stable and the walls sweat clean. They learn counting by drip cadence, direction by thermal draft, memory by route: pressure-zone, hatch index, overflow stair, safe pylon corridor. They inherit not districts, but **responsibility bands**.

---

They are not the only life there, just the only life that resembles intention in a familiar shape.

The mineral ecologies are part of their world and, increasingly, part of them. Headkeepers culture bacterial veils on heat exchangers the way other societies might plant orchards. Some wear black crystalline films on gloves to seed specific surfaces. In certain old families, skin hosts harmless sulfur symbionts that mark pH shifts with faint color changes—a living warning strip along the wrist.

And above them, always, those pale kite-wing scavengers circling thermals: watched, sometimes fed, never fully tamed.

---

If you stay long enough, you understand the deepest resident is still the city itself—  
but not as a machine and not as a ghost.

As a **metabolism** shared between stone, water, heat, microbe, and the sparse people who keep the arguments balanced.

That is what lives there: not a population, but a pact.
