From far off, it looks like nothing lives there but weather.

Then the mud moves.

Not in one way—first as a stitched line of shell-backs ticking over crust, then as a dark ribbon of low runners pacing the ebb, then as heads lifting in a channel all at once, nostrils flaring toward the same unseen pulse. Life here is less “creatures in places” and more **timed appearances**: mouths opening with current, feet touching down between surges, bodies vanishing under sheen.

And among them, the people—if you call them people first, you miss them.

They are usually mistaken for wet stone until they stand. Broad-shouldered but low, wrapped in seal-hide and fungal felt that darkens to basalt when soaked. Their skin runs from salt-pale to iron-brown, always wind-burnished, often smeared with clay and ash to break outline. Hair is worn tight or shaved; anything loose tangles, freezes, or gets grabbed. They move in crouches and side-steps, conserving height the way desert folk conserve water.

Their faces are built for this coast: heavy lids against spray, a second blink-membrane for brine, flattened noses with closable slits, deep ear ridges that help sort useful sound from the constant roar. They are not stronger than the place, just better at reading it. A child here learns three things before knots:  
**where the water will be, what the silence means, and which sound is food.**

They call themselves something that translates badly—closest in trade speech is **Tide Listeners**.

---

They do not speak to conquer noise; they braid with it.

Conversation happens in short bursts timed between channel booms, with clicks from tongue and teeth, wrist-bone clacks, and low throat notes that carry through fog better than shouting. At distance they use shell horns tuned to local rock chambers. Each camp knows the “voice” of its nearest cliffs and can tell, by echo, who is calling and whether they are calm, hungry, or running.

Their maps are not drawings but recitations: “follow the split where water coughs twice,” “cross after third vent-thump,” “avoid bright clay after warm rain.” Stone grooves, shell cairns, and hanging bone chimes mark temporary truths, never permanent roads.

Ownership is brief by design. A weir belongs to whoever repaired it last and fed those waiting at it. Ring-walls are inherited as responsibilities, not property. Even the dead are not buried deep—groundwater takes all pits—so bones are cleaned, pinned with traded soft metal, and set into high crevices above reach of king tides. Ancestors are literally kept on the little height there is.

---

Their year is measured in pulses, not seasons.

There is the Spawn Drift, when channels turn alive with silver fry and everyone sleeps in shifts beside woven funnels.  
The Fat Fog, when grazer herds crowd flats and smoke racks run for days.  
The Lean Noise, when storms erase weirs and camps go quiet except for tool work and story.  
Then the Hard Clear, rare days with long sightlines, when scouts range far to trade for pins, cord, medicines, and stone that takes a sharper edge.

They waste nothing because the coast wastes everything eventually. Hooks are bone until they aren’t; nets are unraveled and rewoven into clothing ties; broken blades become scrapers; old hides become drumheads. Children wear net-float collars until they can read current by shin pressure alone.

---

If you ask what *rules* them, it is not a chieftain and not a god in the sky.  
It is timing, memory, and sound.

Their elders are called **Stone Readers**—not mystics, just patient experts in old flow-lines, vent rhythms, and cliff tone changes before collapse. A good Stone Reader can stand still, eyes half closed, and tell you where tomorrow’s channel will cut by how tonight’s surf is resonating in hollow rock.

Their hunters are fast, but their finest skill is restraint: knowing when not to chase, when to let a front pass, when to leave full traps because a pressure drop means run now or drown later. Heroics kill. Rhythm feeds.

---

So what lives there?

A whole loud republic of grazers, clackers, gliders, and scavengers—yes.  
But at the center, a people who survive by becoming almost grammatical parts of tide and stone: never fixed, never tall for long, never trusting quiet.

In a world being rewritten each day by water, they are not the authors.

They are the best readers.
