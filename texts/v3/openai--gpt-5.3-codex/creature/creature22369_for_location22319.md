What lives there is not one thing but a **timed civilization**—animals, machines, and people evolved into a single slow metabolism.

The dominant tool-using species (whether descended from human stock or engineered long ago, no one local cares much which) are often called, in translation, **the Anchored**.  
They are tall by leverage rather than mass: long legs, broad feet, deep chests, and layered sinuses to reclaim moisture from every breath. Their blood chemistry is aggressive about oxygen capture; their skin is matte and mineral-oiled, more like sealed leather than soft flesh, to resist dust abrasion and static burn. Many wear conductive braid-work at wrists, spine, and jaw to bleed off charge when crossing active canyons. Children are taught, before numbers, how to touch stone with the back of the hand and listen for field crackle.

They do not farm in fields. They farm **interfaces**.

A city’s true harvest is not ore; ore is old wealth.  
New wealth is what condenses, plates, blooms, and accumulates where heat, pressure, and trace gases meet engineered surfaces. The Anchored maintain condenser forests of black fin-towers, microbial lace vats in lava tubes, oxidizer beds on sun-facing terraces, and nutrient traps under freight rails where metal dust and biofilm mingle. Their cuisine is built from ferments, chelates, fungal mats, salt proteins, and rendered animal fats—complex, precise, and seasonal according to maintenance cycles rather than weather.

They organize life by pulses:

- **Still Days**: inspection, mending, migration counting, silence.
- **Run Days**: foundries wake, pumps hammer, slag moves, valves sing.
- **Afterflow**: scavenger species flood in; people collect what the city leaks on purpose.

A child in one of these wall-cities learns migration calendars the way a sailor learns tides.  
Not “spring and autumn,” but “third condenser purge,” “north array degauss,” “rail grease week,” “stack descaling month.”

---

The non-sapient fauna are everywhere once you know the signs.

Across abandoned yards and old pipeline flats move the **varnish herds**—six-legged grazers with ceramic incisors and flexible tongues studded with electroreceptive papillae. They scrape conductive films, rust skins, and bacterial lacquer from metal and basalt alike. Where they pass, infrastructure is cleaner, less prone to shorting. Cities pretend to tolerate them; in truth, many routes are designed around their migration corridors.

In cliff settlements nest the **sail-rakers**, membrane-winged scavengers with translucent skin stretched over folding ribs. In flight they are pale kites, riding thermal knives between towers; perched, they collapse into narrow, tool-like silhouettes along ledges. Their guano seeds key microbial consortia used in industrial bioreactors. Apprentices are assigned to rookery watch not from sentiment but because losing a rookery means losing next year’s catalyst stock.

Under saltstone live the **dusk augers**, blunt-headed burrowers that chew evaporite and surface at evening to feed on condenser sludge and discarded filter cake. Their tunnels aerate crust basins and expose buried mineral bands; prospectors track auger mounds to choose drilling sites. Their hide, once processed, becomes excellent valve leather.

High above drift the **thread floaters**, nearly weightless colonial organisms like braided glass hair. They climb and sink through dust strata, feeding on charge gradients and airborne microbes. When fronts shift, they descend in silver veils and snag on rigging, antennae, and cliff thorns. Children collect them with grounded poles. Refineries buy them by mass: dried floater mesh is a superb dielectric.

Predators exist, but nothing frantic. The apex forms are mostly pursuit minimizers—animals that force errors in open terrain rather than outrun prey. A famous one, the **hinge cat**, hunts by patiently shadowing ridgelines until herd static disorients weaker individuals, then striking once. Most kills happen in seconds after six hours of stillness.

---

The Anchored are not separate from this web. Their own survival depends on keeping leak, waste, and disturbance in carefully tuned ranges. Too “clean,” and nutrient chains starve. Too dirty, and corrosion outruns repair. So civic ethics here are engineering ethics: calibrate outputs, preserve corridors, never shut a system cold without seeding alternatives first.

Their law has an old phrase: **“A dead valve is a dead valley.”**

Because each city is a machine-oasis, each is also a bioregion.  
Turning off a compressor bank can collapse local floater swarms; sealing a slag trench can starve burrowers; overfencing a rail spine can break varnisher migration and raise infrastructure failure rates a decade later. So maintenance teams include ecologists, and ecologists carry torque wrenches.

Religion, where it exists, is practical reverence: not for gods in sky-water myths, but for continuity—bearings that outlive dynasties, load paths that don’t fail, obligations inherited across centuries. Their monuments are often infrastructural: a perfectly leveled bridge chain over a moving fault shelf, a condenser tower kept in operation for three hundred years, a migration gate that has never closed in storm season.

---

If you stand on an upper ledge at night, you can tell what lives there by ear:

- far click-rattle of varnishers on pitted steel,
- membrane snaps of sail-rakers braking in crosswind,
- one chain lift taking a load into darkness,
- then a call from ridge to ridge, answered minutes later.

Not a loud world.  
A world of scheduled effort, patient appetites, and shared dependency.

What lives there is a society that learned the oldest rule of dry high places and made it literal:  
**nothing survives alone, and nothing runs all the time.**
