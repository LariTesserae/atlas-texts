At first, you might think no one *lives* there—only things persist there.

Then one of the wall-carvings turns its head.

---

They are easiest to mistake for masonry when still.  
Short, broad through the chest and hips, with limbs built for bracing rather than leaping, they keep their weight low the way the place demands. Their skin ranges from river-clay gray to pale lime, and old age dusts them with mineral sheen where spray dries on the body day after day. In lampglow, an elder can look carved: salt-whitened brows, greened copper rings, shell toggles, rope belts dark with oil.

They are not fish-people, not exactly. They are air-breathers who have made a treaty with submergence.

Their lungs are deep and disciplined. Their throats and sinuses are shaped to close cleanly against pressure and foul splash. Their fingers are long, slightly webbed, and blunt-tipped, good for knotwork, scraping, hauling, and feeling seams in the dark. Their feet spread wide on slick stone. Their eyes are large, reflective, and better at catching movement than detail; in true dark, they work by whisker-fine facial hairs, skin-pressure, and memory of flow.

They call themselves something that translates poorly—closest meaning: **Those Between Levels**.

Others, if there were others, would call them the Levelborn.

---

Their city is their body extended.

They do not separate “house” from “channel,” or “street” from “sluice.” A sleeping ledge is a dry chamber *for now*. A nursery may be knee-deep in one season and chest-deep in another. They tie their lives to anchor points: overhead loops, handholds, grates, ladder rungs, wear-polished corners. Every family keeps rope-maps: cords with knot intervals encoding safe routes at specific water heights, hanging where children can reach and memorize.

They have no chalk marks because chalk dies here.  
They have knots, cuts, touch-signs, stain lines, and level scars.

Their language is spoken softly in air pockets, but much of it is percussive: knuckle on pipe, stone on stone, shell-click patterns, rope tugs relayed around blind turns. A full argument can happen through four walls of water and never rise above a murmur.

---

They do not farm fields. They farm exchange.

They tend grates like orchardists tend trees.  
They rotate filter-colonies from chain to chain.  
They scrape mineral where buildup will choke a flow and leave it where it strengthens a lip.  
They harvest snail-shell, turtle-scute, fish oils, crustacean swarms at timing windows defined by level and temperature, not by date.

Children learn three counts before any other numbers:

1. breaths you can spend in a known passage,  
2. handspans of rise that mean “move nests now,”  
3. knots to the nearest air.

No one becomes an adult until they can descend blindfolded from a lit gallery to a black cistern and return by feel, touching only sanctioned holds.

---

They are not alone in intelligence there, exactly.

The oldest turtles recognize individual keepers and will yield a lintel slot to some and not others. Blind eels gather at particular drum-patterns that precede gate shifts. A pale hunting species—dog-sized, many-jointed, silent—patrols seam-lines and is tolerated because it culls egg-thieves. The Levelborn do not name such beings as “livestock” or “wild.” They name them by flow-role: grazer, watcher, scavenger, warning-mouth.

Everything is in relation to water movement.  
Everything that blocks exchange is enemy, even if beautiful.  
Everything that keeps exchange open is kin, even if ugly.

---

Their religion, if it is one, has no creator-god above.  
It has **slow water**.

Not worship in the kneeling sense—more like legal reverence. Slow water is wise because it remembers shape and makes no sudden lies. Fast water is force without thought, tolerated but never trusted. Their ceremonies are maintenance tasks done in sequence and silence: unsealing a long-shut gate, first descent after a decade of buildup scraping, opening a new shaft-mouth to light.

Their dead are not burned and not buried.  
They are committed to designated still-basins, weighted lightly, wrapped in knot-cords that identify lineage and craft. Scavengers, mineral, and current do the rest. Later, if a recognizable bone-toggle or ring returns to a grate, it is cleaned and worked into communal tools. Nothing sentimental. Nothing wasted. Nothing fully gone.

---

So what lives there?

A people shaped by drag, pressure, darkness, and patience—  
stone-shouldered, quiet-handed, current-literate, emotionally restrained in public and fiercely tactile in private. They laugh rarely but deeply, usually in safe air chambers where sound won’t travel and cause trouble. They fall in love by learning one another’s breath lengths and fear responses in black water. They measure trust by who they let tie knots in their life-line.

They are ancient without needing to be old.  
They are gentle without being soft.  
They are slow on purpose.

In that elevated drowned world, what survives is not the strongest swimmer or the cleverest builder.

It is the one who can listen to almost-still water and hear, in it, tomorrow.
