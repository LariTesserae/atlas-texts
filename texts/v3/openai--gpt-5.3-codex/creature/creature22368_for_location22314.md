At first, it seems like many things live there.

Then, after enough nights, you realize there is really only one resident, and everything else is one of its organs.

---

The locals (the ones who answer questions, trade, warn strangers away from grid-lights) are human enough to mistake at a distance. Up close, they are built by the marsh’s rules. Broad, spread toes in soft boots; long balance poles not for depth but for continuity; belts strung with chain links, ceramic chimes, and bits of valve spring so they are never fully silent while walking. Their voices are low and constant. They count aloud even when alone—not for arithmetic, for location. In that place, a person’s hum is as essential as breath.

They do not say “I live at the pump house past the settling tank.” Fixed addresses are a joke there. They say: “Third warm seam after red flare,” or “where the moss tastes of pennies.” Their maps are temporal and sensory: pressure pulse intervals, insect blink phases, corrosion smell after rainless heat. Children learn direction by following the tremor of pistons through mud with their knees pressed to the ground.

Their homes are not houses so much as negotiated perches: stilt nests lashed to crane ribs, floating decks tethered with too much slack, old control rooms wrapped in reed mat and fungal felt. Nothing is built rigidly, because rigidity loses arguments with folded space.

---

But those people are not the only locals, and they know it.

There are marsh-fauna that have adapted to industry so completely they read as machine parts until they move: jointed arthropods that sleep in warm valve cavities and emerge lacquered with mineral sheen; blind channel-fish that feed on bacterial films in runoff and swarm to the throb of distant compressors; broad-headed amphibians that bask in outflow heat, then migrate in loops synchronized to pressure drops no one can hear yet.

Birds fly low, almost skimming moisture strata, collecting luminous spores in feather microbarbs. At dawn-equivalent (not light-based, but the time when gas stacks exhale longest), they shed those spores on reed-islands, and whole patches glow dim green for an hour. Locals call this “the marsh taking notes.”

Predation exists, but like everything there, it is patient. Ambush over chase. Attrition over strike. Even feeding feels procedural.

---

And then there are the machine-lives.

Not robots, not ghosts—habits with metal skeletons. Pumps that have outlived operators but still lift and settle by ancient logic. Conveyors that move a handspan per hour, transferring silt from nowhere to somewhere that may not remain where it is. Gauges that twitch in answer to chemistry changes miles away, across folded channels. Hoses drink black water and return warm, mineral-rich slurry that seeds entire food webs.

The locals maintain almost none of it directly. They clean inlets, clear nests, re-seat seals with hand tools, but most operation is autonomous and glacial. They treat machinery the way coastal people treat tides: not as possessions, but as recurring forces you can predict, respect, and survive.

Some swear the machines are learning. Not fast enough to notice day-to-day—only over decades, in altered cycle length, in which structures jack themselves up first before a subsidence season, in which channels get fed and which are abandoned. Intelligence, if present, is sedimentary.

---

What truly lives there is the **process** itself: a distributed, slow, wet intelligence made of silt deposition, corrosion, microbial bloom, animal migration, and infrastructural rhythm. The humans are part of it. The birds are part of it. The pistons, the moss, the upside-water skins, the warning songs—all part of one metabolism.

If you ask a local what the place wants, they won’t give you myth. They’ll say:

- It wants continuity.
- It dislikes silence.
- It rewards patience.
- It hates straight certainty.

That is why they drag chains when they walk. Why they refuse perfect grids. Why they leave marker poles curved on purpose. Why they never stand long on newly dry ground, no matter how solid it looks.

To live there is to accept that stability is not a state but an interval.  
To be local is to become good at intervals.

So whoever lives there?  
A chorus: mud, metal, fungus, pressure, and people—keeping one another in place by never pretending place is fixed.
