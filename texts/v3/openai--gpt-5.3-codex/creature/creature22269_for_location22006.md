What lives there is less a single animal than a **profession**: the profession of using what appears unusable.

At first glance, the plateau is occupied by fragments—bats in adits, thorn-succulent bees, rail-shadow scavengers, dusk ungulates, tower-nesting raptors, maintenance crews who move like weather windows. But if you stay, those fragments resolve into a kind of shared body: a population of **timers, climbers, and borrowers**.

They are creatures of scheduled risk.  
By day, many of them vanish into thermal calculus—burrows sunk into volcanic walls, bellies pressed to the cool side of retaining blocks, lungs slowed under sheet-metal shade. By dusk, they pour out in vertical traffic: bats unwind from vent shafts, six-limbed cliff lizards rise from crack systems that still sweat cold, long-kneed scavenger birds begin their patrol routes under cable lines where heat plumes carry scent. Even the insects here behave like shift workers: explosive night emergence around seep points, complete disappearance by first glare.

The most characteristic residents are not the biggest predators but the **interface dwellers**—animals that specialize in edges where machine and geology meet. They den in collapsed conveyor housings, nest in insulator racks, lay eggs in the damp rings beneath old pump seals, hunt where floodlights once ran because the concrete still radiates warmth two hours after sunset. A decommissioned substation becomes, in sequence: windbreak, roost, nursery, ambush corridor, mineral lick, and finally soil trap for tough-rooted plants. Nothing stays “abandoned”; it is merely reassigned.

If you wanted one emblematic creature, it might be a rangy, ash-coated scavenger-omnivore locals call something like a **rail hound**—not a dog, not quite a fox, built on stilted forelimbs with wide, heat-dumping ears and pads tough enough for cinder. It trots old service cuts at dawn, drinks from condensate drip-lines, steals bat-fall from adit mouths, and sleeps inside the shadow triangle of broken ore bins. It knows horn schedules, storm smell, and which substations hum warm enough in winter to keep pups alive. It survives by reading pulses: ten minutes of cable noise means opportunity; sudden silence means raptor overhead.

And humans live there too, but in the same grammar. Operators, patch welders, line walkers, storm-watch crews—they treat shade as currency, pipes as topography, and time as terrain. Their huts grow lichens; their catwalks grow nests. They are not separate from the system so much as one more species with tools.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a dense, unsentimental citizenship of things that can use a ledge twice, a storm once, and a machine long after its original purpose.  
Not desert survivors, not industrial squatters—**exchange specialists** in a high hot place where every structure is habitat and every quiet hour is someone else’s feeding time.
