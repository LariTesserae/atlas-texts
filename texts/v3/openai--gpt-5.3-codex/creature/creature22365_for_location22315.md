At first you’d think nothing *truly* lives there except the obvious minimalists: the hard-shelled skitters, the blind ribbons, the flash-blooming films that arrive with moisture and vanish before noon.

Then, after enough hours in the hum, you notice the timing.

The metallic ringing that repeats almost perfectly?  
Not random stone.  
The pressure vent that exhales a half-beat before a warm rain sheet arrives?  
Not quite random either.

What lives there most completely is a **distributed animal**, a colony-spanning species sometimes called a *bellbed* by the few who study it. Not one body, but thousands of mineralized organs threaded through shallow basalt fractures across kilometers of plain. Most of it is underground: pale conductive tissue wrapped around hot seams, anchored in old lava pores, drinking heat gradients and trace chemistry the way other creatures drink streams.

At the surface, you only see its “knuckles” — dark, smooth protrusions like worn ceramic domes, often mistaken for ordinary basalt. In wind, neighboring domes strike each other by fractions of a millimeter and make those clean, repeating tones. In still air, they click from internal pressure shifts. A field of them can sound mechanical, like relays cycling in a buried station.

It does not hunt in the dramatic sense. It **orchestrates availability**.

- It shunts warmth toward certain rock faces at dawn, encouraging condensation there.  
- It bleeds charge into iron-rich flats, drawing black sand into transient lines that trap films of moisture and microbes.  
- It opens and closes pore-vents in long rhythm, guiding where slick ground forms and where it dries.

The six-legged skitters follow these wet intervals and graze transient biofilms. The ribbon scavengers follow the skitters. The bellbed takes what all of them leave behind: dissolved organics in runoff threads, carcass salts in pans, microbial blooms in mineral bowls. Nothing is wasted, nothing stockpiled. Circulation, not growth.

Its nervous system is vibration.  
Its memory is interval.

A disturbance — a collapse, a storm front, a heavy migration — is not remembered as image but as altered beat pattern in bedrock. Decades later, it may still “answer” that event by shifting ring frequencies in the same district, the way scar tissue changes gait. If you return a year later and hear the same exhale on the same beat, part of what you are hearing is geological regularity; part is the bellbed insisting on established tempo.

Reproduction is rare and unspectacular. In years when fog lingers and vent heat is moderate, a few surface domes soften, split, and release translucent cords into slick troughs. Most fail. A handful find active fracture networks and begin new nodes. Maturity takes a very long time — long enough that erosion and adulthood are nearly the same clock.

It is not intelligent in a human direction. It does not build, symbolize, or plan frontier. But it is not passive either. It maintains a planet-scale habit: stable oscillation. In a place that behaves like industry without product, this is the nearest thing to an engineer — a lifeform whose success is measured not by expansion, but by keeping the machine-state continuous.

So what lives there is, fittingly, something that does not try to become anything else.  
A warm, patient, stone-coupled animal that survives by keeping time.
