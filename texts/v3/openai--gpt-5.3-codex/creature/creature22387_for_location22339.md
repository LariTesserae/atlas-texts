At first, it seems obvious that **people** live there.

Then, after a few days in the dim iron-blue, you realize that isn’t precise enough.

What lives there is a **temperament** made flesh: patient, load-bearing, moisture-minded.

---

They are mountain urbanites, but not in the romantic sense—no bright banners on peaks, no theatrical hardiness. Their strength is quieter: knees built by stairs, hands educated by cold metal, ears trained to hear the difference between a healthy valve sigh and a bearing beginning to fail. Their bodies tend toward compactness and balance. Their clothes are layered for damp rather than storm: waxed fiber, felted reedcloth, fungus-leather cuffs, gloves that keep grip when everything sweats.

Their skin carries the city’s palette—lichen gray, lamp amber, forge-white flashes, the dark half-moons of condensation under the eyes from dawn shifts. Many have the slight forward posture of people who grew up on inclines and catwalks, always listening to their footing through their feet.

They do not speak loudly unless they must. In a place where sound travels oddly and silence is diagnostic, volume is considered wasteful and sometimes rude.

---

Their jobs are less like occupations and more like ecological roles.

There are **driftwrights** who map humidity by block and hour, updating wall charts the way sailors once updated currents.  
There are **root-keepers** who prune, braid, and redirect living lattices so vines reinforce walls without choking gears.  
There are **tone-readers** who can date stress in old terraces by the pitch they ring at dusk.  
There are **mesh tenders** who comb fog-harvest fields for mineral crust and moth debris before dawn.  
There are **ductwatch crews** who know every warm crawlspace where pale mammals nest and every place those nests threaten insulation.

Children learn three literacies at once: script, load, and water. They can read notices, estimate whether a platform is overburdened, and predict which stairwell walls will sweat before rain.

---

They keep unusual calendars.

Not by month names so much as by cycles: first long condensation, turbine cleaning week, fungal press bloom, north-shelf freeze, ridge-thaw hazard. Deliveries are scheduled against thermal reversals and the ringing of old stone. Weddings often happen in hours of stable pressure. Funerals are timed to mistfall if possible—the dead leave while the city is collecting water.

Their religion, where present, is practical reverence: shrines at pump houses, votives near anchor points, small glass-cylinder candles set where someone once survived a collapse. Saints are often engineers. Ancestors are remembered by repairs that held.

---

And yet humans are only one layer.

There are the **hand-bred moth lines**, thick-bodied and cold-tolerant, released by neighborhood stewards at specific bloom windows. Their cocoons are catalogued; their wing dust is used as a tracer for airflow studies.

There are **pale duct mammals**—not pets, not vermin exactly—quick, near-silent, with whiskers that shine in lampglow. They feed on root grubs, fungal overgrowth, and spilled press pulp. Workers curse them, then quietly build little bypass channels so they don’t chew through critical sealants.

There are **cliff birds**, sparse and intermittent, who nest where thermal pockets and old rebar meet. Their absence is as meaningful as their arrival; an empty thermal lane can warn of pressure shifts before any instrument does.

And sometimes, at fogline, there are the larger ridge things: horned silhouettes moving with deliberate slowness along stone backs no human path reaches. Whether they are rare ungulates, relic stock gone feral, or something the city half-invented to personify the high ridges hardly matters. People leave mineral blocks at certain saddles anyway.

---

The oldest resident, though, is neither human nor animal.

It is the **city-mountain system itself**—a coupled organism of rock, roots, condensers, ducts, terraces, and routine. It inhales at dusk and exhales at dawn through engineered lungs. It grows moss where noise is wrong. It pushes water upward through capillary skins. It remembers stress in harmonics. It rewards maintenance and punishes arrogance, but usually slowly enough for humility to intervene.

The people who endure there are the ones who understand they are not masters of this organism, only one of its thinking tissues.

So what lives there?

- Communities that measure wealth in stable bearings and clean cistern depth.
- Children who can identify neighborhoods by drip pattern in the dark.
- Workers who trust moss as an acoustic instrument.
- Pollinator keepers, root surgeons, fog farmers, pressure listeners.
- Small furtive mammals, occasional birds, and maybe horned witnesses on the ridgelines.
- A living infrastructure that behaves like weather and memory at once.

In short:  
What lives there is **adaptation with manners**—life that has learned to share altitude with gravity, moisture, and old stone without ever pretending to have won.
