At first glance, you’d say workers live there.

Not “workers” in the abstract sense—people with jobs—but people whose bodies have learned the tempo of bolts, fog horns, lift schedules, and shifting stone. They move like they’re always listening through their feet. They pause before stepping onto a bridge, not from fear, but from habit: test the resonance, feel whether today’s structure agrees with today’s gravity. Their clothing is all layers and repairs—oilcloth over wool, moss-proof capes, gloves with conductive fingertips, belts carrying chalk, rivets, folded route-strips. Everyone looks slightly equipped for weather and slightly equipped for negotiation.

Then you notice it isn’t just labor that defines them; it’s maintenance as identity.

In most cities, maintenance is backstage. Here it’s citizenship. A person earns trust by what they keep aligned: a stair run, a fog sail, a district clock, a tram brake, a culvert biome. Whole family lines are known by their specialties. There are echo-attendants who open or close freight bridges based on footfall harmonics. There are path-keepers who walk “seasonal paths” each dawn and repaint waymarks when streets lengthen overnight. There are clock-braiders who carry synchronized mechanical watches from ward to ward and gently force local time back into agreement before drift becomes social trouble.

They speak in short, practical bursts—not because they’re cold, but because sound is crowded. A sentence here is often two nouns and a verb, then a hand signal. People have rain vocabularies, fog vocabularies, and machine-overload vocabularies. Children learn three maps of their district: dry-weather, wet-weather, and “if the stairs add a landing.” Addresses include memory instructions: *third gantry after the whistle that comes too early; stable in morning mist; avoid after dusk rain.*

The people who are born there are subtly different from people who arrive.

Not in a mystical way—more in calibration. Ridge-born kids have extraordinary balance and uncanny spatial memory for routes that don’t stay fixed. Their play involves timing, echo, and safe misdirection: running alley loops that only close correctly if you count turbine beats. They can often tell, by smell alone, which district they’ve entered: copper steam, cold mineral seep, greenhouse rot, basalt dust. They grow up with animals as co-citizens rather than wildlife. A tagged cliff grazer blocking a tram line is not “nature intruding”; it’s a traffic problem with a known protocol.

And yes, animals live there as fully as people do.

Wind-kites patrol the updrafts above chimneys like municipal janitors of the sky. Bell-throated bats pour from vents at dusk and are tracked on transit boards because their swarms alter tram visibility. Burrow-foxes are tolerated in insulation banks until breeding season, then carefully relocated by night crews with padded traps. Pipe eels are harvested, traded, released, and occasionally worshipped in a practical way: as proof that every system is connected whether planners intended it or not.

Plants, too, behave less like decoration and more like neighborhoods.

There are greenhouse guilds who cultivate exhaust vines in stacked crowns around heat towers; reedwrights who maintain rooftop filtration beds; scaffold gardeners who anchor rope-root orchards to cliff faces and prune by harness in sleet. People know which moss can stabilize a crumbling wall, which creeper indicates a warm fracture beneath stone, which pale fruit trees produce iron-sweet globes that keep through dry spells. A good gardener is half botanist, half structural engineer.

Some residents are harder to classify.

There are those who spend years in borderline districts—surveyors, couriers, salvage crews—who become “offset” in reputation. They arrive exactly on time according to one ward and thirty minutes early according to another. They remember doorways no one else can find in rain. Nobody calls them cursed. They’re consulted. In a city where geometry is occasionally negotiable, people who can feel small misalignments before instruments catch them are valuable.

And in a way, the largest thing living there is the city itself.

Not metaphorically—functionally. It sweats, vents, shifts, scars, regrows. Cranes graze the skyline. Rails pulse ore through its spine. Fog collectors breathe. Waste heat feeds leaf and root. Districts molt and re-bolt. Residents don’t talk about “beating” the mountain or “taming” the wild. They talk about staying in conversation with a young world that hasn’t finished setting.

So what lives there?

A population of mechanics, gardeners, listeners, herders, clock-menders, route-memorizers, and weather-readers. Creatures of moss and thermal wind. Machines treated as temperamental colleagues. And beneath all of them, around all of them, the unfinished mountain—restless, formative, and very much alive—raising a city whose people have learned that permanence is overrated, but attention can be sacred.
