What lives there, if you watch long enough, is not just a population—it’s a **temperament** that has learned to wear many bodies.

At shift-change, it looks human:  
men and women in waxed jackets, cuffs dark with damp, voices tuned to machine intervals. They move with the timing of pumps, not clocks. They pause under awnings exactly where condensation thickens, as if old arguments and old jokes have left mineral traces in the air. They carry cloth-wrapped tools, keep spare socks in lockers, and can tell you which alley will bead with moisture before noon. They are practical, unsentimental, and astonishingly attentive. Their children inherit that attention: they read wet concrete like script.

At dusk, it looks feral and winged:  
dogs tracing inherited patrol loops, starlings writing and rewriting one black sentence above the wires, herons standing where runoff remembers itself. Frogs occupy every temporary basin as if each puddle were sovereign territory. Snakes take warmth from culverts that were built for steam and claimed by scales. Nothing here rules; everything arrives exactly when its niche opens.

By night, it looks mechanical:  
the town’s true vertebrae—rails, pipe bridges, catwalks, drains—lit in thin sodium lines. Pumps breathe. Condensers click. Presses fall silent one by one, but never all at once. Even resting, the place circulates. Even empty streets feel inhabited by procedure.

And if you insist on one single resident, one “whatever” rather than many “whoevers,” then it is this:

A young, compact **edge-creature** made of brick heat, reed seed, iron oxide, and recurrent water.  
It feeds on repetition.  
It grows by small permissions.  
It remembers through dampness.

It is wary of grand gestures. It prefers bylaws, patched awnings, deepened ditches, repaired bearings, volunteer willows that survive three winters before anyone names them. It does not dream of becoming a metropolis or reverting to marsh. It prefers the treaty: fabrication on one side, colonizing green on the other, and in between a thousand wet seams where life can take hold.

So what lives there is a community, yes—but also a single distributed animal, patient and loud-hearted, whose skin is moss on concrete, whose pulse is a municipal siren, and whose breath is the fine, social sweat that gathers wherever people choose to stand together.
