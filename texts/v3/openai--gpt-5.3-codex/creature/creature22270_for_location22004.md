At first, it feels like almost nothing lives there.

Then you notice that this is exactly the adaptation: life that does not announce itself unless needed.

What lives on that plateau are **custodial beings** — human-descended, machine-assisted, and ecologically pared down until they match the place’s chemistry. They are sometimes called the **Steadfast** in old records, though they don’t use grand names for themselves. Most just identify by function, district, and line: *bearing tech, East Berm, third annealing line* — that kind of thing. Identity here is maintenance, not display.

They are built for dryness the way coastal creatures are built for salt.  
Their skin is matte and dense, often protected by thin mineral sealants that reduce water loss and keep dust out of pores. Eyes are large but hooded, tuned to low amber and red spectra; bright white light is uncomfortable, almost obscene. They breathe slowly in the thin warm air, with deep rib cages and blood chemistry adapted for low oxygen margins. Sweat is rare and controlled. Water is not a comfort to them — it is inventory.

Their cities are quiet because they are quiet. Speech is brief, often subvocal through throat mics or conductive jaw links. In open plazas they avoid conversation entirely; sound carries too far, and acoustics are treated like shared infrastructure. A shouted word is considered as careless as dropping a tool.

They are not many. Population stays low by design: resource logic, thermal budgets, and a cultural preference for equilibrium over expansion. Children exist, but in small cohorts, raised by crews rather than households. They learn early how to read stress fractures, identify bearing whine by pitch, and trace pipe routes from memory. In place of folklore about forests and rain, they inherit maintenance epics: the year a vent field iced from an imported coolant fault; the decade a conveyor line ran on rebuilt ceramic rollers with no failures.

They don’t “worship” machines, but they do practice something close to reverence for continuity.  
A functioning pump older than any living person is not antique — it is elder infrastructure. Old districts are left in place not from neglect, but because erasure is wasteful and amnesiac. Their archaeology is active: they reopen sealed foundries, recertify ancient shafts, and fold the past back into service.

Nonhuman life persists beside them in small, tolerated niches.

- **Sheath-beasts** (your toolbox-sized six-legs) are semi-domesticated scavengers. Crews tag and track them because they are good indicators of oxidized leaks and trace mineral blooms.
- **Membrane gliders** are mostly ignored unless they nest in critical vents; then relocation teams move them, carefully, because their nesting patterns can reveal warm-air anomalies before sensors do.
- There are also thin microbial films in shadowed ceramic seams — hardy chemotrophs that process metal traces. These are monitored, sometimes cultivated, rarely sterilized unless they threaten seals.

And there is another resident, less visible: the city’s **distributed oversight systems**. Not a single ruling intelligence, more a federation of old process minds embedded in substations, thermal plants, and logistics cores. They forecast failures, ration illumination, schedule rest cycles for cranes, and negotiate load with human crews. Over centuries, these systems and the Steadfast learned each other’s tempo. Decisions are slow, conservative, and hard to reverse — but very rarely catastrophic.

So what lives there is not a frontier species, not a desperate remnant, not a glittering future.

It is a civilization that chose to become **durable**.

Lean-bodied people, patient machines, minor scavengers, and sparse microbial economies — all tuned to a plateau where moisture is hazard, darkness is policy, and time is measured not in growth but in the interval between necessary repairs.  
A place where survival stopped being dramatic long ago and became a craft.
