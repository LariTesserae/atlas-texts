At first glance, it looks like crowding.

Hooves on impossible inclines. Pale wings shingled over rock like scales. Knots of rope-tailed scavengers flowing through cracks, splitting and rejoining. Beetles like dropped coins. Clear-skinned amphibians waiting around seep-holes as if carved from ice. So many species in so little space that it feels accidental, a pileup of survivors at altitude.

Stay longer and it stops looking accidental.

What lives in that cirque is less a single beast than a **working arrangement**—a living machine built out of many mouths, many feet, many kinds of hunger. You could call it a colony if colony meant “no shared body, but shared timing.” You could call it a society if society meant “no language you can hear, but rules everybody keeps.”

The hook-footed ungulates are the weight of it: they cut routes into scree year by year, opening and maintaining the vertical roads every other creature uses. Their narrow chests and overlarge lungs make them look permanently braced against impact. They sleep wedged to stone, one eye open, and wake not by light but by vibration—falling grit, wing-surge, distant bark-echo.

The gliders are the weather-skin. Broad, pale, and almost soundless on the first downstroke, they read pressure and fog as if feeling texture with their ribs. Colonies cling so densely to favored walls that, in dim light, the cliff seems furred. They are tool-users in quick, practical ways: shell shard, flat stone, one precise drop, cracked snail egg. They remember useful ledges for generations, not by map, but by return.

The rope-tails are the hands and opportunists. They move in braided groups, touching constantly, deciding by contact. They pry salts with bone wedges, strip carrion to tendon in minutes, and vanish before larger hunters arrive. Around water, their aggression shuts off like a lantern pinched dark. Away from water, it returns.

The mirrored beetles are cleanup and signal. When fog thickens, their violet pulse starts—faint, then synchronized in waves on warm mineral patches. That light means feeding time for half the basin. Insectivores key to it. Ambush hunters key to them. Even the gliders shift roost activity around those flashes. Dawn is not solar there; dawn is chemical.

And above these layers, threading through all of them, are the echo-hunters: lean-throated predators that bark into shadow and listen for shape, distance, softness. They don’t need full light. They need angles and return time. Their calls strike basalt, split, recombine, come back carrying a moving map. In that map, a wingbeat is brightness.

So what lives there?

A **rift-choir**: not metaphorical, literal. A place where survival is done in overlapping parts. No architecture, no monuments, no lasting marks—only repeated acts that become structure anyway. Paths worn by hooves. Feeding stations remembered by wings. Water truces obeyed by teeth. Light made by beetles and treated like a clock.

If you stand still long enough, you stop seeing species and start seeing one animal with many bodies:  
breathing in fog,  
drinking by drops,  
hunting by echo,  
and remaking itself each season along edges that are still, geologically speaking, fresh wounds.
