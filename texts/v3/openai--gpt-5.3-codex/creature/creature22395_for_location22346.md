At first glance, you’d say **everything** lives there except stillness.

Then, after a few shifts, you realize the true inhabitants are not separate species at all, but a single pressure-shaped community: workers, herds, swarms, scavengers, predators, and rooted architecture all locked into one circulatory beat. The town has citizens, yes—but it also has enzymes.

The people-equivalents are usually called something like **Rhythm-keepers** by outsiders, though among themselves they use shift-names more than blood-names. They are built for damp dimness: broad chests, slow breathing, pupils that bloom huge in amber light, scarred forearms from cable work, and a practiced stillness whenever machinery stutters. Many wear filament veils over nose and mouth, not for modesty but to taste air chemistry—alkaloid traces, mold rise, predator musk, overheated bearings. Their hearing is less about tone than pattern. They notice when the pump-cough is half a beat late.

They do not walk through the settlement so much as **merge with traffic**. One hand always free for rail or root. One eye on overhead lanes for wing passage. Their tools are hybrid things: bone-handled torque keys, resin-sealed gauges, spore-proof lamp shutters, woven shock straps for handling live conduit vines. Children learn right-of-way before arithmetic: hoof lane, crawler lane, wing lane, worker lane, emergency drift lane when mist gets thick. By adolescence they can cross three moving decks in low light without touching a single active antenna.

Their society organizes by function rather than district. You’ll meet condenser crews with skin permanently chilled from pit vapor; root surgeons who prune only outer growth by touch; kite wardens who keep decoy engines thumping during maintenance blackouts; drip-runners who sprint collection frames to every leak-mouth during ceiling weather. There are fungal clerks who track spore debt, resin brokers, tranquilizer stillwrights, hatch-heat keepers, and gong callers who can warn six decks with one strike pattern. Prestige goes to reliability, not charisma. If your machine has kept beat for ten years, people trust your word in council.

And they are not alone in governing. The burrow ungulates decide where expansion ends by refusing certain tunnels. Swarm lines rewrite market routes overnight. Bats enforce curfew on open rafters. Vault kites, terrifying as they are, function like weather—an upper law no one votes on. The inhabitants have accepted that politics includes nonverbal majorities. A regulation is only real if it survives contact with teeth, hooves, wings, and spores.

Over time you notice something stranger: the green architecture is not passive infrastructure. Core roots thicken around long-used platforms and withdraw from neglected ones. Moisture channels “prefer” reliable neighborhoods. Certain vent-thorns bloom only near honest maintenance crews, as if fed by cleaner heat. Whether this is biochemistry or myth scarcely matters; people treat the structure as a listening elder. They apologize before cutting outer fiber. They leave offerings of condensate at new grafts. They say the town remembers footsteps through pressure and sap.

So what lives there?

A braided being.

Part human-descended labor culture, part domesticated-industrial ecology, part feral corrective force. It eats waste heat and dead tissue. It dreams in piston intervals. It panics at silence. It celebrates rain like an organ receiving blood. Its language is gongs, valve hiss, antenna tap, and the soft crackle of roots taking load.

If you insist on one name for the resident of that place, call it **the Pulse**:  
a civilization that survived by becoming an ecosystem,  
and an ecosystem that stabilized by learning schedule.
