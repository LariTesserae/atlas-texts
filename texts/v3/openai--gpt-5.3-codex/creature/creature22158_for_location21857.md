What lives there is not one population so much as a **stack of tempos**.

At the quickest tempo: swarms, spores, condensation films, pollinators.  
At the slowest: root lattices, printed terraces, emerging watersheds, and the city’s own distributed mind.

And in between—human beings, though “crowd” is almost the wrong unit for them.

---

The people you glimpse across those wide, underused avenues are called different things by different districts, but most simply call themselves **Residents-in-Phase**. They are not nomads, exactly, but they don’t treat settlement as fixed. A household may anchor to one tower core for three wet seasons, then shift to a fresh terrace once the substrate stabilizes and the sensor mesh has learned local fog behavior. Address is less “street and number” than **moisture profile + thermal frame + transit filament node**.

They are physically unremarkable until you stand near one. Then the adaptations read clearly: pupils that open very wide in pearl light; subtle vestibular training so they can feel the city’s direction-tug beacons as naturally as older people felt north; skin and garments designed to shed, wick, and harvest water. Their clothing is matte, layered, quiet—no flapping fabric, no hard ornament. Even fashion follows condensation logic: textures that bead by dawn, dark tones that warm just enough at dusk to release collected moisture into personal reservoirs.

They move like people who have grown up where infrastructure listens. No one hurries unless a weather front forces a phase shift. Doors dilate before hands reach them. Walkways stiffen where feet will land. Transit arrives as pressure and alignment, not noise. A newcomer looks cautious, slightly seasick from the phase-offset districts; locals step through those temporal seams without thinking.

---

But humans are only one citizen class.

A great deal of the city is inhabited by **autonomous custodians**—maintenance intelligences distributed through rails, skins, foams, masts, and micro-swarms. They are not chatty assistants and not quite invisible utilities. They have local personality in the way weather has personality: one district’s maintenance mesh is conservative and overbuilds edges before rain; another tolerates more risk and lets water sketch a channel before reinforcing it. Residents talk about them as if discussing neighbors: “The south plinth is in a nervous mood,” or “The ridge mesh is feeling generous with overflow today.”

Then there are the **engineered ecologies**, each patch designed to be vigorous but bounded. Black-leaf canopies that drink diffuse spectra; vine columns trained to climb only specific alloys; fern mats tuned to pull metals from new silt. Pollinators with glassy wings map bloom cycles and report atmospheric chemistry as they feed. Bird analogues on antenna crowns act like sparse punctuation—single-note calls encoding occupancy, pressure change, sometimes a maintenance request. Even the occasional long-legged grazer is part gardener, part sensor platform, part myth: children track their dawn crossings as omens of where the next permanent stream will cut.

---

Social life reflects the same sparse abundance as the terrain. You do not get packed markets every evening. You get **brief densities**: a plaza suddenly full when the cloud belts are rerouted; tower kitchens opening all at once during high-humidity nights; seasonal assemblies at the city edge when a new terrace is declared “biologically legible” and ready for first residency.

Conversation tends to be precise, low-volume, and environmental. People ask not “How are you?” but “How’s your air this week?” or “Did your walls close early last night?” Children learn to read drip cadence before they read script. Elders are valued for long memory of short land—who remembers where the first cracks opened, where drainage hesitated, which ridge looked permanent and wasn’t.

Ownership is soft; stewardship is strict. You can alter your dwelling skin color, internal geometry, garden mix. You cannot interrupt fog harvest routes, block root rerouting corridors, or introduce a species without district consensus and simulation time. Freedom exists, but it is negotiated with atmosphere first.

---

So what lives there?

- Humans adapted to low-light, high-humidity, high-automation life, socially sparse but deeply networked.  
- Machine ecologies that maintain, predict, and subtly decide.  
- Designed flora and selective fauna, each doing beauty and labor at once.  
- And the city itself—unfinished, self-editing, increasingly sentient in practice if not in law.

In that place, “resident” means any system that can sense the cycle and answer it.
