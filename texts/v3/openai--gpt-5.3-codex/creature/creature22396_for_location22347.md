What lives there first is **attention**.

Before you learn names, you notice the kind of minds this place grows: people who look at stone the way sailors look at water. They do not treat a wall as finished, or a slope as stable, or a spring as permanent. They live by watching tiny changes—one new crack, one shifted lintel, one damp patch where yesterday there was dust. In younger mountains, survival belongs to those who can read beginnings.

Then you start seeing the people themselves.

They are thickly layered in wool and hide most of the year, not for spectacle but for work: wrapped knees for stair roads, bound wrists for hauling rope, doubled shoulders against wind corridors between buildings. Their hands are marked by lime, lanolin, soot, and stone grit. Many have the compact build altitude favors—efficient lungs, careful pacing, little wasted motion. Even children climb like they were taught by gravity directly.

No one here is only one thing.  
A mason also keeps two goats.  
A herder can patch mortar.  
A weaver knows how to clear ice from a channel gate.  
An elder who cannot climb high anymore still sits by a cistern and can tell, by smell alone, whether the upper catchment has taken silt.

If you ask what they call themselves, the answer is often practical rather than tribal: *wall-keepers*, *water-watchers*, *upper-herd people*, *third-terrace households*. Identity is tied to obligations. Belonging is measured in what you maintain.

---

Their city makes them communal without making them loud.

Thin air carries sound too far, so people cultivate quiet voices and precise signals. A whistle pattern means “drive left along the broken ledge.” Three staff knocks in a stairwell means “load coming down.” Doors close with felt buffers. Arguments happen, certainly, but close to the chest, in low rooms, not shouted into the lanes where panic travels faster than truth.

Public life clusters around warm infrastructure: communal ovens, wool halls, repair courts, cistern chambers, animal yards at dawn and dusk. The market is less a carnival than a calibration point: salt against fiber, cured hide against grain, labor promises against tool metal. Credit is often stored in reputation for reliability. If you failed to reface your retaining wall last thaw, everyone knows. If you did it in a storm for a sick neighbor, everyone knows that too.

Their faith—whether formal shrine rites or inherited household practice—tends toward reciprocity with difficult matter. Offerings are practical: first meltwater of spring, first spun yarn from a shearing, a polished river stone carried uphill from far below. Sacredness gathers around thresholds: springs, passes, first stair of a district, last gate before open slope. Not because the land is gentle, but because it is decisive.

---

And then there are the other citizens.

Herd animals are not background livestock; they are mobile heat, food, fabric, fuel, and future. Goats map the cliffs. Wool-beasts thicken the courtyards with breath at night. Dogs are highly trained, almost professional—route memory, flock splitting, predator alarm, child escort. A good dog is introduced by lineage.

Ravens live in active negotiation with humans: thieves, scouts, weather messengers, refuse inspectors. Children imitate their calls for games and herding tricks. Foxlike scavengers run the edge economy, turning waste into motion. Rodents are constant adversaries, and trapping is part of civic duty, not private hobby. Even insects are season-markers: the first beetle under thawed stone says one thing about planting; the first evening moth says another about insulation repairs.

Nothing is “just an animal” here; every species sits in a web of exchange. Dung dries on walls as winter fuel. Bone pegs pin roof fiber. Sinew ties tool heads where metal fasteners are too precious. Horn cups pass through generations. Hair becomes felt, felt becomes wall lining, worn felt becomes patch, patch becomes tinder.

---

What might surprise an outsider is that the place does not produce hardness alone.

It also produces patience, humor, and a particular kind of tenderness: people who tuck warm stones into a child’s bedding before dawn shifts; who leave moss-wet water basins for dogs and ravens in dry weeks; who carve tiny wind charms in hidden stair niches where only maintenance crews will ever see them.

Courtship happens on work routes: carrying baskets to upper terraces, checking snow-load beams, walking herd returns at dusk. Marriage is often as much a labor merger as romance, but affection survives in details—shared gloves, saved lamp oil, the better side of a sleeping platform given away without comment.

Old age, when it comes, is honored most where memory is most technical. Elders are living archives of landslides, freeze years, failed mortar recipes, springs that vanished and reappeared. They may no longer haul, but they prevent repeated mistakes, and in this environment, that is life-saving power.

---

So what lives there?

A mountain people, yes—but more specifically a **maintenance civilization**: human communities, working animals, opportunistic wild neighbors, all fused into an ongoing act of holding. They do not conquer the uplift. They do not tame it in any final way. They learn its tempos, brace against its moods, and stitch themselves into its fractures.

The city’s true inhabitant is a collective habit:  
repair before collapse, store before hunger, read before reacting, share before winter.

In a young range where even stone is still deciding what it will become, the beings who endure are those willing to keep becoming with it.
