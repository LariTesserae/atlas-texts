At first, what lives there seems to be **procedure**.

Not people. Not animals.  
Procedure.

Pumps wake before dawn (if dawn means anything under stone). Vent baffles open in an order older than anyone still breathing. Corridor membranes flex and reseal. Warning glyphs sweep through color checks no one requested. Doors test themselves against humidity signatures from families long relocated or long dead, and some still unlock at the right moisture, as if a hand had just passed.

In that sense, the first resident is the city’s own continuity engine: municipal reflex distributed through wall-veins, duct nerves, field anchors, and memory in materials. It does not think like a person, but it behaves like a caretaker that never received word to stop.

Then, if you wait through one full breath-cycle—warm rise, fog event, drain drawdown, fan-thunder lull—you begin to see the smaller lives inside that larger one.

---

The human population is thin and clustered, less a citizenry than a set of **specializations that survived**.

They live in lit stacks called occupied blocks, usually three or four towers linked together so heat can be shared and watch rotations overlap. They do not waste movement: every crossing between blocks is timed to weather pulses, pressure shifts, and valve schedules. Children learn maps as sound first, shape second. By ten, most can identify sectors blindfolded from fan harmonics and drip cadence alone.

Their jobs are almost liturgical:

- **Hydro-right crews** enforce water precedence: clearing troughs, unclogging gutter-grids, negotiating with anyone trying to build too close to a seasonal flow line.
- **Quiet-watch teams** patrol for silence. A dead sector is treated like a medical emergency.
- **Condensation stewards** read fog fronts, salt gradients, and mineral deposition on rails; they decide when elevators lock and when bridge skins need de-icing films.
- **Lumen cartographers** maintain functional light, not beauty—edge strips, stair lips, hazard corners. They map visibility the way surface cities map traffic.
- **Linguistic maintainers** keep signage intelligible. Since old wayfinding panels still update in legacy tongues, these people mediate between living speech and municipal syntax.

Most households keep humidity journals the way older cultures kept calendars. Meals are planned around dry hours and mist hours. Clothing is layered for wet-warm rather than cold-hot. Skin is often marked with mineral stains, sealant traces, and soft burns from emergency repairs.

They are not cave people in the primitive sense. They are profoundly urban. Just urban at one-tenth occupancy, in a machine-city that assumes full attendance.

---

The nonhuman life is opportunistic and precise.

The **blind skitterers** in the drains are real and common, slick-backed and quick-jointed, feeding on fungal lace and biofilm where warm condensate meets trace organics. They are tolerated because they are cleaners.

The **long-winged swifts** are almost co-tenants. They nest in turbine housings and ride thermal plumes from vent stacks, flashing pale bellies through violet maintenance light. Some blocks protect nesting runs because swifts suppress insect swarms around diagnostics.

In bioremediation trays, **reed-analogs** persist in half-dormancy, translucent stalks flexing whenever the air-pressure doors cycle. Heat-moss is harvested carefully in a few sectors—not as food exactly, more as substrate for filters, medicine cultures, and emergency oxygen mats.

And then there are the uncertain things in the shafts.

The old records call them maintenance organisms: self-limiting, nonaggressive, utility-adjacent. Generations later, people use broader names—**runners**, **pads**, **wirehounds**, **soft units**. Some are clearly biomechanical: cartilage sheath over actuator lattice, glandular coolant loops, toothless sensor snouts that taste corrosion. Some may be descended from engineered biology with machine prosthetics layered over time. They usually avoid lit human paths. They appear where structural foam has bloomed overnight or where a blocked drain is mysteriously open by morning.

No one agrees whether to classify them as fauna, tools, or another labor caste that stopped taking verbal orders.

---

There are also lives that are not bodies.

Traffic prediction lamps still pre-light corridors minutes before anyone arrives. Sometimes they are wrong; sometimes they are right in unsettling ways, guiding people toward encounters they did not plan—an injured worker, a burst pipe, a newly quiet sector. Pattern memory lingers in infrastructure. In a city built for thousands, absence itself becomes an active presence: systems searching for expected density, compensating, rehearsing, waiting.

So what lives there?

A small human commons built around maintenance and listening.  
A thin ecology of damp-adapted scavengers and thermal fliers.  
A feral fringe of legacy repair organisms, half service and half species.  
And above, below, through all of it: an urban organism made of policy, plumbing, code, and heat that still believes the city is full.

If you stand in a dark span between active blocks, you can feel all of them at once. Drips in the trough. Swift-call under rotor echo. Far pump-note in your ribs. A door unsealing for no visible reason. Footsteps—human, maybe—followed by a soft padded movement in the utility wall.

Life there is not abundant. It is **persistent**.  
Not thriving in the usual sense. **Maintaining quorum**.  
Enough voices, enough circuits, enough wet and warmth and noise to keep the cavern from becoming just rock again.
