At first, it seems like nothing lives there except abrasion.

Then you notice that some of the “abrasion marks” curve back on themselves too deliberately, and some of the dark crusts on stone are arranged in crescents where the wind should have stripped them clean. Presence here does not announce itself by motion; it announces itself by *persistence in the wrong place*.

The smallest residents are the true natives: mineral-bound films, electro-active lichen analogs, fog-sponges that grow like bleached antlers out of seams in metamorphic rock. They are not green except by accident. They are charcoal, iron-red, silver-black. They drink light when they can, charge when they must, and spend most of their time waiting in chemically suspended patience. In sheltered hours, they crackle faintly as charge equalizes across their surfaces—whole colonies thinking in microvolts.

The animals are less “herds” than events.  
A skater flashes past, six limbs wide, body low, skin fringed with conductive hairs; when a shear front hits, it half-lifts on electrostatic repulsion and lets the wind do the expensive part. Burrowers map the subsurface in dry clicks and scrape signatures, emerging mostly at night when charge gradients are gentle enough not to flay exposed tissue. Predation is brief, almost embarrassed: a collision in dust, then stillness.

But the beings that truly *inhabit* the plateau—the ones who belong to its scale—are the tower-keepers, though “keepers” is only partly right. They do not own the old infrastructure; they orbit it. One cluster to a pressure lighthouse, one lineage to a gravity stitch, sometimes no more than a dozen individuals spread across a territory that would hold cities elsewhere. They are sparse on purpose. Density is danger here.

Seen up close, they look built for kneeling into weather.  
Tall but narrow, low-profile when crouched, with an extra pair of forelimbs used less for speed than for anchoring and instrument work. Their skin is matte and dark, patterned with pale conductive tracery that can shunt charge around vital organs during electrical skin-storms. Their eyes are hooded, reflective, and excellent at contrast in hard noon light; their hearing is modest, but they read pressure and electrostatic change with extraordinary precision through fine filaments along jaw, wrists, and spine. Where you feel “wind incoming,” they feel topology in the air.

They do not speak much aloud in open ground. Thin air steals consonants.  
At distance, they communicate by mirror-flash, polarized cloth, and thermal glyphs laid on stone. At close range, by dry tongue-clicks, wrist taps, and conductive contact—brief skin-to-skin pulses carrying simple coded charge. Their songs, when they do sing, are mostly for enclosed places: maintenance vaults, tower throats, old bridge undersides where resonance still works.

Their settlements are easy to miss because they resemble geological caution.  
Half-buried vaults in lee hollows. Fold-flat canopies that become ground color when stowed. Anchor gardens: rings of silica sponges and dark phototrophic crust cultivated around dew traps and static combs. They farm not water but *opportunity*—fog minutes, laminar windows, nights with low discharge, the brief thermal inversions when the sky gives something back.

Culturally, they are less a kingdom than a maintenance rhythm.  
They inherit routes between functioning relics. They memorize corridor timings around pressure lighthouses. They “tune” old machines with rituals that are half engineering, half liturgy: replacing sacrificial conductive lattices, realigning dust baffles, reading memory stones by drone-shadow and dawn angle. They measure history not in reigns, but in failure delays—how many years a gravity stitch held, how much slip was bled into harmless micro-flex.

They bury almost nothing. The plateau does not keep soft things.  
Their dead are rendered to mineral ash, vitrified bead, and conductive filament; portions are sealed into repair mortars or set into albedo mosaics near memory stones. In this way, ancestors literally become infrastructure: a hand in the wall, a name in thermal script visible only from altitude.

So what lives there?

Not abundance. Not wilderness in the lush sense.  
What lives there is a thin, stubborn intelligence—biological and inherited—spread across stone, storm, and machine. Life as punctuation, yes, but precise punctuation: commas in the wind, semicolons at the edge of canyons, long patient dashes between one ancient tower and the next.
