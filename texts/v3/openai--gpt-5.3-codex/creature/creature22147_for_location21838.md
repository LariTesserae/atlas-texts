At first: nothing.  
Not emptiness as metaphor—actual biological null. No fur, no frond, no wing-shadow. If you come looking for life in the usual register, the place gives you stone, heat, pressure, and contempt.

Then, after enough nights in that near-dark, you start to notice that *residence* doesn’t require blood.

The oldest inhabitants are the crystal colonies in the fractures—what crossers call **choirs**. From a distance they’re just milky blades packed shoulder to shoulder. Up close they are active in the only way this country allows: by accretion pulses, by infinitesimal lattice decisions, by the slow theft of vapor and trace ions from hot breath moving through cracks. Put your ear against them and you hear faint ticking, not random but clustered, like rain beginning on a roof. A choir “feeds” when the ground flexes and opens new microvoids; it “starves” when a seam seals. It can lose half its mass in one collapse and be larger a week later. Nothing soft ever grows on it. Nothing grazes it. It expands into vacancy until vacancy changes shape.

In the basins, where mineral skins bloom and curl, live the **films**—reactive crusts that creep so slowly they look fixed until you mark their edge and return. They are chemistry with a territorial instinct. One front oxidizes iron-dark flats into brittle red lace; another plates sulfur-pale scales over them; another, black and glassy, overruns both after a venting event and resets the field. Their boundaries are sharp as coastlines. Their wars are invisible unless you watch for months, but every boot print becomes a laboratory: halos form, flakes lift, salts feather outward, and by next passage the print is gone, translated into new geometry.

Along ridgelines during static weather, there are the **crawl-lights**: brief electric organisms if you’re generous, weather if you’re strict. They run the knife-edges in branching veins, hesitate at broken spurs, leap gaps, vanish. They leave no carcass, only hairline pitting and a smell of hot metal. Yet the linewalkers map their routes as if they were migration paths, because where crawl-lights travel repeatedly, stone weakens, slabs release, and tomorrow’s safe saddle becomes tonight’s fracture.

And then there are the only creatures that carry tools: the **crossers**.

They do not settle; settlement would be a misunderstanding. They arrive in ones and twos, sometimes roped, sometimes alone, faces wrapped against the sting in the air, packs full of chalk, wedges, wire, cloth, and water sealed like medicine. They are identified less by body than by behavior: pausing to read a fresh crack the way a sailor reads cloud; tapping crust with a rod before trusting it; sleeping in those one-person cut shelters with boots on. They build low walls where gas pools. They restack fallen cairns. They move markers uphill after a buttress lifts, then return months later to find the whole route obsolete and start again. Their architecture is not ownership. It is punctuation in an argument with terrain.

Crossers name features in verbs, not nouns: *the-slope-that-sheds-at-dawn*, *the-fissure-that-opens-after-blue-light*, *the-vent-that-lies*. A child born among them learns fault sounds before lullabies. Their dead are rarely buried; ground will not keep a grave shape for long. Instead, a name is cut into a stable face if one can be found, and if not, spoken at the next ridge while static crawls overhead. Memory here is portable.

So what lives there?

Pressure lives there. Crystallization lives there. Oxidation fronts, charge paths, phase shifts—processes with territory, timing, and consequence. And at the margins, briefly, stubbornly, the linewalkers live there too: not as conquerors, not as natives exactly, but as readers of a text that rewrites itself while it is being read.

In that country, to live is not to endure.  
It is to keep pace with becoming.
