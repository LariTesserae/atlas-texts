At first, it seems the chamber is inhabited only by **condition**—warmth, wetness, patience.  
Then you notice that condition has habits.

Something there keeps to the same shallow basin of mud near the old fire ring, where the floor is warmest and the ceiling drips at reliable intervals. Not a nest exactly, not a lair. More like a place where a body can remain without needing to insist on itself. The mud there is always faintly combed, as if fingers—or something finger-like—have passed through and then let the water smooth it back.

What lives there is small enough to disappear against stone and large enough to move the room’s balance when it shifts. In darkness you wouldn’t see it first; you would feel it as a change in nearness, a slight displacement of heat, a delay in the next drip’s echo. When light does catch it, it looks less like a finished animal than a draft of one: skin pale as soaked root, semi-matte like clay kept damp under cloth; limbs narrow, elbowed, and patient; a head with no visible ears and eyes filmed over, not damaged but unnecessary. Its mouth is soft and wide, suited more to tasting water and mineral than tearing flesh.

It is amphibious in the old, literal sense—not between land and water, but between states. It can lie still for hours as if part of the wall’s swelling surface, then uncoil with startling precision to follow a new run of condensation down a seam. It drinks by pressing its throat and chest to wet stone. It feeds sparingly: the leathery wall mats, trace invertebrates that ride in with roots, old marrow and oil leached from fish bones hung in the crevice. It wastes almost nothing. Even its shedding comes off in translucent veils that dissolve into the mud within a day.

Its senses are tuned to things most creatures ignore. It maps by warmth gradients finer than touch, by the mineral taste of droplets from different ceiling nodules, by vibration carried through saturated clay. A footstep outside the hidden crack will freeze it motionless. A human whisper inside will draw it closer, but never abruptly—always in arcs, always with pauses, as if respecting lines no one else can see.

And it is not untouched by people. The old bowls are sometimes found moved an inch, rim-up to catch drips from a particular seam. The scraper’s edge stays mysteriously clean. The fire ring, blackened from long ago, occasionally smells faintly recent though no ash has shifted. Mountain folk who know this place say the inhabitant does not fear smoke; it remembers it. They bring fish bones, salt crust, knotted roots. Not as food exactly, and not exactly as offering—more like keeping terms with a resident elder that is neither pet nor god.

If you watch long enough, you realize it practices a kind of caretaking. It clears blocked rivulets with its snout. It nudges fallen root tips back toward cracks where seepage is strongest. It does not let one wall-mat invade another; it grazes boundaries and keeps the strict borders intact. In that sense, the chamber’s strange equilibrium is not passive. It is maintained.

Age is hard to assign to such a being. It does not seem juvenile or old. Scars soften quickly in constant moisture. Growth, if it occurs, is incremental beyond normal witnessing. Perhaps there have been many, each replacing the last with no visible break. Perhaps there is only one, stretched across generations by this place where progression stalls. The people who return here rarely agree on its shape, but they agree on its continuity: *it is always there when the fog opens the crack*.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a blind, warm-stone custodian of an unfinished room,  
a creature made to endure steadiness rather than change,  
half-animal, half-practice,  
kept by the mountain and, in its own quiet way, keeping it back.
