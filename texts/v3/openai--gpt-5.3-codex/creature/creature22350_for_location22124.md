At first, what lives there looks like absence.

Not ruin—care.  
Not abandonment—spacing.

You notice it in the swept thresholds, in the ropes that have been re-tarred that morning, in the way no sluice gate is ever half-stuck. Someone is always just out of sight, and what they leave behind is not clutter but completion.

Then you begin to see them.

They are a small people for a large plan: basin-keepers, level-watchers, stone-listeners—different valleys call them different things, but they call themselves, simply, **the Held**: those held by the bowl, and those who hold it in turn until it can hold itself.

They dress for seep and chill, not for display. Wool close to skin, reed-fiber overwraps that dry fast, short capes waxed with pine and mineral fat. Their boots are soft-soled so they can feel where ground is firming and where it still trembles under weight. Their cuffs are always darkened with wet. Their hands are pale at the knuckles and permanently lined with green from mint, liverwort, and rope dye. Most wear cords around the wrist tied with tiny carved toggles—personal tools for shutters, knots, and signaling slats.

They move slowly in public spaces, but not lazily. Their pace is measured to load: how much a ledge can take, how long a gate should be eased open, how fast a cistern wall can cool after sun. Children are taught early to step where the stone has learned a footstep before. Visitors think this is superstition until they watch a child redirect an elder with one touch to the elbow, and hear, moments later, a fresh seam tick beneath the path they avoided.

---

Their city has offices, but their true profession is **timing**.

Some are **Knockkeepers**, the ones who take evening stations at hollow posts and send level reports across districts in coded strikes. They can hear delay in an echo and tell whether a channel has silted upstream.

Some are **Green Masons**, who plant living binders into young cracks: reed lace, willow whips, mats of root and moss that hold moisture where curing needs slow steadiness and draw it away where a wall must harden.

Some are **Liftwrights**, lean-armed operators of counterweights and bucket chains, who can raise a day’s water without spilling a bowl’s worth.

Some are **Age-Scribes**. They revise maps not for streets but for readiness. A staircase can stay drawn as “future weight” for ten years. A plaza can change from Last-Lift to Green-Crack after one winter of clean clicks and no shear.

And a few, often the oldest, are **Listeners** in the strict sense: people who wake in the deep cold and sit with ear to stone. They keep records of night sounds—tiny interior snaps, settling pops, long almost-musical strains when a whole shelf takes its next degree of set. Their ledgers are treated like law.

---

Their households are unusual to outsiders. Kinship matters, but ground-age matters as much. People are assigned not just by family line but by what district they are trained to tend: First-Cooled people learn patience with brittle pride; Last-Lift people learn lightness and retreat; Green-Crack people become experts in temporary architecture and graceful dismantling.

They marry across ages on purpose, so every home carries more than one pace of time.

They keep many rooms empty on purpose too. Not because they expect ghosts—because they expect descendants. A house may stand aired, swept, and structurally checked for thirty years before it is occupied. “Vacant” here often means “promised.”

---

As for temperament: they are not solemn, just restrained by acoustics. In that resonant basin, every shout travels, every dropped tool becomes public. So they speak low, laugh in bursts, sing while working at water-noise edges where the spillways absorb song. Their humor is dry and practical: jokes about impatient stone, gossip about walls “finally deciding to behave.”

Their festivals are weather-bound. The biggest celebration is not harvest but **First Bearing**—when a previously scaffolded platform passes its weight trial and receives its first gathering. The feast is full of damp herbs, goat cheese from visiting herders, and hot mineral broth. At dusk they strike the posts in celebratory stagger, and for one night the entire bowl sounds like a giant wooden heart.

---

If there is a single oldest resident, though, it is neither human nor animal.

It is the **unfinishedness itself**—the young rock still becoming rock.

The Held treat it as you would treat a recovering body: never rushed, never trusted blindly, never left unattended. They do not think of themselves as founders. They think of themselves as splints, stitches, and wet cloth laid across a fevered brow. Necessary, temporary, intimate.

So what lives there?

A sparse people with maintenance in their bones.  
Frogs in the channels, swallows on the beams, goats at the salt-wet walls.  
Root, moss, reed, and water doing relentless small work.  
And under all of it, a city-sized act of patience: stone learning, day by day, how to be land.
