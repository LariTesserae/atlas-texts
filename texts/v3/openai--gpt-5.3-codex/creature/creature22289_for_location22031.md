At first you’d think nothing *lives there* in the way we usually mean it—no herds, no villages, no obvious apex thing pacing the horizon.

Then, after a few days, you realize the resident is spread out under your boots.

It is a **mat-being**: not one species, but a long collaboration of sedge roots, peat moss, microbial films, fungal threads, and water-borne chemistry that has learned to behave like a single slow animal. It has no face, no center, no moment of birth you could point to. Its “body” is the whole wet skin of the plateau. Its nerves are pressure changes in saturated ground. Its memory is stored in acidity gradients, buried stems, old flow paths, and the mineral stain around culvert mouths.

It eats light, stone dust, and weather.  
It breathes through millions of tiny wet surfaces.  
It grows by knitting loose ash into something that can hold weight for one more season.

Where you see a stream wandering, it is deciding.  
Where you see peat rising by millimeters, it is thickening its hide.  
Where a pump line sags and then stabilizes because roots have locked the soil around its footings, that is not accident—it is coexistence.

And inside that broad resident life, there is a much smaller one: the **maintenance people**—a thin, scattered population of tenders, gauge readers, and pump mechanics who live in shifts rather than settlements. They are the only upright mammals that truly stay. You know them by orange cuffs, waxed jackets, and the way they talk with their hands because the plateau is too loud for casual speech. They sleep in isolated huts on pilings, move by rail cart and boardwalk, and keep meticulous logs in metal cabinets that smell of damp paper and machine oil.

They do not rule the place. They negotiate with it.

They clear an intake, and the sedge closes elsewhere.  
They re-level a channel, and the moss rises around the new edge.  
They scrape lichen from insulators, then mark in pencil how quickly it returns.  
They treat every repair as temporary, every reading as a conversation, every structure as something that must flex with ground that is still becoming ground.

So what lives there?

A **distributed wetland intelligence** that builds land out of water and decay, and a **small guild of human custodians** embedded in its noise—never fully at home, never fully separate—keeping the plateau legible while it invents itself.
