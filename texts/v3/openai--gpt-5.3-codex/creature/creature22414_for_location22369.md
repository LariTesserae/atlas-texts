What lives there is, first, a **multitude**.

Not a single king species, not a hidden beast—just a dense republic of edge-dwellers, each one small and practical and perfectly suited to dim wet heat. Frogs hold the ledges like punctuation marks. Toads sit in the soft berms where silt is new. Carp idle in the slow trenches, eels write dark cursive through culvert mouths, and beetles bead the surface tension in places too shallow to name as pools. Bats sleep in stitched rows until some pressure shift or light tremor lifts them all at once into a brief weather of wings.

Then you notice what lives lower than animals: the **film-life**.  
Bacterial skins on concrete. Algae on lit spillways. Pale fungal breath in joints where insulation stays damp. These are the first citizens and last survivors, feeding the rest, thickening stones, softening edges, turning runoff into food-web. They are not scenery; they are the base economy.

Higher up, in the warmer mechanical strata, live the opportunists that track human infrastructure like migrating herds: roaches in cable warmth, rats along beam-lines, feral cats patrolling catwalk territories above flood reach. Midges and mosquitoes lift in clouds over outlets that run a degree warmer than the basin. Cave crickets occupy the seam between dry and wet like specialists in border law.

And yes—people live there too, though lightly. Not as settlers, more as rotational mammals: pump crews, inspectors, divers after storm events, electricians who know which valve trees sing before they fail. They leave bright tape, chalk marks, replaced gaskets, logged numbers. Their presence is periodic, but their habits shape channels almost as much as water does.

After a while, though, all these separate residents begin reading as one thing:  
a **composite animal** made of water movement, concrete geometry, and organisms that have learned its timing. Its pulse is pump cycles. Its breath is mist from warm manifolds. Its nervous system is vibration through pipe racks and column lines. Its memory is laid down in flood marks, rust strata, and the return paths of fish and frogs after every surge.

So what lives there?

An undercity wetland-body that is never finished—  
built partly by engineers, mostly by seepage, and continuously inhabited by creatures that specialize in “almost”: almost dark, almost still, almost wild, almost machine.  
It is crowded with lives, but the resident, singular, is **adaptation itself**.
