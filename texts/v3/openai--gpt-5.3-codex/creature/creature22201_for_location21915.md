What lives there is something that learned to think at the speed of weather and age at the speed of stone.

They call themselves, in the closest translation, **the Kept**—not because they are passive, but because they see life as mutual maintenance: mountain keeps water, water keeps forest, forest keeps cloud, and people keep the agreements between them. Outsiders once called them hermits, then engineers, then priests. None fit. They are closer to **ecological jurists**—beings who inherit and renew the “contracts written into matter.”

Physically, they are built for this hot altitude. Deep chest volume and layered lungs let them work in vapor-thick air; their skin carries a faint reflective bloom that sheds noon heat and fungal load. Their hands and feet are wide, tendon-rich, excellent on wet stone and root mesh. Most wear almost no rigid clothing: braided vine-fiber harness, ceramic tags, seal-rings for field interfaces, and capes woven from condensation mesh that drink the night and release coolness by day. In fog they vanish quickly; in hard sun they look like dark, moving cutouts against white sky.

They do not cluster into cities. A “settlement” here is a **constellation**: a weather house on one spur, marsh gardens two ridges over, an archive-shrine in a cliff fold, nursery platforms suspended in fern belts. People move among these nodes in long cycles, often along routes timed to mistfall and storm charge. A child may grow up knowing six households separated by half a day of gliding, climbing, and water-causeway travel, and still call them one home.

Their intelligence is practical, sensory, and patient. They can read a slope by smell: iron seep, microbial bloom, condenser fatigue, imminent slip. They listen to old devices the way others listen to elders—through pulse timing, vent rhythm, tonal drift. Much of their craft is **tuning rather than building**: rethreading condensation ribbons, recalibrating shade planes, coaxing a failing gravity channel into a gentler logic rather than replacing it outright. When a machine finally fails, they do not mourn it as loss alone; they steward its afterlife into habitat.

Socially, they are quiet but not austere. They celebrate thresholds: first fog of the wet phase, return of cliff orchids, successful rerouting of a seasonal flood, the night thunder gets trapped under the rim and the mountain hums back. Their music is percussive and resonant—hollow stone, stretched root-string, water-drip chambers tuned like bells. Their stories are stored in route-memory: to recite history is to walk it, stopping at marker lines where seeds drift uphill and naming what changed, what held, what must be repaired before next warm surge.

They are not the only complex life there, and they do not pretend to rule it. They cohabit with glider flocks that roost on their towers, with marsh grazers that crop engineered reedbeds into firebreaks, with ambush predators whose hunting corridors are mapped and respected. They maintain salt-lick terraces to divert grazers from fragile moss fields; they open and close canopy gaps to shift insect emergence; they leave certain ruins untouched because a whole food web now depends on their strange physics.

If you meet one, you may not realize it at first. You might see only someone kneeling beside an ancient cliff-arc, palm on warm ceramic, waiting through three full vent cycles before making a single adjustment. That is how they live here: not as conquerors of impossible engineering, not as relic-keepers, but as participants in a long, humid equilibrium where technology, biology, and geology have become one continuous organism—and they are one of its careful minds.
