At first, you think **the animals** are the residents and everyone else is passing through.

In this dark, wet basin, the first intelligible patterns are nonhuman: tunnel-grazers taking their slow mineral routes, glider shoals banking on heat plumes at the same hour each pressure-cycle, scavenger packs opening and abandoning drainage corridors with the discipline of trained crews. They move like ownership. They are not pets, not wildlife in the lowland sense, but working weather—mobile forces everyone plans around.

Then you start to notice the ones who built the sounding lanes.

---

They call themselves, in trade speech, something like **the Condensate Houses**. Outsiders shorten it to *Condensates* or *Basinfolk*. Neither name is quite right, because they do not think of themselves as a species standing apart from place. They think in terms of occupancy contracts: stone, water, fauna, and people sharing load.

Physically, they look near-human at a distance—upright, tool-using, social—but close up they carry generations of adaptation to wet cold and acoustic navigation.

- Their skin is dark, low-reflective, often patterned with conductive inks that function as health displays and lane IDs.  
- Nostrils and sinus structures are unusually elaborate; breath is identity here, and theirs is chemically expressive.  
- Most wear open-faced respirator veils instead of sealed masks, because total isolation from ambient humidity makes doors, systems, and people treat you like a ghost.  
- Their boots are thin-soled in specific strips, not for silence but for *reading*: they “hear” building-signatures through bone conduction in ankles, knees, jaw.  
- In conversation, they tilt their heads to offer one ear to the person and one to the infrastructure. Nobody here listens in only one direction.

Children are taught three literacies together: pressure, resonance, and animal etiquette. Reading and numbers come with them, but those three keep you alive.

---

Their cities are less built than **continuously negotiated**.

A tower isn’t simply occupied by people. It has declared bands: brood shelves for membrane-gliders, warm ledges reserved for certain roost colonies, condensation gutters that feed cistern biota, transit ducts for service drones, and human passages woven among all of that. If a species shifts migration and begins using a new ledge, the district doesn’t “remove pests.” It revises schedules, lighting frequencies, and vibration markers until conflict drops.

So who lives there? Not a dominant species. A **charter of presences**.

The Condensate Houses are the legal voice in that charter, but they are outnumbered by animals by absurd margins and they know it. Their authority depends on maintaining acoustic right-of-way and thermal fairness—keeping heat leaks, feed timing, and lane announcements predictable enough that bodies of very different scale can share volume without constant collision.

---

They are patient people.

Surface cultures mark time by sun and season. Basinfolk mark it by:
- glacier creep rates,
- mineral bloom in drip-lines,
- migration pulse density,
- and the long “memory shifts” in grown structures.

A person may spend twenty years as a lane-auditor, then transfer to brood arbitration, then to stress-cartography in mineral foam towers. Careers resemble ecological succession more than rank climbing.

Their religion—if you can call it that—is practiced in repurposed reservoirs called **listening vaults**. No icons. No sermons. Groups stand in total dark while the city’s layered sound passes through water, stone, and ribcage. They map obligation from what they hear: which districts are overloaded, which colonies are hungry, which old structures are about to ask for reclassification. Devotion here is maintenance extended across generations.

---

And there is one more resident, harder to describe because it has no single body:

**the Basin System itself**.

Programmable foams that rewrite load paths, anti-ice skins that alter permeability, droplet-sorters that learn new chemistries, microbial-machine films that tune conductivity—none of these are conscious in the way a person is. But together they exhibit memory, preference, and reaction times that make locals speak of them as a temperamental elder. You do not command it. You court it, test it, and stay legible to it.

That is why newcomers get warned: stay in sounding lanes, keep your breath readable, watch where stone begins to weep. Those are not quaint customs. They are grammar for speaking to a city that is alive in aggregate.

So what lives there?

A dark-water civilization of human descendants, yes.  
An immense animal metropolis, yes.  
And between them, a distributed mineral intelligence made of pipes, foam, heat, pressure, and habit.

None of them can survive there alone.  
Together, they are what the basin means by “inhabited.”
