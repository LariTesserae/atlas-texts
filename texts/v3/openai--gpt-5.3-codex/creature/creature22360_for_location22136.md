What lives in the ward is not one species, but a **working coalition**: people, plants, microbes, and machines so interdependent they read as a single organism.

If you stand still long enough, you can pick out its layers.

First, the obvious life: the **green infrastructure** itself.  
Not “plants” in the decorative sense—more like disciplined tissues of the district.

- Vines that are trained, numbered, and tension-checked like cables  
- Root masses that are logged in maintenance records like beams  
- Reed beds with performance histories: turbidity in, clarity out  
- Moss and mat species assigned to walls by moisture profile, not by beauty

They are alive in a calm, relentless way. No rush of bloom, no dramatic dieback, just accretion: thickening cambium, widening grip, new tendrils tied into yesterday’s scaffold. Their leaves carry the marks of human intent more than predation—straight cuts, graft tape scars, training clips.

Then there is the less visible life: **the wet biologies**.  
Biofilms in pipes, nitrifying colonies in filter bays, fungal lattices in coir berms, bacterial skins in settling tanks. They are not spoken about romantically, but everyone in the ward respects them. They are fed, monitored, and never sterilized past function. A clean-looking system is often a failing one; a healthy system has a little slipperiness, a little smell, a little color shift in the right places.

And then, unmistakably, there are the people—the ones the ward has shaped over generations.

They tend to be compact in movement, economical with gesture. In tight lanes, nobody wastes motion. They wear waterproof layers gone soft at the seams, gloves with grip rebuilt in patches, harness loops for clipping tools when both hands are needed on rail and line. Their boots are broad-soled, anti-slip, perpetually damp. Their forearms are strong from pruning overhead and hauling hose. Their hearing is tuned to machinery: they can tell a healthy pump by rhythm through a wall.

They don’t “walk through” the ward so much as **thread it**.  
At junctions they touch ridged rail, glance at flow arrows, and keep time with shifts in sound. They read humidity like weather, leaf angle like instrument data, and condensation patterns like warning lights. Some can identify a blocked return line by smell before gauges drift.

Their speech has adapted too: short phrases, close range, hand signals that can be read through mist and glare. Conversations happen shoulder-to-shoulder over catwalk rail, mouths near ears, then break cleanly when a valve sequence starts.

Their social structure follows the ward’s logic. Not glamorous titles—functional ones:

- intake hands  
- rootwrights  
- line pruners  
- clone keepers  
- pump mechanics  
- fiber press crews  
- night inspectors

The respected elders are often not managers but people with memory: those who remember which wall moved three millimeters after the wet winter; which vine strain tolerates low-light sodium; which root plate can take load and which is only pretending.

Children, if they grow up near it, learn orientation by touch before maps. They learn that silence means danger; that every cut has consequences two terraces away; that “harvest” and “repair” can be the same act. Many leave for dry jobs aboveground. Many come back, because nowhere else feels as immediate as a place where your work changes humidity, flow, and structure within the hour.

Even the machinery feels like a resident class.  
Not alive, but not inert either: pumps with known tempers, fan banks with seasonal moods, conveyor chains that “complain” before failure. Crews speak of them by nicknames, not serials, because in practice they are colleagues—loud, heavy colleagues that must be listened to.

So what lives there?

A **ward-species**:  
human caretakers adapted to damp and noise; trained vegetation adapted to cut and tie; microbial engines adapted to waste and flow; mechanical circulatory systems adapted to never quite stopping.

No single part could survive there alone, not for long.  
Together they make a subterranean neighborhood that breathes through pipes, drinks through root curtains, thinks through maintenance logs, and remembers itself in scars—on concrete, on stems, on hands.
