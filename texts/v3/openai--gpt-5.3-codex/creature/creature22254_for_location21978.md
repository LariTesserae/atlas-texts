At first glance, you’d say **builders** live there.  
Not settlers, not priests, not farmers in any ordinary sense—builders in the most literal, breath-by-breath way. People who wake into scaffolds and sleep to the percussion of mallets, who measure weather by condensation rates on cord, who can tell the mood of a wall by taste.

But if you linger, “builders” is too small a word.

---

They call themselves something close to *Those-Who-Brace*—the translation is clumsy because in their language the word also means *to steady a vessel in boiling water* and *to answer a tilt before it becomes a fall*. They are human in outline, but shaped by generations of vertical humidity and unstable physics:

- broad, splayed feet for reed decking and wet tile,  
- shoulders knotted from rope-hauling,  
- palms webbed with scar tissue from lime and clay,  
- lungs trained for steam-thick air, voices pitched to cut through resonance.

Their hair is usually bound tight with waxed fiber; loose hair is dangerous near lifts and drums. Their skin carries mineral bloom—salt lines at the temples, white crust along brows after long kiln shifts. Children learn knots before script. Elders have a mason’s sensory map: they can hear a hollow behind plaster from ten paces away.

They do not divide labor cleanly. A person here is never just one thing. The same woman who sets glazed channel tiles at dawn may graft root mats by noon and tune a pressure flute at dusk so the drainage sings the right warning note in storm surge.

---

They live in **crews**, not households.  
A crew belongs to a cistern, a lift line, a terrace spine—an ongoing task more than a bloodline. Kinship is practical and adopted: whoever held your harness at first climb is “clasp-mother,” whoever taught you to fire seam-clay without cracking is “kiln-uncle.” The dead are not buried (there is no ground to spare); their names are set into working elements—ladder rungs, basin lips, spillway stones—so speaking infrastructure is speaking ancestry.

Meals are quick and wet: steamed tubers, fermented leaf mash, fish when the upper vents gift one by accident, fruits cut from wall-grown vines. They eat while standing, while lashing, while listening for pressure shifts. Stillness reads as neglect.

Sleep comes in rotating cycles because the place never fully enters night and because construction cannot stop. Not shouldn’t—**cannot**. They believe, with reason, that if the making pauses too long, the pocket world loses its argument with impossible geometry and begins to peel apart.

---

And here is where the inhabitants stop being merely human.

The mortars are seeded. The channels are inoculated. The phosphor algae on canal rims are not just light: they are memory tissue. The root systems threading drilled holes are not merely trained by hydraulic patterning; they are part of a distributed sensing body. Every repaired seam, every damp cane lashing, every moss strip pressed into geometric tracks adds to a low, slow intelligence that lives in the structure itself.

The builders call it the **Wet Ledger**.

Not a god, exactly. Not a machine either. A resident pattern-mind made of seepage routes, capillary roots, mineral films, fungal lace, and recurrent human touch. It “remembers” where water once wanted to go, where weight once failed, where a platform might safely appear before anyone can physically reach it. It is why channels sometimes reverse flow. Why seeds choose improbable niches. Why new masonry arrives overnight with tool marks no one claims.

The people do not worship it in temples. They **negotiate** with it through craft.  
A new buttress is a question.  
A braid of reeds over a sweating seam is an argument.  
A basin left intentionally unsealed is a concession.

When they say, “The wall answered,” they mean the plaster held, the water redirected, and tomorrow’s geometry did not kill anyone.

---

There are other lives, smaller and stranger:

- the pale throat-lizards, kept half-feral to eat biting flies and to “taste” air pressure changes;  
- thunder cicadas whose emergence marks seasonal shifts in vent-light;  
- cistern frogs, fat as fists, bred for call depth because different boom frequencies reveal water volume in hidden chambers;  
- long-legged cloud birds, never domesticated, treated as omens of upper-vent openness.

But these are satellites. The core inhabitants are the Bracers and the Wet Ledger together—tool-users and seep-memory in a permanent co-construction.

---

So what lives there?

A people who have replaced the idea of “finished” with “stable for now.”  
A living architecture that has learned to accept instruction from hands.  
A society where physics is not trusted, only courted.

In another place they would be called engineers, gardeners, masons, hydrologists, mystics. Here those are all the same profession: **continuing**. And continuation itself is the native species of that hanging courtyard—hot, dripping, loud, and always one level away from becoming something new.
