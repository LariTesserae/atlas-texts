At first, it looks like no one *lives* in the Lower Intake so much as survives it in shifts.

A school hangs under a lamp until the flow turns.  
Crabs comb a seam until a surge strips it bare.  
Eels borrow a conduit, leave it, return on the next tide like workers on staggered hours.

It feels communal, temporary, ownerless.

Then you start noticing places that are too deliberate.

A valve chamber where every loose shell has been stacked to one side.  
A maintenance recess where mussel drifts have been cleared from a single hand-width path.  
Cable trays plugged with stones, glass, and crab carapaces to tame a current that would otherwise rip through.  
The same kind of shell midden, over and over, at the mouths of old actuator housings.

Something is editing the architecture.

---

What lives there, most completely, is an octopus lineage that has occupied the Intake so long it behaves less like a species in habitat and more like a tenant in inherited rooms.

Not giant in a mythic way—just old, thick-armed, scarred, and perfectly proportioned for this machinery. Mantles dark as wet iron; skin that can go from rust-pitted orange to lime-fouled concrete gray in a heartbeat. Suckers strong enough to hold on through gate reversals, dexterous enough to peel barnacles from bolt heads one by one.

They den in obsolete valve casings because valve casings are ideal dens: circular, hard-backed, one or two defensible openings, near predictable flow, close to food routes. Each den accumulates generations of debris: clam shells, crab claws, fish vertebrae, bits of insulation, snapped zip ties, rounded nuts and washers carried in for no obvious nutritional reason and kept anyway.

The living animal is one thing.  
The den is another.  
Together, they are the resident.

---

You could call the current matriarch the Keeper, though no one named her. She knows the six-hour breath of the complex down to minutes. She does not fight the water; she schedules herself around it.

On flood turn, she waits at a corridor pinch where silver fish must flatten and commit.  
On slack, she leaves the main throat and patrols lamp halos for crabs blinded by competition.  
When warm plumes pulse from a pump housing, she checks the bacterial seams for shrimp bloom and easy feeding.  
When storms load the shafts and the deep thrum rises, she moves deeper into the old filter galleries before debris starts flying.

She has scars that read like the Intake’s map: one arm tip cleanly missing (gate shear), mantle pitting (eel bites), sucker rings polished smooth from endless contact with the same rail edges.

She is not the only octopus in the complex. But she is the one whose routes are copied.

Juveniles settle in satellite housings and learn by following chemical traces, shell cues, and the practical archaeology of her middens. They inherit not territory lines but timing: where to be when the water inhales, where not to be when it exhales, which lit patch is worth contesting and which is a death funnel.

In that way, “the one that lives there” is also a behavior that persists longer than any body.

---

Around her, the Intake keeps its crowded cast:

- Mussels turning suspended grit into flesh along calmer ribs.  
- Tube worms in rust gardens where iron feeds films that feed grazers.  
- Congers in blind conduits, taking what misses her ambushes.  
- Predatory fish running patrol loops like clock hands between buttresses.  
- Shrimp and small crabs cleaning the leftovers at den mouths, tolerated until they aren’t.

Nothing in this is mystical. It is infrastructure ecology, tight and repetitive, where intelligence pays because geometry repeats and timing matters.

So what lives in the Lower Intake?

A whole city of tide-timed animals, yes.  
But most of all: an old, practical, tool-using octopus household, generation after generation, making a home inside a machine that never stopped moving.
