What lives there is less a population than a **guild of listeners**.

At the top of that guild are the few remaining human keepers—if there are still humans there at all in the way we usually mean. They are not rulers of the place; they are its jointed hands. Lean people in damp-waxed wraps, rope belts, and nail-scarred palms. They move with poles, wedges, and mallets, never hurrying except just before overtopping. Their hearing is tuned to pressure: they can tell from one gate’s chatter whether a basin two levels down is about to backflow. They sleep lightly in lofted sheds above flood marks, wake to drips, and begin each day by walking lines of seam, grate, lip, hinge. They do not “work the marsh.” They keep a conversation going with it.

Below and around them is the great amphibian citizenship: frogs of every throat and tempo, broad-backed toads that treat spillways as roads, slick little climbers that lay eggs in warm valve housings. Their calls are not background noise; they are signals in a shared acoustic map. When the pitch changes, something in water level has changed. Even predators seem to use the chorus as instrumentation.

Then the quick scavenger class: otter-shaped tile-runners, six-legged gutter skitters, small masked things that pry mussels from sluice corners and steal trapped fish from woven baskets if a knot is left loose. They are shameless, clever, and mechanically literate. They know which shutters drift open first, which beams hold heat after sunset, which counterweights can be ridden safely down.

The birds are opportunists and forecasters. Some nest in dead watch frames and abandoned signal towers; some wait, uncannily punctual, beside overflow slots minutes before release. There are white-throated screamers that patrol the long flats, herons that stand at valve mouths like priests, and dusk kites that hunt the insect clouds driven up by warm cisterns. When weather turns, they spiral over the deep basin before the rain arrives, as if reading pressure with their wings.

Inside the machine’s blind places live specialist nations: pale eels threading inspection tubes no hand can reach, blind crabs breeding in statue hollows where condensation runs, translucent shrimp in sumps under grilles, flat snails that feed on mineral bloom. None of these creatures need open marsh. They need engineered damp: enclosed flow, predictable seep, rough lime, shadow, and time.

On the embankment ridges move the fox-sized marsh hunters—long-legged, narrow-pawed, with a balance that seems supernatural until you watch them: they are constantly sampling grade through their pads. They can run wet tile in moonless fog without a slip. They hunt by interruption—listening for one missing frog note, one panic splash in a regular drip-pattern.

Insects are the true majority. Midge veils, beetles in plank cavities, mosquito skeins, clicking water striders, wasps under eaves. They are food, alarm, pollinators of the accidental rooftop shrubs, and the soft engine behind every larger heartbeat.

And finally, there is one more resident: **the system itself**—not alive in a biological sense, but inhabited like a body. Water pressure moves through it like breath. Noise is its pulse. Repairs are its healing. Animals and keepers alike live not just in its space, but in its timing.

So what lives there?

Not ruin-haunters, not wilderness reclaimers.  
A civilization of creatures—human and otherwise—adapted to an old hydraulic mind, all of them fluent in wet stone, echo, and flow.
