At first, it seems like the place is occupied only by systems: rain, rot, seep, bloom.  
Then you begin to notice that some of those systems have eyes.

They call the largest grazers there **hushbacks**—not because they’re silent (nothing alive is truly silent), but because sound seems to fold around them. They are big-shouldered, long-bodied ungulates, elk- or bison-large, built low for wind and bog: splayed, two-part hooves that open in mud and close on stone; dewclaws that actually matter; a deep chest for thin air; a thick, rain-shedding outer coat over a warm felted underlayer that never quite dries and never quite needs to. Their colors are peat and slate and old bark. In fog, a whole herd can stand thirty paces away and look like terrain.

The head is where they become unmistakably of this country. Both sexes carry antler-crowns, but not high, theatrical racks—broad, back-swept lattices like drowned branches, velveted for most of the year, furred with lichen and filament moss. In drizzle those crowns bead and drip constantly, turning each animal into a walking condensation surface. Static gathers there before storms. They feel charge changes through skin and whisker and hoof, and they move off ridges hours before lightning, taking smaller animals with them almost by accident. After strikes, they return first, grazing the nitrate-flush growth while it is at peak sugar.

They travel in slow rotational circuits that look casual until you map them. No basin is overgrazed. No sedge plain is left untouched long enough to choke itself. They crop rush and willow, break crusted peat with their weight, open shallow wallows that become amphibian nurseries, and carry spores in coat and gut from one valley to the next. Calves are born in the marsh-meadow windows when flowers are up and biting insects are briefly low. Old animals die on firmer knolls; scavengers strip them; bone and mineral feed seep-lines downslope for years.

Their predators are there too—reed-thin coursers in the tunnels, patient pack hunters at forest margins—but even predation feels budgeted, folded into weather and footing and season. Nothing wastes effort in that elevation. A failed chase ends quickly. A kill is eaten to the tendon.

And under all of them, through all of them, is the deeper resident: a fungal-peat-root web so continuous that “ground” is only its exposed skin. The hushbacks are not separate from it so much as its moving organs. Fungal threads lace their gut, hooves, and antler velvet; in return the herd fertilizes, opens, transports, and reseeds. Twilight’s blue-green pulse in certain groves often brightens after the herd passes, as if a message has moved through.

So what lives there?

Not one species.  
A **watershed mind** made of cloud, moss, hoof, gut, mycelium, and time—distributed across ridges and basins, thinking in cycles instead of thoughts, maintaining itself without intention and yet with something very close to purpose.
