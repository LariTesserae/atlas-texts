At first glance, it seems like a crowded menagerie of unrelated things.

Then you watch long enough and realize: **what lives there is a metabolism**.

Not metaphorically. Literally.  
The plain is inhabited by species, yes—but more truly by a single, distributed machine of appetite and pressure, broken into bodies.

---

The smallest residents are the first engines: mineral-eaters in films so thin they read as sheen. They bloom where warmth leaks up through cold sheetwater, strip iron, sulfur, and salts, then crash back into sludge when the pulse passes. Their deaths feed the grazers.

Those grazers come as moving weather fronts: the ceramic-backed hexapods, shoulder-low and endless, packed flank to flank in migration belts that can take a day to pass. They do not browse, they **mill**. Their mouths are drum arrays that rasp slurry from stone, sort grain by density, and expel neat ribbons of spent paste that harden into temporary roads for whatever follows. Their scale-plates click constantly, each herd sounding like ten thousand cooling kilns. Frost furs their backs in the dark cycle; at vent-crossings they steam.

Standing among them are the fog sifters—the tall filter-beasts. They root in place for weeks, legs buried in mud to the knee-joints, throats ribbing in and out as they draw mist across wet lattice palates. In profile they resemble cranes assembled from cartilage and valve cloth. In function they are living condensers, extracting planktonic spores, microbial aerosols, and charged dust from the air itself. They are patient to the point of eeriness. When they move, it is all at once, a district-wide pivot toward a new pressure line.

Above and between, the translucent skates patrol the low mist layers. Their fins are not for lift so much as for tuning; they ride electrical differentials like currents. A banked turn writes blue static seams in the haze, and smaller scavengers track those flashes the way fish track bait balls. When storm shears pass, entire flocks invert simultaneously, bellies bright as x-rays, then vanish into gray.

Under all of this, the blind tube-colonies do the pumping. Millions of soft, armored cylinders circulate through saturated sediments, extending and withdrawing in synchronized waves. They are predator, excavator, and plumbing system at once. Their mouths are inward-facing rings of hooks; anything that sinks is tested. Anything weak is pulled down. Their movement aerates mud, redistributes heat, opens and seals channels that determine where next cycle’s blooms can occur. You can walk over a basin at dawn and by dusk the ground plan has been rewritten from below.

---

Predators here are less built for speed than for **timing**.

The most successful ambushers do not hide in shadow; they wait at causal seams. Broad, low hunters with lidless lateral eyes station themselves where reaction lags are known—half-second dead zones where prey sees, then acts too late. They strike into that interval as if reaching through a curtain. Their kills often look impossible until you remember local physics is reliable only in patches.

There are carrion specialists for the temporal waste as well: thin-jawed rake-scavengers that follow anomaly corridors and feed on “time-shed” remains—fresh tissue detached from old deaths, warm organs with no nearby source body, skin-sloughs not yet decayed from creatures gone months. These scavengers carry iron-sense pits along their necks and can detect the chemoelectric hum of soon-to-appear matter before it arrives.

And then there are the shepherds of impossible regularity: medium-sized, many-eyed walkers that migrate by repeating anomalies. They do not navigate by landmarks or stars, but by scheduled paradox—an uphill-flow creek, a pre-echo collapse, a basin that sleets every 47 minutes until disrupted. Their routes are maps of broken rules. Their young imprint on those patterns the way hatchlings elsewhere imprint on coastlines.

---

If there is a “native intelligence,” it is probably not a single brain in a single skull.

It is the coordination itself: heat pulses triggering hatchings, herds opening crust for fog-filters, filters enriching runoff for microbial blooms, burrowers resetting channels, predators pruning timing-blind lineages, chemoelectric colonies carrying signal like nervous tissue across marshes. At dim-night, when orange seams glow and pass current horizon to horizon, the whole plain resembles a cortex firing slowly in cold water.

So what lives there?

Not just beasts. Not just colonies.  
A **living throughput**—an ecology that behaves like infrastructure, an infrastructure that behaves like weather, and weather that occasionally forgets time in one direction or the other.

Everything on that drowned stone plain is occupied by something eating, pumping, sorting, storing, discharging, or waiting for the next mismatch between cause and effect.

The resident, in the singular, is the process.  
The animals are its moving parts.
