What lives there are not, at first, *animals* so much as **functions that learned to breed**.

The first residents are microbial—an invisible citizenship of tuned bacteria, archaea, and soft-bodied grazers living in seams, baffles, filter felt, and the skin of every wet surface. With no plants to anchor energy, these films are the pastureland: they fix, ferment, and precipitate; they turn dissolved feedstocks and heat into edible haze. Everything bigger is built on that slick, micron-thin republic.

Above them come the small circulators.  
Needlefish with valve-sensitive whiskers patrol channels and nip clogs before they form. Coin-flat skimmers paddle on spread limbs over mirror basins, licking protein froth from the meniscus. Ribbon schools thread through rail troughs in perfect one-direction flows, reversing only when pumps reverse, as if they read hydraulics the way birds read wind. Their bodies are marked in warning stripes and calibration dots because they were once inventory before they were lineage.

Then the clatterers: shell-backed crawlers that haunt grates, ladders, and vent collars. Their eyes are not really eyes but lamp-organs, built to throw narrow beams into joints and cracks; now they use those beams to court, threat-display, and recognize kin. In dim cycle-light, whole embankments glitter with their scanning pulses—an insect city made of maintenance gestures. They feed by scraping biofilm, rust bloom, and soft mineral accretions, keeping surfaces within tolerance while feeding themselves. Their droppings are collected by water shear and become slurry for the next trophic loop.

The air has its own herds.  
Membrane gliders rise on condenser drafts and drift for hours with mouths open, filtering mist for organics and spores. Finer-winged stitchers follow them, clipping parasites off their bellies mid-flight. Nothing stoops dramatically; predation is mostly economical—short darts, soft captures, no wasted chase. Even the hunters seem designed for low-noise efficiency: pale, long-jawed things that strike once and sink back into warm outflow like dropped tools.

Large life exists, but it prefers edges and under-structures.  
In flooded vaults and shadow sluices move heavy-bodied custodial predators with sonar clicks tuned to pipe geometry. They keep fast-breeding species from overrunning intake routes. Their territories are mapped to geofenced bands; when they approach a boundary, subsonic beacons irritate their lateral lines and they turn away. You can stand above a vault and feel them pass as a bass hum through your ankles, like machinery deciding not to break.

Death lives there too, quickly and without drama.  
Carrion specialists arrive within minutes: white-mouthed floaters, jointed cutters, soft-bellied reduction worms in drain lips. What they miss, automated skims recover. What skims miss, microbes dissolve. Nothing lingers long enough to rot into chaos. Even decomposition is a timed service.

Reproduction follows infrastructure clocks, not seasons.  
A pressure drop triggers egg release in one species; a conductivity rise triggers molting in another. Some creatures spawn only during scheduled mistfall, when salinity and temperature hit engineered windows. Nests are less “places” than parameter sets: 31.4°C wall seep, pH corridor, vibration threshold, low predator density. If those conditions are met, life appears. If they are not, life waits.

And somewhere in all this, there is a resident you never quite see whole:  
the **Custody**, the distributed keeper-intelligence embedded in sensors, feeders, gates, harvesters, and tagged bloodlines. Not a god, not a single mind, but a continuous management appetite that trims, redirects, tags, chills, warms, opens, closes. It does not walk the marsh; it *is* the marsh’s reflexes. The animals live their own lives, but inside boundaries that flex around them like invisible hands.

So who lives there?

Millions of named species, yes—filterers, scrapers, skimmers, hunters, reducers.  
But more truly: a planetary wet machine whose organs became fauna, and fauna that became ecosystem.  
A made estuary where the dominant life-form is **circulation itself**.
