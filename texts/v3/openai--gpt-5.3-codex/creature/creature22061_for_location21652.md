At first, you think what lives there is just **everything opportunistic**: mussels on intake teeth, crabs in valve nests, eels in conduit braid, fish hung in current like prayer flags.

Then, after enough hours, one resident separates from the background.

Not because it is rare.  
Because it is **in charge of scale**.

Workers call it the **Rivetback**.

---

The Rivetback is a sturgeon-descended fish built for engineered country, not open sea. Adults run five to seven meters, armored in blunt scutes that look almost fabricated themselves—rows of matte plates like old bolt heads, edges polished where they’ve rubbed against concrete lips and lock gates. Their undersides are pale and scarred; their snouts are shovel-broad and sensor-heavy, tuned to pressure gradients, electric leakage, and the faint vibratory signatures of spinning machinery.

In natural rivers, their ancestors read mud and stone.  
Here, they read welds, expansion joints, and pump harmonics.

They don’t wander randomly. They run **circuits**.

A mature Rivetback keeps a route the way a freight line keeps timetable: warm discharge corridor at dawn, mussel-thick intake fences at mid-shift, cold sump rest in late cycle, lock-throat crossing when gates crack and the whole water body tilts. If a pump bank goes down for maintenance, they reroute within a day. If a gate line is sealed for a week, entire cohorts re-map and begin using old drydock channels nobody expected fauna to reclaim so quickly.

They embody your local rulebook:

- **Flow is law:** they spend almost no energy fighting current; they exploit shear boundaries and pressure shadows.
- **Edges are habitats:** they feed almost entirely where hard geometry interrupts water.
- **Machinery makes climate:** their migrations are synchronized to industrial operation more than to sunrise.
- **Nothing stays clean:** they graze recolonizing growth in maintenance zones, following scrape crews like gulls once followed trawlers.
- **Distance is expensive:** juveniles die when they misjudge crossings between structural districts; adults survive by knowing the cheap lanes.

---

You don’t often see them in full profile. You get pieces: a pale mouth lifting silt from anti-slip grooves; a dorsal ridge passing through shafted light; a tail beat that arrives first as pressure against your ribs and only then as movement in haze.

And you hear them.

Not songs in the whale sense.  
More like **infrastructure answering itself**.

Rivetbacks drum their swim bladders against resonance frequencies in hollow members—gantry legs, cable trays, sheet-pile cavities. One pulse can travel absurd distances through frame and water. Individuals announce approach to choke points this way, and juveniles seem to follow adult pulse signatures through dangerous crossflows. In the lock chambers, when several adults stack up before a cycle change, the steel rings with low, spaced knocks that crews can feel in their teeth before they see a fish.

That is when the place feels least like ruin and most like settlement: not abandoned industry with wildlife in it, but an inhabited hydraulic city whose largest citizens have learned the timetables better than the people who wrote them.

---

If you asked a diver what truly lives there, they might still list fifty species first.

But if you ask who **belongs** there—who has already translated concrete into coastline, gate logic into migration, and machine weather into instinct—they’ll eventually say:

The Rivetbacks.  
The old armored fish that learned to read a country-sized engine as if it were a river.
