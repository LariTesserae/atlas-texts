At first you’d think almost nothing *human* lives there—just weather, root, and the patient machinery of growth.

Then you notice signs of a people who have learned to inhabit distance itself.

They are sometimes called **Knotkeepers** by outsiders, though among themselves names are usually local and temporary. You don’t meet them in crowds; you meet one at a stair bend, two at a drip cistern, a family crossing a bridge at noon with reed baskets and pruning knives. They dress in layered barkcloth and oil-dark fiber wraps that shed mist. Their hands are always nicked with old cuts, resin-stained, callused in precise places: thumb web, first knuckle, heel of palm—hands made for binding, scraping, grafting, and testing load by touch.

They move quietly not out of fear, but etiquette. In a landscape where echoes can misbehave, loudness is a form of carelessness. Their speech is close and angled, often palm-to-shoulder distance, with many pauses for listening—to drips, to tension in a bridge vine, to the tiny cracking sound that means a wet beam is taking new weight. Children are taught routes by touch before map: “this handrail has three fungus ribs, then a shell inset, then the cold seam.” In fog, they walk with one hand on the structure as naturally as breathing.

Their settlements are small and discontinuous: smoke-dark alcoves in trunk vaults, woven shutter nests under arch roots, reed platforms above suspended marshes. They do not “own” districts so much as keep maintenance relationships with them. A person may be responsible for a particular tension grove for decades, while sleeping two basins away. Most labor is preventative and devotional at once: trimming stitch-vines, patching clay seams, retying direction knots, thinning moss from load joins, reading root-archives after heavy mist to see what has shifted in possibility.

They are preindustrial in tools and post-monumental in mindset. Flint, bone awls, fired clay wedges, rope, fire, patience. A child with a shard blade can do work that keeps a causeway standing another century. They don’t talk about “infrastructure” and “nature” as separate things; to them, cutting a vine can be surgery, architecture, and prayer in one motion.

And they are not alone.

The high structure teems with co-inhabitants that know its rules better than visitors: rope-mimic snakes along tension lines, marsh antler-beasts in suspended bogs, gliders that feed on canopy skin, broad silent hunters under arches who avoid Quiet Frames as if those spaces have no scent of time. Insect veils comb the seep walls clean. Burrowers aerate ancient earthen buttresses. Even fungi along handrails are half lantern, half warning system.

But the oldest resident is the place itself.

Not metaphorically—practically. The grown range behaves like a distributed organism with memory in ring relief and choice in moisture. It does not think like an animal, yet it edits pathways, nudges alignments, accepts some repairs and “refuses” others by cracking or rerouting flow. The Knotkeepers live as negotiators with that vast vegetal will. Their taboos are field-tested diplomacy: don’t name twice, don’t cut at dusk, don’t drink reflections.

So what lives there?

A sparse people of gardeners-engineers who practice quiet as navigation.  
A thousand layered animals occupying vertical weather.  
And beneath both, an ancient living architecture that is less a city than a slow, rain-fed intelligence—one that allows habitation, provided you move gently and keep your agreements with direction.
