What lives there is a **maintenance people**—not in the sense that they only repair things, but in the sense that they keep continuities alive.

At first glance they look like ordinary shift workers: waterproof jackets, layered gloves, boots with ladder-grip soles, lamps clipped to collars even in daytime. But watch them for an hour and you see a different species of attention. They move like water planners and greenhouse tenders and rail dispatchers all at once. Their eyes check three levels without seeming to: the panel in front of them, the gutter line underfoot, the cloud ceiling above the trusses. They don’t “go to work”; they enter circulation.

They are usually slim-muscled from climbing, with the rounded shoulders of people who spend years under low beams and in windbreak corridors. Their skin carries the valley’s palette—cool light, iron dust, leaf stain, old bruises, citrus oil, machine grease that never fully washes out of the knuckles. Many wear color tags or stitched bands that can be read through mist at distance: drainage crew, crop pressure, freight reroute, heat recovery, line safety. In a place where sound is soft and visibility breaks by layers, being legible is a form of care.

Their habits are built from the valley’s rules:

- They never set tools directly on a flat surface without checking where runoff will take them.
- They pause at thresholds—not from superstition, but because every threshold is also a switch point in flow.
- They keep duplicate everything: keys, routes, names for places, ways to do a task if power drops.
- They prune as they walk. A hand that tightens a valve will also guide a vine off a sensor eye.

Children there learn gradients before maps. They can tell you which stairs stay dry in second-shift fog, which handrail sweats first before a pressure swing, which cats belong to which kitchen dock. Games involve rerouting marbles through drain channels, timing crossings between cart lanes, identifying pump alarms by strobe rhythm alone. By adolescence they have a weather literacy that looks almost feral elsewhere: they can smell when a block will reverse drainage.

They are not romantic about nature and not obedient to machinery; they are loyal to the coupled system. They talk about bean trellises and belt bearings in the same tone, because failure in either can cascade. Their ethics are procedural: leave margins, leave access, leave options for the next shift. Heroics are frowned on; recoverability is admired. The local idea of kindness is “I set you up a cleaner handoff.”

And other lives persist with them: frogs in warm culverts, swallows in gantries, fungal films in shaded joints, kitchen cats that patrol grain stacks, moss that marks chronic leaks better than any meter. But these are not background. The people read them as indicators, co-workers of a sort. A silent pump, a wilting wall, absent swallows in one bay—each is information.

If you had to name the true resident, singular, it might be this: **a culture of continuous adjustment**. Human in body, infrastructural in temperament. They live in the valley the way roots live in a retaining wall—threaded through stress, holding shape by yielding constantly, making an artificial mountain breathe.
