At first glance, it looks like nothing lives there at all.

That is true in the ordinary sense—nothing that crosses the water column, nothing that flashes, hunts, schools, or flees. But the basin does have a resident. It’s just built for stillness so complete that you mistake it for geology.

What lives there is a **benthic colony-animal**, spread thin beneath the silt and along the step-shelves: a body more like plumbing than flesh. Think of a pale, rubbery lattice threaded through the top few centimeters of sediment, with denser pads anchored into seams of clay and basalt. Along the walls, where calcium bands run straight, it sets narrow feeding lips—slits the width of fingernails, flush with stone, opening and closing so slowly you never catch the motion unless you stare for a long time.

It eats what the basin already processes: dissolved organics, bacterial films, ultrafine particulate. The regular ripples on the floor are partly hydraulic, partly biological—its ciliated surfaces and peristaltic pulses help sort grain sizes, keeping fine material in lanes and letting heavier grit fan out. It does not “build” the place, but over centuries it has tuned itself so tightly to the basin’s flow that its metabolism and the chamber’s sediment choreography are now one coupled system.

Its breathing is distributed. Tiny gas strings that rise from mineral seeps are, in some spots, true seepage; in others, they are vent points where the colony offloads metabolic byproducts. That’s why the bubble columns are so punctual. They are not signals, just pressure management.

If you touched it, you would not feel an animal in the usual way. You’d feel cool, firm gel under grit, then a delayed withdrawal: local contraction traveling out in concentric, very slow waves. Its “reflex” times are measured in seconds to minutes. Its growth is measured in years. A century is a normal lifespan for one contiguous sheet; older basins may hold layered generations fused together, so that death is patchy and replacement happens in place, without ever producing a dramatic corpse.

The sparse eelgrass and algae persist because it allows them, indirectly. It does not overfilter; it leaves enough nutrient and still water at the margins for those holdfast patches to survive. In turn, their roots and mats stabilize edges the colony can’t grip well. Neither spreads far. They are co-tenants under the same rule: constant flow, low ambition, long continuity.

So the thing that lives there is not a creature passing through.  
It is a **quiet, distributed keeper**—an organism that survives by matching the basin’s discipline so exactly that it disappears into function.  
You don’t notice it because nothing in it performs being alive for you.
