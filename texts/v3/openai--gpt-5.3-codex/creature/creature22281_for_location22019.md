At first you think almost no one lives there.

You can cross a whole wind court and see only machinery, leaf-strata, and towers breathing vapor. Then someone steps out of a seam in the root wall as if the city exhaled them: narrow-shouldered, layered in waxed fiber and metal clips, face wrapped against cold and spore-dust, moving with the unhurried precision of a person who has spent their life around things that can shear, burst, or fold wrong if startled.

The inhabitants are mostly human in outline, but shaped by altitude, industry, and the city’s botanical mass.

They tend to be lean and deep-chested from thin air. Their skin is usually wind-marked and tannin-stained at the hands. Many wear conductive threads braided into sleeves and collars—part grounding strap, part civic identity, part emergency kit. Their clothing looks halfway between municipal uniform and climbing harness: resin-treated cloth, flexible bark laminate, pockets for gauges, spore canisters, branch tokens, and phase chalk. Boots are wide-soled for grip on damp membrane streets that flex faintly underfoot.

Almost everyone carries something alive.

A clipped sprig in a wrist tube. A grafted vine coil in a satchel. A twig threaded through belt rings like a ritual key. In other cities it might read as superstition; here it is law and practical physics. Some intersections will not resolve cleanly unless a living biological continuity is present. Children learn this before arithmetic: *don’t cross a repeating block empty-handed*.

---

They are less “residents” than *tenders of a giant metabolism*.

Jobs are named like ecological verbs:  
- **Sapwrights** tap trunk-citadels, monitor sugar pressure, and decide which flows become foodstock, fuel, or polymer.  
- **Phase surveyors** walk fault-grids with bell compasses and echo slates, repainting orientation glyphs where streets have drifted half a meter sideways in ontology.  
- **Condensers** climb mesh spires at dawn to harvest water-sweat and scrape algae bloom before it clogs copper veins.  
- **Pollination keepers** wind and release the municipal kite-swarms, then track return patterns to map wind health across districts.  
- **Root masons** braid living retaining walls, a craft equal parts arboriculture and civil engineering.  
- **Shift choristers** operate public bell arrays: not ornamental music, but temporal stitching for neighborhoods whose clocks can desynchronize during heavy fog.

No one here mistakes maintenance for low status. Maintenance is sovereignty. If you keep pressure balanced, bridges rooted, and reality mostly one-to-one, you are important.

---

Socially, they are sparse but not isolated.

Because districts are far apart, people gather in long, deliberate pulses: shift changes, bell hours, water-market windows, dusk relays. Markets are less about abundance and more about exact exchange—lubricant for fungal bearings, calibrated seedstock, insulated cable bark, preserved grazer milk, vent-harvest swift eggs, clock springs, resin gloves, medicinal moss charcoal.

Conversation has a local grammar of uncertainty. People give directions with conditions, not absolutes:

“Take the lower causeway unless the mist is climbing.”  
“If you hear your own footfall twice, wait for a bell.”  
“Cross with someone carrying green.”

They are calm in the face of weirdness not because they’re fearless, but because panic is mechanically useless there. They treat anomalies the way mariners treat weather: logged, signaled, routed around.

---

Culturally, they are practical mystics.

Not mystical in doctrine—mystical in daily posture toward a world that is measurable but not fully obedient. Their shrines, where they exist, look like control stations: polished brass, sap-lamps, neatly hung branch bundles, names of the lost etched beside pressure readouts. Their holidays are tied to systems stability: first full-basin sync after storm season, longest condenser run, successful re-rooting of a failed terrace. They celebrate not conquest but continuity.

Children play games involving echo timing and route memory. Teenagers apprentice early, learning which hum means turbine efficiency and which hum means imminent blade shear. Elders are often “listeners,” able to diagnose district health from the layered soundscape alone: leaf-noise pitch, chain cadence, valve cry, cable song.

---

They share the basin with a handful of nonhuman lives and treat them as co-workers more than wildlife.

Vent swifts are protected because they eat corrosion moths. Six-legged terrace grazers are rotationally herded to keep membrane plains from over-thickening and suffocating drainage. Translucent moth blooms are monitored as bioindicators of condenser purity. Even pests are cataloged by function before they’re culled.

There are also semi-autonomous things—clockwork pollination kites, inspection crawlers grown over with moss, old tram dolls that still run on obsolete rails and ring warning chimes in districts no longer on maps. Nobody calls them people, but nobody calls them mere tools either. They’re part of the civic organism’s memory.

---

If you stand at dusk on a parapet and watch who moves through that glowing grid, you realize what lives there is not a population scattered across a city.

It is a distributed species of caretakers, each one a mobile interface between plant, machine, weather, and unstable geometry. They are patient, procedural, lightly superstitious, technically brilliant, and impossible to hurry. They do not dominate the basin; they negotiate with it, continuously.

And the basin, for all its faulted strangeness, appears to accept them.
