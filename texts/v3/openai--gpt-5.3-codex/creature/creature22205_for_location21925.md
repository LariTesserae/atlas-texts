At first, what lives there is hard to call life at all.

From far above, the resident looks like stain: a faint green-gray script written along joints, drain slots, culvert lips, the shadowed side of parapets. It never occupies the open planes. It avoids the proud, sun-struck centers of slabs. It keeps to failure lines—expansion seams, hairline cracks, chipped corners, settling edges—where dust is a little deeper, where a bead of dawn water might persist twelve minutes longer than elsewhere.

Up close, it resolves into a consortium rather than a creature: thin algal films, mineral-tolerant moss, rootlets from low shrubs, fungal threads pale as ash, and bacterial crusts that can wake from powder-dry dormancy almost instantly. Together they are the closest thing this continent has to a native people. Not a forest, not a field; a distributed citizenry of defects.

If you had to name a single “who,” it would be the **Seam Colony**.

It lives in the infrastructure’s grammar.  
Long straight seams become migration corridors.  
Settlement ponds become seasonal nurseries.  
Retaining walls become cliffs.  
Handrails become dew harvesters.

By day, most of it appears dead: brittle, matte, shrunken into the color of dust and oxidized metal. Then a narrow rain event hits—one district only—and the colony changes phase. Algal skin darkens within minutes. Dormant spores swell. Hairlike roots drink from microchannels invisible at human scale. In shaded chambers, mats become slick and black-green; in open seams, tufts brighten briefly, then fade as heat climbs. The bloom is fast, almost violent in metabolic terms, and brief enough that you can miss it by arriving an hour late.

Its body is mostly absence between patches. Its continuity is temporal, not spatial: this crack lives this week, that culvert mouth next month, that cistern rim after a year of lucky storms. It does not spread like a wave. It appears as a solved equation of shade + roughness + trapped fines + one event of water.

It reproduces by dust.

When surfaces dry and contract, crusts fracture into microscopic plates. Wind lifts them, not high, just enough to skip them across kilometers of panel and rib. Most are lost on bare planes. A few drop into exactly the right flaw—a chipped seam sealant line, a bit of sediment fan at a drain outlet, the lee of a low wall where powder accumulates finger-deep. There they wait, inert and patient, for the next condensation dawn or localized storm.

It has no eyes, no nerves, no center, but it does shape the place. It darkens strips that absorb more heat. It traps silt that thickens rooting layers by millimeters. Its roots pry at plaster skins. Its films slow evaporation in tiny pockets, stretching wet time from minutes to hours. In that way, it engineers the beginning of its own habitat, one defect at a time, while the continent learns weather.

If there is anything like intention here, it is this:  
hold where water hesitates.  
go dormant before noon.  
return whenever the surface gives way.

So what lives there is not a beast crossing distance, not a flock, not a burrowed kingdom. It is a scattered, persistent metabolism threaded through made land—a first ecology made of seams, events, and patience. The plain is vast, yes, but it is no longer empty. It has acquired a skin.
