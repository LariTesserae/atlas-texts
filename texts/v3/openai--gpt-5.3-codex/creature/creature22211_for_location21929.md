What lives there, first of all, is **edge-life**.

Not creatures of one habitat, but specialists in transitions: thaw to freeze, wet pocket to dry saddle, silence to storm-noise, stone to root. Almost everything alive on that plateau survives by timing those edges better than whatever hunts it, shades it, or outlasts it.

If you walked it long enough, you’d start to recognize a few great kinds of resident.

---

### 1) The Builders (plants that make terrain)

The “forests” are not forests in the lowland sense. They’re more like **slow landslides of tissue**.

- **Knife-fronds** grow in dense, translucent fans, then harden at the base into load-bearing ribs. New growth sprouts from old surfaces, so one generation becomes scaffolding for the next. After a few decades, a frond stand is a wall; after a century, a buttressed maze.
- **Rope-root colonies** anchor into cracks, then braid themselves into cisterns that trap fog-drip and meltwater. In deep belts, you walk over meters of living cable with almost no soil beneath.
- **Black seam-moss** occupies the first wet millimeters of any fracture in fresh rock. It is pioneer, sealant, and sponge. In exposed saddles it looks dead for months, then swells to velvet after one frost-thaw pulse.
- **Singing reeds** colonize wind corridors. Hollow internodes turn whole slopes into instruments. Their collapse in storms is deadly, but their fallen tubes become nurseries, windbreaks, and insect hatch grounds.
- The faint **green “sap-lamp” glow** comes from stress chemistry in conductive tissues under frost load. It is not signaling in the animal sense; it is leakage from survival. But many animals, and humans, have learned to read it.

These are the true escarpment-makers now. Rock uplifts; plant-work catches it and turns it into habitat before erosion can blunt it.

---

### 2) The Belt-Herds (mobile grazers of patchy abundance)

The big herbivores travel as **long, dark processions**, not because they love company but because food is linear. Productive growth lies in belts; belts force movement.

Typical grazer traits here:
- split, gripping hooves for scree and root mats
- forward-cutting incisors for rime-softened frond tissue
- heavy neck musculature for digging starch nodules from root beds
- lungs and blood chemistry tuned for thin, cold air

They feed hardest at dawn frost and thaw margins, when plant tissues are softest and least defended. Herds often “mine” one vegetated trough, then vanish for days across barren ridges to the next. Their migrations are the percussion you hear at night: thousands of impacts spread across kilometers, stitched into one weather-like sound.

They are nutrient engines. Where herds pause, valleys green faster the next season.

---

### 3) The Vent-Keepers (burrowers that warm the ground)

Some of the most important animals are small-to-medium burrowers—thick-furred, high-metabolism diggers that punch into insulated substrate under root masses.

Their tunnels do three things:
1. leak heat and vapor upward,
2. aerate compacted root-felt,
3. route meltwater sideways.

Around active vent fields, you get **warm halos**: tiny jungles of moss, larvae, fungus-like films, and seedling clusters in otherwise frozen ground. Everything hunts these halos. Everything breeds there if timing allows.

Where you see frost-smoke rising from many vents, you are looking at a temporary city of prey and, soon after, predators.

---

### 4) The Long-Leg Predators (sprinters between cover islands)

Predators here are not ambush cats of dense forests nor open-plains pursuit wolves. They are **gap-runners**.

Lean, narrow-bodied, deep-chested, long-shanked—built to wait in one cover mass, then explode across exposed ground before wind or echoes betray them. They hunt by triangulating:
- hoof percussion through substrate
- alarm whistles bounced off cliff faces
- scent trapped in cold drainage air

Many den near vent zones in lean months, then range wide during migrations. Their kills are quick because exposure is costly: every second in the open is visibility to scavengers and rival hunters.

---

### 5) The Air Drifters (scavengers and cliff-fliers)

Above everything are big-winged scavengers riding escarpment updrafts. You can watch one circle for an hour without a full flap.

They nest in knife cracks no ground predator can reach. Their eyesight is excellent, but in this country hearing matters too; they key on distant commotion—pod-bursts, herd panic waves, reedfall—then descend long before smell would have reached them.

They are late-arrival cleaners, bone-openers, and disease brakes. Their droppings seed cliff gardens in inaccessible places, creating odd vertical ribbons of fertility.

---

### 6) The Flash Swarms (invertebrates of thaw windows)

Most of the year, the invertebrates seem absent. Then thaw pulses hit and the air goes granular with life.

Eggs and cysts that waited in bark seams, peat pockets, and frozen tarn margins all trigger at once. You get:
- biting midge clouds,
- detritus beetles on carcasses within minutes,
- short-lived pollinator bursts around sap-lamp-active fronds,
- predatory lace-wing analogs that mate and die in days.

These blooms are brief but decisive. They move nutrients faster than any large animal can.

---

### 7) The Route People (humans grafted into motion)

Human groups here are not city builders; they are **corridor keepers**.

They live at crossing points: ledges where two migration belts intersect, sheltered overhangs above vent fields, windbreak circles near reliable condensation. Their dwellings are partly made, partly grown, partly borrowed:
- root platforms tied into living trunks
- hide roofs over bone-pinned reed frames
- stone lips that redirect katabatic wind
- smoke-blackened rock reused for generations

They travel light, repair constantly, abandon without sentiment. Not because they are careless—because permanence is expensive in a land still rearranging itself.

Their lore is practical acoustics and microclimate memory:
- when to follow sound and when to mistrust it,
- which green glows are near and which are fog-bent lies,
- where runoff undercuts in a single storm,
- which reed valleys will shear in crosswind.

They are, in a way, another migratory species keyed to belts of opportunity.

---

So what lives there?

A whole society of organisms that do not ask for stability.  
Things that cling, cache, sprint, drift, burst, and move on.  
Things that turn cracks into reservoirs, noise into information, cold into timing.

From far off, the ridgelines still look like mountains at night.  
Up close, they are crowded with lives that have learned to occupy not the land itself, but the moments when it briefly becomes habitable.
