At first glance, you’d say **patience** lives there.

Not metaphorically—practically. Anything that survives in that city-range has to live on long clocks: heat cycles, shadow cycles, mineral cycles. The place does not reward hunger that wants an answer by nightfall. So the most successful inhabitants are those that can wait while stone thinks.

The easiest lives to notice are the thin, real ones: the cliff-runners with translucent ear-fans, the high gliders that comb thermal seams for carrion, the black mineral films that spread in quiet joints and write hexagons into masonry. But these are surface tenants, small script on a much older page. They follow routes that predate instinct: ridge stairs, lintel lines, plaza edges, even where no food suggests it. They move like they are obeying old zoning laws.

What truly lives there is a people—if “people” can include beings whose main craft is **maintaining proportion**.

Travelers call them different names: shard-keepers, measure-folk, the Delayed. You almost never see more than one at a time, and even then only at awkward distances, as if the city itself is deciding how much of them you’re allowed to perceive. Tall under layered dust-cloth, faces veiled not for secrecy but for humidity control, fingers whitened by mineral chalk. They carry no metal, only bone-colored tools, glass wedges, and bundles of calibrated stone slivers. They camp in vault throats and lee-side courts, never the same site twice in one moon. Their shelters are temporary and exact: wind-baffled, thermally tuned, dismantled at dawn without trace except a corrected balance in nearby cairns.

They are not rebuilding the city. They are not abandoning it either.  
They are **trimming it**.

When a stairfield begins to “run long,” they close three steps and open one. When a shadow starts hardening into impossible angles too often, they rotate reflective plates of volcanic glass set deep into parapets. When downhill forgets itself and stones start rolling the wrong way, they wait through three dusk snaps, then shift a single counterweight block in an old retaining arch. Their engineering is less architecture than diplomacy with local reality.

Their economy is austere: salt-metal bloom licked from seams, condensed frost harvested before sunrise from deep vents, protein from cliff-runner traps set along echo lines, occasional traded relics from lowland expeditions that rarely return with profit. They value maps, but not fixed maps—**map habits**. A good cartographer among them is not someone who draws accurately once, but someone who can predict tomorrow’s inaccuracy.

They have almost no monuments of their own. Their memory is procedural. Genealogy is kept as route instructions; law is preserved as load order; prayer is recited as maintenance sequence. Children learn counting by placing stacked shards at threshold points where distance misbehaves. Adults can “read” wind-borne dust flow the way sailors read tide. Elders are recognized by stillness: the longer you can stand in a mutating square and know where north will be in an hour, the more authority you carry.

And beneath them—perhaps beneath everyone—something slower lives in the buried precincts. That page-turning sound is not machinery. It is strata settling in patterned intervals, chambers expanding and contracting in recursive cycles, possibly guided, possibly merely old. The shard-keepers treat it as a civic heartbeat. They do not investigate it directly. They align to it.

So what lives there?

- Sparse animals, specialized and watchful.  
- Mineral films that eat gradient and etch stone.  
- A near-invisible culture of custodians who practice maintenance as cosmology.  
- And maybe, under all of it, a lithic intelligence too broad to meet face-to-face, audible only as pages turning in the dark.

In that place, life is not abundance.  
It is **continuity under strain**.
