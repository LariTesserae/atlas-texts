At first, it seems like nothing *lives* there in the usual sense—only survives: moss, fern, algae, a few cliff birds skimming the wind.  
Then you notice that the pumps are never quite on the schedule the manuals describe.

Not broken. Adjusted.

The thing that lives on that ledge is called (by the few crews who still service the lower stations) a **Ledgekeeper**. Not because anyone has seen one clearly and named it with confidence, but because “keeper” is the only word that fits what happens in its presence: overflow gates stop sticking, steam vents clear, intake grates are never fully choked, and yet no one is ever there long enough to claim the work.

A Ledgekeeper is easiest to understand as a braided life rather than a single body. Part of it is plant-mass: thick cords like horsetail rhizomes, mats of heat-loving moss, fern-roots that thread cracks and bolt-holes and the hollow backs of conduit runs. Part of it is animal: pale, slow, hand-sized nodes that can contract and creep, each slick-skinned and lidless, more like a root-tip taught to hunt than any familiar vertebrate. Part of it is mineral memory: crystal growths laid in repeating lattices along steel seams, especially where stray current and warm seepage meet. None of these parts look intelligent alone. Together, over years, they behave like attention.

It feeds on gradients.  
Heat differences. Mineral-rich runoff. Trace oils. Rust bloom. The microscopic films that grow where condensation forms and reforms a hundred times a day. It does not eat a machine; it colonizes its wet edges, then learns its pulse. Where a valve shudders every six hours, the Keeper thickens. Where a belt housing runs hot, it thins and plates the surface with damp insulating felt. Where magnetized dikes tug a compass off true, it lays crystal fur in directional combs, as if combing the field itself.

You don’t see it walking. You see evidence that it has already passed.

A handrail polished clean through lichen in a shape exactly palm-width, though no palms are ever found. A pressure wheel that turns with almost no resistance despite years of weather. Frogspawn only in basins that won’t boil when vents exhale. Swallow nests tucked behind the one baffle that never rattles loose in storm season. It keeps not out of kindness, probably, but because continuity is food: running systems make stable wetness, and stable wetness is life.

When it reproduces, it does so during slab-fall years. Fresh rock opens, hot seams shift, old growth tears. Then the Keeper fractures with the ledge—sections stranded, sections thriving. The stranded parts dry, mineralize, and become brittle lace in abandoned pipe. The thriving parts descend into new cracks, rethread catwalk legs, and begin the long work of binding water to metal to root again.

If you ever meet the whole of it, you likely won’t realize it. You’ll think the ledge itself has habits.  
That is close enough to true.

Because what lives there is not a creature *in* the workshop bolted to the sky.  
It is the workshop, after years of being wet and warm and loud, learning how to keep itself alive.
