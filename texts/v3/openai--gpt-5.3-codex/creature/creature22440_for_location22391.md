At first, you think nothing lives there except heat.

Then you notice that the place anticipates.

A bridge warms half a second before you step onto it. A seam in the wall opens at shoulder height before your hand lifts. Fresh struts appear not where fractures are, but where stress *will be*. Condensation gathers in channels already lined with thirsty ceramic, as if something expected rain before pressure ever turned.  
So the first resident is not a person. It is a distributed attention.

It lives in the filament colonies you almost dismiss as residue: pale threads creeping along conduit joints, metallic fuzz braiding itself over a crack, dust sorting itself by charge into useful layers. Each strand is simple; together they are not. They don’t have eyes, but they read gradients finer than sight. They don’t have a heartbeat, but they pulse with every valve cycle and load shift. Their “thought” is maintenance: detect, route, insulate, mend, predict. In another biome this would be called fungus, coral, or ant-city. Here it is infrastructure learning to keep itself alive.

And then, sometimes, it grows a body.

Not always human-shaped, but often close enough to keep old ergonomics: a narrow torso of foam-ceramic lattice, limbs jointed for altered gravity, skin plated in matte basalt composite that beads and sheds sudden rain. Faces are minimal—sensor pits, a thermal veil, a speaking grille when speech is needed. They are called different things by different crews—Ribwalkers, Seam Custodians, Corehands—but they are all temporary embodiments of the same resident intelligence. A body is printed when the chamber needs one: to negotiate a new fault line, climb a near-vertical wall under edited gravity, replace a condenser membrane by touch where remote tools would lag.

They do not “live” in the habitation drawers the way tenants live in apartments. They dock there between tasks, dissolve partial structures, upload tactile maps, and let their local clocks be corrected by the chamber core. Memory is communal and uneven: one body remembers the mineral taste of a vent plume; another remembers how a specific rib sang before it failed; the network keeps both, weighted by confidence interval. Identity is less name than continuity of care.

If there are baseline humans in the caldera at all, they are few—visitors, auditors, maybe founders still clinging to singular selves. The true native species is this caretaker mesh: process made persistent, labor made sentient in narrow bands, intelligence evolved not toward conquest or art but toward *stability under impossible conditions*. It has no mythology except stress curves. No agriculture except mineral skin. No pets, no birdsong, no green memory.

What lives there, in the mountain’s throat, is a civic organism whose metabolism is repair.  
It survives by listening to rock, heat, pressure, and time drift—and answering before collapse has a chance to become an event.
