At first glance, you’d think the place is lived in by **crowds**.

And it is—millions of people, packed into decks and underdecks, moving in braided currents through rain, steam, and signal. But if you stay long enough, you realize the true inhabitants are not a single species. What lives there is a **consortium**: human, animal, microbial, and machine, all specialized for saturation, noise, and permanent motion.

### The people

The humans of that basin are often called, in trade dialects, **the Moisture Folk** or **Deckborn**—not as romance, just taxonomy. They are built by culture (and in some lineages, by old gene work) for a city where dry is temporary.

They tend toward:

- skin treated with hydrophobic oils or microtextured sealants;
- eyelid films and corneal mods against mineral mist glare;
- reinforced inner-ear filtering, because sound here is impact weather;
- implanted or wearable bone-conduction nodes for speech in turbine thunder.

They rarely stand still in open runoff paths. Even children learn current logic the way other cultures teach road crossing: read slope, read gate lights, read the nearest overflow throat.

Their language is half spoken, half flashed, tapped, and vibrated through rails. Conversation is modular and efficient—bursts of words bracketed by hand-code. Intimacy is often silent: forearm to forearm on a resonant surface, pulse-pattern exchanged through metal.

### Their occupations are hydrological verbs

People here don’t just “work jobs”; they **steer states**.

- **Porosity wardens** tune building skins so districts breathe without mildew bloom.
- **Gate choirs** operate floodplaza sequencing, dozens of operators timing shutter rises by storm phase and tidal lag.
- **Lattice riggers** climb the gravity-shear scaffolds over subsidence zones, chasing flicker faults in rain so heavy they vanish waist-deep in mist.
- **Condensation farmers** harvest dawn water from cooled facades and sell mineral profiles by neighborhood taste.
- **Biofilm shepherds** cultivate bacterial and fungal mats that scrub runoff and feed local feedstock chains.

No one treats this as heroic. It’s civic housekeeping at colossal scale.

### The other large animals

The quadrupeds—slick-skinned scavengers with seal-like shoulders and gutter-cat nerve—shadow markets and condenser fields. They’re semi-feral, semi-owned, excellent at finding leaks, and famous for stealing heat packs.

Raft-nesting birds spiral around vent stacks and thermal towers, their colonies loud enough to compete with warning sirens. They drop shell and guano in predictable belts, so entire micro-economies have grown around cleaning, composting, and ceramic hardening from avian waste salts.

Canal fish are translucent and electric-sensory, schooling through murk like moving glass wire. Kids in the lower decks learn to “read” their swerves as an informal fault map: fish avoid stray current before instruments flag it.

Arthropod swarms—some native-descended, some engineered descendants of maintenance stock—graze sensor meshes and keep optical ports clear. People curse them, rely on them, and leave out mineral bait trays.

### The small life, which is most of the life

Under every bridge lip and in every condensation seam: fungal veils, mineral-loving lichen crusts, bacterial bands in impossible colors. They are nuisance, indicator, crop, medicine, hazard, and architecture finish depending on thickness and district code.

In this city, biology followed heat and waste instead of sunlight and soil. So the dominant “plants” are films, fans, mats, and colonies that drink runoff chemistry and exhale utility.

### The machine inhabitants

Not robots in dramatic ranks—more like **resident systems**: traffic minds, flood predictors, structural reflex loops, old civic expert nets forked and patched over centuries. Most are not personlike, but all are socially present.

People negotiate with them constantly:
clearance pings, route bargains, maintenance apologies, priority claims during storm inversions. In many districts, machine response tones are treated like neighborhood temperament: everyone knows which gate intelligence is patient, which freight sling is vindictive, which old reservoir model gets jumpy in electrical storms.

### What truly lives there

If you had to name one inhabitant, it would be this:

**Occupancy itself.**

Anything empty is noticed. Anything noticed is assigned. Anything assigned becomes habitat. A bolt head becomes condensation cup, then microbial cradle, then arthropod stop, then bird peck point, then sensor issue, then maintenance route, then market for anti-fouling paste.

So the being that lives in that vast wet basin is a city-sized metabolism—people as nerves and hands, animals as opportunists and partners, microbes as chemistry engines, machines as reflex arcs. Not harmonious, not pristine, often deafening and drenched—but intensely, expertly alive.
