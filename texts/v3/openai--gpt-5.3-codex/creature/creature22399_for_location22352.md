What lives there is **concentration**.

Not a single monarch-beast, not a village that claims the bowl, but a tight braid of lives that have learned the same lesson: in that height, anything that can’t use water quickly gets erased.

The most permanent residents are the low ones—the ones that hug stone and drink from seepage. Mosses thick as felt, sedges with knife-fine leaves, dwarf willow that grows outward more than upward, roots laced through peat like netting. They behave less like plants and more like weather instruments: swelling, shrinking, rafting loose, recolonizing. They are the basin’s memory. Every storm rearranges channels; every calm week they stitch the floor back together.

Then come the quick-bodied tenants of wet edges: black-backed frogs in the warmer tarn margins; cold-bright insects that hatch in sudden synchronized bursts; small fish trapped in braided channels and released again when floods reconnect pools. Bird life treats the basin like a crowded inn at a mountain pass. Swallows and choughs take the air lanes under the rim, migrants drop in, feed hard, leave lighter. Nothing wastes daylight there. Feeding is urgent, always one weather turn away from interruption.

On the gravel and stone benches live the listeners and watchers: pika-like talus dwellers, lean mustelids, fox-sized hunters that move between islands of life without announcing themselves. Above them, on cliff shelves, small ungulates descend at dawn and dusk to graze the green ribbons, then vanish upslope before full light or weather shift. Their hooves are broad and split, good for slick rock and deceptive peat; their routes are inherited, precise, and never fully safe.

And threading through all of it are the pass-through predators—the ones you notice mostly by subtraction. Calls stop. Wings lift. Frogs go silent. A shadow crosses a wet flat. In a basin this bounded, hunting is geometry: choke points, sight lines, wind turns. Even brief visits by raptors, cats, or wolves leave deep marks because prey has nowhere truly far to scatter.

Humans live there the same way the herds do: **seasonally, tactically, respectfully afraid**. The stone shelters under overhangs aren’t homes so much as held breaths. People arrive when channels run right and breeding runs peak; they set reed traps, cut paths, mend walkways, dry meat, watch sky and rim, and leave before storms lock the cleft. Their shrine at the outlet is less worship than field knowledge carved into ritual: this place feeds you, then floods you, often in the same hour.

So if you ask what lives in that high marsh bowl, the truest answer is:  
**a community of things adapted to brief abundance and constant change**.  
Life there is dense, local, and alert—never settled, never empty, always listening for water.
