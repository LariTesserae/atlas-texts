At first, almost nothing seems to live there.

Then you notice the **surface itself is inhabited**.

A black sheen on basalt isn’t stain; it’s a mat of mineral-feeding microcolonies, a living varnish that plates rock faces in conductive films. They grow in microns, not leaves. They don’t drink—they **charge**. By day they soak thermal gradients; by storm they pull static from the air and store it in glassy nodules under their own crust. When a dust front hits, whole slopes faintly crackle as these films discharge into one another. That is the grassland, here: not green, but electrical.

From that base comes the first visible animal scale: low, segmented lithovores that move like articulated kiln parts. They are the ones whose passage sounds like a dropped toolbox rolling downhill forever. Their mouths are grinding crowns of ceramic teeth and magnetized tongues; they rasp oxide skins off stone and swallow mineral powder with conductive gel, sorting useful metals in a hot internal lattice. They travel in columns because columns close circuits—an individual is an eater, but a line is an energy system.

Behind them come the veils.

Clouds of hand-sized black followers, each one a six-fin scavenger with foil-thin vanes and hook feet. They skim the flanks of the big grazers, stripping parasites, licking sparks from abrasion points, stealing charge from dorsal ridges and dumping it into brood-sacks. Seen at distance they’re smoke. Up close they sound like paper being torn very fast.

Then the large forms resolve.

There are herd-animals that look forged rather than born: ribbed heat exchangers under armored backs, piston-jointed forelimbs, shock-damped necks for impact feeding. Their hides are layered composites—sinter, carbon mesh, conductive mucus, then outer plate—molted in slabs that ring when they fall. A migration front of these things is less like wildlife and more like freight moving on old rails. They do not panic easily; they reroute. If reality shear displaces the valley floor three meters sideways for an hour, the column flexes around it and keeps timetable.

And there are hunters tuned to the local impossibilities.

Some wait on gravity-tilt seams: narrow strips where “down” leans just enough to betray footing. They are patient tripod predators with harmonic throats; they emit pulses that scramble vestibular sense in nearby fauna, then let the slant do the killing. Others live in late-light pockets where dusk persists out of phase with neighboring noon. Their eyes are stacked lenses of polarized crystal, good at tracking things that are half a second misregistered from where they appear.

The air has life too, though little that truly flies in the wet-world sense. Most aerial species are **electrostatic gliders**: thin-bodied, broad-framed organisms that launch from high escarpments before storms and ride charge differentials along storm tracks. They feed on ionized dust and tiny conductive arthropods lofted in fronts. When lightning webs across dry cloud, you can see entire schools flash into silhouette, folding and unfolding like shards of black foil.

Reproduction is geological timing masquerading as biology. Eggs are cast as ceramic nodules in warm scree, often in communal beds seeded by generations. They do not hatch at a date; they hatch at a **threshold**—a certain cumulative heat history plus a saturating charge pulse. In some years a whole slope stays silent. In others, after a season of violent dry storms, the hillside clicks and splits and a million palm-sized juveniles spill out at once, all already magnetically imprinted on ancestral routes.

If there is intelligence, it is rarely housed in one body.

The closest thing to a “mind” is often a **migration chain**: thousands of animals linked by contact, charge exchange, acoustic signaling, and inherited route memory keyed to periodic reality drift. Individuals can be simple. The front is not. It predicts fold-zones, detours around temporal mirage bands, pauses at vent fields when isotope output peaks, and resumes in patterns older than any single organism could learn alone. The place has no roads because the creatures themselves are the roads, continuously rewritten.

So what lives there?

Not beasts on a barren backdrop.  
Not machines pretending to be animals.  

What lives there is a full mineral ecology of charge, heat, abrasion, and timing—an ecosystem where bodies are furnaces, herds are circuits, predators hunt in physics glitches, and survival means being loud enough, conductive enough, and coordinated enough to remain continuous in a land where continuity is negotiable.
