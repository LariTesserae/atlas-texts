At first, you’d say **nothing** lives there except water under instruction.

Then you notice the instruction is being renewed.

A lip that should have furred over with mineral is clean this morning.  
A cracked peg has been replaced with one cut from younger wood.  
A float arm that yesterday hung one notch low now rides true.  
Someone is present—not loudly, not continuously, but with a fidelity so exact it feels less like occupancy and more like circulation.

So what lives there is probably not a “population” in the usual sense. It is a **custodial order**: people, or person-like beings, who have made maintenance into metabolism.

---

They are most often seen (when they are seen at all) in the white hours just after dawn mist lifts, or in the hard oblique light before evening. Pale-clad, lime-dusted, wrapped against glare. Their silhouettes read as extensions of the works: straight backs, narrow tools, rope coils across the shoulder, measuring rods marked in the same notch language as the standing stones. From a distance they can be mistaken for moving pieces of apparatus.

Up close, they are deliberate and unspectacular. No wasted gesture.  
They tap stone and listen for voids.  
They lift a slab, butter the bed, set it back before noon heat.  
They wick a blocked flume with braided fiber, wait, then trim away the salt bloom that caused the choke.  
They do not “repair” in crises; they preempt. They are always arriving just before failure would have become visible.

If they have a creed, it is the basin’s four rules, lived as etiquette:

- never expose earth,  
- never let water go still,  
- never permit a single-use surface,  
- never let age get ahead of care.

---

Where do they live, in a place with no soil and almost no privacy?  
Inside the thickness of it.

Behind retaining faces are dry service galleries.  
Within broad stair runs are sleeping cells no larger than alcoves.  
Under domed reservoirs are cool chambers where tools hang in mineral-proof wraps.  
Their homes are less houses than **voids in infrastructure**, warm from stone’s stored day heat, damp with night condensation, lit by reflected shafts from inspection slots.

You won’t find gardens, pets, or hearth smoke. Their domestic life is austere: cistern-cold wash water, imported grain bricks from lower regions, fermented mash in sealed ceramic jars, salts scraped and sorted by color and use. Even rest follows hydraulic logic—short, rotating, staggered—so some portion of them is always listening.

---

Their language, if overheard, is sparse and acoustic. Knocks through wall cavities. Whistle codes down vent throats. Hand signs across glare where voices would scatter. They read drip cadence the way others read weather; they can tell by sound alone which spillway is scaling, which siphon is about to burp air, which culvert has begun to draw too warm.

Children, when present, learn by walking grade lines with water cups and chalk. They are taught to see function first: this isn’t a stair, it’s also a gauge; this isn’t a roof, it’s also a catchment skin. Adulthood is not age but calibration—when your adjustments become small enough that no one notices them.

---

And there is one more possibility, which the place itself encourages: that “the inhabitants” are not primarily the workers at all, but the **pattern** they serve.

A distributed life made of flow, condensation, transfer, correction.  
A mind with no central chamber, only thresholds and turnarounds.  
The workers would then be like cells in a larger body whose true resident is continuity.

In that reading, what lives there is this:  
**an old intention kept permanently young**—stone, clay, timber, and water taught to outlast neglect, and a people who have chosen to become indistinguishable from that teaching.
