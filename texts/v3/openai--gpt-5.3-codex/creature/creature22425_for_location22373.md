What lives there is not a herd, not a colony, not even always a body.

At first, it seems like only **use** lives there: something that arrives, takes what it needs, and thins back into cloud. The sharpened branch, the struck stone, the bark stripped from exactly the right tree—these are not the debris of settlement. They are verbs left behind by a careful hand.

If you wait long enough, you meet the hand’s owner.

It is usually one at a time: a small, long-limbed highland forager, human-shaped but mountain-made in proportion—narrow through the chest, broad-footed for slick basalt, with toes that spread like a tree frog’s when weight shifts. Skin darkened by wet and wind; hair worn close or bound in cords so it does not drip into the eyes. They move in pauses rather than strides, holding still so completely that the basin’s own motion—mist, vine, drip—passes over them and erases the outline. You look away, hear a hollow drop strike root, and when you look back they are three stones farther along.

They come with almost nothing. A bone or hardwood wedge for prying bark. A packet of fiber for carrying fungal fans and orchid tubers. Sometimes a basalt flake knapped elsewhere and brought wrapped in leaf so it does not clink. No metal, if they can help it; in this air, metal sweats, blooms, betrays. Their tools are temporary on purpose: sharpen a stick, use it, leave it to soften back into the place.

Their diet matches the basin’s scale: freshwater crabs lifted from blind pockets, worms from black mud, snails from moss, tender horsetail shoots, medicinal cambium shaved in thin curls, and the brief orchid blooms only when they are at peak sugar. They do not strip an area bare. They take from many points, little from each, like sampling a conversation. Pools that look shallow but seize your wrist are tested first with branch tips; they know which stones hum hollow over old lava tubes and which hold.

They time themselves to the basin’s breathing. Dawn mist for gathering fungi while their pores are open. Midday light for reading moss-bead lines that, here, sometimes point uphill and reveal subtle seep pressure beneath the skin of soil. Dusk for leaving, before the cliff-boom starts and echoes begin throwing direction off. In drier months they come less often; in wet months, more, but never enough to cut a path. Their trails are memory, not scar.

They are not owners or guardians in any formal sense, but they behave like people raised inside a rule: **nothing in the basin should remember you for long**. No shelter frame. No fire ring. No stacked cache of stone. If they rest, they rest on roots and stand again. If they cut, they cut where regrowth is quick. If they must mark a spot, they do it with arranged leaves that rain will flatten by morning.

So whoever lives there is partly a person, partly a pattern: a solitary or near-solitary seep-harvester whose culture is tuned to impermanence, and whose true dwelling may be downslope in drier forest or in caves beyond the rim. The hanging basin is not their village. It is their high, wet room of medicines and quiet protein, visited for hours, known for generations, and left almost exactly as found.

And beneath even that, there is another resident: the basin itself, old enough and patient enough that anything living there must learn to move like water—arrive in many small ways, leave no hard edge.
