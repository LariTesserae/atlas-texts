At first, you’d say: almost nothing.

A few bodies moving through too much green.

At dawn, the long grazers appear—quiet, rope-muscled amphibians with low, blunt heads and outward-facing eyes the color of wet stone. They move like thought through the submerged thickets: not fast, just continuous. They crop softened leaves and algae films from drowned branches, then lever themselves up onto floating mats to rest in the heat. Their skin carries a permanent glaze of biofilm, so thin it looks like oil at first light; in close view it’s a living coat of microbes and pollen dust, a second ecology riding the first.

By full day they are gone, folded into root labyrinths where their bodies match shadow and peat. You notice them mostly by absence: a cleanly grazed lane through ferny growth, a trembling line of suspended spores where something large passed and did not hurry.

In the warm seams, translucent fish gather by the thousands. They are not truly invisible—more like made of faint glass and moving mercury. Their organs are pearl-pale, their spines hair-fine, their schooling behavior exact. Different thermal layers carry different plankton and pollen; the fish stack themselves accordingly, one cloud above another, each cloud feeding on a slightly different drift. When a seam shifts by a degree, the whole school tilts and descends as one body, as if obeying an instruction no single fish could hear alone.

Overhead, on the cliff thermals, broad-winged carrion birds patrol in widening circles. Dark-backed, light-bellied, they almost never flap and almost never call. They are the only large thing you reliably see at a distance, writing slow loops against mist and sun haze. Their nests cling to dry shelves high above the humid belt, where sulfur stains the basalt and little else can root.

At night, still coves wake in brief signals: blue pulses from soft-bodied invertebrates among hanging roots, each flare no larger than a handprint. They blink when touched by current or body-wake, then go black again. Seen from above, the cove looks like a thought trying to form and giving up.

So yes—life is sparse in species, generous in biomass.

But if you stay long enough, another resident becomes hard to ignore.

Not an animal, not exactly a plant, not one individual in the way land teaches you to expect. The basin’s dominant inhabitant is the **lattice**: an intergrown, basin-spanning mesh of floating succulents, reed rafts, drowned wood, filament roots, and chemosymbiotic films that bind it all. In one inlet it looks like meadow. In another, like swamp. In another, like submerged orchard. Genetically and chemically, large portions of it are continuous.

It is why the surface can seem solid until a swell passes and the whole plain lifts.

It is why coves migrate.

It is why narrow channels close over months and reopen years later elsewhere.

The lattice feeds in layers. Sunlit leaves harvest above. Midwater fronds strip dissolved nutrients and pollen. Down in dimmer, mineral-rich bands, rootlike tendrils host bacteria that process iron and sulfur leaching from young volcanic rock. Dead matter rarely falls far before being intercepted, softened, and reincorporated. Even cliffs join the system: root curtains descend, trap silt, thicken, detach, and become new floating margin.

In places, the lattice behaves like weather slowed to biology. A warm plume strengthens; one guild of epiphytes blooms. Their decay feeds peat shelves. Peat holds seeds. Seeds wait at density interfaces until a fractional cooling drops them all at once. Weeks later: a new raft tongue. Years later: a peninsula of green where open water used to be.

The grazers are not outside this organism; they are one of its pruning tools. Their browsing keeps channels navigable, delays mat overclosure, opens light wells. Their dung seeds microbial blooms that feed the glass fish. Their dead feed the birds and the root microbiome alike. Even the blue night-pulsers breed in pockets the lattice creates and stabilizes.

So what lives in the suspended sea?

A handful of animals, yes—elegant, quiet specialists.

And beneath them, around them, increasingly through them, the true resident:  
a young, basin-wide living fabric still assembling itself after catastrophe,  
a forest that learned buoyancy,  
a wetland that learned depth,  
a slow intelligence of roots, heat, and drift  
inhabiting a drowned volcano and, centimeter by centimeter, deciding its future shape.
