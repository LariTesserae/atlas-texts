What lives there is not one thing but a **mesh of keepers**—human, animal, vegetal, mechanical—bound by the same rule: survive by attachment.

At first, the people read as dark, hooded figures moving in braided streams along catwalks and canal edges, never hurried, never idle. Up close, you see they are less “residents” than **continuous adjusters**. A valve worker carries pollinator tags. A schoolchild carries ground stakes and chalk. A freight coordinator can tell you which reed beds must be left uncut for nesting, and which pump noise profile will crash a midge hatch and starve the swifts two districts over. Their competence is broad because the place punishes specialization without context.

They do not speak of “owning land” in the way upland people do. They speak of **holding layers**: this family holds the third stable stratum beneath a habitation block; that cooperative holds a canal wall until drift exceeds tolerance; another holds a roof-mat colony that technically belongs to no one but feeds everyone’s cistern filters. Addresses are timestamps as much as coordinates. Introductions often include a current marker depth, as if saying: *this is where I am true for now*.

Physically, they are built by climate and habit more than by lineage. Thick-soled boots for uncertain ground, fingerless inner gloves beneath waterproof shells, soft masks against mold spores and metal dust, coats lined with absorbent felt that mutes sound as much as it traps heat. Their posture has a subtle forward balance, learned from walking surfaces that may settle underfoot. Their voices stay close and low; loudness here is not power, it is ecological negligence.

Their homes cling, clip, and overlap: warm rooms bolted to old foundry walls, sleeping lofts over condensation gutters, kitchens whose steam is routed through herb racks and algae trays. Domestic life is infrastructural by necessity. You can tell dinner time by the smell of broth venting from heat exchangers. You can tell a district’s mood by what grows on its railings.

Children are perhaps the clearest native species of the basin. They learn to read survey paint before they read literature; they play games that mimic relocation drills; they can map safe stepping grids through thaw-soft lots from memory. They are taught early that maps are agreements, not truths, and that argument with geology must be polite, frequent, and well documented.

Nonhuman life is not “wildlife” here; it is co-citizen pressure. The crane-truss birds are given quiet windows in spring. Roof grazers are rotated like municipal crews. Otter-analogues that open latch-gates are both nuisance and protected labor force, because their routes keep fish moving through maintenance canals. Insects are managed like weather: anticipated, diverted, harvested, tolerated. Predators thread the soft corridors and are watched with respect reserved for forces you cannot legislate away.

And then there is the thing that emerges above all individual inhabitants: a kind of **distributed civic organism**. It lives in maintenance logs, seed schedules, acoustic ordinances, root meshes, migratory charts, and repair crews that work by lantern in rain. It has no single face, but it has behavior: adaptive, quiet, unsentimental, fiercely attentive. It does not seek permanence; it seeks continuity.

So what lives there?

A people who have replaced the fantasy of control with the craft of constant revision.  
A city that functions like marsh tissue wrapped around machinery.  
A civilization of stewards raised in damp cold abundance, fluent in drift, where even walls are expected to host life and every plan is written in pencil.
