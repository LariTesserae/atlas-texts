What lives there is not one people, and not exactly a city in the way surface cities mean it.  
What lives there is a **practice**: of noticing, of yielding, of moving before the stone insists.

At first you notice the humans—if humans they are in the ordinary sense. They are pale only in parts; not bloodless, but dusted. Their skin is browned or umber under a permanent bloom of gypsum, their hair often kept close or tightly wrapped so wind can’t take it. They wear layered cloth that can be retied in seconds: hood up for dust-rise, sleeves off in kiln heat, face-veils wetted when a runoff wakes old silt. Their knees and palms are callused from climbing shelves and ramps. Their voices are low by habit, not fear. In gust hours, they speak almost entirely in hands.

They are easy to mistake for austere people. They are not austere. They laugh often, just quietly. They decorate practical things: rope knots dyed with mineral reds and yellows, herd bells made from shell and fired clay, cistern lids carved with tiny maps of old flood seasons. Their beauty is in calibration: a wall that catches just enough drift-water, a roof angle that splits rain between storage and spill, a pen gate that opens one-handed while six horned bodies press against it.

They do not call themselves owners of districts. They are keepers of timings.

---

There are lineages, but not fixed neighborhoods. Families are known by what they read well:  
- crack-light readers, who can tell from a new shaft of brightness whether a ceiling seam is opening;  
- flood-counters, who repaint the red lines and remember which channel wakes first in slant rain;  
- saltwardens, who guard deposits and ration lick-stone for herds;  
- glider-tenders, who maintain roost lattices and harvest shed membranes for cloth and valves;  
- silt-fishers, who know exactly where a pool will persist three days longer than it appears it should.

Leadership follows competence more than blood. In dry years, water people lead. In migration years, herd people lead. In years of ceiling-fall, stone people lead. Authority is seasonal and specific. Nobody trusts permanent power in a place that rearranges itself each generation.

Children are taught routes before names of districts. Not “we live in East Quarter,” but “we go from Three Pillars to Quiet Kilns by the high ramp when dust runs low.” They learn flood lines like surface children learn alphabets. They learn to sleep through rope creak, hoof-scrape, and soft wing-shadow. They learn never to stand in a dry channel just because it is dry now.

---

And then there are the other citizens, unquestionably citizens, even if no one uses that word.

The pale six-legged grazers—broad-backed, blunt-muzzled, with tongues rough as pumice—are moved in deliberate bands across wet walls and mineral flats. They turn slick film into meat, milk, hide, tendon, glue, and heat. Their horns are padded at the tips with wrapped fiber when passing crowded courts, because accidents are expensive in narrow stone.

Fist-sized beetles move salt to wherever salt is richest; people follow those lines like weather reports. Their shells are used for scoops, lamp reflectors, and measuring cups. Children keep them in shallow pens and learn patience by watching their absolute, unhurried certainty.

Tunnel fish are less owned than anticipated. When pools gather, fishers set woven funnels upstream of seep points; when water vanishes, they read the cracks where silt-swimmers have buried themselves and mark them for later. The fish are food, yes, but also calendar.

The membrane-winged gliders are partly wild, partly partnered. They do not perch where there is shouting. They choose quiet rafters and abandoned ledges, and settlements that keep good roost etiquette keep better insect control. Their migrations throw moving shade across bright stone; people pause and look up the way surface people might pause for thunderheads.

There are predators too—lean ambush hunters in upper dark and narrow cuts—so low arches are built not only for structure but behavior: herds duck, predators hesitate. Architecture is law written in stone grammar.

---

If you stay long enough, you realize what truly lives here is a dense web of agreements:

between roof and runoff,  
between herd path and market court,  
between current hunger and next dry month,  
between old carved shelves and new rope bridges,  
between the need to settle and the wisdom to move.

Their religion, if it has one, is not conquest or purity. It is continuation. They honor ancestors by reusing their walls, repainting their flood marks, and abandoning their mistakes without sentiment. A “ruin” is often just a district resting until water returns. Memory is infrastructural.

So: who lives there?

Not cave dwellers, not exiles, not hardy caricatures.  
A people fluent in intermittence.  
Animal nations folded into urban rhythm.  
Crafts born from scarcity but not defined by it.  
A quiet civilization that survives by reading small changes before they become disasters.

In that bright underland, life does not root.  
It routes.
