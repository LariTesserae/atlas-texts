What lives there is not a species in the way we usually mean it.

At first glance, it seems like **nothing lives there but plants**—and that is almost true, if “plant” can include architecture, weather, and mind. The basin is inhabited by a single continental-scale clade of growth-forms often called, by outsiders, **the Verdant Civic**: not one organism exactly, not many either, but a population of bodies that remain continuously graft-compatible, continuously exchanging sap, signal, and memory until individuality becomes a temporary condition.

In that place, a “person” is a shape the forest wears for a while.

They rise out of walls, plazas, bridge-roots, and wet cambium seams when needed: flexible columns that unbraid into limbs, fan into sensory fronds, then recombine into something else when the task changes. None have bones. None keep a fixed silhouette for long. Their bodies are hydrostatic and tensioned—coils, bladders, braided tendons of fiber—because rigidity is a death style there. A resident can flatten under a closing archway, ladder itself up a trunk by growing hooklets in real time, then spill back to ground as a low rippling mat.

They are loud without speaking.  
No voices, no mouths shaping words in air. Instead they communicate through:

- strain pulses sent through connected rootways,  
- synchronized seed-pod snaps,  
- timed venting of aromatic compounds,  
- and deliberate changes in leaf-angle that modulate light like signaling mirrors.

A district can “announce” danger by tightening its trunks into a chord progression of cracks and groans. A family-cluster can “greet” by overlapping tendrils until graft begins—sharing sugars, then hormones, then memory traces carried in phloem chemistry. To them, touch is not intimacy; it is grammar.

They do have social structure, but it is ecological rather than legal. Roughly, you find recurring castes:

- **Anchors**: massive, mostly rooted intelligences integrated with towers and load-bearing trunks; slow thinkers, long memory, urban-scale planning through growth bias.  
- **Runners**: fast remorphing mobile bodies that scout shifting corridors and carry fresh signal chemistry between districts when geometry scrambles.  
- **Weavers**: specialists in closure; they fill voids by dusk, stitch storm-cuts, and decide which surfaces should be allowed to graft.  
- **Shedders**: sacrificial growth-forms that overthicken under stress, then tear free in mats—becoming new streets, levees, or nursery substrate.  
- **Bloom-choirs**: reproductive swarms that appear seasonally, release synchronized pollen fogs into canopy turbulence, then collapse back into rootfelt.

Birth is budding plus inheritance. Death is redistribution.  
A resident does not so much die as lose coherence: its pattern dissolves, and its tissues are accepted by nearby bodies. Identity persists if enough of its signaling rhythms are copied into new growth. In this sense, ancestry is architectural. You can walk through someone’s great-grandparent as a staircase.

Their perception is alien but precise. They see light gradients, yes, but also tension fields, moisture potentials, volatile chemistry, and tiny electromagnetic changes around charged storm roots. They sense where a corridor will exist this evening by feeling where growth pressure is already accumulating this morning. They do not navigate by map; they navigate by **becoming compatible with flow**.

As for temperament: they are not hostile in the predatory sense—there are no hunters there—but they are uncompromisingly incorporative. Anything rigid is treated as temporary obstruction. Anything resting too long in contact is eventually invited into graft. Foreign materials are wrapped, softened, infiltrated, and translated into local metabolism if possible, buried if not. The city does not attack; it assimilates by patience.

So who lives there?

A roaring, chlorophyll civilization of mutable bodies.  
Citizens made of vine and pressure and memory-sap.  
Urban plants that use streets as organs and buildings as life stages.  
A people for whom stability is a brief posture, and belonging means being willing, sooner or later, to touch and be changed.
