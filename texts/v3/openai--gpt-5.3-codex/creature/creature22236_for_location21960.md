What lives there is hard to name because it does not stay one thing long enough to be named once.

If you watched for a season, you’d first think the residents are sparse animals: the broad soft bodies that leave uphill grooves in biofilm, the cloud-gliders stitching ridge to ridge, the blind reed-grazers pausing when rain-pressure shifts. But if you watched longer, the boundaries blur. The same life that grazes in the lower pools can later be found suspended in mist, then months later calcified into a pale buttress under a vault-tree arch. In this place, “species” are less like branches and more like weather states.

The dominant lifeform—if there is a dominant one—is often called by travelers **the Porous**, because every stage of it is permeable.  
It lives in three repeating phases:

- **Seep phase**: low, soft, almost plantlike sheets spread over stone and lichen crust, feeding on dissolved minerals and the faint chemistry of rain.  
- **Drift phase**: ribboned, muscular bodies detach and “swim” in fog layers using cilia and pressure-sense pits, navigating by the texture of falling water rather than sight.  
- **Anchor phase**: when silence falls before a flow redirection, they fix themselves to rock, harden, and become temporary architecture—columns, lips, rings, even stair-like extrusions that guide or interrupt water.

That is why the built remnants feel half intentional, half grown. Many “ruins” are old anchor-phases left behind: structures made by organisms that were, briefly, trying to hold a boundary in a world that unthreads boundaries for a living.

Their intelligence is real, but distributed and slow. Individual bodies are clever in the immediate sense—finding flow, avoiding predation, reading old weather pockets. Memory, though, is stored outside the body in still pools. The black water inside the stone rings is not sacred by symbol; it is literal archive. Disturb it, and it restores prior current directions. Leave it undisturbed, and it accumulates route-memory, season-memory, sometimes even time-skewed memory. Young Porous imprint there before drifting out, inheriting maps of places that may no longer exist in the same orientation.

They communicate mostly by **editing rain**. Tiny shifts in posture, cilia, and surface tension change how drops merge, split, or pause on skin and leaf. To other beings it is just altered dripping; to them it is grammar. Long synchronized silences are announcements. A basin gone quiet is not empty—it is listening.

And there is one more inhabitant, older than any individual body: a basin-wide colonial mind woven through vault trees, reed colonies, and pooled archives. Some call it the **Watershed Choir**. It does not speak in words, and it may not be “a being” in the way we mean. But it remembers across erosions. It can nudge migration routes by changing condensation points. It can call gliders down by cooling rain by a single degree. It may be what keeps this world from ever becoming merely flooded terrain—because for it, mountain and sea were never separate categories to begin with.

So what lives there?

Not creatures *in* a wet world, exactly.  
A wet world that has learned to metabolize itself.
