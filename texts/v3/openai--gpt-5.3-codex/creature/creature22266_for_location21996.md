At first glance, it seems like nothing *lives* there except equipment and runoff. Then your eyes adjust, and you realize the place is crowded with specialists.

The first residents are the obvious wet-edge opportunists: herons and egrets standing like inspectors at the channel margins, gulls that have learned forklift schedules better than tides, kites circling where warm exhaust lifts them. Frogs occupy every culvert with a pocket of quieter water. Dragonflies stitch patrol routes over retention pools with military regularity. Rats use the pipe racks as elevated roads, slipping between valve shadows and cable trays. Stray dogs claim the cool undersides of parked trailers, one eye always open for the handcart that leaks fish blood. Nothing here wastes movement. Every animal follows lines—drain lines, rail lines, shift lines.

Then there are the small, soft residents that don’t look like much until you notice what they’re doing: algae skinning concrete in permanent damp, lichen writing pale maps across retaining walls, fungal films in gasket seams, mosquito larvae in any bolt-hole that can hold half an inch of warm water. Corrosion lives here too, as surely as any bird: rust blooming around welds, green oxidation around fittings, salt crust feathering out from joints. The place is so wet that decay doesn’t arrive as catastrophe; it arrives as routine maintenance.

People live there in that same practical way—not as settlers of a neighborhood, but as operators embedded in a cycle. They are shift crews, pump technicians, riggers, cleaners, drivers, watchmen, welders, line mechanics, fish handlers at transfer docks. They dress for splash, grime, and heat: rubberized boots, thin shirts gone dark at the spine, reflective tape dulled by mist and oil. They know where concrete sweats first before rain, which intake grill clogs with reeds at each moon, which dog can be trusted near a moving trailer, which birds mean baitfish are thick in the outflow. Their speech is short over the noise; their memory is long.

Most of them do not romanticize the place, but they are tuned to it. They walk catwalks without looking down. They can smell an overheated motor through diesel haze. They can hear, inside the constant roar, the one wrong bearing before it fails. They live by intervals: valve checks, break whistles, pump cycles, lamp glow, shift change. Time is less sunrise-to-sunset than gauge-to-gauge.

And if you had to name the true long-term inhabitant—the thing that owns the district more completely than any bird or worker—it would be **the system itself**: a brackish metabolism of water levels, heat, fuel, waste, salvage, feeding, draining, loading, waiting. It breathes through fans and vents, pulses through pumps, sweats through every steel surface. The marsh never left; industry never fully won; they formed a truce and gave it machinery.

What lives there, in the end, is a community of creatures and people shaped by that truce: alert, heat-tolerant, unornamental, and patient. They do not make the place beautiful. They make it function. And in that relentless, damp equilibrium, function is its own kind of life.
