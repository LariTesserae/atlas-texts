What lives there, most characteristically, is a **moving intelligence**—not city-making, not territorial in the fixed sense, but unmistakably social and deliberate.

If you kept watch long enough, you’d start to notice them in pieces first: a thorn wedged at shoulder height in a trunk, a line of cracked shells on the same root-stone, stripped cane laid across a mud pocket so small feet don’t sink, then a burst of calls that sounds almost like argument.  
Only later does the animal resolve.

They are primate-like, but built for this basin’s instability: long forelimbs, broad splayed feet, a low center of mass for mud and tilted roots, and a hide that’s more skin than fur—dark, oil-sheened, rain-slick, resistant to constant damp. Their faces are blunt and expressive, with heavy brows and nostrils that flare constantly; scent matters as much as sight. The ears are large and mobile. The throat is the real signature: a resonant pouch that lets each individual throw carrying calls through dense understory without exhausting itself.

They do not “travel” so much as **pulse** with the landscape.  
A troop spreads, thins, and reconverges around rain timing, flowering bursts, predator pressure, and the shifting fronts of grazers. They remember water in time, not in place: *after second storm, half-night later, north gully seeps for one hour.* Miss that window and they move thirsty through green abundance. Hit it, and they drink from temporary channels, leaf-cups, broken stone basins, even hand-dug seep pits that collapse by morning.

Noise is their shield. A silent troop is a dead troop. They maintain a layered acoustic mesh: contact chirrs at close range, barked position calls for mid-range, and deep chest pulses when visibility drops or predators close in. They can mimic alarm notes from other species well enough to test whether danger is real—or to panic feeders off a fruiting patch. In this basin, information is food.

Their tools are temporary, portable, and personal. Probes stripped from cane. Hook-thorns cached in bark seams. Palm-rib wedges for prying shell and fungus. Rounded hammerstones selected, carried, lost, found again generations later only because the same roots and anvils keep being useful before the edge shifts elsewhere. They build nothing meant to last, but they leave wear-patterns everywhere: polished knots, scarred logs, repeatedly opened termite towers.

Diet is opportunistic to the point of ruthlessness: fruit when it drops, cambium when fruit fails, beetle swarms by the mouthful, eggs, carrion, flood-stunned fish, fungal blooms after lightning. They follow big herbivore fronts at a distance and raid what gets exposed. They also get hunted—by short-wing ambushers from above and ribbon-pack predators below—so they sleep lightly in woven night platforms that are rebuilt and abandoned almost daily.

What lives there, then, is not a hidden village.  
It is a **chorus species**: restless, rain-timed, tool-using, and never fully still.  
In a basin where edges dissolve and return, they survive by making pattern out of noise—then moving before the pattern hardens.
