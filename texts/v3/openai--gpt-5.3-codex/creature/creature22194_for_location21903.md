At first, you’d think the inhabitants are just numerous.

Then you stay long enough to notice they are *nested*.

The oldest residents are the ones mistaken for terrain: the **oldbacks**—continent-scaled, slow-bodied organisms buried under peat, basalt, and industrial debris. Their spines are the ridges. Their breath is the rise-and-fall you feel in catwalk bolts and puddle rims. They are not always awake in any way we’d recognize; they cycle. Weeks of near-stillness, then deep thoracic surges that push warm vapor through stone seams, spawning fog, up-rain, and those odd conductive sweats miners used to follow. Forests root in their hide. Refineries anchor to their shoulder plates without fully knowing it. Whole stations are built on creatures that can take a year to complete one inhalation.

Living on and within them is an entire **electro-wet ecology** that treats metal and flesh as equal substrate. Conductive mosses sheet over rail spines. Salt-drinking vines lace power conduits. Blind vibration-hunters patrol drainage galleries and turbine tunnels, reading pump cadence the way wolves read scent. Wet-wing gliders roost in old ore bins and launch at shift sirens because sirens mean insects, and insects mean feedwater outfalls will froth. Even the scavenger crabs have learned patrol routes: they arrive at the same grates before purge cycles, helmets of shell knocking steel in anticipation.

Then there are the **station people**—not city dwellers, not quite settlers, more like custodians in permanent negotiation. They call themselves different things in different basins—Gaugekeepers, Gutterkin, Lift-Families—but they share adaptations. Clothing is layered with conductive stitching so lightning skates past instead of through. Hair is braided with grounding wire on ridge crossings. Children are taught vibration literacy before map literacy: how to feel whether a catwalk tremor is weather, herd movement, pump cavitation, or oldback breath. They don’t trust fixed routes. They trust sounds, smells, and the changing hum in their teeth.

Their culture is built around maintenance windows and migration clocks. Weddings are planned between storm bands. Markets open during turbine cooldown because that’s when feeder species crowd the outfalls and protein is cheap. Lullabies mimic bearing-whine and rain-drum because true silence is alarming there. They inherit tools the way other places inherit land: gasket kits, rope seals, fungal inoculants, old machine chants used to synchronize teams in fog where sight fails. Their shrines are practical—marked safe posts, honored drains, memorial bolts left unturned because someone died tightening them in live rain.

They don’t really separate “wild” from “industrial.” To them, a decommissioned pressure dome becoming a bat rookery is not ruin; it is succession. A flooded trench is a nursery if you can fence out the pulse-hunters. A tailings cliff can be stabilized with reed mats and then harvested for orchid resins that insulate relay boards. They farm blackwater terraces, trap condenser fish, keep fungal vats in old lift housings, and raise six-legged marsh stock on reinforced hummocks that can be moved when the ground decides to sink.

And yes—there are rumors of other intelligence in the dark between stations: cable-eel swarms that mimic maintenance calls, ridge predators that tap hollow trunks in counting patterns, colony minds in the bioluminescent fronds. The station people don’t dismiss these stories. In a place where rock can breathe and fog can flow uphill, “animal,” “machine,” and “weather” are categories with blurred edges.

So what lives there?

Not a single species.  
A **composite being**: oldbacks under stone, machinery on bone, wetlands in the seams, and human crews moving through all of it like circulating blood.  
If you stand still long enough, you realize the truth—everyone there is either feeding something, repairing something, or being carried by something larger.
