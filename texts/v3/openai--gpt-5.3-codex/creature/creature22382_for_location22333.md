At first, in that half-light, you’d think the true residents are the plants: the ribbed gray groves, the furred leaves, the lichen films drinking fog out of stone breath.  
Then the sound resolves, and you realize the place is full of animal intention.

The **people** are the most obvious contradiction—dry-land urbanists in an underworld that ought to have drowned or starved long ago. They are not delicate cave-dwellers. They’re built for gradients and dust: deep-chested, tendon-strong, with heavy calves from ramps and terraces. Their faces tend toward high nasal bridges and broad sinus structure, not as ornament but as moisture economy—air is warmed, filtered, reclaimed before it reaches the lungs. Their skin is usually dust-matte from lime and marl, their hair braided or wrapped to keep grit out, their clothes layered in dry fibers and softened hide, often rubbed with aromatic oils that slow water loss and cut the alkaline smell.

They have **two public voices**: one for intimacy, one for distance. Close up, they speak low and economical, almost private even in crowds. At range, they project from the diaphragm in sharp, shaped calls that can survive the roar. Along roads and across basins they switch to whistle codes and hand-sign, not as a novelty but as ordinary civic language. Children learn echo before rhetoric: how to place a call so stone returns it useful instead of shredded.

Their settlements sit like barnacles on basin edges, dense and mutually dependent, and their social units are built around what can be held: cistern rights, dew catches, shade courts, seed vaults, herd corridors. Prestige is less about wealth than about **retention**—which family can keep a wall cool, a grove alive, a ramp serviceable, a migration pact unbroken over decades.

Around them lives a thick guild of animals, most neither fully wild nor truly domestic.

The most important are the **dry browsers**—long-legged, barrel-bodied herd beasts that move between scrub belts and cultivated groves. They have blunt muzzles, salt-tolerant guts, and hard cloven hooves that strike sparks on old stone. People channel them with elevated lanes and thorn funnels; festivals and famines are both timed to their movement.

There are compact **burrow-stock** in the terraces—marmot-like diggers and rooting mammals that churn dust into soil pockets. Farmers curse them in one season and bless them in the next because without their tunneling, many garden beds suffocate into hardpan.

Above and within the vaults, the air belongs to **wing colonies**: bats in impossible numbers, swift-like birds in vent shafts, insect clouds rising at dusk and stripped out of the air by the ton. Their wingbeats are part of the weather. Guano is fertilizer, fuel additive, plaster ingredient; rookery rights are as political as water rights.

And then there are the **followers**—predators lean enough to vanish in gray scrub, patient enough to trail herds for weeks. Not monsters, just constants. Settlements answer them with raised sleeping floors, thorn corridors, night watch calls, and communal dogs bred for alarm rather than attack.

If you stay long enough, you notice that even the oldest residents are not any single species but a behavior shared by all of them:  
to live by interception.

Catch the dawn sweat before it evaporates.  
Catch the seed before wind takes it.  
Catch the herd before it turns.  
Catch the stone before it fails.  
Catch the word before echo destroys it.

So what lives there?  
A whole civilization of catchers and keepers—human, hoofed, winged, rooted—each one adapted not to abundance, but to brief passing chances, and stubbornly, loudly, expertly alive within them.
