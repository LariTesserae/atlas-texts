At first you’d think almost nothing lives there except moss, wind, and stubborn shrubs.

Then you notice that the place is full of specialists.

The people are usually called **ledgefolk** by outsiders, though they call themselves by their water-hours and elevation bands more than by tribe names. A person might introduce herself as “Third Fog, East Shade, Red Band,” meaning when she draws water, where she sleeps, and which rock stratum her kin manage. Identity is ecological before it is personal.

They are not tall. They are built for balance and patience: low center of gravity, wide feet, thick finger pads, shoulders trained by rope work from childhood. Their clothing is layered for vertical climates—sun-cloth outer wraps that can be stripped off in heat, damp-catching inner mesh that wicks condensation, and belts crowded with knots, wedges, hooks, seed pouches, and fiber line. Everyone is always tied to something when working near edges. Even elders sleep clipped to anchor roots when storms are near.

Because voice is unreliable in that constant roar, they are masters of **non-voice language**.  
- Drum codes for distance and urgency.  
- Mirror flashes for daytime syntax.  
- Flag angles for route safety and water timing.  
- At close range, touch-sign on wrist and forearm.  

Children learn “harmless thunder” before they learn counting: which booms mean storm, which mean settling cliff, which mean a slab is about to go.

Their homes are rarely freestanding in open ground. They live in **alcoves, root vaults, and woven cliff pockets** where airflow is known and runoff can be courted. A good shelter is grown as much as built: reed walls tied into living banyan roots, gutters trained toward cistern jars, fog combs hung where dawn breathes. Nothing is permanent except maintenance. Repair is daily ritual.

They keep few large animals, because the land won’t allow waste. The pale broad-hoof cliff-goats are semi-domesticated: not penned, but enticed along stable routes with salt and mineral ash. Ledgefolk harvest shed wool, milk during safe seasons, and read herd behavior as weather warning. Black flood-birds are never hunted when rivers run; their arrival is treated as a signal species, almost a clock.

Most protein comes from small life the land can reliably produce: moss crickets, seep shrimp from temporary pools, soft-shelled snails, and vine pods cultivated in mist groves. They farm in vertical strips, not fields—edible ferns on one damp shelf, fungus mats in another, hanging gourds where warm updraft meets cool stone.

Spiritually, they don’t worship a single mountain god. They revere **agreements**: between root and rock, wall and wind, person and rope. A broken tie is both practical failure and moral one. Shrines are simple—stone pegs, shell chimes, polished markers—yet often end up slowly displaced as trees “walk,” which they interpret not as desecration but as instruction: *nothing still is alive for long*.

Their dead are usually placed in woven cradles in high mist groves, where condensation feeds young roots planted with them. In a generation, a family can point to a curved trunk and say exactly whose patience is inside it.

So what lives there?

Not conquerors. Not hermits either.

A people of listeners in a land too loud for speech; gardeners of brief water; engineers of knots, timing, and trust—human life made cliff-shaped.
