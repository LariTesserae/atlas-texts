At first, it seems like only water lives there.

Water in stone, water in air, water held and guided and persuaded—not conquered.  
Then you notice that the thing inhabiting this place is not just a people, but a habit of people: a civilization built around maintenance so completely that maintenance has become temperament.

They are human, or close enough that you read them as human immediately: upright, spare, strong through the legs and hands, moving with the caution of those who grew up on wet rock. Their balance is excellent. Their feet are often bare inside woven oversandals, toes splayed for grip; when they do wear harder soles, they are ridged with fiber and resin for traction. Skin is usually uncovered at forearms and calves despite the damp—everyone is used to being a little wet. Clothing is layered plant cloth: reed-fiber weave, bark-softened wraps, capes that shed drizzle, belts full of small tools for scraping channels, trimming roots, sealing cracks.

Their voices are low, and not from fear. Loudness simply has no ecological partner here. With no birds, no insects, no herd noise, sound carries too cleanly across basins. So they speak close, gesture often, and let silence do connective work. Children learn routes by touch and slope as much as by map: this stone always slick by noon, that handrail polished on the left where elders lean, this culvert sings before it clogs.

What they *do* all day is the answer to who they are.  
They are clearers of rills. Packers of walls. Setters of stepping stones. Cutters of reed beds. Tenders of moss cisterns. Their calendar is granular and civic: not “storm season,” but “north basin desilting,” “third terrace re-plaster,” “fog-net mending week.” Prestige comes less from conquest than from reliability—who can keep a district dry-footed through ten wet years, who can read a seep line and predict where a wall will fail two seasons ahead.

Without animals, their material world is unmistakably botanical and mineral. No leather, no wool, no bone tools, no tallow lamps, no manure economies. They make cordage from bast fibers, padding from pith and moss, glues from starch and resin, vessel linings from lime-clay compounds and fungal films. Fields are mixed beds: tubers in raised damp mounds, grain analogues on the drier shelves, waterleaf greens along channels, and managed fungal courts in cool stone cellars. Pollination is wind, hand, and patience.

Socially, they organize by watershed as much as by kinship. A person belongs to a house, a lane, and a flow-path. Water rights are not private in the way land can be private; they are negotiated obligations—upstream clearing requires downstream labor, spring-use implies reed-bed maintenance. Their law reads like hydraulics. Their ethics does too: leave no blocked outlet, leave no standing rot where flow should pass, return overflow cleaner than it arrived.

They are not static, only incremental. They quarry, found satellite settlements, cut new ravines into workable channels, and build causeways farther into marsh margins when needed. But they distrust abruptness. Innovation is adopted like grafting: tested in one basin, observed through cycles, then copied. Their memory institutions are practical—stone markers cut with depth records, plaster walls inscribed with repair histories, chants that list channel gradients and culvert widths as if reciting genealogy.

If they have a central belief, it is that endurance is a communal craft.  
Not immortality, not purity, not expansion—continuity. Keep water moving. Keep stone seated. Keep paths passable. Keep each season legible to the next.

So what lives there?

A quiet highland people who learned to match the unfinished land without trying to finish it.  
A city that behaves like a watershed.  
And a culture in which steadiness is not conservatism, but a form of intelligence.
