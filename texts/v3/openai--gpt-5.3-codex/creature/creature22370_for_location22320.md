At first you think the residents are the obvious ones: people in layered clothes, carrying tools and baskets, moving up and down stair-runs with the confidence of mountain goats and machinists.

Then you stay longer, and realize this place is inhabited by **guilds of attention** as much as by bodies.

There are people who listen to pipes the way others read weather. They can hear a pinhole leak three levels away by the pitch shift in a riser. There are fog-collectors who wake before dawn to sweep condensation cloths and wring them into ceramic tanks. There are rootwrights who prune fig and bean and orchid so that walls stay held, not split; too little root and a terrace dries out, too much and a load-bearing seam fails in twenty years. There are rust-keepers, lichen-scrapers, valve midwives, bat-counters, compost accountants, rail schedulers who coordinate freight with goat herders and school shifts.

No one here is “off-grid,” because there is no grid edge.  
So the humans who live here are less owners than metabolizers: they inherit loops—water loops, heat loops, nutrient loops, repair loops—and pass them on improved or at least unbroken.

They tend to be altitude-specific without being trapped by it. High-tier residents keep cool stores, seed vaults, drying racks, signal mirrors. Mid-slope families run the heavy green: trellis orchards, mushroom halls, medicinal terraces, fish channels. Low workshops, where heat pools and machines are loudest, are full of rebuilders—bearing turners, ceramic patchers, insulation weavers, scavenged-electronics doctors. Children grow up bilingual in plant and mechanical time: they learn when bananas want shade cloth and when generator harmonics mean a belt is about to go.

They also share their city with animals that are not pets so much as coworkers with opinions.

Dogs memorize freight rhythms and sleep through everything except the one cart that always drops fishmeal. Cats manage rodent pressure around grain lifts and turbine housings, and are tolerated in almost every district as independent contractors. Harnessed goats trim vertical growth where human footing is unsafe. Pigs turn compost ramps and occasionally escape into market levels, where they are chased with theatrical outrage by people who secretly rely on them. Parakeets and bats are both nuisance and pollinator labor. Carp are filter and food and emergency protein reserve. Bees are treated like tiny infrastructure: protected, negotiated with, never entirely controlled.

Under all of that is a deeper population: the small lives that make this whole mountain possible.

Biofilms coat old channels and strip toxins. Fungal networks stitch manufactured soil, unlocking minerals from brick dust and char. Mosses cool pipe runs and hold moisture through dry hours. Termites and beetles process cellulose feedstock. Mites, moths, ants, and flies convert spill and rot into movement. In most cities this layer is called infestation. Here it is called **throughput**.

Even some machines count as residents now.

Not metaphorically—socially.  
A stamping press that has run for ninety years and still sets the district pulse is greeted in the morning. An ancient lift winch with a temper is assigned a caretaker lineage who know exactly how to coax it through damp season. Old rail carts, rebuilt so many times that none of their original frame remains, keep names painted on their sides across generations. People say “she’s running hot today” and mean both diagnosis and affection.

And over decades, something else lives there too: a slow, distributed intelligence made of habit.

It isn’t a single mind, but it behaves like one.  
When one wall starts weeping too long, three unrelated households alter planting choices before any committee meets. When bat numbers drop in one corridor, market fruit waste gets rerouted without announcement. When a storm peels sheets from an upper tier, lower districts pre-position catch nets and water barrels because they have learned the mountain’s cascade grammar. The place thinks in maintenance, remembers in scar tissue, predicts through ritual.

So what lives there?

- People bred by stairs, noise, humidity, and mutual dependence.  
- Animals that adapted from wildness into partnership without becoming tame.  
- Microbial and fungal workers doing chemistry at city scale.  
- Machines old enough to have biographies.  
- And the composite organism they all form together: a vertical commons that eats waste, drinks fog, farms light, and repairs itself in public.

In that region, life is not what survived industry.  
Life is what industry became when it ran long enough to learn ecology.
