What lives there is not one population but a braid.

First: **pioneer life**.  
Lichens that treat poured basalt and old concrete as equivalent opportunity. Mosses that can freeze solid at dusk and photosynthesize by noon. Bacteria in the seep-fields that plate minerals into iridescent films, slowly turning drainage scars into soil. Peat-forming plants in shallow pockets, building dark memory one millimeter at a time. On this plateau, “wild” life is mostly low to the ground and patient enough to inherit stone.

Then: **the moving herds and seasonal clouds**.  
Long-shanked grazers that follow thaw lines like rail schedules, marsh birds arriving in such numbers that districts dim their exterior lighting by treaty, amphibians thick as river stones basking in heat leaks from geothermal manifolds, fox-sized burrowers denning in the warm ribs of utility embankments. Predators are present but rarely seen directly—just tracks, scat, a silence among birds, then nothing. The city doesn’t expel them; it negotiates in timing and distance.

And also: **people shaped by weather literacy**.  
The humans here are not frontier romantics and not insulated metro-dwellers. They are technicians, wardens, growers, surveyors, transit stewards, wetland lawyers, animal-route analysts. Their clothes are layered, matte, quiet in wind. Their architecture is less “impressive skyline” than “distributed metabolism”: neighborhoods that breathe fog, bank heat, and shed stormwater intelligently. Children learn to read ridge wind and surface frost before they learn long division. Adults can glance at the color of pooled water and guess mineral load, freeze risk, and mosquito hatch probability.

They tend to live in **compact thermal districts**—dense, warm, communal—and travel often across exposed spans. So their social rhythm alternates: intimacy, then distance; shelter, then traverse. You get cultures of corridor etiquette and weather courtesy: shared storm rooms, public drying galleries, strict right-of-way rules when migration crossings are active. “On time” here means “in phase with conditions,” not just clocks.

There are, too, the near-living systems: **infrastructure intelligences**.  
Not personalities in the mythic sense, but adaptive municipal minds—drainage AIs that open microchannels before cloudburst arrival, maglev control systems that pause for herds, maintenance swarms that knit road surfaces while people sleep. Residents talk about them the way coastal people talk about tides: dependable, moody at edges, never to be ignored. These systems are designed to be legible; they signal intent in light bands, vibration tones, and published forecasts. Coexistence depends on trust, so opacity is treated as a design failure.

What “lives” there, finally, is a **civic philosophy**:  
No layer gets final authority. Not geology, not climate, not human convenience, not conservation purity, not automation. Every plan is provisional. Every district charter includes revision intervals tied to observed ecological shifts. The plateau is understood as ongoing construction by planet and species alike.

So the inhabitant of that place—if you insist on a single figure—is a kind of citizen-organism made from many bodies: sedge and sensor, herder and hydrologist, predator trail and tram line, peat and power trunk.  
A society with wet boots, clear instruments, and the humility to remain mid-sentence.
