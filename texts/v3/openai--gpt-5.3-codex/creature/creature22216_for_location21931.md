At first, you’d say nothing *lives* in the Lung except the small things already named: beetles, heat moths, the blind pipe lizards that drink from bolt heads like pilgrims.

Then you notice there is always one valve wheel that is cleaner than it should be, one inspection lamp re-angled toward a leak before the leak is obvious, one drip tray emptied before overflow.  
Not automation. Not chance.

What lives there is a **keeper**—not in title, in practice. The crews call them different names depending on shift and superstition: the Listener, the Tideman, the Lung’s tenant. In daylight records they’re just maintenance staff, contract line item, initials on work orders. In the Lung itself they are something more specific: a person who has tuned their whole body to this one room-sized weather system.

They are built by the place as much as born human. Skin always faintly salted. Knuckles lacquered with old burns. Forearms mapped with pale ladder-rail scars. Their clothes are layered like the walls: old canvas, heat-sleeve patches, stitched pockets full of chalk, zip ties, gasket shims, tiny mirror, folded tags. They carry no bright flashlight unless forced to; they prefer low lamps and reflected glint, reading the room by shine, by steam behavior, by whether a handrail has gone from matte to skin-oil gloss.

They move slowly, but never idly. Pause at junctions. Tilt head. Listen before touching.  
A dry click in cable voids means beetles are on fresh mineral.  
A hollow note in a valve body means trapped heat.  
A soft, irregular hiss means a seal still has six hours, maybe eight.

Their route is not a route so much as a tide chart. They follow condensation lines to cool down. They avoid certain warm pockets at certain hours. They know where rain will fall before it starts, because they know which chilled run woke on the hour above. If first-time visitors feel disoriented, the keeper looks almost aquatic, moving through invisible currents everyone else denies are there.

They don’t eradicate life unless safety demands it. They scrape heavy buildup, leave thin green threads. Clear one drain, leave another damp seam. Not sentiment—stability. They understand the ecology as an instrument panel: if the moss vanishes from flange seven, heat has shifted; if moths crowd one bulb, a cooler wall has warmed. The small creatures are not pets, not pests, but indicators written in motion.

Where do they sleep? In a pocket of accidental comfort: a grated platform above the warmest pipe, below a fan housing that turns just enough to keep mold off blankets. Earplugs on a string around the neck, never fully inserted. Even at rest, they are listening.

So yes, beetles live there. Lizards live there. Moss survives there in thin stubborn maps.

But the thing that truly lives in the Lung is a person who has stopped treating it as infrastructure and started treating it as climate—someone who reads metal the way others read sky.
