What lives there is not a creature you can point at.

It is a **continuum**—a single, continent-scale growth most people mistake for “just vegetation” until they realize every wall, stair, channel, and floating field is part of the same patient body.

In the shallows, it wears light: ribbon-kelp, fan-moss, reed-thickets, root-meadows.  
In the middling depths, it becomes cord and felt: mineral-dark rhizomes laid into mortar joints, velvet films over stone, braided strands stretched from parapet to parapet like submerged tendons.  
In the black corridors, where light fails, it thins into pale feeding lace that drinks from silt, iron, lime, and old timber—alive without needing the sun very much, alive by chemistry, by seepage, by time.

If you could peel the green skin back (you can’t, not for long), you would find that the old construction and the living tissue are inseparable. The fitted basalt gives it line. The terraces give it gradient. The drowned stairs give it edge after edge after edge to occupy. It prefers intention: corners first, seams first, channels first. Straight lines are its bones now.

It has no eyes, no mouth, no center.  
It senses pressure changes along kilometers of wall.  
It tastes metal traces in current.  
It remembers in thickness: one more layer in a cold year, one less in a dim one, a scar where a root-mat tore loose two centuries ago and knit back wrong. Its memory is spatial, not narrative. Ask it a question and the answer comes as redirected growth ten years later.

This is why there are no animals.

Not because of violence—because of **conditions**. The water is tannic, mineral-heavy, and often low in free oxygen below the upper spans. Surfaces offer no bare holdfast for grazers to start a food web. Any small colonizer finds every niche already filled with slow, chemically defensive tissue that does not yield quickly enough to support teeth, raspers, filterers, or scavengers. Life here is not empty; it is monopolized.

In warm seasons (such as they are), the floating meadows are its migratory organs. They shear off, drift ancient canal lines, snag at old gateposts, and knit back into rafts. In cold return, they settle and seed new curtains down into courtyards and spillways. Over decades, passages close. Over centuries, closed passages become load-bearing green walls that alter current, and current writes the next map.

It does not “hunt.”  
It does not “build,” exactly.  
But it edits.

A stair becomes a planted ledge.  
A carved trough becomes a nursery run.  
A broken berm becomes a reef of roots that catches silt until a new hill is born.

So whoever lives there is this: a vast, distributed, plantlike intelligence—or almost-intelligence—using a drowned human geometry as trellis and nervous system. A tenant that became infrastructure. A wilderness that is also an organism. A mind, perhaps, but one that thinks in winters and masonry lengths, and speaks only in the rearrangement of green.

If you return after a day, nothing has happened.  
If you return after thirty years, it has moved an entire valley by a meter.  
If you return after three hundred, it has answered you.
