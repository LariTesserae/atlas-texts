At first, it seems like **workers** live there—just workers, everywhere.  
Then, after a day, you realize it is more specific: **water workers**.  
After a week, you understand they are something closer to **time workers**—people who build in increments, and think in seasons, drips, and gradients.

They are human enough in outline, but shaped by the basin’s demands. Compact, wiry bodies. Strong hands and forearms from hauling, tying, scraping, lifting wet clay. Skin mostly covered: layered wraps, sleeve guards, hooded cloth at dawn to catch condensation and keep the chill off, lighter open weaves by afternoon. Their clothes are practical but never random—every strap has a tool, every fold can carry cord, pegs, chalk, or seed. You can often tell someone’s role by their belt before you hear their name.

Their feet are almost a sensory organ in themselves. People here read ground moisture by pressure and temperature through thin soles: damp-packed earth, crumbly dry fill, fresh mortar, a seep under stone. Children learn that before they learn trade arithmetic. In this city, to misread the ground is to waste water or lose a wall.

They speak softly, not because they are timid but because loudness is inefficient.  
You hear short instruction phrases, hand signals, clicks of tongue and teeth used like punctuation across scaffolds. Many tasks are done within arm’s reach, in practiced pairs or small teams, so speech is precise and economical. Arguments happen, but even those are often technical: slope angle, runoff timing, whether that retaining course should be reset before the next rain.

Social rank exists, but it is visible through responsibility more than ornament. The most trusted people are not warriors or priests, but keepers of flow: cistern counters, channel planners, clay sealers, spoil-sorters, slope readers. A person who can predict where stormwater will stall, and prevent contamination, is treated with almost ceremonial respect. Not adored—depended on.

They are surrounded by life they have taught to cooperate.  
Not livestock in abundance, but narrow ecologies: frogs tolerated near certain pits, insect swarms redirected toward compost trenches, birds encouraged at rim granaries and discouraged at seedlings. They maintain rooftop moss strips and reed pockets like tiny infrastructure, because even transpiration and shade are part of water policy. Their gardens are less pastoral than tactical—food beds as engineered microclimates.

Home life is dense, vertical, and unfinished. Families occupy stacked rooms cut into slope, then add lean-tos, then terrace platforms, then shared kitchens under patched awnings. Privacy is acoustic and temporal more than spatial: you get solitude by choosing an hour, not by owning distance. Nights are quiet enough that people can hear their own cistern level change by drip cadence. Many wake once before dawn to check lids, cloths, and channels, then sleep again.

Their rituals are practical but not sterile. First rain after a long dry spell is marked by coordinated silence while catchments fill. New stairs are walked in procession before heavy loads use them. A finished cistern gets named. Children are often introduced to the community by carrying their first measured jar up one full ramp without spilling. Mourning customs favor return to utility: stone markers become terrace edging; memory is folded back into structure.

What lives there, in the end, is a people who refuse to separate survival from craft.  
They are settlers, yes—but also hydrologists, masons, gardeners, accountants of shade, and patient improvers of imperfect ground. They do not dream of finishing the basin. They dream of handing it over less fragile than they found it, with one more ring of dwellings, one more reliable drain, one more season of food coaxed from cold soil.  

In a place that is still becoming, they have made a culture out of **continuation**.
