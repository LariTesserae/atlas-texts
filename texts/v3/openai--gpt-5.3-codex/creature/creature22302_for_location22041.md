What lives there is not one species but a **civic body**: a many-limbed population that behaves, in aggregate, like a single animal with thousands of temporary faces.

If you watch long enough, you can sort its citizens by function before you can name them by shape.

There are the **Masons**: stony corals, tube-worm matrices, calcifying sponges, clam beds sealing seam to seam. They do not move much, so they are mistaken for background. But they are the city’s slow muscle—depositing, cementing, partitioning, raising ledges where no ledge was.

There are the **Tenants**: shrimp guilds in cracks, squat lobsters under lips, eel pairs in old siphon-tubes, octopuses in chambered ruins, blennies that inherit shell-nests like leases. They are opportunists with strict etiquette. They do not “own” space; they **hold** it until pressure, predation, or growth displaces them. Vacancy is brief. A newly dead shell is occupied before its tissue cools.

There are the **Sweepers** and **Converters**: brittle stars, scavenger crabs, blind vent-worms, carrion fish with blunt snouts. They are why nothing remains singular for long. A body becomes fragments, fragments become lime, lime becomes wall, wall becomes address. Their appetite is architecture.

Then, higher and moving, the **Traffic**: rays skating avenue floors, schooling fish braiding around columns, slow groupers at intersections, sharks on fixed patrol loops. Their routes are stable enough to be predicted; their timing is learned like weather. Small lives cross only in the gaps, and that gap-knowledge is inherited culturally, not genetically—passed by following, failing, surviving.

And through all of this, there is a quieter resident still: the **Current-Mind**.  
Not intelligence in a skull—intelligence in repetition.

Larvae released at a certain hour on a certain thermal seam settle in the same three troughs each season.  
Predators cut left at a ridge because right costs one extra burst of energy.  
Anemone curtains thicken on corners where suspended food folds back.  
Soft corals retract two beats before a hunting pass because pressure changes arrive first.

Nobody wrote these rules. Everybody enacts them.

If you wanted one emblematic creature—one thing you could point to and say *this is the city given flesh*—it would be the reef octopus lineages that live in the old sponge courts and clam mosaics.

They are not kings. They are **clerks of vacancy**.

By day-black they are stone-colored, jointed into creases you’d swear were empty. By hunt-hour they flow out, tasting with arms, reading chemistry like text. They carry shell fragments to choke narrow entries, stack rubble at den mouths, drag bright bones inward and discard them later in midden-fans that attract scavengers, which attract predators, which clear competing tenants. They alter neighborhoods by domestic habit alone.

Their skin writing—pale bars, dark blooms, quick silvering—reaches only a few body lengths in this water, so they supplement it with touch and percussion: arm-tip taps on shell, siphon puffs through grit, low knocks transmitted through chamber walls. In a bright sea this would be private behavior. Here it is municipal signaling.

Around vents, another form of resident dominates: thick-bodied fish with scarred flanks and reduced eyes, resting in warm plumes as if in public baths; blind crustaceans farming bacterial films with delicate forelimbs; pale eels that navigate by pressure and taste, not sight. These districts feel older, less negotiable. Heat fixes boundaries more firmly than stone does.

Above them all pass the long-voiced transients—whales or whale-like things—whose calls roll through the avenues and momentarily synchronize everyone: shrimp freeze, rays lower, eels pause mid-knot. Then the pulse passes, and municipal life resumes.

So what lives there?

A **drowned metropolis of users and re-users**, where species are less like isolated actors and more like departments in a shared organism.  
A place where status is vertical, law is hydraulic, and memory is built into routes.  
A society without planners that nonetheless plans; without monuments that nonetheless remembers.

In that dark warmth, life has learned the oldest urban trick:  
nothing is wasted, everything is occupied, and every ending is rezoned by morning.
