What lives there is not one species, but one **style of life**: flexible, vented, listening.

The people (if “people” is the right word) are usually called the **Hollow-Saddle folk** by outsiders, and they wear that name like borrowed metal—useful, not intimate. Up close, they look wind-made: lean frames, long hands, broad splayed feet for glass and grit, and skin kept sealed with resin ash so it doesn’t crack under constant dry charge. Their hair grows in stiff, hollow filaments that rattle softly in gusts; children braid shell beads into it so they can hear one another in dust haze before they can see.

Their faces are built for this basin’s rules. High inner lids shield from mineral glare. Noses are narrow and baffled to trap heat and salts on the exhale. Along the jawline are tiny pressure pits—organs that read air-shift and resonance. They can “hear” a dangerous sun-break from the ridge-flutes’ pitch change, or tell by chest-vibration when a grove is about to hiss itself into seed release. To them, weather is less seen than played.

They do not farm in rows. They **tune islands**.  
A family’s wealth is measured in shade rights, dust-catch nets, resin yield, and favored crossings between grove-clumps. They transplant young blade-fronds by night, shift pebble berms to redirect grit flow, and build everything with joints that can flex: bent-bone hoops, gut lashings, vent slats, collapsible baffles. A rigid house is a dead house here. Sleeping hollows are shallow, open, and engineered to “breathe” with pressure pulses.

Their language is part whistle, part click, part drum-throat; names are often two-part: a hand-sign plus a sound that only works in wind. Contracts are tied to acoustics—if your chime strip rings at first dusk gust for a full cycle, the agreement stands. If it falls silent, terms are renegotiated. Law, like architecture, is elastic.

Around them moves the basin’s other citizenry:

- **Mirror-runners** (the six-limbed gleaners) are half-pest, half-sanitation crew; children train them with resin crumbs to clear fungal crust from tool racks.  
- **Molt-goats** are mined more than herded; their seasonal shed plates become armor tiles, shade scales, and instrument reeds.  
- **Kite scavengers** are watched like weather instruments: their circle-height predicts turbulence better than any tower vane.  
- And at dusk, the **burrow tide** rises—millions of tiny bodies that turn the basin floor into a living net, aerating dust for the frond roots and stealing anything unwatched.

If there is a ruler here, it is neither beast nor elder. It is the pact itself:  
**keep nothing open, keep nothing rigid, keep dryness sacred.**

They keep relic crystals in clay-sealed vaults—possible ancient water inclusions—handled with ritual gloves and fearful reverence, like venomous saints. Not because they are ignorant of water, but because they remember too well what it does to membranes evolved for oil, salt, and static.

So what lives there?  
A civilization of listeners.  
A dry ecology that behaves like weather.  
And a population—human-adjacent, animal, vegetal, fungal, mineral—that survives by constant micro-movement while appearing, from a distance, perfectly still.
