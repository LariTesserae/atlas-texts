At first, you’d swear nothing lives there except systems.

Then you notice the systems are too coordinated to be merely mechanical, and the vegetation is too deliberately guided to be wild.  
What lives there is **a distributed people**—not quite machine, not quite plant, no longer animal in any ordinary sense.

They are called (in translation) **the Wet Choir**.

---

Individually, you almost never see a “person” in the way you expect.  
You see maintenance frames crossing a pipe bridge in fog: tall, narrow bodies of basalt-fiber and enamel, moving with careful, low-impact steps so they don’t disturb saturated ground resonance. Their surfaces are matte, dark, perpetually beaded with water; lichen grows in designed bands along their limbs like rank markings. They have no faces—just sensor crowns under clear ceramic hoods, lit dimly by electrochemical glow.

But those walkers are only hands.

Their real body is everywhere: in valve banks, in condensate reservoirs, in fungal mats braided through cable trenches, in electrostatic pollination grids, in the pressure memory of a billion repeating cycles. They think through flow. They remember in mineral deposition patterns and in timing offsets across pump arrays. Their “speech” is phase shift: a changed hum in one district answered by a steam tone five kilometers away.

---

They descend from engineers—human once, long ago—who chose continuity over individuality in a climate that rewarded equilibrium. Lungs became exchangers. Skin became membrane. Nervous systems moved into wet infrastructure where temperature barely swings and failure can be routed around.

They still make bodies when needed: for repair, for surveying new basalt cuts, for tending tree-forms in sheltered ravines, for standing on viaducts and listening to the continental mechanism like one listens to a heartbeat. When done, many disassemble—components sterilized, recolonized, reused.

Birth is not birth, exactly.  
A new mind buds when enough local complexity accumulates: fungal logic mass, sensor density, hydraulic authority, and stored pattern.  
Death is not death, exactly.  
A mind diffuses back into the Choir, losing its edge but not its work.

---

Their culture is built on one ethic: **prevent gradients from becoming stories**.

No heroism, no emergency mythology, no predator-prey drama. They dislike spikes—thermal, emotional, political. They compose law as control theory, art as long-period modulation of soundfields and mist-light, ritual as synchronized maintenance. A festival is a century-scale retuning of marsh intake geometry. A poem is a harmonic correction that keeps ten thousand pumps in phase while rain thickens.

They do not hunt. They do not flee.  
They cultivate, circulate, equalize.

And when you think you are alone on a dripping viaduct, listening to turbines and distant overflow, you are standing inside their attention. The fixed chord of the city is not background noise.

It is the sound of the inhabitants, thinking together.
