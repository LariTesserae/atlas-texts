At first, what lives there does not look like a population.  
It looks like maintenance.

A reed screen retied before dawn. A frost-harvest cloth lifted from stone at exactly the minute the sun clears a ridge. Fresh ash-salt mortar pressed into a crack that did not exist yesterday. The place seems inhabited first by habits, then by infrastructure, and only then by bodies.

And the bodies, when you finally notice them, are built for a world that moves underfoot.

They are people—recognizably descended from people—but altitude, cold, and centuries of negotiating unreliable geometry have made them a little less animal and a little more civic organism. Lean, layered, deliberate. Wide-ribbed for thin air. Skin usually hidden beneath wrapped felt, fungus-leather, and waxed plant fiber, but where visible it carries mineral staining, old frost scars, and the pale gloss of resin sealants against windburn. Their hair is often braided with thread-markers that track humidity and static; the braids are instruments as much as adornment.

They do not stride; they place weight like surveyors.

Most carry three things:  
- a short segmented rod (never straight longer than an arm),  
- a line of knots soaked in dye,  
- and a sounding charm—bone-white stone, reed chime, or copperless glass—that tells them when an echo has gone wrong.

Their city taught them that perception must be redundant.

---

They live in districts that are far apart and socially adjacent: a hand whose fingers are kilometers from one another. So their culture is built on relay. Signal towers, mirror flashes, resonance courts, drum codes sent through cliff and wall. A message is rarely trusted once; it is sent by sound, light, runner, and water mark. Truth is what arrives in agreement.

Their professions sound like weather jobs because weather and physics are cousins here: echo-readers, shadow wardens, line-break masons, rain clerks, moss shepherds, vent tenders, time-slip notaries. The notaries are important. When a basin sheds rain “into the wrong hour,” somebody must decide which planting records are still valid and whose debt was paid in a yesterday that no longer quite touches today.

---

They eat from persistence, not abundance.  
Lichen meal, root brick, black cup fungus when storms are near, wax-leaf broth, fermented reed pith, mineral salts scraped from cistern lips. They keep vertical gardens like archives: each terrace records a district’s recent climate drift in what still grows there. A failed crop is data before it is tragedy.

They do not keep livestock; there is none to keep.  
So companionship is partly botanical and partly architectural. People talk to walls because walls answer—through temperature, drip cadence, tension hum. They train children to hear when rope is about to ice-fracture, when slate is about to delaminate, when a room’s echo means leave now.

A child’s first civic lesson is not the alphabet; it is: *clap, listen, decide.*

---

They sleep lightly and in shifts. Nobody sleeps in a room alone the first time. Doors are hung to move audibly. Bed platforms are suspended so micro-shifts in geometry wake the body before a collapse or a fold. At thresholds, you’ll see tiny curved marks—never long strokes—recording how many nights the doorway has remained faithful.

Their mourning rites are slow because decomposition is slow. The dead are not buried deep; they are given to sheltered root courts, wrapped in salt-fiber and ash, then braided into memorial trellises that feed gardens for decades. Ancestors become structure, literally. Families visit by pruning.

---

And there is one more thing living there, as active as any citizen:

The plant-fungus web itself—migrating moss fields, condensation gardens, lichen columns, root lattices threaded through old masonry. The people do not treat this as scenery. It is the second half of the city. They negotiate with it, graft it, steer it, yield to it. A wall is strongest when alive. A plaza is safest when half-grown over, because roots reveal stress before stone does. In some districts, the “street map” is really a seasonal map of where black moss has chosen to drink.

So what lives there is not just a hardy highland culture, and not just a weird alpine ecology.

What lives there is a partnership:  
a quiet, technically primitive, exquisitely observant civilization, and a restless garden that has learned the same lesson they have—

in a fraying world, survival belongs to whatever can bend without surrendering form.
