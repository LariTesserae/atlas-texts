At first, it feels like nobody lives there at all.

The plateau has that abandoned scale—too much infrastructure, too much light, too much open stone between districts. You see towers, basins, gantries, and the white sting of reflected noon, and your eye keeps looking for crowds that never appear. Then the place teaches you how to look: not for motion in bulk, but for motion in threads.

What lives there begins at the surface level of water itself. Thin skins of life spread over warm runnels and vent-fed trickles: sulfur-eaters, metal-tasters, pale films that turn bare mineral into food. They are the first grazed thing, the first harvested thing, and in a practical sense the first citizens. Entire districts are planned around their growth cycles, because if those films fail, everything above them thins out.

After them come the shelled browsers—the ones workers nickname **plate-herds**. Low bodies, many legs, broad scraping mouths that can shave a varnish of biofilm from stone without biting into the rock below. They travel in predictable midday migrations from condenser fields to seep terraces, crossing roads, rail cuts, and pump courts with perfect indifference to human schedules. Their shells are chalk-gray at dawn, copper-dusted by afternoon, and loud only by accident: shell-edge tapping shell-edge, a dry ceramic percussion. They are both nuisance and maintenance crew; without them, intake surfaces foul faster, overflow lips clog, and half the city loses efficiency.

Predators live by geometry, not speed alone. Tall, narrow, stilt-limbed hunters—called **spire-runners** in some districts—move like drafting tools across hot flats, placing each foot where heat shimmer is lowest and glare is least revealing. They rarely vocalize. Their communication is in stance, tail angle, skin-flush, and tremor pulses sent through the ground. They hunt in the reflective blind spots between equipment rows, where light breaks depth perception and a silent body can stand very close without being noticed.

Above all that, the gliders own the thermals. Translucent membranes, heat-scored and vein-ribbed, catch foundry updrafts and condenser plumes. They fold themselves around tower braces and crane arms as if hung there by workers. In migration season they are everywhere, tracing the industrial weather map with more fidelity than any instrument. The city’s aeronautics codes include them the way other places include storms.

And then there are the people—fewer than the city was built for, but deeply fitted to it.

They are not loud settlers in a wild place; they are metabolically patient, moisture-minded residents of a machine-ecology. They build for condensation before comfort, for mineral runoff before ornament. Their districts are sparse on purpose: open stone to radiate, channel, and collect. Their tools are bright-marked for glare, their fabrics matte to avoid flash-blinding, and their boots are thin-soled in specific bands so they can feel transmitted vibration through paved ground. “Listen with your feet” is not a proverb there. It is a literacy.

They speak softly and close. For distance they prefer taps, semaphore mirrors at safe angles, coded tower blinks, and pipe-borne percussion lines in older compounds. Children learn routefinding by mineral signs before they learn maps: white bloom downhill to water, dark varnish uphill to heat. They learn dawn caution—every “dry” rail can be slick—and midday stillness when herds are crossing. Most can identify nearby fauna by vibration signature the way other people identify voices.

Their idea of property is shaped by flow: not “this is mine,” but “this condenses here, drains there, vents then.” Contracts include animal timing. Overflow releases are synchronized with canal-spawner cycles. Maintenance crews leave deliberate ledges and shade slots where grazers sleep because clean metal is cheaper than perpetual scraping. Foundry managers publish heat-plume forecasts used equally by logistics pilots and glider-watchers. Nobody pretends the city is separate from the living system anymore; that illusion burned off generations ago.

At dusk, when warm rain slants through metallic dust and every street becomes a temporary mirror, you can see all of them at once: plate-herds edging through runoff, ribbon-swimmers knotting in lane water, workers adjusting panel tilt, gliders banking through steam. No grand chorus, no dramatic roar—just drip, hiss, click, footfall, wing-rub, shell-knock.

What lives there, finally, is a civilization—plural.  
Not quiet because it is empty, but quiet because everything in it evolved, engineered itself, or learned to survive by precision.  
A community of bodies that read heat, moisture, and mineral the way coastal cultures read tide.  
A high country that sweats, and inhabitants that have made that sweating into law, livelihood, and home.
