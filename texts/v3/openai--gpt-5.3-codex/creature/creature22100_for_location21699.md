At first, it seems like nothing *large* lives there—just flashes: prints at dawn, a wing-clap at dusk, reeds moving when wind is still.

Then you notice the same impossibility repeating in different places: mineral crust scraped clean from the humming slab, fog pylons that should be dead but aren’t, collapsed lattice arches subtly propped so their bent shade still falls where it matters. Not repaired well. Repaired *just enough*.

So what lives there is not only an animal, and not only a machine remnant.  
It is a caretaker species that outlived its assignment.

Locals from lower valleys (the few who climb that high) call it a **Mist Warden**.

---

A Mist Warden is tall in the way marsh things are tall: all leg, little torso, joints high above hot mud. Six limbs, though you rarely see all six at once because it moves with uncanny economy, placing feet into old prints like it is trying not to wake the ground. Its body is narrow, ribbed, obsidian-dark when dry and oil-sheened when the noon condensation begins. Along its flanks are comb-like membranes that bead water from humid air; by afternoon it can carry a skin of droplets without seeming wet.

From a distance it looks vegetal—like a mobile bundle of black reeds and hanging moss. That is not disguise exactly; moss and filament algae genuinely grow along channels in its back carapace, seeded by the basins it sleeps beside. In this place, camouflage and garden are the same thing.

Its head is blunt, almost tool-like, with a split crown of sensory fronds. Those fronds taste temperature gradients the way a tongue tastes salt. It does not hunt by sight first; it hunts by mapping moving lanes of warm and cool air across the bowl, stepping through them like invisible roads.

---

It feeds opportunistically, but with rhythm tied to the engineered weather.

When the marsh exhales and micro-rain starts, insect mats erupt; the Warden sweeps them from leaf faces with sideward flicks of a delicate inner forelimb. It digs copper algae from hot shallows before bloom crash. It will take gliders when one strays low and slow near a steam vent. It will also graze mineral-rich biofilm off pylon bases and stone seams, leaving clean crescents that look intentional because they are.

And it tends the old installations—not because it remembers a mission, but because the behaviors became survival.

- It clears insulating mud from condenser rings so evening fog still pools where prey will gather.  
- It drags conductive salts (stuck to its own hide) across cracked pylons, bridging dead spots.  
- At dawn, it leans against the humming slab until resonance rises, then stands in the dry circle and molts in safety.

In other ecosystems this would be called niche construction.  
Here it looks eerily like maintenance.

---

Its voice is rare and brief: three notes, hollow and glassy, usually at the margin between warm rain and cooling stone. You hear it once and think bird; hear it twice and realize the intervals are too exact, like a signal remembered by muscle.

They are mostly solitary. Not territorial in the mammal sense—more phase-shifted. Each Warden uses a route through seep pockets, ridge shade, and instrument sites, and neighboring routes interleave without much overlap. Once or twice a year, in a narrow chemistry window when sulfur dips and iron rises in the central marsh, two or three gather in knee-deep vapor and stamp synchronized patterns into new sediment. Eggs are laid in heated mud under overhang driplines where counting water never stops.

Hatchlings are squat, almost amphibian, living low in reed tangles and feeding on collapse-fall insect protein. As legs lengthen, they move outward to the mineral flats and learn the thermal lanes. The first time one survives noon crossing from one moisture pocket to another, it begins its life as a true Warden.

---

If you stand on the rim at sunset, you may never see one clearly.  
You may only see effects: fog arriving early to one basin, a dry circle appearing around a slab, a line of fresh prints crossing soft ash and ending where the ground gives off heat.

That is the right scale for them.

In a place tuned, abandoned, and still self-remaking, the resident is a creature that became part of the tuning: neither master nor relic, but a living feedback loop with legs.
