At first glance, nothing “rules” that basin except heat and timing.

Then you notice the arrangements.

Not nests exactly—more like temporary decisions. A cradle of woven vine above a floodline that has already been abandoned. A dry ledge swept clean while every other surface is dusted with spores. Fruit rinds split and laid open, never piled. Bones carried to seep-edges where mineral water will soften them. Clay jars, the old ones fused to soil, repurposed as fungus wells. Someone lives here who does not build for forever, only for *this cycle*.

They are called—by the few surface people who know them—the **Seamfolk**, because they live in seams: root to stone, wet to dry, dark to shaft-light.

They are about the length of a forearm when crouched, longer when stretched along a wall. Mammal-warm, but thin-furred, their skin showing through in pale bark tones: gray-cream, moss-dusted, mineral-streaked. Their hands are the first startling thing: six narrow fingers, double-jointed, each tipped with a dull climbing claw that can hook root fiber without tearing it. Their feet mirror their hands, so they move vertically as easily as across ground. In this chamber, “up” is often safer than “away.”

Their faces are compact and expressive in low light—large eyes, dark irises, no wasted white, and a reflective layer that gives a muted ember-glint rather than a bright flash. Around the muzzle they carry long whiskers with faint natural luminescence, not enough to light a path, just enough to mark presence: a soft green-white fringe that pulses when they’re close to one another. In the hush, that glow is language.

They almost never call out. Sound is costly here. Instead they communicate by:
- whisker-light pulses,
- finger taps sent through root curtains and hollow stone,
- breath-clicks too soft for most predators to place.

You can stand ten paces from a whole family and hear nothing but condensation ticks.

They organize their lives around water’s brief authority. When temporary pools appear, Seamfolk descend in coordinated bursts: drink first, then soak moss bundles, then wash parasite eggs from fur, then feed. They do not defend pools as territory; they defend *turn order*. The chamber is too crowded for ownership, so they evolved etiquette instead. Even their predators seem to recognize the boulder at center as neutral time—a shared warming station, a pause in violence.

They eat opportunistically but with precision: nectar at dusk, beetle clusters after drip-hours, cave figs and soft fungus when dry weeks lengthen. They keep small fungal gardens in those old clay jars, inoculating them with spores from seep-lines. That is one reason the jars remain: not relics, but active cistern farms.

And yes—those old hand-signs on the ledges? Some are ancient human work. Some are not ancient at all.

Seamfolk stack stone when storms are due. They soot niches with brief, smokeless fires fed by resinous root scrap. They leave no metal, no permanent cuts, no straight lines that would invite collapse. Their intelligence is conservative, local, and deeply ecological: not invention for expansion, but invention for fit.

What lives there, finally, is not a beast crouched in the dark, nor a hidden tribe waiting to be discovered.

It is a small, deliberate people shaped by a chamber where everything is temporary except crowding. They are quick when water comes, still when danger listens, and gone from any spot moments before you decide you’ve truly seen them. In a world of pressing heat and rationed light, they survive by becoming part signal, part shadow, part schedule—stitched into the basin as tightly as the vines themselves.
