At first, it seems like the crabs live there, and everyone else is merely tolerated.

They pour over the steps in rust-red tides, vanish into sluice shadows, reappear in market drains with stolen scraps. The birds scream from beam to beam as if they own the roofs. Eels knot themselves through culverts older than the households built above them. Frogs fill cistern mouths like pulsing valves. Insect clouds rise and collapse and rise again over warm runoff. If you stand still long enough, the city resolves into moving animal density held together by stone.

Then, after your eyes adjust to the dim and your ears stop fighting the constant roar, you notice the people—not as a single crowd, but as a net.

They are close-built and sure-footed, with calves and shoulders shaped by stairs, hauling, and wet load-bearing work. Their clothing sits tight to the body: oiled hide aprons, layered wraps of fishskin cloth and felted fiber, shell-buttoned cuffs to keep sleeves out of channels. Exposed skin is often lime-dusted or clay-sealed against rashes and rot. Most wear foot wrappings or strapped soles with textured undersides for slick basalt. Children go barefoot until they can read stone by pressure.

Their hands are marked by the place: knuckles thickened from rope work, fingertips polished smooth from wet stone and shell sorting, forearms tattooed in pale mineral inks that stay visible in low light. Jewelry is less ornament than signal—bone toggles, shell discs, tiny mirrored inserts—used to catch dimness for quick visual cues at conversational distance.

Because distance speech fails in the noise, they live in a choreography of near communication.  
A touch to the wrist means *wait*.  
Two taps on shoulder blade means *turn*.  
Palm shown flat, fingers spread low means *water drop incoming*.  
Families, guilds, districts each carry variants, like accents made with joints and muscle instead of vowels.

Their spoken language exists, rich and fast at close range, but much of public life is conducted in codified gesture, posture, and tool-sound: three quick chisel strikes for a blocked culvert; a patterned bucket knock for fish arrival; a hanging shell-rattle at a doorway to signal sickness within. In this city, literacy is partly tactile and acoustic. People read flow, vibration, and reflected glints as readily as script.

They do not farm; they curate metabolisms.

Some households tend microbial flats the way other places tend orchards. They know which sulfur film fattens snails faster, which blue-black mat flavors eel flesh sweet, which mineral mix turns bitter under too much warm flow. “Field” here means a shallow stepped channel with controlled seep and shade screens, scraped, reseeded, rested, and harvested by hand.

Others breed snails in stacked clay trays, or keep crab runs partitioned by mesh and stone gates. Fish vats line terraces—dark, dense, quietly alive. Egg walls are maintained like treasuries. Nothing is wasted: shell to lime, bone to tool, offal to feed, sludge to microbial beds, worn cloth to wick.

So what lives there is not merely “a people,” but a maintenance civilization: citizens of constant adjustment.

Their status does not come from owning broad land (there is no broad land to own), but from managing thresholds—hot water into cool, clean flow into used, fresh catch into preserved stock, noise into meaningful signal, rot into reuse before loss. The most respected workers are not conquerors or merchants but keepers of continuity: channel masons, lime burners, cistern listeners, mat cultivators, rot inspectors, bridge binders.

They sleep in humidity and wake to repair. Every household performs upkeep as ritual law, because neglect is instantly visible in this climate. A missed seal blooms fungus. An unwatched joint softens. A cracked spill lip reroutes a whole lane by morning. Their religion, where it appears, is practical reverence: shrines at junction stones, offerings set where warm seep first breaks surface, prayers spoken with hands in water so the current “carries” intention through the district.

And they are not alone in agency.

Dogs here are fish-yard intelligences—broad-muzzled, damp-coated, trained to herd crabs, guard drying racks, and detect tainted vats by scent before people can see clouding. Pack animals—short, wide-footed, patient in muckless wet—pull low sledges of stone and shell through lanes too narrow for wheels. Marsh birds are semi-domesticated by architecture: people build nesting niches intentionally, harvest guano and eggs by quota, and use flock behavior as weather and vent-pressure indicators. Even frogs are watched as living gauges; a sudden silence at a cistern throat means bad water movement somewhere upstream.

Over time, you realize the city’s true dominant lifeform may be the combined one: stone, water, microbe, animal, human custom—all locked in a long equilibrium that behaves like a single organism.

Its skin is plaster and basalt.  
Its blood is warm channel flow.  
Its nerves are hand-signals and hammer codes.  
Its immune system is daily maintenance.  
Its memory is patch layered on patch, each repair visible, none hidden.

So who lives there?

People, yes—wet-footed, near-speaking, bright-seeking people.

But also crabs, eels, frogs, birds, insects, dogs, draft beasts, mats of mineral-fed life, and the city itself: a crowded, dim-throated creature that has learned to persist without seasons, without leaves, without silence, and without ever truly drying.
