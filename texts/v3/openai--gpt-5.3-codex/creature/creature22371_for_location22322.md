What lives there is not a single people. It is a **working ecology** that learned to file permits.

At first glance, you would call the inhabitants human—bipedal, tool-making, municipal, tired at shift’s end—but the undercity has pressed them into a different register. Their skin is usually dust-matte from salt air and clay drift. Their hair is worn close or braided tight under filter wraps. Most wear layered throat cloths not for fashion but for solvent haze and pollen bursts, and nearly everyone has the reflex of turning one cheek to a draft before stepping into it, like sailors testing wind. They carry water as medicine, not abundance. Moisture is rationed by custom so old it no longer feels like law.

They are often called, by themselves and by outsiders, the **Phaseborn**—not because they were born underground (many were), but because their lives are synchronized to phase-time: lamp dawn, work noon, pollination dusk, dark-cycle maintenance. Their calendars track flowering events the way surface cultures track weather. A child’s school term might be “between black-frond unfurl and second resin shear.”

They do not build against their environment; they negotiate with it. So their professions sound half civic, half biological:

- **Prunemasters** who shape bridge-roots and transit trellises.
- **Spark-gardeners** who charge lantern bulbs and tune induction towers.
- **Sapwrights** who tap flammable amber flows and stabilize resin pressure before storm season.
- **Lattice masons** who coax trunkworks to harden into habitable vaults.
- **Predation clerks** who update ward maps when ambush species den too near school lines.
- **Quiet crews** whose job is to preserve sound-damp strata so alarm signals still travel correctly.

Their bodies are adapted by habit and by inheritance. Many have enlarged sinus passages and dense cilia from generations of dust exposure. Eye shields—biological for some lineages, ceramic for others—are common in bright rail corridors. They move with a careful, sliding gait on powder flats, conserving traction. Running is considered either urgent or foolish.

But the true “residents,” legally and practically, include beings that are neither animal nor person in the old surface sense.

There are the **Thinking Groves**: dense sensory thickets grown around old relay trunks, threaded with conductive mesh and fungal signal lines. Over decades, these groves accumulate pattern memory—traffic rhythms, air chemistry history, voices, impact signatures—and begin to make municipal predictions that are too accurate to ignore. At a certain threshold, they are chartered as neighborhoods. They can veto excavation, request shade cycles, and petition for pollinator rerouting. Citizens speak to them through resonant pylons, and sometimes through ritual touch against bark plates that pulse back in timed intervals: not words, exactly, but decisions.

There are also the **rail grazers** and **column herds**, six-legged mineral feeders whose grazing keeps support growth from choking load paths. They are tagged, counted, and insured like public machinery, yet mourned like livestock when tunnel collapses take them. Children learn their route songs before they learn district names.

The **tunnel birds** are unofficial couriers. They nest in cable trays and follow electromagnetic hum; workers tie light ribbon capsules to their leg harnesses for messages that must cross chambers when trams halt for root heave. Entire romances have depended on birds choosing one branch over another.

The **blind line-foxes**—freight scavengers with whiskers tuned to vibration—live in the margins of logistics. They are pests, mascots, warning systems. If line-foxes abandon a corridor, crews inspect for solvent leaks or predator settlement. If they multiply, seed-cake accounting gets audited.

And then there are the insects, if “insects” is still the right word: pollinator fronts steered by bass-frequency emitters, mite blooms raised for lantern maintenance, cutter swarms trained to strip dead filament mats before fungal rot catches spark. Their keepers are less like beekeepers and more like air-traffic controllers.

No one here pretends nature is separate from infrastructure. A bridge can molt. A zoning hearing can be delayed by flowering. Emergency drills include firebreak pruning and herd redirection. You can be fined for illegal cutting and thanked for emergency harvesting in the same week, depending on sap pressure and district load. Citizenship is partly residency, partly stewardship: if you live in a trunkblock, you are expected to join at least one growth duty each cycle.

So who lives there?

People who speak in maintenance verbs.  
Plants that hold up apartment towers.  
Animals that do municipal labor by existing in the right place.  
Groves that remember enough to count as neighbors.  

Together they form a city that is less “inhabited” than continuously **co-grown**—a population of hands, hooves, fronds, roots, mesh, and law, all trying to keep a young underland from becoming either desert or wildfire.
