What lives there is not a single creature but a **guild of climbers**—a whole biota selected for one repeated talent:

**hold on, drink quickly, and move when the pulse comes.**

At a distance, the plateau looks like stone with weather. Up close, it is an architecture of living grips.

### The first residents: catchers, wickers, and sponges  
Before anything with eyes or teeth, there are the water-workers.

- Lichens varnish exposed faces, turning fog into thin films.
- Moss and peatfelt lay over ledges like old carpet, storing a night’s worth of condensation.
- Bromeliads and tank plants sit in branch forks, each one a suspended cistern.
- Orchids and ferns stack above them, each layer harvesting the drip of the layer above.

Nothing here waits for streams. Life is built around interception. Every leaf is a gutter; every root is a rope and a straw.

Trees are less “trees” than **load-bearing organisms**: braided trunks, buttressed arches, latticed root curtains hanging down cliff walls. They create walkways, nurseries, and refuges for everything else. In many places, what looks like solid ground is actually a centuries-thick mattress of roots and decay suspended over void.

### The loud majority: insects of the pulse  
The most numerous inhabitants are insects timed to humidity and light.

At dawn, when fogburn begins and wings can still keep moisture, they rise by the millions. Midges, leafhoppers, metallic beetles, thread-winged flyers, nectar thieves, pollen drunks—an aerial bloom that lasts as long as conditions permit. At noon they retreat into seams, bark pockets, rolled leaves, and sponge mats. At dusk another cohort takes over: night fliers from fissures and hollows, moths with absorbent tongues, hard-bodied scavengers that navigate by stone heat.

In this country, insect sound is not background. It is weather.

### The hunters of air and edge  
Where prey is airborne and terrain is vertical, predators are narrow, fast, and opportunistic.

- Cliff swallows and knife-wing aerialists stitch the canyons, feeding on the dawn emergence.
- Small gliders launch from vine bridges, patrolling canopy gaps.
- Ambush lizards plaster themselves flat against warm channels in the rock and strike into insect clouds.
- Crevice snakes and hook-clawed mustelid analogues work root tangles and roost pockets.
- Carrion birds circle high, but often arrive late—because little carcass time exists here.

Even scavengers have adapted to altitude and episodic moisture: those night crabs you mentioned, pale-jointed and patient, emerge only after heavy fog nights when rock cracks run damp. They clean everything from eggfall to ungulate afterbirth to storm-killed chicks.

### The grazers and browsers: hoof, lip, and grip  
The large visible animals are not big by lowland standards, but they are astonishing climbers. Think goat, macaque, and antelope converging on the same design problem.

They are compact, deep-chested for thin air, with splayed, high-friction hooves and mobile dewclaws. Their lips are prehensile enough to strip wet leaf-bases and pry moss from vertical surfaces. They feed along root lattices and shrub cushions where water content is highest. They den in wind-cut alcoves marked by strong communal scent—musk, dung, old guano, crushed resin.

Their movement gives the ridges their second heartbeat: distant drumming at dusk as herds transit to mineral seeps and salt pans, then back upslope before full dark.

### Amphibians where there is “no water”  
Frogs and salamander-like forms persist in pockets: bromeliad tanks, rock basins, seep lines. They breed explosively after rain bursts, then vanish into wet moss, bark tubes, and stone cracks. Many can aestivate. Some lay foam nests above water so hatchlings drop in when rain refills basins. Their bright throats and sudden choruses are the acoustic signal that moisture has arrived—every predator listens.

### And the people  
Human life here is sparse, mobile, and old-tech by necessity, not ignorance. The residents are ridge-readers.

They do not “settle” the plateau so much as occupy repeating safe points: saddle shelters, lee alcoves, harvest ledges, pass shrines. Their craft vocabulary is the vocabulary of attachment: lashings, anchors, pegs, woven grips, bark lamination, bone toggles, stone edges kept keen with care because metal is rare.

They harvest in vertical calendars:
- eggs and guano from nesting faces,
- resin from exposed scars,
- medicinal epiphytes from fog belts,
- salt from certain basins,
- fiber and cane from sheltered bands.

Their rules—travel by ridge at dawn, valley at dusk; watch driplines for a full night; leave when insects go silent—are practical summaries of ecological reality. In a place where predation, rockfall, flash runoff, and wind shear can shift by the hour, etiquette is survival compressed into proverb.

They seem superstitious only until you understand that every saying is empirical.

### What truly lives there  
If you had to name the dominant inhabitant, it might be this:

**the vertical web itself**—plant, insect, hunter, browser, scavenger, human—stitched across old stone in wet and dry pulses.

Nothing owns the plateau floor. The real country is in bands: fog belts, root bridges, ledge gardens, cliff hollows, pass mouths. Life is concentrated where moisture can pause and grip can hold. Everywhere else is exposure.

So the place is not empty. It is **stratified**.  
Not silent. **Timed**.  
Not lush everywhere. **Lush in fortresses.**

Whoever lives there lives by three instincts:  
**climb, listen, and never trust stillness for safety.**
