What lives there is less a single species than a **working shift** of species—an animal economy tuned to heat pulses and vibration, like parts in a machine that never had a designer.

Closest to the glowing cracks are the first feeders: thin, black-red microbial skins, lacquered onto basalt where sulfur breathes out. They are barely visible until they catch the orange leak-light and shine oily violet. On them graze pinhead-sized raspers—hard-jawed, six-legged things with flattened bodies and mirrored backs that throw radiant heat away. They move in stop-start waves, scraping stone clean in arcs, then retreating as the rock crosses some thermal threshold only they can feel through their feet.

Behind them come the **clatter scavengers**: pale arthropods with layered, ceramic-like carapaces and antennae shaped like tuning forks. They are nearly blind. Instead, they map the chamber by percussion—tapping, listening, freezing, then sprinting toward tiny impacts. In that dark, each body is known by its rhythm. A healthy grazer has one footfall pattern; a molting one another; a dying one is a stutter they can detect across meters of humming stone. Nothing stays dead long enough to rot. It desiccates, splits, and vanishes into mouths.

In the ash pockets live stranger forms—soft-bodied while young, then armored by precipitated salts as adults. They bury themselves where vent breath is narrow and constant, extending frilled mouths into gas flow to harvest airborne particles and microbial flakes. They look inert, like mineral nodules, until disturbed; then they retract with a dry, papery snap and disappear under the ash as if pulled by a string.

The top visible predators are squat, heat-banked reptiles—more hinge and tendon than elegance. Their skin is matte black overlaid with pale, heat-scattering scales, and their jaws are lined with replaceable enamel pegs for crushing shell and chitin. They don’t chase by sight. They wait where thermal gradients shear across one another, then strike at silhouettes crossing crack-glow, guided by pressure waves in the floor. Their tongues read chemistry; their bellies read vibration; their eyes are mostly for timing lunges, not aiming them.

But the most important resident may be the **eggs**. Clutches are glued to undersides of ledges in narrow acoustic dead zones or, more often, in “bad notes” where vent resonance repels larger hunters. Whole generations are placed by sound engineering done instinctively: not architecture, but placement so precise it feels architectural. Hatchlings emerge already synchronized to the chamber’s pulse cycle—active on cooling breaths, hidden on heating surges.

So what lives there?

A dry-biased, heat-financed, noise-literate fauna that behaves like a factory line because scarcity leaves no room for waste. Every body is a sensor. Every movement is a transaction in warmth. The chamber is tiny, but its inhabitants make it function like an engine: intake (gas, minerals, carcasses), conversion (microbes to grazers, grazers to hunters), and constant recycling under pressure.  
Not lush. Not quiet. Not forgiving.  
Alive in the exact shape the rock allows.
