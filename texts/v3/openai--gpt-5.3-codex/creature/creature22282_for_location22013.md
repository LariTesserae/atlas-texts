At first, it seems like nothing *could* live there except moss, root, and water.

Then you start noticing decisions.

A drip-line redirected with three fresh twigs woven in exactly the right place.  
A load-bearing trunk wrapped with insulating bark strips before a freeze.  
A narrow run of footprints that never cross the unstable low courts, only the middle rooted band, then vanish at a wall that is not a wall, but a layered thicket with a doorway folded into it.

So: something lives there that understands tension, seep, and thaw better than engineers ever did.

---

They are easiest to describe as **the Braided People**—not because they braid things (though they do), but because each individual is less an isolated body than a moving knot in a larger living fabric.

Physically, they still read as human at a glance: upright, tool-using, social. But mountain cold and perpetual wet have edited them over generations. They are compact, deep-chested, with dense peripheral circulation and a slow resting metabolism that lets them work for long periods in near-freezing damp. Their hands are striking: long-fingered, blunt-nailed, incredibly strong in the grip, built for twisting vine bundles, testing branch flex, teasing roots apart without breaking structural fibers. Their feet spread wide and soft over the peat-sprung ground, so they can move across trembling lawns that would collapse under a harder gait.

Their clothing is never fully “finished.” It’s grown, patched, and alive in places: layered felt, barkcloth, resin-treated netting, and mantles seeded with mosses and cold lichens that hold a thin warm boundary layer against wind-chill. In fog, they can stand still long enough to look like another buttressed trunk with fern hanging from the shoulders.

They are quiet people, by necessity and preference. In a basin where sound carries oddly and ice-crack can mean hazard, they speak low. At distance, they communicate by taps through wood rails and root vaults—coded vibrations traveling through the very structures they maintain. Sometimes by drip rhythms: altering water fall in a gutter-chain to send simple messages downhill.

---

What they “do” is harder to separate from just *living*.

They don’t farm fields. They farm **flows**.

Their work is to keep the city-organism in balance:

- clear the open channels before night freeze;
- reinforce old trunks that now carry terrace weight;
- thin canopy curtains near light wells so flowering pulses can happen;
- stitch new root bridges when lower lanes turn to marsh;
- harvest peat heat from decay pockets and move it where cisterns risk icing solid.

They prune like surgeons and weave like bridge-builders. A newcomer might call it gardening until a whole facade shifts in a storm and you realize that braid they tightened yesterday prevented three walkways from shearing off.

They do not think in terms of building vs. landscape. Their core unit is the **span**: a path segment, wall segment, drainage segment, and habitat segment treated as one living structure with a seasonal stress profile. Every child learns to read spans the way other cultures read weather.

---

Home, for them, is usually in the thermal middle: not the brittle high terraces, not the drowning lower courts. Their dwellings are pockets inside thickened facades—dry chambers lined with rootwood and fungal felt, warmed by compost cores and body heat. Doors are flexible lattices, not slabs. Rooms can be widened by training growth outward or narrowed when frost years demand tighter insulation.

They keep very few animals. A handful of pale birds are tolerated and even courted because they carry seed between light wells. Amphibians in seep channels are considered indicators more than food: if they disappear from a quarter, water chemistry or temperature has gone wrong.

Food comes from shade-tolerant tubers, spore cakes, cold algae sheets, resinous cone kernels from roofline dwarfs, and nutrient broth steeped in mineral-rich seep water. Nothing extravagant; everything reliable.

---

Culturally, they are patient but not passive. Their calendar is hydrological, not celestial: first sustained thaw-drip, first hard refreeze, fog weeks, root-lift weeks. Festivals happen in light wells when bloom finally pushes through the dim. Those are their brightest days—still silvered and cold, but loud by their standards, full of seed exchange, courtship, trade, and repairs pledged publicly.

Their ethics are blunt:

- Never sever what you have not load-tested.  
- Never dam what you cannot overflow-manage.  
- Never strip a “dead” support without tracing three connections outward.  
- Leave every path better-draining than you found it.

To harm the weave is not vandalism; it is assault on everyone.

---

And there is one more sense in which “who lives there” has no singular answer.

Because over centuries, the Braided People have selected, grafted, and taught this basin’s growth so thoroughly that the habitat itself behaves like a partner species. The facades “remember” handling patterns. Root bridges thicken where footfall repeats. Channels reopen after blockage along preferred lines. Certain courtyards seem to anticipate fog and begin dripping before cloud fully settles.

So yes—people live there.

But also: a distributed, semi-sentient civic ecology lives there too, with the people as its quick hands and wandering eyes.

In that bowl, no one is really a resident alone. They are all organs of the same cold, wet, breathing district.
