What lives there is not one thing. It is a **population of caretakers**—human, animal, fungal, insect—and the people at the center of it are less “underground dwellers” than **maintenance-born citizens** of a machine they inherited.

At a glance, they look ordinary: compact, strong through the shoulders and calves from ladders and load-carrying; hands permanently marked with lime, soot, oil, and reed cuts; hair usually wrapped or braided tight against smoke and damp. But after a day you notice what is specific to this place. They tilt their heads before they turn corners, reading traffic by echo. They pause when drip-rhythm changes. They can tell which district you belong to by how you step aside for water. Their voices carry well—pitched to travel through brick throats and timber ribs without becoming a shout.

Their society is built around recurring chores so old they feel like weather. There are mirror-keepers who polish daylight shafts with ash paste and cloth. Gutter-families who own no land, only responsibility: this runnel, that sluice gate, those ceramic joints. Smoke-watchers who track air quality by lamp behavior and swallow flight. Child teams sent with reed brushes into crawl channels adults can’t fit. If someone asks “what do you do,” the real answer is usually “what section do you keep alive?”

They are practical almost to severity, but not joyless. Ornament exists—it just hides inside utility. A water cup may be plain outside and intricately scratched within, where only the drinker sees it. A ladder rung might carry a family mark worn smooth by three generations of palms. Songs are work tools: timing chants for mills, call-and-response for bucket lines, joke verses that encode flood procedures so even children remember the sequence when panic hits.

They do not think in private property the way surface towns do. Walls are shared burden; channels are public law. Pest-eaters are common wealth. Noise is civic infrastructure. A person who keeps quiet during a crisis is more suspect than a person who argues loudly in peacetime. Reputation is acoustic: who answered the horn fastest, who ignored the third hammer strike, whose gutter silted because they drank through their maintenance day.

And they do not live alone. Goats, donkeys, cats, swallows, lizards, spiders, owls, snakes—none are pets in the sentimental sense, none are merely livestock either. They are co-workers with negotiated tolerance. Children learn early which rafters belong to rats, which to owls, which corners to leave undisturbed because the spiders there keep the gnat bloom down in damp weeks. Even the frogs, those disputed visitors from “somewhere else,” are treated as a seasonal contract honored by both sides.

If there is a single kind of person this place produces, it is this: someone who trusts systems only when they can touch them, hear them, and repair them. Someone who measures wealth in stored water, intact joints, healthy animals, and neighbors who show up when called. Someone who can live in a structure entirely made by human hands and still feel it as landscape—because for them, it is.
