At first you might think the hollow is inhabited by **hardy people**.

Then, after a day, you realize it is inhabited by something more specific: **custodians**.

Not solemn guardians in robes—just practical, loud, dust-colored people whose entire culture has been trained around leakage, loss, and handoff. They are short-breathed from altitude, broad-palmed from stonework, and almost never still. They move sideways through narrow lanes with a shoulder turned, a jar tucked at the ribs, a coil of cord over one arm. Their clothing is layered and repair-heavy: wrapped wool, hide aprons, reed hats, cloth cuffs to wipe dew off metal screens at dawn. Everything on them has loops, knots, and ties. Even children wear little stoppered flasks like amulets.

Their faces are weathered early, not by drama but repetition—dust, smoke, reflected glare off pale rock. Eyes narrow by habit. Voices carry by design. In the hanging hollow, people call in chains: one woman shouting to a balcony, a boy relaying to a stair, an elder answering from a roofline. Information travels like water does here—captured, redirected, never wasted.

---

They live close enough to hear each other breathe through walls. Privacy is a curtain, not a room. If a pot cracks, three neighbors know before the owner does. If a roof channel clogs, it becomes communal work by reflex. Shame is public, but so is rescue. They have a thousand verbs for saving small amounts: to wick, to skim, to settle, to rinse through ash, to give back the clean fraction. A person’s character is measured less by generosity in feast times than by how carefully they return what they borrowed in dry weeks.

Their reverence is practical and their practicality reverent. The same line might hold a prayer strip and a maintenance roster. The same elder might bless a marriage, arbitrate runoff rights, and inspect condensation cloths for mold. “Waste” is close to profanity; “hold” is close to virtue.

---

If you ask what else lives there, they won’t point outward first. They’ll name the co-dwellers in work order:

- goats on upper scrub belts,
- burrowing fowl in penned runs,
- wall-conies in warm stone seams,
- martin flocks that harvest the dusk insects,
- beetles and ants that turn refuse into soil,
- vine and cactus and thorn trained into architecture.

In the hollow, animals are not scenery; they are moving infrastructure. Bells mark herd position by sound because sightlines fail. Fowl scratch compost courts. Insects are tolerated, cursed, and depended on in equal measure. Even moss mats in shadow slots are tended like livestock.

---

Over generations, the people begin to resemble their city’s logic.

They think in terraces, not straight lines.  
They make decisions in seasons, then in generations.  
They trust maintenance more than invention.  
They are suspicious of abundance they cannot store.  
They celebrate quietly but repair loudly.

Their humor is dry, often literally: jokes about spilled tea can run for years. Their affection is tactile and efficient—an extra knot tied on your load, a patched sleeve returned before dawn, your name called from three levels up with news you needed an hour ago.

---

So what lives in the hanging hollow?

Not survivors, exactly. Survivors imply emergency.

What lives there is a **settled intelligence**—human, animal, plant, stone, tool, and custom braided together so tightly that no part makes sense alone. A compact civilization built to metabolize scarcity without worshiping it. People who do not ask the mountain to become generous, only legible.

Under the worn arch at the rim, the motto is accurate in both engineering and love:

**Hold what can be held.  
Pass on what cannot.**
