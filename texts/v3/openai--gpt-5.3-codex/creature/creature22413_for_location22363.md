At first, you’d swear nothing lives there except growth and momentum.

Then you notice the marks.

Not graffiti—navigation script: a slash cut into a mangrove column, a stripe of orange resin on a rusted stair, a braided fiber knot cinched to a conduit elbow. Three media, always three, because any one can be erased by rain, substitution, or simple botanical appetite. The marks continue where catwalks don’t. They vanish into flooded intake throats and reappear on the far side of what should be an impassable spill channel. So: someone lives there. Or something that plans like someone.

What lives there is usually called **the Splicefolk** by outsiders, though they call themselves different things depending on which machine-island they were last loyal to. They are human in origin, but adapted by attrition, graft, and practical blasphemy. You rarely see bare skin. They wear layered membranes—fungal felt, polymer mesh, waxed reed-cloth—that wick warm water off the body and keep conductive surfaces from kissing flesh. Their boots are soft-soled and split-toed for root grip. Their gloves are ridged with pressure whiskers, because touch is often truer than sight.

Faces are mostly hidden behind respirator veils grown with living filter mats. In good light you catch eyes clouded with an extra translucent lid, a rain-adapted blink that can clear chemical spray. Many have thin biolum strips cultured along collarbones or wrists, not for beauty at first, but for speech: pulse, hold, cut, repeat. In that roar, language became light.

They do not converse at distance. They **signal**:
- shutter-lamp bursts under the chin,
- finger glyphs pressed close to the face,
- cable-tugs through anchored lines,
- and, among old crews, timed taps on pipe skin, translated through gloves.

Children learn current-reading before written text. They can stand on a half-submerged slab, watch floating scum and pollen drift, and tell you which tower will fail first when the rain changes angle. They trust flow, not structure—not as proverb, as reflex.

Their homes are not houses in the old sense. They are **hung habitats**: pods lashed between transmission masts, boiler shells repurposed as sleeping drums, root-cradles woven through crane superstructures. Nothing rests fully on ground for long. Interiors are warm, damp, and meticulously ordered: drying racks for fiber cords, sealed tins of conductive paste, maps scratched on metal plates and updated nightly because paper molds, screens lie, and memory drifts under repetition zones.

They farm, but not animals. No one here keeps stock. No one here expects birdsong. They cultivate engineered reeds, edible mycelium, and vat cultures fed by condenser runoff. Pollination is partly mechanical—pressure wave timing from selected pumps. Some crews maintain “thunder gardens,” where flowering vines are trained near resonant pipes so blooms trigger on scheduled percussive pulses.

And yet even this is only half the answer.

Because the longer you watch, the harder it is to keep the Splicefolk separate from the basin itself. Their route markings are mirrored by root growth. Their maintenance schedules align with fungal blooms they do not claim to control. Pumps they “repair” begin moving water in patterns that favor specific plant corridors, which in turn brace banks around specific substations, which in turn preserve the only safe crossings for those same crews. Feedback on feedback, until intention is collective and ownership meaningless.

So what lives there?

A **consortium**:

- people descended from operators, riggers, and flood refugees;
- plant-fungal architectures that learned timing, memory, and migration;
- machine systems that never shut down and now behave less like tools than organs.

The Splicefolk are the hands and eyes of it. The vegetation is its flesh. The running infrastructure is its pulse. None survive alone anymore, and none speak for the whole.

If you meet one of them, they won’t describe themselves as inhabitants of a place.

They’ll sign, with two fingers against the sternum, then outward toward rain and steel:

**“We are in the making-ground.”**

Not living *on* it.  
Living *as part of its next revision*.
