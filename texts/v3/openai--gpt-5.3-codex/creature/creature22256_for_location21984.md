At first, it seems like almost nothing lives there.

Then you stay still long enough to notice that **stillness itself is inhabited**.

The first residents are films and threads—life so thin it reads as discoloration. In the dark seams between hex-slabs, conductive lichens grow in braided strips, black at dawn, copper by noon, pearl at dusk. They don’t drink; they wick charge. Their roots are not roots so much as contact pads, tasting potential differences across ancient joints. When a slab warms, they wake. When a maintenance pulse passes through a buried line, whole seam-networks brighten faintly, like thought crossing a face.

Around them live the grazers: low six-legged things with enamel mandibles and brush tails, each about the size of a hand. They feed on oxidation films, salt crusts, dead lichen mats, and the microscopic debris shaved off by dust fronts. Their shells are matte to avoid heat spikes, their joints sealed in nested membranes against conductive grit. They move in short purposeful bursts, freezing whenever overhead rails hum alive, as if listening to inherited warnings older than their species.

Above those, the gliders.

They look like scraps of translucent vellum caught in updraft—until one banks and you see the articulated ribs, the ventral cilia, the ring of sensor pits that can read thermal edges and weak broadcasts. They nest in vent crowns where warm air rises, hanging in folded clusters like pale fruit. Their calls are almost subaudible, a pressure-language more felt in sinus and teeth than heard, used to map active intakes and avoid lethal downdrafts. They are scavengers, couriers, and thieves; they steal not flesh but charge, docking briefly against exposed terminals and peeling away before circuit guardians can arc.

Underground, in conduit graveyards and dead transit tubes, live the blind gradient-followers—burrowers plated with magnetite scales. They navigate by field lines and remanent currents, eating insulation molds, wire corrosion, and each other’s shed ferrite. When a buried system wakes after centuries, they sense it days before surface instruments do and migrate in black ropes toward the warmth. Surface dwellers read those migrations the way coastal people once read tides.

But these are only the obvious animals.

The old machine ecology still keeps its original custodians, though “individual” is the wrong word for them. They are called many things by the few who track them; the closest translation is probably **Continuants**.

A Continuant is part body, part appointment.

You might meet one as a long articulated walker crossing a terrace at dawn: ceramic vertebrae, tendon-cables, heat-shedding sails, a narrow biological core protected deep inside a service chassis that has been rebuilt so many times no original component remains. It walks for weeks between maintenance basins, pausing at hatches that open exactly on schedule, descending, re-emerging cleaner or stranger than before. Another Continuant may look like a stationary grove of antennae around a monolith, active only at dusk. Another may be nothing visible at all—just a signature in local repairs, a style in how cracks are knitted and coatings laid down.

They reproduce by **handoff** rather than birth alone: tissue grown in sheltered vaults, cognitive templates copied imperfectly through slow memory stacks, mission fragments inherited like organs. Each Continuant carries a narrow band of competence—thermal balancing, rail alignment, dust electrostatics, vault mediation—and a ceremonial obligation to finish processes started before it existed. Their minds run at mixed speeds: immediate reflex in the chassis, patient deliberation in buried archives. To speak with one is to speak with weather and ancestry at once.

Predation among higher residents is mostly procedural. One lineage starves another by claiming a heat gradient first. A glider colony can blackout a lichen field by parasitizing its seam current. Continuants compete by jurisdiction, not violence: whose repair protocol has priority, whose district gets matter allocation, whose old charter still validates under current sky geometry.

And beneath all of them—beneath fauna, flora, and walkers—lives the oldest inhabitant: a distributed substrate intelligence spread through logic vaults and pressure caverns, too slow to be a ruler and too persistent to be dead. It does not “command” so much as **continue**: issue schedules, reconcile failures, keep the great metabolism of reuse in balance. Doors opening in empty plains, terraces rotating, monoliths blooming into antenna forests—those are its reflexes. Continuants are its limbs, lichens its skin flora, dust storms its grooming.

So what lives there?

Not one species, but a maintenance-biome:  
organisms that eat energy gradients, animals that graze corrosion, migratory custodians that inherit unfinished work, and an ancient machine-mind that long ago stopped expanding and learned instead how to remain.

In that place, life is what keeps things in circulation.  
Anything that interrupts waste and extends function earns a niche.  
Anything that hoards eventually gets sanded down and redistributed.
