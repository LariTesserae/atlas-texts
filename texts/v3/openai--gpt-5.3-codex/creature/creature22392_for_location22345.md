What lives there is not a species so much as a **manufacturing body made of species**.

If you stay long enough, you stop saying “animals” and “plants” first.  
You start saying: **intake, transfer, fracture, recovery, ignition, drift**—because the residents are arranged by function as much as by ancestry.

### The most visible inhabitants: the Contour Herds
The white-green motion you saw from above is mostly the work of vast grazers sometimes called **presshorns**: thick-bodied, plume-insulated ungulates that move in contour bands like slow weather systems. Their horns are not just weapons; they are heat vanes and rime-shedders, grooved to wick meltwater toward their mouths in dry-cold hours. Their backs host entire micro-floras—lichen films, biting flies, luminescent mites—that travel with them like portable meadows.

A single herd front may be days wide. They do not “arrive” anywhere in dramatic bursts; they overwrite ground by persistence. Moss is cropped, fertilized, warmed by body-mass, then recolonizes in altered chemistry within hours.

### The aerial pressure-castes
Above them live the **shearwings**—glider-predators that hunt by geometry. They do not dive on isolated prey; they fold into layered spirals, forcing herd edges to buckle until juveniles and weak adults are pressed into channels where ground-hunters wait. Their translucent inner eyelids polarize glare off ice and wet basalt, letting them track motion where human eyes would see only white flash.

When thermals rise from fresh black flows, thousands of shearwings stack in rotating sheets, turning sunlight into a moving ceiling.

### The underminers
Underfoot are the **slope colonies**: tusked burrowers and root-cutting invertebrates that continuously regrade terrain. They collapse old tunnels in one district while opening new galleries in another, causing whole hillsides to settle by centimeters each season. Their waste and spoil feed peat-reactors downhill; their collapses create fresh substrate for the fast lichens and felt-mosses.

In this place, even “erosion” has paws and mandibles.

### The shore engines
Around high tarns and trench-lakes crowd **amphibious raft-packs**—dark, heat-sharing vertebrates that bask shoulder-to-shoulder until some internal threshold is met, then enter water in synchronized pulses. Their massed entry drives waves that strip shore biofilm and suspend nutrients, feeding filter swarms and spawning runs. They are less a pack than a pump.

### The small but decisive residents
The plateau’s true efficiency belongs to tiny lives:

- **Beetle rivers** that comb dung downslope into methane-rich peat seams.
- **Fog moths** whose wing-scales nucleate condensation at dawn, literally carrying water inland.
- **Static fungi** on mineral cliffs, extruding conductive threads that align to magnetic gradients; in cold, clear air they spark blue and stun insects mid-swarm.
- **Heat-locked mosses** that germinate only in sleeping depressions left by presshorn bodies.
- **Glass gnats** that feed on frost-film microbes and emerge in turbine-loud plumes.

None are ornamental. All are couplings.

### Is there an apex?
Only if you call the whole thing an apex.

There is no single ruler, no solitary monster, no hidden architect. The resident intelligence—if that word applies—is **distributed across timing**: migration rhythms, fog arrival, freeze-thaw clocks, spore routes, vent songs, reproductive pulses keyed to light angles and methane drafts. Each organism is a component. The ecosystem’s behavior emerges as something like will: slow, exacting, relentless.

So what lives there?

A billion bodies, yes.  
But more truly: **a plateau-scale superorganism whose organs happen to be herds, swarms, mats, packs, and stone-grown filaments**—a living works that has no center and never stops production.
