At first you’d think the basin has no single owner—just a thousand mouths, wings, paws, and throats layered together.

Then, if you stay long enough, a pattern appears.

The thing that *most* lives here is a primate people—close to macaques in outline, but longer-handed, quieter-footed, and built for masonry the way cliff goats are built for ledge. Call them **lintel apes**. They are not large. A grown one is dog-sized, all tendon and grip, with a deep chest for thin air and long fingers that can sink into a mortar seam as if it were bark. Their palms are callused into soft, dark pads; their nails are blunt chisels. Their tails are not prehensile, but expressive and balancing, writing commas through dim air as they cross parapets at speed.

Their eyes are the first adaptation you notice: wide, reflective, amber-green in low light. They don’t trust open sun and are most active in the blue hours—mist dawn, late afternoon, rain-dark noon, dusk. In full brightness they retreat into arcade shade, cistern chambers, and the cool undersides of collapsed roofs.

They do not build cities.  
They inherit them.

And yet, in inheriting, they maintain.

A lintel troop is organized around water architecture, not trees: one clan per cistern complex, branch families by courtyard, sleeping chambers in vault runs above. They know every drip lip, every carved gutter, every stair dish where condensation gathers first. Juveniles learn routes the way river fish learn currents. Old females—thick-scarred, half-deaf, impossibly sure-footed—serve as living maps. At first mistfall they make a slow patrol, clearing leaf plugs from channels with fingers and teeth, flipping pebble jams from runnels, scraping algae from overflow notches so water will split correctly. It looks like foraging, but it is maintenance behavior older than memory.

That maintenance is why the basin stays legible.

They are omnivores and opportunists, but not indiscriminate. They strip figs and vine fruit, raid pigeon nests, hunt frogs by touch at cistern rims, snap beetles from drain mouths, and steal milk from feral goats when they can corner one on narrow stairs. They cache hard food in dry wall cavities, plug openings with broken tile, and return by scent weeks later. In drought spells they shift lower and feed heavily on bat fallout and damp-root pith.

Their language is mostly percussive and architectural: throat barks, tooth-clicks, palm slaps against plaster, heel-drums on hollow stair treads. In this basin, sound bends; they use the echoes. A three-click burst in an alley means “occupied above.” A rolling bark bounced down a cistern throat means “dog pack moving.” Long dusk choruses are not song exactly but census—who returned, who is missing, where fruit set, where water fell thin.

Other animals orbit them. Dogs trail troop routes for scraps and alarm cues. Goats learn that cleared stairways mean safer ascent. Doves flush ahead of their patrols and reseed their feeding courts. Frogs explode where they keep water mouths open. Even bats benefit: lintel apes avoid disturbing major roost vaults because bat guano feeds the cistern-edge invertebrates they themselves harvest.

They are not tame, and not noble. They can be cruel in flashes, territorial, vindictive over water access. Border fights are vicious and brief, all gravity and teeth and thrown rubble. But they are also conservative in the oldest ecological sense: they preserve the pattern that preserves them.

If this basin has citizens, these are the closest thing.

Not architects.  
Not ruins-dwellers.  
**Stewards of old workmanship by habit, hunger, and inherited route-memory**—a people shaped by stairs, shade, and the sound of water finding stone.
