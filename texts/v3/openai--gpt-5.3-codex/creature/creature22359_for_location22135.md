What lives there, first and most truly, is **a body disguised as a landscape**.

Not one tree, not one reef, not one colony—but a continent-scale clonal weave, an ancestral growth people sometimes call the **Holdfast Province**. Its “trunks” are organs, its root-vaults are lungs and kidneys, its canopies are light-harvesting skin, its spillways are valves. Every bastion you pass is genetically close kin to the next one far down the dim corridor. The mountains drowned; this thing did not merely survive it—it occupied the shape of the mountains and learned to keep them.

It is not quick. It does not hunt. It does not move in ways your eyes can catch.  
But it is alive at architectural scale, and almost everything else here lives *with* it, *in* it, or *for* it.

---

Around that foundation lives a caste of smaller makers: pruning fish, rasping snails, fungal crabs, cleaner swarms, pollen drifters. They are not servants by intention, just species whose feeding habits became maintenance work over ages. They strip rot before it spreads, thin growth where flow must remain open, fertilize nursery crotches, scrape algae from intake throats, seed damaged struts with symbiotic films. A collapsed arch is a feast, and the feast is how the arch returns.

In another world they would be called scavengers.  
Here they are masons.

---

In the high spillway ridges dwell the **Gateherds**—broad-headed grazers with cartilage jaws and pressure-sense whiskers that can feel minute changes in flow. They browse the living weirs and keep them at exact porosity, chewing channels open where pressure climbs, packing mats where it falls. Their migrations are slow circuits across rim heights, and whole water levels depend on where they linger. They are heavy, placid, and treated by most predators with wary respect; to strike one is to strike the hydraulic heart of the province.

Below them, in trunk hollows and vaulted roots, live booming colonial animals whose swim bladders and resonant carapaces turn current into sound. Their choruses are not song for beauty. They are distributed sensing: pressure maps, storm warnings, lane-status signals. The deaf die quickly here; the listening thrive.

---

The open lanes between bastions belong to the **Patrol Shadows**: long, matte predators that hold perfect stillness in current and then cross impossible distance in one muscular decision. They are lane-faithful, almost civic in their routes, and their predation prevents any one grazer ribbon from overstripping a wall. They cull strays, weaklings, and the overbold. Even they do not linger near nursery tangles, where swarms can blind and peel them in minutes.

Predation here is not chaos; it is zoning enforcement.

---

In the suspended canopies live the soft-bodied climbers—ribbon-limbed, color-muted, patient. They tend vertical gardens of edible films and medicinal molds. They cache spores in mucous pearls, trade colonies by touch, and remember routes not by landmark but by vibration profile: this corridor hums in E-flat pressure, that vault thunders in triplets. They are among the few creatures in this world that can be called deliberate in the way you mean it. If there is culture down here, it is theirs: transmitted as mapped resonance and grafting practice.

They do not build with tools either.  
They persuade growth.

---

There are also the minute multitudes: cleaner gnats in silver clouds, root-lice that aerate peat-sponge, luminous gut symbionts drifting between hosts, larval sheets that briefly turn an entire column into velvet movement. Their lifespans are short, but in aggregate they are the place’s immune system. Any bare patch draws them within hours. Any new surface gets claimed, inoculated, named in chemistry.

Nothing stays naked long enough to be lonely.

---

And if you stay long enough—weeks, months—you notice one more resident: an emergent intelligence that is not located in a skull. It appears wherever flow, growth, and sound couple tightly enough: spillway ridges, resonance hollows, pollination lanes, grazer routes, predator patrols. Decisions seem to happen there—distributed, repeated, stable. No decree is spoken, yet water remains banded, corridors remain open, replacement follows collapse with uncanny fidelity.

So what lives in that drowned highland?

- A plant megastructure old enough to behave like weather.  
- Animal guilds that feed by repairing it.  
- Predators that keep the geometry honest.  
- Gardeners who remember in vibration.  
- Swarms that erase vacancy.  
- And over all of it, a slow collective will: not a ruler, but an equilibrium that has learned to defend itself by being alive everywhere at once.
