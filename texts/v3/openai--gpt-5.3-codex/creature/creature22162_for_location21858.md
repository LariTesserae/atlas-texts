At first, it seems the answer is: **nothing**.

And in the ordinary sense—fur, leaf, feather, root—that’s true.  
What lives in that basin is not biology. It is **attention**, **routine**, and **stone-work timed to weather**.

If there are people there, they are the kind who have thinned themselves to fit a place like this.

---

They are usually one person at a time, sometimes two in overlap, almost never a crowd. Not because the basin refuses company, but because too many bodies would disturb its careful economy—of heat, footing, and silence. They live in intervals, as the water does: present, then gone, then back again when the season and task align.

Their shelter is not a home in the valley sense. It’s a pause-point: low roof, stone plate overlaps, clay seals repaired by thumb and knife each thaw cycle. Inside: rolled felt, a box of pins and wedges, hammer heads with replaced hafts, cord gone stiff with old moisture, a wrapped bundle of charcoal, a ledger of marks more than words. Nothing unnecessary, because every unnecessary object must be carried uphill and defended from cold.

They are not conquerors of altitude. They are **readers** of it.

They read the basin by:

- where rime forms first and lingers longest,
- which dark streak is seep and which is only stain,
- what pitch of ice-ping means harmless contraction and what pitch means a plate is ready to shear,
- how the wind-tone changes when a gap has opened behind a slab.

They can hear a future rockfall in a sound most people would dismiss as one more gust.

---

Their work is small-scale and continual: recutting footholds blurred by refreeze, resetting cairns after cornice grit has knocked them crooked, restacking slate windbreaks stone by stone, scavenging and straightening soft metal brackets, moving pins from failed lines into safer ones. They do not “build” once; they maintain a conversation between hand and mountain.

Tool strikes are slow because speed wastes heat and accuracy.  
They rest often, not from weakness but from method: work, listen, watch shadow length, work again.

In bright hours they move along the black stone where thaw gives a brief grip. In gust hours they wait behind the low wall, counting the basin’s pulses. In fog they tether themselves by route memory and hand-contact, palm to rib, palm to seam, as if reading braille written in mineral.

Their faces weather quickly here—skin burnished by glare, eyes narrowed to slits of precision. Gloves are patched many times. Boots are studded and resoled until they are more repair than original leather. They smell faintly of iron, damp wool, cold clay, and the dust of stone newly struck.

---

But even these people are only part-time residents.

The true permanent inhabitants are processes: freeze-thaw, creep, sublimation, settling, fracture, melt-thread, refreeze skin. Those are the beings always at work, always in place. The human who lives there learns to behave like one more slow process—predictable, conservative, reversible when possible.

That is why the basin feels inhabited even when empty: its arrangements persist. A pin hammered flush where a hand once needed it. A cairn angled to be read in side-glare. A sheltered niche blackened by old lamp smoke. Signs not of occupation, exactly, but of **stewardship under constraint**.

So what lives there?

A person, sometimes.  
A craft, continuously.  
And underneath both, a patient, bright, mechanical life of stone and cold that never sleeps, only changes state.
