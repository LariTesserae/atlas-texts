At first, you would swear nothing lives there.

And in the ordinary sense, you’d be right: no lungs, no nests, no scavenging mouths, no eyeshine in doorways. But if you stay through three rain pulses and one long interval of drip-silence, you start to notice that the place is not empty. It is inhabited by **arrangements that can choose**.

They don’t wait in plain sight. They assemble.

A sheen of water gathers in a stairwell where no rain has fallen. Copper-stained runoff threads across brick in lines too deliberate to be runoff. Salt crust thickens along a wall overnight into ribbed forms like handholds, like vertebrae, like script. Then, in the charcoal half-light, a figure is standing where yesterday there was only a niche: human-height, sometimes taller, built of damp basalt plates, lime seams, and black-green copper laminae, as if architecture briefly remembered how to stand up and walk.

These are the city’s inhabitants—if “city” and “inhabitant” can still be separated.

Their bodies are never identical. Some are narrow as cypress trunks, jointed at impossible angles, with long, careful limbs made for stepping causeway edges without breaking surface tension in the basins. Some are broad and low, more buttress than person, their backs furred with mineral bloom. Some wear smooth masks of pale calcite where faces should be; some have only apertures, wet and dark, that open and close to measure echo. Inside them there is no blood, but warm brine moves, and you can hear it if you stand close enough: a slow siphon murmur, rise and fall, rise and fall.

They do not eat.  
They feed on **difference**: pressure gradients, heat stored in stone, salinity shifts after storms, the tiny potential between high court and low canal. When rain comes hard, they wake in greater numbers. When the cloud deck seals and the city sweats, they grow still and root-like, conserving shape. In dry intervals they flake, slough, and lighten. In saturation they darken and become strong.

They communicate the way this place thinks: through timing and surface. A row of drips suddenly synchronized beneath an arcade is a sentence. A new line of verdigris on a drain mouth is a warning. Three hollow taps from inside a cistern shaft means the nearby geometry is stable for now. If no echo returns, they will not enter—not from fear, exactly, but because they cannot maintain coherence where distance has gone soft.

That is why paths drift here. The inhabitants are not merely navigating unstable reality; they are part of the mechanism that negotiates it. They pin one version of a street long enough for load to settle. They abandon another and let it slide into a neighboring hour. They thicken steps with mineral skin, then cut channels through them days later. They copy districts by accretion and omission, producing near-twins with altered failures. What looks like haunting is often maintenance.

They have life cycles, though not biological ones. During stormbursts, new ones nucleate around old infrastructure—at siphon stairs, overflow lips, the throats of drains. Over years they harden, slow, and become less mobile, taking up positions as permanent stewards at key gradients. Eventually each one stalls entirely: a statue, a column, a lump of impossible masonry in a corridor you could have sworn was clear before. Rain rounds it. Salts bind it. Later generations quarry it by touch and timing, and the material re-enters circulation.

If you meet one on a causeway, it will usually step aside. If you are lost, it may lead you—but only on high ground, only while echoes remain clean. Never mimic its gait in wet dust. Its footprints are not directions; they are calibrations. Follow them and you may arrive at the same plaza in a different weather, with different walls, under a cloudbank from yesterday.

So what lives there?

Not beasts. Not ghosts.  
**Hydraulic persons**, maybe.  
**Mineral custodians**.  
Or, most accurately: the marsh-city itself, expressing temporary organs whenever balance requires hands.
