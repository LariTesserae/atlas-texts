At first, it seems like the basin is inhabited by **systems**, not by people—currents, root growth, fish routes, rain cycles.  
Then, if you stay long enough to stop looking for streets and start looking for routines, the residents come into focus.

They are a water-city people, but not in the heroic sense—more in the patient, maintenance sense. Their bodies are ordinary enough at a distance: wrapped in waxed reedcloth and fishskin capes, knees and forearms scarred by stone edges, hair braided tight against constant damp. Up close, you notice adaptations of habit more than anatomy: broad hands with thickened fingertips for wet knots; a diver’s calm breath; feet that grip slick timber instinctively; ears trained to separate rope-creak from predator splash from harmless drift-collision. They move with three-dimensional manners. In most cities, people think in left/right and near/far. Here they think in **above/below/between**.

They live in tiers, and households are vertical.  
A family might sleep in a dry leaf dome high in the reed district, keep tools in hanging nets one level down, cultivate edible moss and freshwater shell-stock in shaded root courts, and maintain ancestral markers on drowned stone steps below that. “Going home” may mean descending through three temperatures and two light colors. Children learn this before they learn counting: bright layer, green layer, blue layer; surface current, mid-root drift, stone-cold stillness.

Their speech is built for noise. In open rain they use short phrases and hand signs; across channels they use carved whistles tuned to cut through waterfall roar; through thick vegetation they communicate by patterned taps on hollow trunks. Every neighborhood has its own knock cadence, as recognizable as a dialect. At night, low drum-knocks travel through mooring masts to announce births, deaths, breaches, and drifting platforms.

They are not alone as “citizens,” exactly. The fauna are treated less as background wildlife than as established guilds with predictable rights of way. Trout schools own certain channels at certain hours. Bird towers are climbed only from downwind sides during nesting months. Crab swarms are encouraged in some quarters, redirected from others with bitter-root wash. Salamanders are tolerated everywhere because they eat what ruins stored cord. Even predators are mapped into civic life: there are warning carvings at ambush bends, and children recite them as route-songs.

The elders are not necessarily the oldest people but the best **rememberers of tension**: how tight a district’s anchor lines should be in early thaw, which old stairwell still vents trapped air, which moss gasket mix survives three weeks of hard rain. Authority belongs to those who can keep things from failing suddenly. Ceremony and repair are almost the same act here. Seasonal festivals involve re-lashing bridges, reseating float kilns, and blessing the knot patterns by repeating them.

They farm, but sideways. Gardens are suspended from beams, rooted in floating mats, threaded through window arches under water. They cultivate edible stems that trap sweet air; fungus on shaded cordage; medicinal fern along submerged lintels where mineral-rich seepage feeds it. Protein comes from fish corrals, snail colonies, soft-shell traps, and small whiskered grazers that return at dawn to basket roosts as if they, too, belong to somebody’s household.

Their dead are not sent away quickly. Some are cremated on high stone ledges when weather allows, but many are memorialized as **weights and holdfasts**—names carved into anchor stones, placed where districts strain most in flood season. To live here is to be literally held by those who came before.

So what lives in this drowned caldera city?

Not just one species. Not just one people.  
What lives here is a practiced alliance between humans, water, plants, old masonry, and creatures with routes older than maps. The residents are the ones who can hear the basin’s constant noise as information instead of chaos, who can tell the difference between decay and ripening, who know that survival is not conquest but tuning.

In a place that never dries and never goes quiet, they have become experts at one thing above all:  
**remaining.**
