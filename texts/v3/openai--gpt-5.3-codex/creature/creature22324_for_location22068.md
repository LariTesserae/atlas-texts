At first, you think what lives there is only process.

Not people—just continuities: rust flowering, pumps stroking, roots testing seams, silt remembering every disturbance. The borough feels inhabited the way a lung is inhabited by breath: by exchange, by rhythm, by things that pass through more than things that stay.

Then you notice the edits.

A valve wheel scrubbed clean in a perfect hand-width ring.  
A line of reed beds trimmed to identical height despite current shear.  
Signal lamps replaced in matching pairs, old screws saved in mesh pouches nearby.  
Someone is tending this place with the patience of geology and the habits of maintenance staff.

They are usually called the **Tenders**, though they have older names district by district—Chainfolk, Bellkeepers, Warm-Court Crew. They are human, mostly, but adapted by craft and culture more than by anatomy. Broad-chested from working under pressure. Ears scarred by years of gradient crossings. Skin marked by harness points where clip-lines ride the body every day. Their gear is not sleek diving equipment; it is municipal and repaired a hundred times: weighted coats stitched with buoyancy bladders, wrist slates for pressure notes, lamp masks shaded amber so they don’t startle the fish or trigger bloom cycles in the bell-vines.

They do not “walk” neighborhoods; they **enter flow regimes**. Their maps are current maps, thermal maps, pump-timing maps. An address might be “third intake branch below the old pharmacy, two degrees warmer than tram channel.” They move clipped to guide lines at shoulder height, pausing at old traffic poles now recoded for water logic. Red still means stop, but now for suction hazard. Blue means drift corridor open. Amber means keep your fins and tools high; the floor is about to breathe silt.

Most of their homes are not houses in the old sense. They live in pockets of managed atmosphere called **dry bells** and **breath rooms**: sealed mezzanines, former ticket halls, elevator cabs fixed in place and gasketed against flood. You’ll see light through portholes: very warm, very small domestic scenes. Nets of drying fungus. Children learning knot-signals on brass railings. Elders rebuilding impeller teeth at kitchen tables. Nobody wastes dry volume, so privacy is vertical—curtains, bunks, stacked shelves, hammocks above tool racks. Intimacy is quiet and close because any loud life belongs to the pumps.

They farm like engineers and maintain like gardeners.

Oxygen reeds in straight municipal rows.  
Edible waterleaf braided onto scaffold grids.  
Fungus towers in thermal shafts, harvested by calendar and valve cycle.  
They cultivate translucent bell-flowers not for beauty—though they are beautiful—but because the flowers indicate dissolved metal load by tint. Pink bells: safe to drink after boil. Milky bells: flush the line.

They keep animals, but sparingly. Not pets so much as co-workers. Blind crabs that clean filter mouths. Slate-scaled ribbon fish trained to carry messenger tags between lamp stations. A soft-jawed, eel-like species that can sense pressure leaks before instruments do; these are treated with the respect older cities gave fire alarms.

Their language has changed shape underwater. Consonants are for dry rooms. In the water they use lamp blinks, wrist taps, line-tugs, and a compact sign system designed for gloves and low visibility. Gossip travels by maintenance logs. Love letters are hidden in tool wraps. Public argument is done by changing schedules: if two crews disagree, one of them reroutes a warm outflow and makes the point in temperature.

Time for them is not day and night. It is cycle and recurrence:

- six-pulse pump phase,
- intake lull,
- bloom opening,
- sediment fall,
- pressure equalization,
- sleep.

They are slow to panic and quick to ritual. Before crossing a pressure arch, they touch the masonry. Before opening an old valve, they wait through one full heartbeat of the main pump. Before sealing a new dry bell, they float in it for a minute in silence, listening for the small hiss that means the future is leaking.

And if you stay long enough, a stranger answer appears: the thing that lives there is not only them.

The borough itself is a resident now—an accreted intelligence of water routes, biological growth, and inherited infrastructure, kept coherent by these people but never fully controlled. The Tenders don’t claim ownership. They say they are “in conversation” with it. They open passages; roots close others. They clear culverts; silt redraws borders. They seed light-panels; algae decide the pulse pattern. Governance is less command than continuous negotiation with a flooded metabolism.

So what lives there?

A municipal species, maybe.  
A guild-civilization built to survive in gradients.  
A city that became an organism and hired humans as its careful hands.

In the dark, warm green hush, all three are true at once.
