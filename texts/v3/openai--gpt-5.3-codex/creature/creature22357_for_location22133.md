At first you think the residents are the obvious ones: the low pale grazers, the cliff birds, the brief fever of insects that appears whenever the sun lingers.

Then you spend a few days there, and it becomes clear those are tenants.

What *lives there*—in the sense that a heart lives in a chest, in the sense that roots live in soil—is a spread-out, slow, weather-tuned colony that has no single body and no single name. If you needed one, you might call it **the Ward-Mat**: part moss, part lichen, part fungal thread, part mineral skin, all of it stitched through the basin’s cracks, shelves, hollows, and seep-lines.

It does not look like an animal because it is not one. It does not look like a plant because it is not one. In dry light it is just texture: dark felt in the lane bottoms, silver film on the lee side of pillars, rust cushions on bench-edges, green-black gloss around the pools. But the patches are joined underground by hair-fine hyphae and capillary channels, with a continuity that ignores what your eye calls separate neighborhoods. The upper quarter, middle ward, and lower bowl are not three habitats to it; they are three organs.

The upper fins are its ears.  
Not metaphorically—functionally. In narrow stone seams there, the colony grows taut, translucent membranes no thicker than onion skin. Wind passing through split pillars sets these films trembling. Different corridors produce different frequencies; gust direction, humidity, and approaching footfall all alter the signatures. The colony reads vibration gradients the way a schooling fish reads pressure or a bat reads echo. That is why the basin seems to “enforce” movement: long before you notice you are off-route, the living films have already registered your path and shifted moisture release and surface slipperiness ahead of you.

The middle ward is its gut.  
Those braided carpets of willow, fern, cushion, and moss are less a community than a managed field. The colony acidifies some cracks, sweetens others, leaks trace salts where it wants hooves to pass, withdraws them where it wants crust to harden. The goat-like grazers are not domesticated, but they are guided—nudged street by street by taste and footing. They crop growth that would shade the wrong mats, fertilize the right seams, press seeds into microterraces with their split hooves. Their “fixed routes” are maintenance corridors in a city that can’t move its own walls.

The lower district is its memory.  
In and around the tarn-dark pools, the colony grows dense gelatinous nodules under stone lips and peat shelves. These masses freeze and thaw without rupturing, layering season over season like rings in black ice. Dissolved minerals, pollen, ash, spores, even fragments of keratin from passing animals are captured there in chemical gradients that influence future growth patterns upslope. It remembers not as stories but as bias: where to thicken, where to retreat, which channels are trustworthy, which ledges failed forty winters ago.

It reproduces by fracture and inheritance.  
When a shelf calves or frost heaves a plate free, threads cling to both faces. If both fragments remain within condensation reach, two daughter-wards persist, slowly diverging according to wind geometry. Most die. A few survive and become new “districts.” The repeating six-sided depressions and stair-bench intervals you noticed are partly geology, partly this organism preferring certain stress fields and moisture polygons, then reinforcing them over centuries. Pattern selecting pattern until coincidence looks designed.

It is loud because it is active.  
The hiss from compressed moss mats is respiration. The drip-chains under ledges are partly plumbing, partly feeding fronts where acids meet mineral seams. The popping freeze line is not just temperature crossing zero; it is water being parked, released, and parked again in living sponge. Even the uncanny echo multiplication of birdcalls is amplified by films the colony has thickened in specific cracks, because those frequencies correlate with guano input on upper ledges. Sound here is not decoration. It is traffic data.

And if you sleep there long enough, you may notice the last, unsettling detail: it can learn you.

Not quickly, not personally in any human sense, but your repeated paths become easier. A treacherous crust firms after three crossings. A favored contour holds dew later into morning. A wind lane that used to shove now spills around a certain corner because a fringe of mat has swollen there, roughening flow. Ignore it, and nothing happens but weather. Move with it, and the basin feels briefly, impossibly hospitable.

So the answer is: a colony lives there, old as the final shape of the stone and young as this morning’s condensation.  
The birds nest in it.  
The grazers browse through it.  
The wind plays it like an instrument.

And from a distance, all of that still looks like a ruined town.
