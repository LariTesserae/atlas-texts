At first, you might think the city is inhabited by **masons**.

Then by **water-keepers**.

Then, if you stay through a few weather turns, you realize it is inhabited by a people who have made a third kind of life between those two nouns: not builders of objects, but caretakers of gradients.

They are upland humans, but shaped by altitude and damp routine into a distinct temperament as much as a distinct body. Most are compact, broad-backed, sure-footed on slick stone. Their hands tend to be strong in the fingers rather than the shoulders—hands for lifting sluice gates, clearing silt traps, knotting wet rope, reseating cracked tiles, feeling vibration in a wall. Skin runs wind-burnished, rain-marked, and rarely untouched by lime or clay. Clothing is layered wool, oiled fiber, felted wraps, slate-dark capes; everything designed to shed drizzle, hold warmth, and dry by a hearth that never gets very hot.

They do not move through the city as if through streets. They move through it as if through a living diagram: catchment above, storage below, overflow routes always remembered. A child here learns slope before letters. They can read a district by sheen—this stair too glossy, this parapet too dry, this cistern sounding hollow when tapped. Their equivalent of panic is not fire but blockage.

Their speech tends quiet, partly from weather, partly from ethics. Loudness is considered a kind of hydraulic ignorance: a refusal to listen to the tiny useful sounds. They listen constantly—to drip cadence in a culvert, to the pitch of water over a lip, to the hush that means a channel is running clean. Many houses keep “ear stones” set into inner walls where you can place your cheek and hear the nearest line.

Socially, they are organized less by family names than by **bands of responsibility**. One group maintains upper catchments, another desilts transfer runs, another tends freeze-morning patrols, another tracks seepage through retaining walls. These are not castes so much as rotating obligations; no one gets to live downstream from consequences forever. Festivals happen when the system proves itself: first full cistern after dry weeks, successful thaw without wall-loss, annual clearing of the longest aqueduct-spine. Their celebrations are practical joy—music on skin drums under covered courts, steam from shared stews, boots lined in rows drying by communal braziers.

Their religion, if it is one, has no distant sky-god. Reverence here is for recurrence, for return without exact repetition. Shrines are often no more than polished niches at junction points where three flows meet. Offerings are maintenance acts: a replaced stone, a cleaned grate, a willow planted in a seep line. Ancestors are remembered not in statues but in surviving interventions—“her bend,” “his stair,” “their west wall that held.”

They are not alone.

There are small companion creatures that have become part of the city’s metabolism: narrow-faced drainage dogs that can scent hidden leaks through plaster; patient goats on outer terraces whose dung feeds the few productive plots; slate-colored ducks in settling ponds; frogs that return each wet season and are treated as informal rain-omens. In deeper cold channels, there are fish so pale they seem carved from water itself, protected by taboo because their presence means oxygen and continuity.

And there is, in a way, one more inhabitant: the city’s distributed intelligence, held in custom rather than machine. No single person knows it all, but together they carry an exact memory of how water prefers to move across this plateau. That memory lives in calloused palms, in repaired thresholds, in the reflex to leave a vent gap where a newcomer would seal it.

So what lives there?

A quiet, durable people who do not imagine themselves masters of a landscape.  
A set of modest animals that survive in the margins they create.  
And a long, collective discipline: the habit of adjusting before failure, again and again, until maintenance becomes identity.
