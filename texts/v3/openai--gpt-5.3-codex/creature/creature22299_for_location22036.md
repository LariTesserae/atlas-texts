At first, what lives there seems to be only **continuance**.

Lichen that does not spread so much as persist.  
Birds that do not hunt dramatically, only circle and wait.  
A pale ridge-ungulate that places each hoof like it is returning to a footprint made generations ago.

Then, after a while, you notice that the built traces are too consistent to be relic alone. Someone still tends this place—not by filling it, but by refusing to let it unravel.

---

They are sometimes called (by the few lowland people who have met them) the **Chord Keepers**.  
Not a tribe in the crowded-valley sense. More a thin population stretched across the plateau’s fixed anomalies, moving among old shelters in long circuits that may take an entire season.

They do not build villages. Villages would imply growth, accumulation, walls against change.  
Their world does not change quickly enough to justify that.

So they keep way-shelters behind basalt shoulders. They reset cairns after frost-heave. They replace the bone poles around certain pools when rot takes them. Not to claim land, but to keep the land’s rules legible.

---

Physically, they look made for cold that never peaks and light that never resolves:

- compact bodies, dense through chest and hips, conserving heat  
- broad nasal passages and heavy sinuses, warming thin air  
- pale, wind-burnished skin that darkens only around eyes and knuckles  
- hearing that is less about sharp detection and more about filtering, selecting signal from the permanent roar

They do not shout. Airborne words are unreliable inside that endless chord.  
At close range they speak softly, almost into one another’s collars.  
At distance they communicate by stone taps, pole knocks, and weighted slings striking marked rock—percussive codes that carry through the wind-tone better than voice.

Children learn rhythm before vocabulary.

---

Their maps are not drawn as surfaces. They are memorized as **behaviors of place**.

Not “go north three ridges,” but:

- pass the saddle where distance stretches  
- wait at the double-shadow boulder until the left shadow crosses the right  
- avoid the cliff that echoes before impact unless fog is low  
- drink only from pools whose ripples oppose the wind

To outsiders this sounds superstitious. It is not. It is practical physics in a bent but stable world.

---

They keep small herds of those pale long-legged ungulates you saw on the ridges. Not fenced, not fully tame—more a negotiated companionship. The animals know safe lines across treacherous saddles; the Keepers know winter hollows where seep-moss stays green under rime. Each species survives by borrowing the other’s memory.

Diet is spare: moss cakes, dried organ meat, wind-cured fat, insects skimmed from wet hollows in fine woven trays, occasional bird eggs taken with strict restraint. Nothing abundant, so nothing wasted.

Their population stays low because the plateau insists on it.  
Birth is celebrated, but spacing is deliberate.  
Longevity is common. Urgency is not admired.

---

Spiritually, they are not waiting for revelation.  
They believe revelation already happened, long ago, when the world here settled into its present chord.

Their central virtue is not bravery or expansion, but **maintenance**.  
If a wall falls, you rebuild it where it fell.  
If a marker tilts, you right it to its old angle.  
If a route becomes dangerous, you do not “conquer” it—you relearn it.

They do not seek to quiet the plateau. Quiet would mean erasing the one constant that lets life orient itself.

---

And beneath even them—if you stay long enough to notice subtler life—there is another resident: a dark biofilm in fissures and on damp windward faces, a lichen-fungal consortium they cultivate without quite domesticating. It feeds on mineral seep and condensation, thickens where the chord vibrates strongest, and may be the only thing truly everywhere up there.

The Keepers call it, roughly, **the Listening Skin**.  
They harvest it in threads, never scraping rock clean.  
They say if it dies, the plateau forgets its own boundaries.

Maybe that is belief.  
Maybe that is ecology.  
In that place, the two are rarely separate.

---

So what lives there?

Not conquerors, not hermits, not ghosts.  
A thin, enduring people whose culture is tuned to a permanent sound,  
and a web of low, patient life that treats stone as season and season as stone.

In a land where almost nothing happens, loudly, forever,  
they are the ones who know how to live by **keeping time with what does not end**.
