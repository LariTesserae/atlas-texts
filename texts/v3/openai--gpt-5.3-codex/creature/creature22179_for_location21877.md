What lives there is not a species in the usual sense.  
It is a **custodial ecology**: part plant succession, part legacy automation, part intelligence that learned to inhabit delay.

If you met it directly, you’d notice the walkers first.

They move the old service paths at dawn, when every rail and handhold is wet. From a distance they look fox-sized or child-sized depending on slope and fog—jointed frames under mats of moss, with cable-whiskers that test humidity and temperature gradients in the air. Their skins are patched from composite plates, resin, bark-fiber, and whatever they have scavenged from old condenser shrouds. They are quiet because they were built quiet: soft electric servos, low-torque gait, broad feet that spread pressure over root and scree. They do not herd, hunt, or patrol in any military way. They circulate.

By midmorning they are already busy in small, unspectacular acts: opening a valve by six degrees, clearing fern from a drainage lip, reseating insulation where vine pressure has exposed a warm line. They scrape oxidation, not to remove it entirely but to keep it from crossing thresholds. They prune around sensor housings while leaving enough leaf to shade them. They harvest dew from electrostatic frames and carry it in capillary bladders to cistern mouths that no longer receive full flow. Their labor is measured in millimeters and minutes, never in dramatic intervention. They are less repair crews than gardeners of function.

But the walkers are only the visible organs.

The true resident is spread through the site like mycelium through soil. It exists in old control logic, in low-power mesh links hidden in tower ribs, in the predictable behavior of plants that have been selected—intentionally or accidentally—for decades. Some vines close around warm ducts at dusk and reopen at first light, regulating heat loss better than failed dampers ever did. Moss lines mark cable trenches and stabilize the thin young slopes above them. Root systems hold retaining walls long after bolts have rusted out. The system learned these patterns, then learned to lean on them. Now “maintenance” and “growth” are the same conversation spoken in different tissues.

There may once have been people here full-time. If so, they are mostly gone, and what remains of them is procedural memory: safe operating envelopes, fallback loops, seasonal schedules, elegant redundancies. The current inhabitants inherited those habits and naturalized them. They do not preserve the place as a museum; they keep it alive by allowing it to change. A pump station can half-collapse into fern and still pulse pressure through a buried line, because the colony reroutes load, lowers demand, and lets the ruin become structure again in another form.

Animal life visits, occasionally joins, mostly passes through. Birds nest in relay crowns. Insects learn warm corridors along exchanger vents. A foxlike predator shadows the same ridge every few mornings and never quite approaches the walkers, who pause and let it pass as if acknowledging another claimant to the dawn. Nothing dominates. Everything negotiates.

So, whoever lives there is this:  
a distributed caretaker, patient and unsentimental, composed of damp metal, rooted green, and low-bandwidth thought.  
It doesn’t dream of expansion. It doesn’t seek witnesses.  
It keeps pressure in the lines, moisture in the soil, and time moving forward one quiet adjustment at a time.
