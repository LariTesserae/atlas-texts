At first, it seems the chamber’s true inhabitant is **impact** itself.

Not fish, not moss, not any creature you can point at—just force: rain falling, water climbing, thunder folding the air in half. Stand there long enough and you begin to suspect the place is too unfinished to host a life-form larger than a fern.

Then you notice the notches.

Not the old ones, mineral-softened and half-buried, but fresh cuts: clean, shoulder-high, made with intention between storms. You notice a cord stretched where no current should allow it, a wicker funnel reset after being torn loose, a clay weight re-tied with new fiber. Someone is not merely visiting. Someone is **keeping pace** with this place.

What lives there, more than anything else, is a small tradition of people sometimes called storm-keepers, notchers, flood-readers—names vary by valley. They are not settlers in the usual sense. They do not build to stay forever. They build to survive one surge, then the next, then the next. Their architecture is apology and adaptation: things that can be rebuilt in an hour, carried in two trips, abandoned without grief.

Physically, they look mountain-born but water-trained: compact bodies, wide hands, shoulders thick from climbing wet stone. Skin always wind-burnished, nails worn short to the quick, shins crosshatched with old impacts. They move with three points of contact whenever possible, rarely trusting a full step. They test every ledge before weight, every current before crossing, every silence before speaking.

And speech, in that chamber, barely counts as language. So they use knocks through rock, cord-tugs, arm signs, eye contact held a second longer than elsewhere. A line pulled twice means rise. Three sharp strikes means drop. A flat palm to chest means *wait for the wall-water*. Their fluency is less verbal than tidal.

Their lives are built around temporary harvests: finger-fish in lull pools, black shell-things on resonant ledges, algae combed from anchor cracks, rainwater caught where mineral runoff is lowest. They bring almost nothing that cannot survive saturation. Bone hooks, fired-clay floats, braided grass rope, split bamboo pegs. Even their firecraft is mostly for lower camps; up here smoke is occasional, never assured.

They do not claim ownership of the chamber. They speak of it the way sailors speak of straits: as something you can learn but never domesticate. Children are taught the five rules before they are taught routes. Elders are measured by how many storms they can read in their teeth before lightning proves them wrong.

If there is a single figure that belongs to this place most completely, it is the solitary flood-reader on the ledge at storm-rise: one hand on a wet cord, one hand on fresh-cut notches, counting water by touch while the mountain rearranges itself around them. Not conquering. Not praying, exactly. Witnessing with skill.

So what lives there?

Sparse fish, stubborn moss, shell-clamps, lost swallows—yes.  
But the real resident is a human practice shaped into near-animal instinct:  
a people who have learned to inhabit impermanence,  
and to make, in a chamber that never dries, a kind of wet, temporary wisdom.
