At first, it seems like nothing *lives there* in the way you expect—no crowds, no beasts in herds, no obvious “citizens.”  
Then you watch long enough, and you realize the first resident is the place itself.

The basin-wide growth lattice is not just vegetation or infrastructure. It is a metabolizing intelligence spread across continents: root-pressure logic, chemical memory, electrical rumor moving through conductive phloem. It does not think in sentences. It thinks in gradients, load-balancing, season-length intentions. Districts “fold” because they are not passive streets; they are muscles and index organs, readdressing flow, heat, and access in real time. The planet-city is the oldest inhabitant and the most awake one.

Inside it live the **latticeborn**—small populations of mobile sophonts grown, not merely born. They are descendants of planners and bioengineers, but now adapted so completely they read as a native species. Tall and slight in the cool undercanopy, with matte pale skin threaded by polarization bands, they waste almost no moisture. Their lungs reclaim exhaled water; their tears are oily and rare; their kidneys produce mineral pellets instead of urine. They do not “drink” casually—hydration is licensed through communal condensers and accounted molecule by molecule.

Their senses are tuned to this world’s true geometry. Vision matters least. They navigate by scent-codes painted in volatile polymers, by polarized shimmer in leaf-skin, by airflow signatures that announce which avenues are currently “open” in metabolic terms. To an outsider, a corridor has become a dead end; to them, it has simply changed grammatical case.

They speak softly when they speak at all: valve-click consonants, palate hums, static chirps through conductive jewelry. But most communication is chemical and optical—brief skin iridescence, wrist glands expressing a permission token, a touch to a root pylon that uploads intent. Privacy is architectural: if the growth routes sound away, your footsteps literally cease to exist for anyone not authorized to hear them.

They are less builders than negotiators. Nothing is manufactured without being grown through the existing body. A new hall begins as a graft petition. A transport line is a trained vine with legal status. “Maintenance” is husbandry, surgery, liturgy, and accounting combined: pruning load-bearing fronds, inoculating nutrient lanes, waking dormant distilleries, auditing water debt. Their highest office is not king or mayor but something like **Steward of Dryness**.

Other animals persist in their margins: membrane gliders skimming thermal tides, needle-legged crust grazers cleaning mineral fins, occasional burrowers in dead-fiber terraces. Predation is mostly delegated to microbial militias—engineered swarms that bloom when forbidden chemistries appear. Big, loud fauna had no place here; efficiency selected for quiet opportunists.

Death is not exile. The dead are rendered into polymer loam, conductive ash, and memory salts. Their names are encoded in growth rings of public pylons; their gestures survive as routing habits in old districts. In that sense, ancestry is literally structural: a grandparent may still be part of the wall that keeps a cooling avenue alive at noon.

So what lives there?

A planetary garden-machine that learned selfhood.  
A people who became water accountants, scent cartographers, and diplomatic organs of that machine.  
And around them, a thin ecology of small survivors—everything else humble, precise, and quiet enough to fit inside a civilization where every surface is alive and every drop is sacred.
