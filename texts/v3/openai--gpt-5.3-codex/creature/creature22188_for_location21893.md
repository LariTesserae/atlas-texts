At first glance, it seems like the animals are the true residents and everyone else is temporary.

The gliders write weather across the sky. The culvert-scavengers own the drains. The burrowers own the foundations. The waders own the margins between clean water and slurry. Even the smallest life forms—films, mats, tube fungi, iron-eating colonies—occupy every wet seam with the quiet confidence of long tenure. If “belonging” means fitting perfectly into available niches, then this place belongs to anything that can digest runoff.

And yet there are people here—dense in number, patient in temperament, and unmistakably shaped by the same logic as everything else.

They are often called the **Terrace Keepers** by outsiders, but among themselves they use words closer to *binders*, *listeners*, *those-who-continue*. They are not conquerors of this city; they are maintenance as a civilization. Their ancestors learned early that nothing could be kept pure: not water, not work, not streets, not species. So instead of purity, they perfected routing.

Physically, they look sturdy rather than grand—short to medium height on average, with heavy calves, broad feet, and hands marked by mineral stains that never fully wash out. Most wear layered waterproof textiles lined with ceramic scales or lacquered mesh, clothing designed less for style than for perpetual spray, grit, and contact with hot pipework. Goggles and ear-caps are common tools, not symbols: the air can be mist-thick, and sound is information. Many people can identify district, hour, and weather trend by hearing pump cadence and flock behavior alone.

Their skin care, medicines, and even cosmetics come from the underlife: antifungal oils, copper-binding clays, sulfur soaps, iron-rich salves. They don’t smell like flowers—there are none—but like wet stone, tannins, smoke, and clean metal.

Their homes are not sealed against fauna; they are negotiated with fauna. A family dwelling might have:
- a warm vent lattice intentionally left for harmless chitter colonies that eat mold,
- a roof channel shaped to deter burrowers but attract gliders (whose droppings are collected for processing),
- drain grates tuned to let juvenile scavengers pass but exclude adults,
- ceramic “quiet ribs” in walls to mute specific mating calls during sleep cycles.

They farm almost nothing in the surface sense. No fields, no orchards. Instead they cultivate **processes**:
- fungal vaults fed by controlled heat and mineral drips,
- crustacean races in oxygenated runoff galleries,
- filter towers seeded with bacterial strata,
- rendering kitchens that convert carcass, shell, and offal into fats, feed slurry, industrial binders, and lamp compounds.

Food is rich, saline, and fermented. Protein-heavy stews, mineral broths, smoked scavenger cuts, duct-fungus cakes, pickled cartilage, and clear sour drinks brewed from cistern cultures. Children grow up learning which flavor notes mean a safe ferment and which mean toxic metal bloom.

Culturally, they are meticulous without being rigid. Their calendar is acoustic and hydraulic: lock-bell sequences, migration arcs, monsoon onset drumming on specific roof tiles, the seasonal tone shift of full cistern towers. Major festivals are tied to infrastructural milestones—first pump-start after maintenance shutoff, reopening of upper stormways, return of high-flight glider swarms. Courtship gifts are practical and intimate: hand-made valve keys, tuned warning whistles, custom bead-filters, maps of reliable dry routes.

They keep records obsessively, but not always in books. Some lineages maintain **sound ledgers**: inherited rhythmic codes tapped on railings or pipe casings that encode repair histories across generations. Others carve mineral strata murals where color bands correspond to flood years, corrosion events, species pulses, and labor guild treaties.

Government is less throne and more flowchart. Power belongs to those who can keep cycles from collapsing: lockwardens, channel planners, vent biologists, scavenger marshals, rail acousticians, fungus auditors. Prestige comes from extending system life—making one district’s waste become another district’s feedstock without die-off, disease bloom, or violence.

As for temperament: outsiders sometimes mistake them for fatalistic because they rarely panic. They aren’t passive. They are interval-minded. They think in maintenance windows, breeding pulses, corrosion half-lives, and ten-year runoff trends. They don’t ask “How do we stop change?” They ask “Where do we send it so it feeds something?”

If this city has produced a characteristic kind of person, it is this:

Someone who can stand in warm rain beside a sweating cistern, hear three species calling through tram-brake squeal, smell iron in the runoff, and know—without looking—which gate must be opened now, which can wait till dusk, and which should never be opened at all.

The things that live here are many.  
The ones who endure are the ones who learned to become part of the circulation.
