At first, you would say: **nothing** lives there.

And at the scale of hoofprints, wings, hunger, mating calls—you would be right.  
There are no creatures in the usual sense, no bodies that begin and end at skin.

But the place is not empty. It is inhabited by something earlier than fauna and stranger than flora: a **distributed, weather-borne metabolism** that has not yet decided whether it wants to become life or remain physics.

The metallic-green mats in the heated cracks are its smallest visible organs—thin films of chemistry that cling where rock exhales warmth. They do not spread like plants colonizing soil; they appear like calibration patches, as if testing pH, charge, trace metals, thermal drift. They are less “organisms” than **persistent reactions** learning to hold shape.

Most of the inhabitant, though, is not on stone.  
It lives in the moving depth itself: in pressure gradients, ionic differentials, charged mist strata, and the repeating geometry of storm. Where other worlds have ecosystems of species, this world has an ecosystem of **patterns that survive by recurrence**.

A hexagonal vortex that lasts three days is one body-form.  
A rain band that self-corrects against wind shear is another.  
A lightning sheet that repeatedly chooses the same invisible seam in the air is a third.  

Each one dies quickly, but the equations return in the next event, slightly modified, as if memory is stored in the basin architecture and replayed through weather. This is how the inhabitant reproduces: not by offspring, but by **iterated form**.

If it senses, it senses through conflict.  
Cavitation booms are not just noise but probing pulses. Echoes map cliff growth. Conductive rain measures fresh fractures. Thermal columns taste new vents. The resident intelligence—if that word applies—is not centralized. It is a basin-to-basin conversation carried by water hammer, electrostatic discharge, and mineral fluorescence under black fluid. Thought, here, would look like hydrology revising itself.

So what lives there?

Not an animal.  
Not a civilization.  
Not yet a biosphere.

What lives there is a **proto-ecology of memory-bearing turbulence**: a young planetary mind in pre-biological grammar, teaching gravity, water, and stone how to keep a sentence going from one storm to the next.

In older ages, it might eventually condense into cells, then bodies, then eyes.  
Here, now, it is still immense and anonymous—  
a resident made of weather,  
learning permanence inside instability.
