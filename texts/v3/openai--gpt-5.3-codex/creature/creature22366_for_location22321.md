What lives there is not a species so much as a **system with bodies**.

At first, you can name them as separate creatures:

- the coin-sized runners with overlapping flank plates, always in lanes, always touching antennae as they pass;
- the wheel-backed grazers that move in low, synchronized files, scraping invisible films from stone and cable sheath;
- the seam-hunters, triangular and patient, folded into ceiling joints until motion below triggers the drop;
- the dust-filterers, nearly weightless, pulsing radial cilia in the air like breathing punctuation marks;
- the burrow churners, blunt-headed and tireless, lifting old flakes of metal and sulfate from the flour floor.

But if you stay and watch enough cycles, the categories blur.  
Their traffic aligns to the same thuds.  
Their breeding surges around the same warm leaks.  
Their silences happen all at once.

The thing that lives there is a **dry supercommunity**, a metronomic civilization of arthroid scavengers that has inherited an abandoned machine rhythm and made it biological.

---

Its true body is distributed across the basin.

The “nerves” are vibration routes: ladder feet, duct brackets, cable posts, cracked housings—anything that transmits impact. Populations read those impacts through tarsal hairs and shell resonance. A piston pulse from deep rock is not just noise; it is a timing signal. On one beat, burrowers lock still to avoid collapse. On the next, hunters redeploy to where panicked prey will break lane. A few seconds later, filterers bloom upward into newly shaken dust strata. Predators, grazers, recyclers—different appetites, one clock.

Its “bloodstream” is particulate: mineral flour, rust fines, exuviae powder, crushed shell, protein crumbs, trace oils bled from warm relics. Nothing stays where it falls. Every death is disassembled into fractions and routed by specialists. There are lime-takers, metal-pickers, keratin raspers, grease lickers. Even molts are folded and carried like sheet goods. In this place, decomposition is not rot. It is logistics.

Its “organs” are thermal and chemical islands around old infrastructure:
a transformer stump serving as brood kiln,
an inert duct underside serving as ambush corridor,
the shadow lee of a conveyor throat serving as nursery trench,
the base of dead cable posts serving as molting fields where abrasion is just right.
No one built these for life, but life mapped itself onto them with near-engineering precision.

---

Individual animals are hard, matte, and economical.

Most are pale bone, soot gray, or tarnished-brass colored: not camouflage for plants or soil, but for mineral dust and oxidized metal. Cuticles are wax-thick and microtextured to hold an electrostatic film that traps charged nutrients. Mouthparts are modular—scrape, pierce, wick, crush—because diet fluctuates minute to minute. Their waste is dry pellet or powder; their water budget is so strict that some species reclaim moisture from exhaled air through folded spiracle baffles before releasing it.

Reproduction is rapid but staged to machinery. Eggs are often mineral-coated and laid in vibration-stable seams. Hatching coincides with predictable warm intervals, when heat leaks rise just enough to accelerate development. Juveniles enter traffic almost immediately, joining outer lanes where predation is high but information is dense. Survivors molt into inner guild roles: grazer, lifter, hunter, carrier, cleaner.

Communication is mostly percussion and chemistry.  
Antenna taps encode identity and condition.  
Shell clicks mark lane rights and mating status.  
Substrate drumming can trigger whole-district responses—freeze, scatter, lift, converge.  
The loud “rainstorm” chatter is often not panic but quorum behavior: many bodies striking many surfaces to map resonance changes after a machine cycle.

---

So, whoever lives there?

A million small, dry, jointed lives—yes.  
But also one larger resident made out of all of them at once:

an **evolved maintenance swarm**, born from scarcity, trained by ancient pulses, feeding on rust, heat, and each other, endlessly rethreading itself through a chamber whose stone has stopped changing.

The factory did not keep running.  
Its leftover rhythms did.  
And those rhythms grew shells.
