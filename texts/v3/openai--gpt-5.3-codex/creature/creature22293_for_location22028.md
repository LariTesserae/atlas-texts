What lives there is not one thing, at least not at first.

At first you meet **guilds**—species shaped less like animals in a landscape and more like components in a live process.

On the knife-bright rock there are **shear colonies**, skin-thin films of black-green cells that eat trace metals and slowly crawl as a sheet. They move so slowly they masquerade as stain, until you return days later and find a whole cliff-face pattern shifted a few handspans upslope. They are the first occupants of fresh fracture.

In wet corridors, **capillary towers**—those vine-lichen structures—are inhabited by swarms of pin-sized symbionts that run inside the tissue like blood cells in a vascular plant. They clear blockages, reroute minerals, seed fungal nodes. The towers are not passive flora; they are vertical rivers, and these tiny workers are the current’s keepers.

Where magnetic seams run true, the metallic groves host **wirebark climbers**: six-limbed, flat-bodied browsers with keratin-iron plates and soft gripping pads. They move in predictable loops, crop conductive leaves, then excrete mineral-rich slurry in regular bands downslope. Their loops are so consistent that whole hillsides “cycle” from bare to glossy to overgrown with clocklike regularity.

Following them, always just offset in time, are **seam wolves**—not loud, not fast in straight lines, but exquisitely precise at boundaries. They test local gravity with whisker-fans before each leap. In side-slope zones where gravity has rotated, they run “across” vertical faces as if the cliff were level ground. Their jaws close with almost no sound. Most predation here is a correction, not a spectacle.

In the mist belts live **charged insects**, ribbon-winged and glass-veined, that rise at dusk when ridge combs begin to hum. They do not merely swarm: they phase. Whole clouds of them align to electrostatic gradients and pulse as one body, fertilizing bloom fields, triggering spore release, feeding kites, then vanishing into cracks before dawn shutdown. They are pollinators, prey, and spark plugs.

Above and between, in thermal lanes, drift **float jellies**—translucent sacs with ciliated combs and iron-dust bladders. In normal air they are nearly inert. In ionized storm air they wake, filter aerosols, and spawn in minutes. Some are caught in time-thick hollows and persist as hovering lanterns for seasons, slowly metabolizing while whole weather systems pass.

In hot talus live **burrow forges**, wormlike colonies armored in ceramic scales. They digest sulfides and leave sintered tunnels, venting nutrient fog through rhythmic exhalation chimneys. Many other species time migration to these vents. What looks like random steam is actually scheduled release.

Then there are the organisms built for law-break country—the places where gravity tilts or time pools.

These are the **modulars**: bodies with redundant hearts, reversible gut chemistry, eyes that can switch from optical to electroreceptive mapping, vestibular organs that “ping” local constants before committing movement. A modular herbivore approaching a sideways-gravity band will stop, tremble, and run internal diagnostics the way another animal might sniff the wind. Some species even bud temporary organs—extra anchor limbs, pressure sacs, insulating sheaths—then reabsorb them after crossing.

Near time-accelerated fog hollows live short-lived **flash vines** and their specialist feeders, the **hour moths**, whose entire visible lives can occur between one sunset and one midnight startup. Along nearby time-still ridges live ambush predators with almost pathological patience, waiting at temporal edges where fast life and slow life collide.

So: many inhabitants. But if you watch long enough, another answer appears.

The true resident may be the **basin network itself**—a distributed, living system made from conductive forests, copper-precipitating fungal reefs, bacterial etch films, migrating grazers, decomposer vents, and storm charge cycles. Nutrients move through it like inventory. Spores move like messages. Electrical discharge and bioluminescent pulses behave like signaling. Feedback loops self-correct after disturbance. Storage, release, restart.

It has no architect and no center, but it has memory: mineral grooves deepened by repeated flow, migration loops inherited by herd and predator alike, electrochemical pathways reinforced season after season. It learns by persistence.

If you insist on “who lives there,” the smallest honest answer is:

- plated loop-grazers, seam predators, mist pollinators, floaters, burrowers, climbers, and slow colonial lithovores;
- countless opportunists tuned to pulses of wet, charge, and law;
- and over all of them, through all of them, a young planetary metabolism that behaves uncannily like industry.

A wilderness full of organisms, yes.  
But also a single vast organism made of wilderness.
