At first glance you’d say the basin is inhabited by crews, trees, and equipment.  
Then you stay long enough to notice those aren’t separate categories.

What lives there is a **composite organism**—part workforce, part plantation, part weather trap, part machine rhythm. If it has a name, it’s something like **the Throughput**: a resident intelligence made of schedules, roots, condensation, diesel heat, fungal bloom, and people who have learned to move inside permanent noise.

It does not have a single body.  
Its body is distributed across benches, ducts, lamp poles, cut slopes, timber stacks, and bunk modules. Its skin is moss on retaining walls, frost on cable trays, bark dust pasted into mud. Its breath is steam from canteen vents and dryer exhaust. Its pulse is the repeating cycle of loaders, conveyors, shunters, sirens, and shift horns. Its memory lives in mapped drainage channels, in replanted rows, in tagged logs, in the way everyone points before speaking because mouths are useless in that volume.

It has many small organs:

- **Spruce and larch rows** as muscle fiber—grown, cut, regrown, tightening and releasing in contract-length contractions.  
- **Ditches and seep-lines** as capillaries—never full, never dry, carrying just enough cold water to keep tissue alive.  
- **Floodlamps** as low, artificial stars—retinas for a place where daylight is weak and weather sits down and refuses to move on.  
- **Fans and dryers** as lungs—always forcing air, always turning wet matter into transportable mass.  
- **Fungi and lichens** as gut flora—quietly digesting what the loud parts shed, returning spoil and bark waste toward soil.

The humans here are not parasites and not masters; they are a **mobile nervous system**.  
They travel in insulated shells, signal with gloved hands, read pressure gauges and frost lines the way fish read current. Their hearing is protected because the basin is too loud for naked ears, but their bodies still synchronize to it—heart rate settling to machinery cadence, sleep haunted by conveyor rhythm. When they leave, that rhythm leaves with them for days, like phantom surf in the bones. They are the only component that can improvise, and so they are where the organism thinks.

It feeds on latency and rotation:  
planting windows, thinning windows, harvest windows, rail availability, thaw depth, fuel deliveries, and the short summer where everything must happen now. It excretes chips, bundled logs, paperwork, heat, and eventually new ground. It grows slowly despite the violence of its sound. A millimeter crack in a shale face, an inch of spoil settlement, another dark mat of liverwort on north concrete—these are growth rings too.

If it fears anything, it is not cold.  
It fears interruption: long outage, broken rail link, drainage failure, fire in dry wind, contract collapse. Silence is not peace to this being; silence is fever, coma, uncertainty. During shutdowns it lies stunned, and every small natural sound becomes unnervingly loud—as if the basin has briefly remembered it was once only a hole in cold land.

So: what lives there?

Not a beast you can point at.  
A **young industrial-ecological lifeform** still assembling itself, still undecided whether it is a forest with machinery in it or a machine that learned to photosynthesize. It is unfinished, loud, damp, and stubborn. And it is very much alive.
