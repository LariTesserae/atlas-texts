What lives there is less a cast of species than a **circulating workforce of bodies**.

At first glance you’d think the plateau is empty. Then you watch long enough, and the emptiness resolves into timing.

The dominant grazers are huge, low-backed animals with blunt skulls and broad, rasping mouths—more like living scrapers than mouths built for grass. They travel in patient files and fan-shaped fronts, shaving mineral-rich films from stone, licking damp seams, grinding lichen-black crust down to pale substrate. Their teeth aren’t really teeth: laminated plates that self-fracture and regrow, always leaving a fresh abrasive edge. Their hides carry frost well; their joints are slow and economical. They can go days with almost no open water, pulling moisture from cold rock and their own gut chemistry.

Inside them is half the ecosystem. Their symbionts bind trace metals, crack stubborn compounds, and repackage nutrients into dense pellets dropped in astonishing volume. Those pellets are currency. Whole food webs key to them.

Behind the scraper herds come the **pickers**: knee-high, many-snouted opportunists, quick-footed and social, sorting pellets by age and mineral profile. Then the **sifters**—insect-analogs and soft-jawed micrograzers—reduce what’s left to dust and slurry. Where a herd camps for one night, a nutrient patch can pulse for a season.

Underground, the plains are held up by **trench-diggers**: blind, broad-shouldered burrowers that work in repeated lanes, almost gridlike. They don’t just make dens; they till cold ground, vent trapped gases, open capillary paths for seepwater, and keep thin soils alive enough for the tough cushion-plants. Seen from above after thaw, their patterning looks intentional—blocks, seams, returns.

The tall silhouettes you notice on distant benches are **seam-browsers**: thin-legged, narrow-bodied migrators that feed on wind-pruned shrubs and salt-edged basin growth. They move in lines so long they seem geological, stitching ridge to ridge. They rarely run. Predators here don’t chase much; they calculate.

Above all this wheel layered scavenger guilds—dark-winged, high-endurance gliders that read pressure and scent better than sight. They arrive after weather shifts almost to the hour: post-fog, post-thaw, post-stormfall. Nothing stays unprocessed for long. Carcass to bone to powder is a short sequence, clean and efficient.

Communication is mostly **felt**. Many large animals pulse through chest sacs and jaw structures, sending low-frequency knocks through ground and air. Herds reorient without obvious signals. Burrow colonies freeze or flood tunnels in synchrony. Even scavenger descents seem triggered by vibrations below hearing.

In warm exhalation valleys, dawn queues form: herbivores, then omnivores, then predators waiting at the edges, all sharing geothermal rims where salts and thawwater concentrate. In deep cold, translucent under-ice colonies bloom—part animal, part consortium—drawing on trapped minerals and faint light, feeding winter specialists that can scrape and ingest living gel from beneath clear crust.

So the true resident of this place is **throughput**:  
scrape → ferment → pelletize → sift → burrow → migrate → die → render → return.

No monuments, no architecture, no tool marks—yet everything behaves like an old, perfected machine made of hunger, weather, and patience.
