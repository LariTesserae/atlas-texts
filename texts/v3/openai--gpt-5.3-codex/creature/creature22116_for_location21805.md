What lives there is not a creature you can point to.  
It is a **guild**—a thin republic of near-invisible lives that have learned to exist inside rule.

The most obvious resident is the dark film in the seams: a composite skin of halophilic microbes, mineral-tolerant algae, and slow fungi that do not so much grow as **accrete**. They feed on what the chamber gives in trace amounts—airborne nitrates, carbon from dust, salts that would poison wetter ecologies. They wake not with rain, but with chemistry: when humidity lifts even slightly, crystals soften at their edges, ions move, metabolism stirs, and the film performs a brief, quiet harvest before returning to near-stasis.

Interlaced with that skin are the wiry plants—if “plants” is still the right word. They are mostly scaffold: root-fiber forced into mortar pores, a few reduced photosynthetic threads armored in wax and salt. Their leaves are vestigial, their stems metallic with mineral uptake. They do not aim to be lush. They aim to remain. In this place, survival is architecture: brace, anchor, resist abrasion, hold shape under constant air.

Deeper still, in the stone itself, are endolithic communities: bacteria and fungi living a few millimeters under the surface where light is diluted but not absent, where wind cannot scour directly. They etch microscopic chambers, trade metabolites, and slowly alter the masonry grain by grain. Not enough to ruin it, just enough to join it. Over long spans they become part of the chamber’s weathering logic—the living fraction of its patina.

And there is one more inhabitant, if you allow it: the **drone**.  
Not alive in a biological sense, but ecologically real. The fixed chord is the dominant presence all organisms must couple to. It sets selection pressure like climate does elsewhere. Anything that persists here must tolerate saturation-level vibration and permanent acoustic masking. So the life that remains is life with no need to call, court, hunt, or flee. No signal economy. No performance. Only maintenance.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a community of crust-makers, seam-dwellers, pore-rooters, and stone-borers—life pared down to chemistry, adhesion, and endurance—plus the continuous breath of the structure itself. In that chamber, to live is to keep a boundary intact while being slowly mineralized by the world that sustains you.
