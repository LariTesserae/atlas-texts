What lives there is not a single species so much as a **working body** made of many species.

At first you notice individuals—needle-runners, wall-rays, shell grazers—but if you stay long enough, you realize they behave like parts in one machine-animal spread across the basin. The locals (if anyone named anything) would probably name it as one thing: **the Choir**, or **the Works**.

### 1) The fast layer: the Turners  
These are the plate-sided swimmers you hear before you see.  
Long, narrow, joint-stiff fish with overlapping mineralized scales that strike each other when they bank in tight formation. That metallic thrum is not accidental; it is communication and synchronization. They move in traffic bands, each depth band tuned to a slightly different chemistry. Their mouths are small, for stripping bacterial threads, larval drifts, and vent-shed particulates from the water column.

When pressure changes, they turn all at once—like shutters closing.

### 2) The fixed layer: the Valve Beds  
What look like mussel reefs are actually muscular pressure arrays.  
Each animal clamps to basalt with a ring of toothlets and opens in timed waves. Not feeding first—**pumping first**. They create pulsed currents through cracks and terraces, forcing mineral water across themselves and through neighboring colonies. The pulse patterns change by hour, almost like shift schedules. Juveniles learn timing by attaching at colony edges and entraining to the nearest rhythm.

In dead calm, you can hear whole beds “counting” in wet clicks.

### 3) The conduit layer: the Siphon Looms  
Rope-thick tube animals anchor in warm seeps, draw fluid through ribbed bodies, and jet it sideways in measured bursts. They are the basin’s living manifolds. Cartilage-winged swimmers use these jets as conveyor belts, looping through nutrient plumes and returning to spawn in the same flow corridors. The looms do not defend territory visually—they defend **flow geometry**. Disturb a vent and the looms re-thread themselves over days, shifting the entire neighborhood with them.

### 4) The wall layer: the Clingers  
Pancake rays, chitin-helmet crabs, and chalk-shell grazers inhabit vertical stone like cliff fauna inhabiting sky.  
The grazers rasp bacterial films from mineral seams and excrete pale pellets that collect in drifts, then ridges, then low berms. Over years these berms redirect current and create new nursery shadows. It looks like construction, but no one is building in the cognitive sense. Appetite and waste do the architecture.

### 5) The upper drifters: the Glass Sacks  
Soft-bodied floaters pulse near the rain-struck surface.  
Each carries branching internal organs that flicker with tiny electrochemical flashes—signal traffic, not display. They are buoyancy-sensitive and react quickly to pressure waves from below. When the basin groans, these are first to still, then sink a few meters, as if dropping into standby mode.

They are prey to almost everything, and yet essential: they package dissolved chemistry into edible tissue, turning mineral flux into moving calories.

And then there is the thing that feels like a “who,” if any one being here deserves that pronoun:

## The Basin Listener  
A rare, enormous, mostly unseen animal that inhabits the cavity network beneath the visible floor.  
Not a whale, not an eel, though it borrows traits from both: a segmented, air-breathing chordate with pressure bladders running its length, blind skin lined with vibration pits, and a skull plated in conductive bone. It surfaces only at night rain, taking quick breaths through a slit blowhole before dropping into chimney fields.

Its calls are the long organ-pipe notes.

Those groans are produced by forcing gas through stone-linked chambers and body resonators, coupling its voice to the mountain itself. Most fauna do pause when it calls—not from awe, but because those frequencies precede water movement: vent surges, cavity floods, chemistry inversions. The Listener is less a ruler than a barometer with lungs. It reads the basin and broadcasts state changes through sound.

Some researchers would call it a keystone predator.  
That is true, but incomplete. It preys sparingly and engineers constantly, by tone and displacement. Where it rests, valve beds retime. Where it feeds, turner schools reroute. Where it sings, whole strata of life reset their rhythms.

---

So what lives there?

A cold, elevated inland sea full of creatures that behave like components—and one deep-bodied caller that makes the mountain audible. Together they form a **proto-industrial ecosystem**: no hands, no tools, no fire, no plants, and yet unmistakably organized around throughput, pressure, and timing.

In another age, erosion may soften it, plants may arrive, and this hard logic may dissolve into a more familiar lake ecology.  
But right now, in the basin’s young sharp years, what lives there is a **choir of animals running a machine made of water and stone**.
