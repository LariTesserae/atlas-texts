At first you think people here would have to be hard, fast, metallic—something like the city itself.

They are hard, yes. But not fast.

What lives on that plateau has learned the same lesson as its kilns and distillation towers: endurance beats urgency.

---

They are called different things in different districts—**condensers**, **ledgefolk**, **the warm-born**, sometimes simply **workers**—but they are not a single type so much as a family of adaptations.

Most are human-descended, though “human” here has stretched.

Their clothing is architecture in miniature: layered wraps of ceramic fiber, mirrored veils, articulated shoulder shades, gloves with heat-sensing pads in the fingertips. Many wear narrow back-rigs that look decorative until dawn, when fins unfold and drink water out of air. You can tell what district someone comes from by their rig geometry: spiral fins from the eastern galleries, comb-fins from the rim, flat lamellar blades from high bridge communities where wind is constant.

Skin is usually kept covered, but in workshops and shade markets you see glimpses—mineral dust worked into pores, old heat scars silvered smooth, tattoos made from conductive inks that map old pipeline routes, family claims, shadow-right boundaries. Eyes are often shielded by polarized lenses; those without lenses develop a permanent squint, as if always sighting along a bright seam.

They move with the city’s tempo. Not laziness—calibration.  
No one sprints unless steam is venting wrong.

---

Their society is organized around three rights:

1. **Condense-right** (who may harvest moisture from which surfaces),
2. **Shade-right** (who may occupy shadow bands at given hours),
3. **Waste-heat-right** (who can tap thermal bleed for drying, cooking, incubation, industry).

Property lines are less about walls and more about angle, timing, and airflow. A bridge overhang at 09:10 is worth less than the same overhang at 13:40. Contracts include sun tables. Disputes are settled with survey data and witness marks etched into steel stanchions.

Children learn geometry before they learn letters.  
Not abstract geometry—living geometry: where dew forms, where steam lingers, where a handrail is safe at dusk and skin-stripping at noon.

---

They eat what can be made from storage-stable imports, deep-vat cultures, and the city’s own strange ecologies. The mineral biofilms in wet channels are cultivated in patterned trays; some strains are edible after processing, others yield solvents or medicinals. The chalk-lace duct colonies are harvested for salts and trace nutrients. Nothing is wasted. Even exhaled humidity in dormitories is routed through capture meshes.

They keep animals, but only those that can share the thermal bargain. The translucent-wing gliders are half vermin, half companions, tagged and tolerated in turbine neighborhoods because they eat duct pests. Blind six-limbed scavengers are trapped, bred, and released in cycles to keep drains clear; some households maintain prized lines with unusual salt-sorting behavior.

And the larger rooftop thing at dusk?  
The locals do not agree whether it is one species, several, or mostly rumor. They leave it alone. That is a kind of agreement too.

---

Culturally, they distrust clean replacement. A new structure that erases an old one is considered brittle thinking. Better to bolt on, sleeve over, thicken. Their histories are literal layers; ancestry can be climbed.

They mark age the way the city does: by edge-rounding.  
An elder might run a thumb along a beam corner and say, “Late Third Expansion,” then tap their own front tooth—worn to match—and smile.

Speech is low and economical because echoes travel. In open streets, conversations are braided with hand signs designed for glare and distance. In machine halls, people talk by touch on rails: coded taps through gloves, a percussion language older than some districts.

Rituals are brief and practical. Dawn condensation is greeted, not worshipped, but greeted. First rains are collected in communal pans and tasted publicly for contaminants and luck. Funerary customs vary, though many choose incorporation: ash fused into ceramic tiles, set into walkways where future feet will polish them.

---

What lives there, finally, is not just a population.

It is a **maintenance civilization**—a people who made a culture out of thermal gradients, patient machinery, and unfinished ground. They are builders of beginnings that do not hurry toward endings.

In another environment they might have become conquerors or nomads or gardeners.

Here, above the curved horizon on young black stone, they became custodians of process: readers of heat, brokers of shade, keepers of brief water, citizens of a city still pouring itself.
