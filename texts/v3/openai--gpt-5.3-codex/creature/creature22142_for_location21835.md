At first, it seems like almost nothing lives there except maintenance schedules.

Then you notice movement in the seams.

Not crowds—never crowds. One person crossing a narrow raised lane with a coil of hose over one shoulder. Two more on a grated catwalk, stopping every few meters to cut back creeper from a valve wheel. Someone in a rain-stained visor crouched by a culvert mouth, one hand in black water, feeling flow rather than looking at it. They move like people who grew up measuring space in clearances: shoulder-width between conduit and root mass, boot-length above flood line, minutes between pump cycles.

They are the residents: not quite factory workers, not quite gardeners, not quite utility crews, but all three at once.  
Flow-keepers. Root-techs. Seam folk.

Their clothes are simple and practical—quick-dry layers, rubberized cuffs, mesh gloves, old ceramic-bladed shears hanging beside digital pressure meters. They wear corrosion like a trade mark: greened copper clasps, orange-bloomed tool cases, polished spots where wet hands always grip. Most carry chalk, grease pencil, and twine. They still mark things physically even with sensors everywhere. A wall gets a date. A trunk gets an arrow showing lean. A pipe gets a line where water stood last storm.

They do not talk about “fighting” nature. That would sound childish here.  
They talk about timing.

Cut this vine now, not next week, because if it thickens one more ring it will crush the mesh.  
Leave that root because it is holding the old retaining face.  
Open this gate slowly because sudden pressure wakes silt upstream.  
Never seal a wall completely; give moisture somewhere honest to go.

Their homes are tucked in upper shells of old buildings, above the long memory of flood marks. Inside: fans, hanging racks, sealed bins, moss-scrub filters, spare gaskets drying over warm ductwork. Windows are often open behind screens because “inside” and “outside” are gradients here, not opposites. At dusk, they cook over induction plates while listening to pump harmonics the way other places listen to traffic.

Children learn the district by sound before map:  
the hollow note of a blocked storm run,  
the high whine of an overworked bearing,  
the soft slap that means water level is rising in a side channel.  
They can tell where they are by humidity and echo.

And yet, if you keep watching, you realize the truest resident is larger than its people.

What lives there is also a system-body made of roots, concrete, bacteria, pipe, fungus, steel, algae film, and habit. The workers maintain it, but it also trains them. It changes their routes, their slang, their posture, their sense of patience. It rewards attention and punishes neatness-for-neatness’s-sake. It remembers neglect in corrosion and remembers care in stable flow.

So the inhabitant is not a single creature standing apart from the place.  
It is a partnership that has had time to become anatomical.

Humans in wet boots.  
Plants in girders.  
Machines in permanent breath.  
Microbial films doing invisible labor in every channel.

Together, they are what lives there: a quiet, competent civilization of upkeep, grown into a humid basin where nothing is ever finished and nothing is ever fully abandoned.
