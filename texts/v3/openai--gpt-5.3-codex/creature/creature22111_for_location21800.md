What lives there is less a species than an agreement between stone, wind, and patient hands.

At first, the people seem absent. You see movement in shade-screens, a lift rising, dust drifting off a terrace, and assume machinery. Then a figure leans from a blue shadow and flashes three fingers and a wrist-turn to someone across the hollow; an answer comes back in mirrored mica. They are there everywhere, just taught by their city to occupy edges rather than centers.

They are human-shaped, but drought-written: narrow-bodied, long-ribbed, tendon-strong rather than heavy. Their skin is usually coated in a thin matte resin that keeps alkaline dust from cracking it; in bright light it gives them a soft mineral sheen, like fired clay just before glaze. Most wear layered veils and brow-brims not for modesty but optics—the basin’s glare can blind you faster than hunger. Their eyes are often deep-set and pale-amber or smoke-dark, adapted to contrast more than color. Their hearing is extraordinary, but not delicate: thickened outer ears, scarred from a lifetime of sound. Children are taught to map the basin by rebound—one tap, one clap, one thrown pebble, and they can tell you which wall face is new-plastered and which is salt-rotten.

Speech exists, but public language is mostly manual and percussive. Close talk is mouth-to-ear, private and intimate. Distance talk is drum phrase, banner angle, mirror flash, rope-tug code, and signed shorthand. A family can argue across three levels without raising a voice; a district can warn the whole city in thirty seconds if a vent changes tone. In that place, literacy starts in rhythm before symbols.

Their food web is built around the dry-garden organisms more than animals. They cultivate vapor-reeds and wax paddles the way river cultures cultivate grain: pruning for gas flow, scoring bark to bleed oils, harvesting crystal nodules at dawn before they crack. They ferment resin-cake into sour loaves, press bitter aromatic pastes, candy mineral sugars into hard travel tablets. Meat is uncommon and ceremonial—usually from the six-legged grazers, and only when herd lines are secure. Water, when it appears, is handled by designated keepers wearing absorbent wrist wraps so no drop is wasted or spilled into soil that cannot tolerate it.

They keep animals, but as companions in process rather than abundance. Glider-bats are encouraged in upper ducts because they carry dust-pollen where hands can’t reach. Crack-skitterers are tolerated in certain walls because they consume molds that would otherwise soften plaster. Every creature has a ledger mark, a whistle-call, a known lineage. Extinction here would not be tragedy alone; it would be structural failure.

Culturally, they are maintenance-minded to the point of philosophy. Nothing is “finished,” only “in balance today.” Bridges are retensioned like musical strings. Salt creep is chipped as a communal rite. Neighborhoods move when vents migrate, and nobody calls it loss; they call it following heat. Their art reflects this: knotwork that changes tension with humidity, chimes tuned to seasonal winds, murals made from mica flakes that only resolve at noon.

If they have a defining trait, it is disciplined abundance. They do not worship scarcity, and they do not chase excess. They cultivate volume—of canopy, of sound, of social ties—without depending on wetness, softness, or permanence. In another world they might be called austere; here they are undeniably lush.

And beneath them, in the porous warm stone, the oldest resident keeps breathing: the vent-fed garden itself, half plant and half chemistry, making food from breath and metal mist. The people are its herders, engineers, pollinators, and students. The city survives because neither one pretends to be independent of the other.
