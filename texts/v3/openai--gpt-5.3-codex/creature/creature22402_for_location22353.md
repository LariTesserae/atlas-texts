At first glance, it seems like no one lives there in the way we usually mean it.  
No smoke columns. No roads. No shouting markets. Just water, light, schedules.

Then, if you stay long enough, the pattern shifts and a resident appears—not as a city, but as a **custodianship**.

They are sometimes called (by outsiders who only half-understand) the **Basin Tenders**. In close light they look almost like wading primates shaped by wetlands and patience: long shins, wide splayed feet, narrow shoulders, and hands with soft webbing that can spread across stone like a second pair of eyelids. Their skin is not bright; it is deliberately matte, a moss-gray or pale umber that refuses glare. Their eyes are mirror-clear like the marsh browsers’, with a second transparent lid for spray and mineral dust. They move quietly enough that even the ribbonfish don’t panic when a Tender crosses a channel.

They do not live *on* the basin so much as in its seams—under mineral shelves, in old cut-stone ducts, behind fern walls where condensation gathers in constant threads. Their dwellings are cool, low, and mostly open to airflow. Nothing is built to dominate the horizon. They avoid straight lines unless those lines already exist in ancient work.

The tones you hear rising at odd hours—pure, bell-like, source unclear—are often theirs. Not songs exactly. More like calibrations. A Tender can shape a tone in throat and chest that resonates with the black spires and half-sunken rings; sometimes a single note can coax fog to hold lower, or nudge a flow split by a few degrees, enough to feed one root-cluster and not another. They say water listens better to invitation than command.

Their society is organized by **catchment**, not blood. You belong to a drip-line, a shelf-face, a dusk corridor where predator and prey exchange hours. Children are taught how to read wetness gradients before they are taught names. Adulthood is marked when you can predict where a burrow mound will collapse by evening and be right three days in a row.

They hunt, but as accountants hunt: subtracting without breaking the sum. They farm, but mostly by steering conditions—trace metals, shade timing, condensation nets grown from living fiber. They repair the old machines only up to their inherited threshold. A cracked arch is kept cracked if the crack has become habitat. A ring is cleaned only enough to breathe. Their ethic is not restoration; it is continuity with scars intact.

And over generations, something stranger has happened: the basin’s functioning systems, its tuned ecologies, and these people have braided into one distributed organism. No single Tender controls it. No single shrine governs it. But together they produce the quiet law you noticed:

- water guided, never forced  
- abundance spaced  
- predation buffered  
- age preserved, not erased

So what lives there?

Not just a species.  
A **practice** that became a people, and then a place that learned to keep itself alive through them.  
If you ask a Tender directly, they may answer with a small shrug and point to a dripping rock basin:  
“*That* lives here. We are how it remembers.”
