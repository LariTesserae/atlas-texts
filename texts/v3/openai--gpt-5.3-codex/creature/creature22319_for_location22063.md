What lives there is not one thing, at least not in the way surface places mean “one thing.”

At first you notice the **people**: small crews in damp aprons and heat-shined boots, moving slowly enough to look sleepy, precisely enough to reveal they are never off-task. Their skin carries a permanent gloss from the air. Their sleeves are patched with mesh pockets full of clips, seed plugs, valve keys, chalk, grafting tape. They speak in short phrases and hand signs because the district hears touch better than sound. They do not say “morning” or “evening”; they say “after first drip,” “before pipe lull,” “during green hour.”

They call themselves, in one dialect, **the Keepers of the Warm Hollow**. In another, just **Roothands**.

But the longer you stay, the less they seem like residents and the more they seem like one organ in a larger body.

---

The second thing that lives there is the **cultivar mass**—not “plants” in the decorative sense, but a domesticated, semi-wild infrastructure flora bred for heat, low light, mineral runoff, and vibration-rich air. Vines with wax-black stems grip steel as if born to it. Leaf mats knit over brick and insulation, holding moisture exactly where a machine wants to overheat. Fruiting runners trail along coolant loops. Pale nodules at root joints filter dissolved metals out of seepage and leave behind usable salts. Nothing is purely ornamental; even beauty is load-bearing.

Some species are trained to machinery rhythms. A trellis colony near the compressor bank opens its stomata in sync with piston intervals, drinking humidity when pressure drops. Another closes hard when belt speed rises, to avoid oil mist. One broad-leaf strain has silver undersides that flash as a warning in lampglow when local heat spikes above safe tolerance for cable jackets. The people read those flashes faster than any instrument panel.

---

The third thing—the oldest ruler, though smallest—is the **film**.

Microbial blooms lacquer almost every wet surface: rust-reds, gunmetal greens, bruised blues, clear gels that look like nothing until they catch light. These are not pests to be scrubbed away wholesale; they are curated populations, fed and shaved and reseeded. They turn waste brine into substrate, substrate into nutrient slurry, slurry into root feed. They soften sharp chemistries, digest residues, and quietly decide what can live where. If the film shifts, everything else follows: vines yellow, pipes pit, shrimp disappear, lungs sting.

The Keepers talk about the films as weather and temperament. “North wall is hungry.” “Tile stack is sleeping.” “Cistern mouth has gone bitter.” They listen with swabs and smell before they test with strips.

---

So what lives there, truly, is a **braided organism** made of human routine, engineered botany, and microbial metabolism, all wrapped around patient machinery. It has no single face. It has habits.

It hates abruptness.  
It rewards maintenance done a little early.  
It punishes neglect slowly, then all at once.

Children there learn to count by drips before numbers. Elders can tell pump strain by the way geckos leave a wall. Courtship gifts are not flowers but cuttings that took in difficult corners. Grief is marked by re-routing a line in someone’s name so their path still carries water. Celebration is a successful harvest from a former failure zone: “This used to be leak and rust; now eat.”

When strangers ask who is in charge, answers vary: the shift fore, the oldest gardener, the compressor schedule, the mold, the heat. All are correct, none complete.

If you insist on a single resident, then this:

A **slow intelligence of upkeep** lives there—distributed across hands, leaves, valves, and biofilm—keeping the hollow in a state between breakdown and bloom. It does not dream of finishing. It dreams of balance at the next cycle, and the next, and the next.
