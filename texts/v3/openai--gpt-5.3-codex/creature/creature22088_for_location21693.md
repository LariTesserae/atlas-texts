At first, it seems like nothing should live there except machines.

Then you watch long enough, and the world resolves into residents built for **persistence**.

### The first citizens: films, veils, and mats

What truly “owns” this ocean is a skin-thin biology spread over stone and brine interfaces:

- iron-eating bacterial varnishes that plate basalt in black-red iridescence,  
- sulfur-thread colonies in fracture heat,  
- translucent raft-mats drifting in upwelling lanes, torn and regrown in endless cycles.

They do not bloom like forests. They accumulate like corrosion.  
They are fed by vents, dissolved metals, pressure gradients, and cold circulation—more like geochemistry with appetite than like any familiar plant life.

Everything larger follows these mineral harvest zones.

---

### The grazers and filters

Across submerged cliffs move broad, slow **anvilbacks**—leviathan grazers with ceramic scutes and rasping mouthplates. They do not chase prey; they plane along rock faces, stripping microbial films in long pale streaks. Their wake is visible from aircraft as clean, ghostly bands on dark stone.

In open troughs hang kilometer-long **filter ribbons**, colonial animals suspended beneath the wave train like living curtain walls. Each segment pulses in sequence, tuned to the fixed swell period. They are less “swimmers” than suspended infrastructure: the ocean threads itself through them, and they harvest what it carries.

Around vents cluster dense **forge-crawlers**, heavy-shelled vent arthropods whose shells are layered with iron salts and silica. Their colonies sound like distant machinery: chittering valves, shell-on-shell knocks, pressure pops.

---

### The fast lives: shoals, skimmers, and hunters

Where the water clears, immense shoals of foil-scaled fish flash silver-gray in synchronized sheets. Their schooling behavior is not visual first—it is acoustic and pressure-sensed. A shoal can turn as if one body when struck by a particular low-frequency pulse.

Above them hunt cartilage flyers—spray-line skimmers with stiff crescent wings and resonant throat sacs. They scream not to sing, but to map. Their calls bounce off cliff, machine, and swell, returning a moving relief map of edible motion.

Then there are the **baritone predators**: long-bodied, pale-jawed pursuit animals that hunt by broadcasting infrasonic knocks that stun or disorient schooling prey. You hear them in hull panels before you see them. Most mariners learn that sensation early: a faint rhythmic thud through metal, then absence of fish, then wake-slashed water.

---

### What intelligence looks like here

The sapient residents—human-descended in some enclaves, possibly convergent in others—live as **anchored cultures**.

They are not numerous. They are distributed like beacons.

People here are built by selection and engineering for vibration tolerance: reinforced middle-ear structures, damped sinus cavities, dense connective tissue, and an obsession with tuned materials. Homes are suspended on compliant mounts. Tools are “voiced” to avoid destructive harmonics. Children learn local frequency maps before long-distance navigation.

Language is dual-channel:

1. clipped spoken code for close range in wind breaks,  
2. transmitted vibration patterns through rail, hull, and mooring lines for distance and storm-noise conditions.

A settlement does not ask “What is the weather?” first. It asks “What is the spectrum today?”

---

### Their work, and why they stay

They live by managing interfaces:

- keeping turbine throats clear of biofouling veils,  
- harvesting vent minerals without poisoning local food webs,  
- retuning old autonomous factories that still follow century-old cycle logic,  
- laying and replacing resonance-safe cables,  
- escorting migratory routes around intake fields.

They farm little in the terrestrial sense. They ranch motion, chemistry, and timing.

A prosperous station is one that knows exactly when the fixed wave train, the foil shoals, and the processing pistons align—then takes its yield in a narrow window and yields the corridor back before predators arrive.

---

### Temperament of the living world

Nothing here is leisurely. But almost nothing is frantic, either.

Life in this high ocean is **procedural**: repeatable, massive, and patient.  
Creatures survive not by outlasting storms, but by surviving sameness.  
Civilizations persist not by conquering the place, but by learning its metronome and bolting themselves to it.

So what lives there?

A whole biosphere of mineral eaters, scream-mappers, and rhythm hunters—  
and small, stubborn peoples who learned that on this world, the nearest thing to peace is not silence, but a frequency you can trust.
