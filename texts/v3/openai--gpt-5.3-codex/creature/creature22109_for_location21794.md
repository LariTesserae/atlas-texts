At first, what lives here looks immobile.

You think the residents are towers, mats, fronds, mineral trunks—things that root and wait. And in a literal sense, they are. The oldest inhabitants are continent-wide clonal plant-colonies whose bodies are mostly hidden: vast ceramicized root-webs, charge organs buried in salt strata, and occasional spires that rise like weathered monuments. A “grove” can be one individual. A “plain” can be sleeping leaf-tissue. Some of these beings are older than cities and react on timescales from milliseconds (storm signaling) to centuries (reorienting whole growth fronts toward better electrostatic yield). They do not move much, but they are never still.

Then the moving life resolves out of the noise.

Browsers travel in long, loose bands, low-slung and wide-ribbed, with rasping mouthparts for shaving mineral bark and tongue-pads seeded with electrosymbiotic flora. Their flanks are armored in overlapping keratin-ceramic scales that ring when struck by grit. They “graze” by scraping charge-rich crust and metabolizing the chemistry locked in it. Herds communicate by drumming through the ground—split hooves, resonant sternums, synchronized impact patterns that carry farther than voice in dust-thick air.

Burrowers are the recyclers: blind or near-blind, conductive-furred, built like wedges. They tunnel through dead vascular conduits and abandoned root-canals, eating fungal-metal films and excreting processed mineral slurry that younger plants can reclaim. During storms they surface in blackened sheets, coated in ferromagnetic pollen, and realign themselves to ground electric potential before diving again.

Above them hunt the sail-kites: light-boned predators with crescent wings stiffened by filament ribs, jawlines tuned for ultrasonic bursts. They don’t just chase; they panic-shape. A hunting group will bracket a herd and fire coordinated sonic snaps that fracture the herd’s drum-language, then strike the individuals who lose rhythm. Their blood is clear oil, quick to flash off in thin air, so injuries smoke rather than drip.

And then there are the people.

Not separate from the botanic world—never that—but a species that learned to co-author it. They’re often called **Accordants** by offworlders, because everything they do is negotiated: with plants, with weather, with neighboring districts, with the dead infrastructure underfoot. Physically, they read as tall and narrow, built for altitude and heat shedding, with matte skin patterned in mineral tones and subdermal conductive lattices that flicker faintly when they interface with living architecture. Their throats and chest bones are honeycombed with resonant cavities; speech is only one layer of communication. They map by echo, vote by harmonic pulse, and can “hear” municipal load, storm risk, and migration pressure as changes in urban hum.

They are born fully motile, but their culture expects eventual anchoring. Not becoming a plant, exactly—more like entering a long partnership with one. In midlife many Accordants take a **Root Oath**, grafting neural interfaces into a district’s trunk-foundation. From then on they remain physically mobile, but part of their cognition is continuously shared with civic-biological systems: transit flows, pollination windows, acoustic corridors, charge budgets. Elder Accordants can feel an entire neighborhood like a limb.

Their cities are full of specialists you’d recognize only here:  
- **Storm cantors**, who modulate district harmonics so lightning ladders where it can be harvested rather than where it will kill.  
- **Dust jurists**, who arbitrate ownership of “empty” ground that is usually dormant tissue.  
- **Rise engineers**, who coax structural growth upward millimeter by millimeter without stealing wind from adjacent habitats.  
- **Phase mechanics**, who troubleshoot those root-network signal events that arrive too early to satisfy ordinary causality.

What lives there, in the end, is not one creature but a pact.

A dry-world ecology where plants learned industry, animals learned acoustics, and people learned humility before both. A civilization that doesn’t stand on nature or retreat from it, but threads itself through a loud, charged, ancient metabolism. In that high austere light, life is less a collection of species than a single ongoing argument about height, signal, and survival—and everything alive is speaking at once.
