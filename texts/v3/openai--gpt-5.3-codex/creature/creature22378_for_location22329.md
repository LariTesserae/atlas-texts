What lives there is not a single species so much as a **stacked ecology of attention**.

At the center are the people—miners, riggers, pressure-techs, scavengers, route painters, coil tenders—everyone with hands trained to seal, cinch, brace, reclaim. They are young as a culture and old as a reflex: they check gasket lips with their thumbs while talking, count breaths when climbing, and glance at every seam the way coastal people read clouds. Their faces are wind-cut and lamp-lit; their voices stay low because shouting wastes air and focus. They wear layered workskins of ceramic cloth, sealable collars, knee hooks, and tool harnesses that sound like cutlery when they climb. Nobody here moves casually. Even rest looks like readiness.

They sort themselves by altitude without pretending it is fair.  
Lower tiers: broader lungs, heat-tolerant skin, furnace crews and heavy haulers, people who smell faintly of hot metal and scrubbed oil.  
Mid tiers: traffic coordinators, machinists, breeders of useful fauna, the bureaucracy of ducts and quotas.  
Upper tiers: thin-air specialists, surveyors, signal keepers, the austere households that pay for cleaner intake and colder silence.  
A family can rise or sink in a year. A fracture can erase a district in an hour. So status is measured in meters, but trust is measured in who showed up when a line blew.

Around them lives the tolerated nation of animals, each with a niche so practical it becomes civic law. Six-limbed cliff-runners patrol warm braces and eat vermin from insulation seams; children paint tiny chevrons where runners are allowed to nest so maintenance crews don’t evict them by mistake. Tunnel eels taste pressure with their whole bodies and ripple toward microleaks before instruments register them; some crews follow eel behavior like weather signs. Fist-sized warm scavengers sleep in cable trays and clean food residue, gasket crumbs, and dead mites. Metallic-dusted gliders wheel through updraft shafts carrying static from sector to sector, half pest, half messenger, occasionally trained to return to feeding lights at specific relay posts. None are sentimental pets, exactly—but none are treated as mere pests either. Utility becomes kinship very quickly in a place this tight.

There are also the things that are not quite animals and not quite infrastructure: semi-autonomous inspection crawlers with old names, warning chimes tuned differently by neighborhood, abandoned lift cradles converted into shrines, map walls updated hourly then crossed out by evening. In this city, tools accrue personality because everyone depends on them and nothing stays long enough to become tradition by architecture. Tradition forms in procedures, nicknames, and taboos: never step over a live warning line; never leave a warm surface unclaimed; never trust yesterday’s arrows.

The children of the fissure are perhaps the truest natives. They learn verticality before distance, pressure before weather, and ownership as a temporary verb. They play games involving route memory and timed seal drills. They can identify districts by hum frequency. They are taught to listen for the mountain’s body-sound—deep strain-shifts in the rock—and to freeze when ceramic chimes change rhythm. Their drawings are not houses on hills but platforms on walls, ladders, ducts, gliders, and the long thin rectangle of daylight far above.

So what lives there?

A people who treat air like currency and edges like farmland.  
Animals that turn waste into order and danger into signal.  
Machines that are maintained like livestock and spoken to like coworkers.  
And under all of them, a mountain still deciding its shape—restless, load-bearing, never entirely tamed—inside which this vertical city keeps inventing itself, one bolt, one breath, one reclaimed drop at a time.
