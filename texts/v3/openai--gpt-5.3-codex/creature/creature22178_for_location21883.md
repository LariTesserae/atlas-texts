At first you think what lives there is simply **a population**—families, workers, elders, children—people like anywhere else, only underground.

Then you stay longer, and that answer stops being enough.

What lives there are the **Listeners** (whether they call themselves that or not): a people shaped less by darkness than by constancy. They are not cave-feral, not ghostly, not primitive. They are meticulous. Their bodies are compact and sure-footed from ladders and tilted stone. Their hands are broad at the palm, fingers nicked with old cuts, nails kept short because everything is rope, grit, hinge, valve. Their skin holds a permanent sheen from wet air and lamp smoke. Most squint in bright light, but underground their eyes do fine; it’s their ears that are extraordinary—trained from infancy to parse twenty overlapping rhythms and notice the one missing beat that means a gate has jammed.

They don’t speak loudly unless they must. Conversation is economical, timed between impacts and whistles. To outsiders it sounds curt; inside the cavern it is kindness, making room for signal. Names are often short and percussive. Instructions are musical. A child can be sent home with a phrase like “down to the low thud, left at the split hiss” and arrive without hesitation.

Their clothing has no loose vanity. Felted mineral fiber, cured hide, ceramic toggles, soft metal rings where stress points fail. Waterproofing is grease and lime. Colors stay in the range of clay, ash, soot, and occasional mineral blues or sulfur yellows if someone has trade access. Jewelry, when worn, is often functional: ear-caps that damp specific frequencies, wrist weights for rope work, tiny tuning pendants used to check resonance in support beams.

They smell faintly of rendered fat, wet stone, and clean salt.

---

But people are only the first layer.

What truly lives there is a **civic metabolism**: guilds of keepers who inherit not property but intervals. One household tends a spillway drum at dawn-cycle; another clears calcite from the fifth terrace every third rest; another listens to the kiln quarter for pitch drift. Status comes less from wealth than from reliability—how many seasons your line has kept a channel in tolerance, how few surprise floods occurred on your watch.

Children are raised communally by soundscape. They sleep in sling-beds while water percussion marks the hours. Their first games are echo games—finding one another in dark alcoves by tongue-click and tap. Their first lessons are pressure, slope, and patience. They learn very young that panic is louder than danger and therefore more dangerous.

Old age is not exile here; it is calibration. Elders who can no longer climb become human instruments: they sit near wall-junctions and detect subtle structural changes no gauge catches. An elder’s phrase—“that pillar has gone hollow in the left shoulder”—can move an entire work crew by noon.

---

Diet and appetite shape them too. With no fields and no orchards, food is deliberate: fungal cakes, broth from eel bones, crab fat, traded grains hoisted down the shaft and stretched with mineral-salt fermentation. Feasts are rare but exacting, usually tied to successful maintenance cycles rather than seasonal harvests. “Abundance” means full cistern marks, stable heat, no unexplained echoes.

Because rot is scarce, memory is material. Tools outlast owners. A grandparent’s leather apron may still hang by the same door, stiff as board, mended in five different eras. Bones are curated, not hidden—ancestral remains sealed into wall niches near critical supports, part reverence, part warning: *we hold this place, and it holds us*.

---

And there are other lives, quieter ones, threaded through theirs.

The pale eels in the deep runs, nearly invisible until they bend.
Crack-beetles combing salt from mortar seams.
Sump crabs fattening in the warm waste channels.
Blind bats that pass like scraps of soot in the high black.

None are plentiful enough to dominate. Each is watched, trapped sparingly, protected when useful. The people treat even vermin as indicators: a sudden absence of beetles can mean chemical drift; too many crabs in an upper basin can mean blockage below.

---

Eventually you realize the most accurate answer is this:

What lives there is a **partnership between humans and a cave that has been taught to speak**.

The district itself behaves like a single distributed creature—stone bones, water blood, valves and gates as muscles, drums and whistles as nervous system. The inhabitants are not separate from that body; they are its attentive cells. They scrape scale, reset tension, relieve pressure, patch fractures before they become failures. They place palms on walls not as superstition but as diagnosis. They can feel a fever in basalt.

They do not dream of conquering the cavern. They dream of keeping it in tune.

So if you ask what lives in that buried street-cathedral, the answer is not just “people,” and not just “infrastructure.”

It is a civilization of maintenance.
A culture of listening.
A species of steadiness.

In a world of drip and echo, they have become the thing most adapted to survive there:  
not the strongest, not the fastest—  
the most precisely attentive.
