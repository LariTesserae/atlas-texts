At first glance, nothing lives there but **cold**.

Not metaphorical cold—actual, procedural cold: scheduled, measured, retained. It sits in the stone like an appointed official, never off duty. If this court has a resident, that is the first one: a climate held in place by design, a tenant older than any worker and stricter than any law.

Then, if you keep watching, a second inhabitant appears: **the municipal reflex**.

It has no body you can point to. It is distributed through valve timing, pressure ribs, warming traces in grates, the faint brightening and dimming lines in the walls. It “breathes” by opening one path while closing another. It “listens” in tiny pressure shifts and changes its posture before weather arrives. It does not think like a person, but it does persist like an organism. In another era, people might have called it a machine. Here, it behaves more like metabolism.

Only after those two do the obvious living things come into focus.

There are the **court tenders**—human, usually solitary, wrapped in insulated coats with cuffs that seal and unseal as they move between wet and dry zones. They don’t linger. Their gestures are practiced and economical: palm to sensor strip, glance to flow display, heel on rung, gone. They are not the kind of residents who decorate. They are the kind who keep thresholds honest. You can tell who is experienced by how little sound they make on the walkways.

There are the **inspection drones**, which are almost birds in habit if not in origin. They tuck themselves into dry pockets under eaves, wake, skim, scan, dock, sleep. Their lives are loops and checklists, but they accumulate quirks: one favors the same docking bay even when others are free; another always hesitates at a particular conduit where wind shears oddly. Mechanical life, but still local life.

And at the scale of dust, there are the **white arthropods**—minute, pale, nearly abstract. They come out near warm junctions and filter runoff, harvesting the thin chemical leftovers of an engineered ecosystem too clean for almost anything else. They are the only creatures that seem pleased by the place’s severity. They do not beautify it; they complete it.

Even the passing **tagged glider birds** matter by contrast. They wheel above, assess, and move on. Their refusal to land defines the court as clearly as any blueprint: this is not habitat in the old sense. It is function under weather.

So what lives there?

A hierarchy of residents:

1. Cold, permanent and employed.  
2. A distributed civic intelligence, always balancing phase against phase.  
3. Brief, skilled humans who keep the organ in tune.  
4. Small machines and smaller scavengers at the edge of survivability.

Together they make a kind of urban life that doesn’t look alive until you understand its rhythm. Then it becomes unmistakable: a tiny heart of infrastructure, beating in drips, clicks, and silver breath.
