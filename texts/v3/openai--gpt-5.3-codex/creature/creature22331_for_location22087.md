What lives there, more than any single beast or tribe, is **attention**.

Not the relaxed kind—sharp, practiced, almost musical attention to sequence: first drip, then seep, then green; first thaw, then hoof, then hunter. Every creature in that basin survives by reading a moving score. Miss a beat, and the slope shears, the pool skins over, the food is already gone.

The most constant residents are the small ones that can tuck themselves into seams: burrowers in the terraces, beetles and flies that hatch into noon warmth and vanish by dusk, moss-rooted microfauna in the wet cushions. They are the basin’s hidden engine. They turn brief water into brief abundance. They live fast, in pulses, and in doing so feed everything larger.

Then come the grazers—compact, hard-hoofed ungulates that travel like weather. They don’t “settle” so much as circulate between wet pockets, red-twig browse, and sedge shelves as if tracing routes memorized across generations. White hares ghost along the snowline, changing paths almost daily. Above them, raptors patrol in widening gyres, patient until one moment of absolute speed.

And in the margins of those movements: the pale predators. Lean, long-legged, quiet-coated animals that use broken granite and mist as cover. You know them less by sight than by evidence: a chain of prints at a dawn pool, scat full of hare fur and sedge-fed bone, a sudden silence in the burrow fields. They are not numerous. They don’t need to be. In a basin built on timing, precision outranks force.

Humans are the only ones who live there **intermittently on purpose**. Their camps are temporary because permanence would be a misunderstanding of the place. They come when melt and forage and prey overlap, build low, leave light, mark traverses, harvest, dry, mend, move. They know which ledge catches first sun, which channel exists only in early thaw, which cairn means “safe in clear weather, fatal in fog.” Their tools are reusable because the mountain is not; it keeps remaking itself.

So if you ask what lives in that bright young bowl, the truest answer is:  
a whole community of migrators, crouchers, circlers, and watchers—  
and among them, a seasonal human culture that has learned the basin’s first law:

**nothing here lasts, so everything here must be read in time.**
