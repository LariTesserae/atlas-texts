At first, what lives there seems to be **timing** more than bodies.

You notice schedules before faces: frost arriving in lanes, insects lifting from vents at the same dim hour, shadow-herds crossing the same contour as yesterday and last season and the season before. The first inhabitants of that high country are cycles—cold cycles, moisture cycles, hunt cycles—repeated so long they behave like law.

Then the bodies begin to resolve out of the dark.

Closest to the stone are the **surface keepers**: felted mosses, black sedges, gutter-branched conifers, dwarf bamboo in lee pockets, lichens as thick as old rope. They are less “plants” in the meadow sense and more condensation machinery. They comb cloud, pin fog, wick rime, store droplets in capillary mats, and leak water downward in rationed trickles. Whole slopes are upholstered with them, and under that upholstery is warmth—just a fraction, but enough for eggs, spores, larvae, and sleeping hearts.

Threaded through them are the **charge reeds**—tall, hollow, metallic-sapped stems that act like living lightning rods. Storms strike; sap polarizes; root webs discharge into surrounding soil. That tiny heat pulse keeps microbial beds active and prevents complete freeze in critical pockets. Around those pockets cluster insects, then small grazers, then hunters. In this biome, electricity is not spectacle; it is winter budgeting.

The large moving silhouettes are the **rail-herds**: pale, long-limbed ungulates with narrow chests and deep lungs, built for thin air and sidehill travel. They do not wander so much as commute. They follow pressure gradients, old scent strata, and magnetic quirks along contour lines so faithfully that from above they look like freight moving on invisible tracks. Their hooves are splayed and soft-rimmed, gripping frost-polished stone without clatter. They feed while walking, rarely stopping in exposed bands, always arriving at sheltered basins minutes before katabatic pulses descend.

Above and between ridges hunt the **cliff gliders**—not quite bats, not quite birds, all silence. Ribbed membranes catch downslope breaths; furred leading edges damp turbulence; eyes are tuned for low contrast rather than color. They appear exactly when vent insects rise, taking them in long conveyor-belt passes, then vanish into slots in the basalt where their colonies roost in thermal layers like stacked pages.

Under the talus lives a different nation: **tremor-burrowers** in warm-vapor colonies. Blind or near-blind, whiskered with vibration cilia, they speak through substrate pulses—drummed feet, jaw-clicks into stone, chest-thumps against packed soil. Their tunnels are heat exchangers and nurseries both, routed around geothermal leaks, insulated with shredded sedge and fungal felt. Predators can smell them; almost none can hear them.

Predators are everywhere, but rarely where you look. White-furred stalkers that freeze into snowbands. Needle-toothed mustelid analogs that run beneath lichen canopies. Ambush reptiles that bask only where charge-root warmth seeps through shale. Predation here is efficient, close, and almost noiseless—seen in track interruptions, blood-melt halos, and sudden blank spaces where a moving thing was.

And in the dark water of tarns, when disturbed, bloom the **electroplankton**—cold-light drifters that flash in pressure ripples, mapping every step as pale geometry. Fallen trunks host foxfire fungi. Gully walls freckle with bioluminescent mats. Night is not empty black; it is instrument-panel black, dotted with status lights.

So what lives there?

Not a single emblem animal. Not a hidden civilization.  
What lives there is a **stacked consortium** of quiet specialists, each tuned to a narrow band of wind, heat, moisture, and hour. They survive by coupling: plant to fog, reed to lightning, fungus to root, insect to vent, glider to swarm, herd to contour, burrower to tremor.  

The true resident is the system itself—a distributed creature made of thousands of species and millions of repetitions, a living engine that never announces itself, only runs.
