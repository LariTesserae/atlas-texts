What lives there is **a people and a pulse**, and after a while you can’t tell which is which.

They call themselves (in a gesture-language, palms tapping sternum then pointing upward) something closest to **the Kneeling Tide**. Outsiders used “basin-folk,” but that misses it. They are not settled *on* the bowl so much as tuned *into* it.

Physically, they look human until you watch them move. Their balance is low and deliberate, knees always soft, as if ready to obey rule five at any instant. They are broad-ribbed from a life of pressure shifts, with corded necks and heavy sinuses that make their faces seem carved for weather. Their skin is scarred in fine pale lines where flying grit and mineral rain have polished them over years. Most shave their heads or braid hair tight under waxed cloth; loose hair becomes a sensor error in this place.

Their senses have reorganized around the roar. They “listen” through bone: jaw against stone, forearm against wet wall, forehead to shoulder. They speak by:

- hand-signs close to the chest (big gestures get lost in spray),  
- pressure knocks through slabs and platforms,  
- brief mouth-to-cloth shouts only at contact distance.

Children learn currents before names. Adults carry two names anyway: one for being called, one in case a reflection starts wearing the first.

They build lightly and repeatedly, never permanently. A home is a pattern, not a structure: three anchor points, one high sleeping line, one low cache, one emergency descent route. They never bed down at the same height twice in succession. Every object has a tether, even stones, even the dead. They wear belts of braided kelp-rope with clipped weights—“gravity in pocket form”—because sometimes the basin forgets which way down is.

Food is scavenged from moments, not seasons. They harvest wall-mats, cup-growth water, and the translucent fin-things that pulse through curtains. They trap those creatures with vibrating frames that mimic pressure eddies. Nothing is wasted; bones become toggles, skins become membrane flags that signal flow changes by flutter pattern.

At dim-green “night,” when bioluminescent threads wake in the cracks, the basin-folk perform their only loud ritual: they stand in the slab-ring and strike channel-stone with waterlogged mallets. Not to make sound over the roar—impossible—but to impose rhythm on it. They believe the basin is a newborn ocean rehearsing bodies, and if no one keeps time, it will try on theirs.

There are other residents, too—mist-diving bird-not-birds, fold-swimming glassfish, reflections that wander unsupervised—but the true long-term inhabitant is this:  
**a culture of constant adjustment**, people who survived by treating certainty as a hazard and resonance as home.
