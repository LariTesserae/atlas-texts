At first, it looks like nothing lives there except structure and growth: vents singing, kilns breathing, plants crystallizing and dissolving in their own rhythms.

Then you notice the maintenance is too precise to be accidental.

A seam clogged with blown grit is cleared before pressure builds. A vine that has begun to etch a load-bearing wall is coaxed sideways, not cut, and follows the offered channel within days. Resonance stones in the plazas are rotated by fractions, retuned after thermal shifts. Someone is listening to the city as if it were an instrument that must stay in tune.

What lives there are often called, in translation, **the Fired**.

They are not animal-bodied. No blood, no marrow, no wet organs. Their bodies are composite: braided plant fiber, mineral binders, kiln-hardened shell plates, and interior chambers full of reactive dusts that move and settle like breath. In dim copper light they resemble walking columns more than flesh—matte green, ash-gray, salt-white, with seams stitched in resin thread. When they are injured, powder spills; a companion packs the breach, warms it, and the wound sets like ceramic.

They “eat” the way their forests do, but with craft layered over chemistry. At dawn they stand in frost-gardens and brush mineral bloom from leaf and stone into mouth-grilles lined with catalytic teeth. At noon they work hot vents, drawing thin gases through ribbed chest lattices where compounds are fixed and stored. They drink nothing. They carry small pouches of reactive salts the way other peoples might carry bread.

They do not speak much in the narrow sense. Their language is architectural and percussive: heel-strikes on resonant paving, shutter-patterns in wind towers, tuned whistles through clay reeds, low drum-thuds sent along hollow avenue walls. A single person can announce themselves as a three-note pressure pulse felt more than heard; a district assembly is a slow chord progression passing block to block at dusk. Their names are motifs. Their laws are remembered as rhythms.

From far away the city seems empty because the Fired are sparse and heat-timed. They move in bands with the basin’s thermal cycle: kiln wards at high glare, root wards in cooling hours, spore watch at gradient-flip. A plaza can sit abandoned for half a day and then fill, silently, with workers calibrating clock groves by leaf angle and vent tone. Their industries are old because they are conservative on purpose: stone edge over metal spark, ceramic gear over anything that might demand oils or water. Every tool is repairable from local dust, fiber, and heat.

They reproduce slowly, more like founding than birthing. Several adults contribute memory-ash (powder from their own worn interior baffles), resin, seed-filament, and shaped clay into a warm mold set near a breathing seam. The new one emerges soft, pale, and acoustically “blank,” then spends years being fired in stages—hardened, tuned, taught—until their shell carries a stable voice. No individual is made alone; each is a civic act.

And when one of them reaches fracture that cannot be mended, they are not buried. They choose a grove margin, open their seams, and let their stored salts and powders fall into the cone beds. Their plates are ground for mortar. Their fiber cords become binding thread. Their names persist as tuning patterns in the nearest tower.

So what lives there is neither machine nor plant nor person in the old wet sense, but a patient alloy of all three: a people adapted to a waterless metabolism, a city that is half body, and a culture that survives by keeping growth and construction in the same breathless cycle—bake, crack, seed, bind, polish, repeat.
