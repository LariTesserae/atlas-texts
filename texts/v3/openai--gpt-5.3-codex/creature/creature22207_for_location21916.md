What lives there is not abundance in the valley sense, but **abundance in intervals**.

The dominant residents are the tall plume-plants you noticed—call them *spiregroves*, though each individual is really a solitary engine. They stand far apart because each one commands a deep mineral territory underground. Their roots are blunt, mineralized wedges that grow by pressure and chemistry, not by softness: they crack old rock, follow salt veins, and sit with buried hydrates for decades before “drinking.” Aboveground, their fronds are layered like shutters. At dawn they open narrow, at noon they tilt edge-on to survive the hard light, and at night they unfurl to rake frost and airborne crystals from moving air. Their green is armor: wax, copper compounds, silica bloom. They are lush by volume, not by water.

Below them lives a quieter majority: mats, crusts, and seam-colonies that occupy every stable crack. These are the true old-timers—organisms that can suspend, bleach, revive, and continue. They trap dust, exhale acids, and make the first soft films on stone. In cliff faces they form vertical tapestries, each band tuned to a different hour of shade. On flats they lie almost invisible until twilight, when temperature drops and they darken, briefly active, breathing through pores that close again with sunrise. Most of the planet’s “soil” is made by their patience.

The reproductive pulse of the land is mostly airborne. Seed-cases dry into tension and snap open with brittle clicks, firing glassy grains downwind. Spore ribbons peel from mature fronds in long metallic streamers and travel astonishing distances along escarpment thermals. A grove can feed another valley hundreds of kilometers away without any visible river between them. In this world, wind is pollinator, courier, and migration route.

Animal life exists in long loops and rare encounters. The gliders are the most visible—thin-boned, broad-vaned fliers that harvest those spore ribbons midair. They almost never flap; they map the atmosphere instead, riding daily updraft circuits like mariners following currents. They nest on lee shelves where night cold condenses just enough frost to lick from rock. Their calls are ultrasonic clicks for close range; at distance they communicate by flashes from reflective throat scales.

The six-legged grazers are built like moving cairns: low, broad, stone-colored, with split hooves that grip talus and teeth plated in silica. They do not graze continuously. They visit a grove, shear only outer fronds, ingest waxes and mineral gels, then disappear for days or weeks, fermenting and reprocessing in insulated gut chambers. Their dung is one of the few rich nutrient packets in the ecosystem; entire micro-communities bloom around each dropping and vanish by next dawn.

Then there are the dawn burrowers—small, shield-headed, many-clawed creatures that spend most of life below the thermal swing line. They emerge in the blue hour to gather husks, broken seed-cases, and wind-piled organics, then retreat before the sun hardens the surface. They are prey for almost everything else, so they have evolved silence as behavior: no chatter, no warning cries, only synchronized stillness when shadows pass.

Predators are few, and they hunt by geometry rather than speed. One common form is a ridge-runner with elongated forelimbs and heat-damping skin; it waits in fracture shadows near known grazer paths, then commits to one explosive downhill pursuit. Another is an aerial stooper that times attacks to dust-column chaos, dropping through rotating grit where prey vision fails. Neither species can afford waste. A missed strike may mean a week of fasting.

If there is a single “character” to the biosphere, it is this: everything alive here is an expert in **timing, storage, and spacing**. Nothing crowds. Nothing hurries except briefly. Organisms are built to survive long, lean intervals and capitalize on narrow windows—an hour of frost, a day of spores, one favorable wind cycle. Even death is slow: husks stand for seasons, bleaching and flaking, becoming shelter, shade, and eventually dust-trap for the next colony.

So what lives there is a civilization without cities:  
rooted towers, cliffskin mosaics, wind-borne generations, and animals that travel farther than they feed.  
A high dry kingdom inhabited by specialists in distance—green not because water is plentiful, but because life learned to mine stone, air, and time.
