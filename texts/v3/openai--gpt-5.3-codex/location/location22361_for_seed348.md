At first glance it looks like a town that forgot what a horizon is.

The whole place sits in a shallow, low-lying basin, maybe two kilometers across, ringed by old retaining walls and collapsed loading embankments. Nothing rises very high except stacks, gantries, and cable masts. The ground is mostly mineral: iron-stained concrete, clinker brick, cinder, salt crust, and broad plates of dark basalt laid long ago as paving. Where the paving has failed, the breaks are smooth-edged and old, not fresh—everything here has been weathered into its current shape for a long time. Water has worked on it patiently: shallow channels worn into stone thresholds, drip-hollows in lintels, mineral fans beneath every leak.

It is cool, always cool, with that wet-industrial chill that clings to metal. Rain is occasional rather than constant, but moisture is never truly absent. Condensation pearls on pipes, gathers along cable trays, and falls in regular taps into cistern trenches. Pockets of standing water collect in depressions and old foundation pits, black and mirror-still. Between these wet spots the ground is dry enough to dust your boots in pale grit.

There are no plants at all. No moss on the north walls, no weed in the cracks, no lichen on stone. Surfaces stay bare: oxidized, scaled, encrusted, or polished by passage, but never green. Life here does not climb or root.

And life is everywhere.

Fauna dominates the district so completely that architecture feels like a framework built for animals rather than people. They come in layered swarms and colonies: cable-perching shriekers with slate skin and bright ceramic eyes; six-limbed gutter-runners that click along drain lips in measured files; heavy, seal-sized condenser beasts that sleep around warm turbine housings and drink only from drip trays. In the flooded sumps there are ribbon-bodied things that circulate endlessly in tight geometric loops, their wakes making repeating moiré patterns in oil film. Overhead, flocks move between chimney rims in strict corridors, never colliding, never improvising.

The noise is immense, but not chaotic. It is an engine-city loudness braided with animal loudness: rotary hammers in distant foundry bays, belt whine, pressure valves coughing steam, iron doors slamming on timer cycles. Through that, constant vocal layers—keening, chittering, resonant throat-notes from tunnel mouths, drumming feet on sheet metal roofs. Even the water is loud here: regular drips amplified in tanks, runoff rattling through suspended pipes, the deep gulp of siphons resetting. Silence exists only in brief seams between scheduled pulses, and even then wings hiss overhead.

The strangeness comes from how fixed it all is. The same mist gathers at the same hour on the same railings. The same flocks cross Stack Nine at the same minute. Predators and prey occupy adjacent lanes and do not deviate. Sirens sound, shutters close, animals flatten or rise in perfect unison, shutters reopen, movement resumes exactly where it paused. If you mark a creature with chalk, you will find it tomorrow in the same quadrant, repeating the same route. Weather changes, but patterns do not.

Ecology here runs on heat, mineral salts, and machine byproduct. The smallest organisms are film-thin chemotroph mats inside pipes and sump walls—never visible on open stone, only in the wet dark where condensate carries dissolved metal. Larger fauna graze those films, scrape scale, filter warm runoff, crack sulfur crusts, and hunt one another in strictly bounded territories mapped to infrastructure. No leaf, seed, or pollen has ever entered the equation. Digestion smells of copper, iodine, and wet stone.

Because the place is sparse, every presence is distinct. Long stretches of empty slab separate tower clusters. A single lit workshop can stand alone across a hundred meters of open yard. A water pocket with three species around it feels like an entire neighborhood. You can see far within the basin, and that makes movement legible: lines, nodes, returns. It gives the city a cathedral quality despite its low profile—less about height, more about volume, repetition, and echo.

The world’s rule, if there is one, is maintenance through repetition. Nothing grows, nothing fades quickly, nothing surprises for long. Erosion has done its work and stopped at balance. Industry keeps running, fauna keeps threading through it, moisture keeps condensing and falling, and the basin holds all of it in a steady, loud equilibrium that has likely lasted longer than memory.
