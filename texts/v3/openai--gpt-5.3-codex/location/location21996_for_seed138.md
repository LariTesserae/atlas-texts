The place is no bigger than a district you could cross on foot in half an hour: a low, hot industrial quarter built at the edge of a marsh that never quite became land.

Everything sits just above water level. Streets are poured concrete on piled foundations, and between them run open drainage channels lined with fresh-cut stone and corrugated steel. The channels are full but calm, dark green where oil slicks spread thinly over rainwater and seepage. The ground feels young—recently claimed, recently leveled. Corners are still sharp. Embankments have not yet softened. You can see where bulldozer tracks once pressed into clay and were simply paved over.

Light is dim even at midday. Not night-dark, just muted: a permanent lid of warm cloud, factory haze, and humidity thick enough to turn distance gray. Metal sweats. Walls sweat. Pipes bead with condensation and drip at a slow, steady rhythm into puddles that never evaporate because fresh moisture is always replacing what heat takes away. It is hot in the way boiler rooms are hot—sticky, close, inescapable.

The built world is orderly but sparse. Warehouses stand apart from one another with broad service yards between: gravel lots, loading pads, fuel tanks, crane rails, fenced transformer boxes humming softly under rain stains. Nothing is crowded; everything is spaced for trucks, tracks, and turning radii. Rail spurs end at water-side loading gantries. Pump stations squat at channel intersections, their intake grills chattering with trapped reeds and floating trash. Utility poles march in straight lines and disappear into vapor.

Vegetation has little say here. A few rank grasses grow in cracks, some sedge along the outer berms, maybe a bent line of salt-tolerant shrubs where runoff is less toxic. No canopy, no grove, no shade except what buildings cast. The dominant “green” is algae on concrete and lichen beginning to take hold on retaining walls.

Animal life, though, is everywhere. Wading birds stalk the drainage channels with patient, formal steps. Gulls and kites ride thermal drafts over the transfer yard. Frogs call from culverts. Dragonflies patrol standing water. Rats move along pipe racks at dusk and dawn. Stray dogs sleep under parked trailers and rise when fish offal carts roll through. Even in an industrial landscape, the water makes room for mouths.

Sound never drops to silence. Fans, pumps, idling engines, conveyor rollers, clanking couplings, warning beeps from reversing loaders, the slap of water against pilings, bird cries, insect whine—layered into one continuous mechanical-biological roar. Not chaotic, just constant. This is a place in equilibrium: locks keep water level fixed, pumps keep channels from flooding, shifts rotate with clockwork regularity, and weather holds in a long, unbroken humid overcast. The same puddles sit in the same depressions week after week. The same birds wait at the same outflows. The same sodium lamps glow amber through mist before sunset, and after.

It feels less like a city that grew and more like a system that was installed: practical, exposed, wet, and loud—an engineered shoreline where land, industry, and marsh have agreed to coexist without ever becoming comfortable with one another.
