At this altitude, the world has already shed softness.

You stand on a tableland of old stone shaved down by uncounted ages of wind, cold, and particle-sand so fine it behaves like smoke. There are no streams, no puddles, no damp seam under rock. Even shadow feels dry. The ground is a pale composite of mineral salts, oxidized metals, and fossilized root-matter turned ceramic by time. Entire ridges look carved, but the carving was done by centuries of airborne grit and the slow creep of living tissue that abrades as it grows.

And yet almost everything is plant.

Not “green” in the easy sense—more like a continent-spanning botanic skin. The substrate itself is alive in plates and seams: tough, fibrous mats welded to bedrock; tower-cacti with faceted trunks like stacked basalt; broad, low fans of silver fronds that fold into armored cones when the wind surges. Between them, distances are long. The place is full of vegetation and still sparse, because each organism is huge, deliberate, and territorial. A single root-colony might underlie a square kilometer, surfacing only in widely separated spires. What looks like empty mineral flats is often dormant leaf-flesh, gray as ash, waiting for a charge pulse from the air.

Water never built this ecosystem. Chemistry did.  
The plants drink ions, not rain. They harvest electric gradients between ground and sky, strip reactive gases from the thin air, crack minerals for oxygen, and circulate oils, ammonia-rich gels, and metallic colloids in place of sap. Their “wood” rings when struck. Their pollen behaves like ferromagnetic dust and aligns in black streamers during storms.

The light is dim most days—not darkness, but a muted, late-afternoon world even at noon. A dusty upper atmosphere and a weak sun flatten color into bruised blues, iron violets, and chalk whites, with sudden neon when a plant surface opens and bioluminescent signaling runs through it. At dusk, whole groves pulse in synchronization: mauve, then amber, then dark. From a distance, it resembles traffic patterns seen from orbit.

You hear this landscape before you understand it.

It is loud, constantly loud. Wind moves through hollow stems and tuned membranes, turning whole valleys into organ pipes. Seedpods burst with rifle-cracks. The urban arrays—because yes, there are cities here—hum in infrasonic bands that vibrate your ribs. Herd fauna drum on resin plains with split hooves made for shock signaling. Predator kites snap their sail-wings and scream ultrasonically, forcing prey into coordinated panic. Even the ground clicks and pops as thermal stresses travel through ancient root-ceramics. Silence exists only in brief pockets, and those feel unnatural, like a failed system.

The fauna are abundant and adapted to this dry botanic machine. Browsers rasp mineral bark and digest it with symbiotic electroflora. Burrowers tunnel through dead vascular conduits, emerging coated in conductive dust. Apex hunters don’t bleed red; their circulatory fluids are clear and oily, and wounds evaporate instead of clotting. Many animals have resonant chambers in bone and throat, because sound is both weapon and map in this low-light world. Courtship, warning, migration, and navigation all happen acoustically, and the landscape itself is built to carry those signals.

Then the settlements come into view: not separate from the vegetation, but grown into it.

Urban districts rise as stepped lattices anchored into colossal trunk-foundations. Streets are suspended in braided root-cables. Transit pods slide silently along magnetic veins embedded in living walls. Towers are porous and matte, exhaling waste heat in patterned plumes that nearby plants feed on. No concrete in the old sense—construction here is mineral-biological composite, seeded, instructed, and then edited by swarms too small to see. Buildings heal. Zoning is ecological before it is political: pollination corridors, migration acoustics, wind-pressure rights, charge-harvest quotas.

Beyond-modern technology is everywhere but not flashy. Matter is persuaded rather than forced. Surfaces reconfigure to direct dust flow. Neighborhoods shift orientation by a few degrees over decades to follow seasonal electrostatic gradients. Communication can run through root-networks at signal speeds that occasionally outpace conventional models; local engineers shrug and call it “phase slipping,” as if causality were a negotiable municipal service. Old observatories on the plateau edge fold starlight into storage lattices, banking night illumination for emergency growth cycles.

Because this is an old world, and old worlds run on agreements.

The first rule: nothing wastes charge.  
Second: anything that can root, eventually will.  
Third: open ground is never empty; it is either sleeping, listening, or owned.  
Fourth: height is survival—frost, predators, dust tides, and signal clarity all sort by elevation—so every species, every district, every machine is in quiet competition to rise, anchor, and endure.

Storms are the main drama. Not rainstorms—dust-electrical fronts that roll across the high basin like dark continents, throwing sheet lightning from plant crown to plant crown. When they pass, entire urban canopies bloom with static fire, fauna go briefly feral with overstimulation, and dormant fields awaken in synchronous unfurling that can cover a valley in two hours. By morning the place looks rearranged: new towers split open, old ones sealed, migration routes rewritten in fresh sonic markers.

From a ridge, the region appears austere: dim light, long spaces, ancient lines worn almost flat. But stay long enough and it resolves into density of process rather than density of objects—an active, noisy, strange civilization-ecosystem where city, forest, and weather are one coupled organism, surviving on rock, charge, and memory in a world that forgot water a very long time ago.
