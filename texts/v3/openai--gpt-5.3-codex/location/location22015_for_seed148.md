At the bottom of a shallow basin, where the land has only recently decided to be land, there is a district called **the Green Hollow**.  
It is not large—if you stood at one edge and looked across, you could make out the opposite wall through the dark—but it feels deep, like a room built inside a root.

The ground is young. Not worn down, not weathered smooth—just assembled: soft seams of peat, slick clay, exposed ribs of stone still sharp from first emergence. You can see places where yesterday’s floor was mud and today’s is a woven mat of stems. Nothing looks finished. Paths seem provisional, as if they were drawn in charcoal and then overgrown before dawn. Even the curb lines are alive: braided roots bound into right angles, then already bulging out of them.

Despite all this growth, the place feels empty.  
There are streets, platforms, switchyards, terraces, drainage channels, signal towers—and huge dark intervals between them. The structures are there, but not crowded. A person can walk for minutes through a corridor of hanging vines and find no one, only the architecture breathing around them. It is urban in layout, infrastructural in intent, but hollow in occupancy: a city shell inhabited mostly by weather, plants, and animals.

### Materials and construction

Almost every surface is botanical, but guided by industry.

- Load-bearing columns are trunks trained through steel hoops while still green.
- Walkways are laminated bark laid over cable grids.
- Pipelines run with water, sap, and low-voltage current in parallel.
- Ventilation stacks are giant reed clusters fitted with turbine throats, turning when cold air drains down at night.
- Factory walls are living hedges clipped into baffles and noise-dampening folds.

Nothing here is fully “natural” or fully “built.” The factories are orchards with conveyor belts. The apartment blocks are trellises seven stories tall, their rooms like seed pods with gasketed doors. Storage tanks are gourds grown around scaffolds, then hardened and lined with resin.

### Moisture, temperature, light

Water arrives in episodes. Most of the time the air is merely damp: cold breath, leaf-sweat, condensation beading on rails. Then a narrow rain passes through, as if poured from a single unseen trough in the sky, soaking one block and leaving the next dry. Water gathers in pockets: stairwell sumps, tire ruts, folded leaves the size of canoes, the cupped crowns of cut stumps. By morning many pools are gone—drained, absorbed, or walked away by the moving ground.

It is cool enough that metal bites skin, cool enough that breath appears inside certain alleys.  
The light is sparse and uncertain. There is no full day here. Illumination comes in scraps:

- sodium maintenance lamps behind fogged glass,
- bioluminescent pollen drifting like dust,
- slow pulses from instrument panels sunk into vines,
- occasional pale openings overhead, quickly veiled by moving growth.

Darkness is the default state, and the settlement is designed for it. Lanes are marked by texture rather than color; handrails are ridged in code; intersections are identified by smell released from planted vents.

### Soundscape

It is mostly quiet in the way a marsh at midnight is quiet: never silent, but hushed and distributed.

You hear drip chains ticking into cisterns.  
A distant belt drive hums, then stops.  
Leaves rasp against corrugated siding.  
Something small runs over cable trays, pauses, then runs again.  
Above all, there is a low pressure-sigh from the whole district as pumps, stomata, and thermal drafts take turns moving air.

When loud sounds happen, they are brief and local: a fruit tank venting gas with a startled bark, a brace cable snapping taut, a flock taking off all at once like a tarp ripped in half.

### Ecology and fauna

Life is excessive here, and mostly nonhuman.

Fauna move along the plant infrastructure as if it were terrain built for them—which it is. There are whiskered gliders nesting in turbine reeds; soft-shelled amphibians in elevator pits; deer-sized pollinators that browse catwalk gardens; blind fish that appear in rooftop gutters after rain, then vanish by noon. Insect density is astonishing, but many species are quiet: wings like felt, mouthparts made for scraping rather than buzzing.

Predation is constant but subdued. You notice absence more than attack: a swarm thinning, a branch suddenly still, feathers drifting from an unseen canopy. Carrion does not last. Detritivores—beetles, molds, rooting microfauna—process death into substrate within hours. The city composts itself in real time.

### Activity and cycles

Everything is in motion, just not in spectacle.

Growth crews come through at irregular intervals to graft walls, prune power lines, harvest structural fibers. Automated cutters move on overhead tracks, trimming and seeding simultaneously. Rain events trigger gate sequences: flood channels open, then close; moss valves swell shut; railbeds lift by a few centimeters to stay dry. Animals follow these cycles, and so do people when people are present.

By dawn-equivalent (there is no true dawn), the district has changed enough that habitual routes fail.  
A staircase has rooted over.  
A service tunnel has become a pond.  
A courtyard has inverted into a thicket, and the thicket contains a new door.

### Rules of this place (where reality loosens)

The Green Hollow does not entirely obey stable geometry.

1. **Distance depends on wetness.** Two points connected by damp growth are “near” regardless of map length. In dry intervals they separate again.
2. **Shadows retain weight.** In very dark corridors, shadows pool like cold water; small animals drink from them and become briefly invisible.
3. **Roots remember pressure.** Walk the same path three nights running and the ground will raise a rib underfoot, acknowledging the route.
4. **Interior and exterior trade places during rain.** Rooms may become weather while streets become enclosed, as leaf vaults fold and unfold.
5. **Unattended metal is eventually planted.** Bolts sprout tendrils of conductive moss, tools sink into bark, rails bud side branches carrying current like nerves.

Because of these rules, planning here is less about fixing form and more about negotiating with tendencies. Engineers speak in botanical tenses. Gardeners carry voltmeters.

---

The overall feeling is of a small, dark, cool city still being born: sparse in human presence, dense in living matter, industrial in method, wild in outcome. It is quiet enough that you can hear condensation form, active enough that nothing you touch will be the same tomorrow, and strange enough that maps are written in pencil and leaf-stain, never ink.
