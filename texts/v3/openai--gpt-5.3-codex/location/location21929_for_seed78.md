At first it seems like a mountain range at night: black ridgelines, cold air, stars sharpened by altitude.  
Then you realize the “mountains” are mostly alive.

This is a high country of **living escarpments**—a continental plateau where rock has only recently broken up through old ice and ash, and plants have already swarmed over it. The stone is young, hard-edged, and angular, not yet rounded by ages of rain. Ridges look cut with knives. Cliffs are fresh scars. But every seam that can hold moisture is occupied: mats of black moss, rope-thick roots, low forests of stiff, translucent fronds that grow from one another until they become walls, buttresses, and overhangs. In places the ground is more root than soil. In others, it is all stone again, empty for kilometers.

The scale is enormous and the density is strange. Life is plentiful, but not evenly spread. You can stand in a barren saddle where there is almost nothing but wind and frost, then descend two hundred meters into a trough so overgrown it feels like entering an interior organ. Between these zones are vast reaches of space: long ridgelines, open air, steep voids. Presence comes in islands and belts, separated by distance and altitude. That emptiness is part of the landscape’s rhythm.

Light is scarce most of the year. These uplands sit under long cloud decks and long winters, and even in clearer seasons the sun arrives slantwise and weak. Much of the world is perceived by contrast rather than color: pale rime on dark leaves, breath steaming against basalt, the faint green pulse of sap-lamps in the plant flesh at night. Not true bioluminescence exactly—more a chemical glow where frost stress and resin exchange overlap. Valleys can hold a twilight all day. Under thick growth, it is near-black at noon.

Water is intermittent but everywhere implied. There are few broad rivers. Instead: condensation dripping from undersides of leaf-arches, meltwater threading narrow channels, fog combed out of wind by forests of needle-fronds, shallow tarns that freeze, crack, thaw, and vanish. The air is damp in pockets, dry in others. You learn to read moisture by smell: iron-cold rock, sour peat, resin, wet lichen. Much of the ecosystem survives on capture rather than rainfall—trapping cloud, hoarding frost, siphoning trickles through woven root-cisterns.

It is loud, astonishingly loud.  
The emptiness amplifies everything. Wind scours the plateaus in sustained tones; whole slopes of hollow reed-trees act like organ pipes. Ice plates split on cliff faces with rifle cracks. Seed pods burst in chain reactions like snapping kindling. Animal calls travel far in thin air, so a hunt a valley away sounds immediate. At night, migration herds moving through dry stalk-forests create a continuous percussion—hooves, wingbeats, branch impacts, alarm whistles—like weather made of bodies.

Fauna is abundant and highly mobile. Grazers travel in dark ribbons along the vegetated belts, feeding on frost-softened growth and digging into root beds for stored starches. Predators are lean and long-legged, adapted to steep terrain and sudden sprints between cover islands. Burrowers heat the substrate and unintentionally irrigate it, punching vent holes that vent warm vapor into freezing air; around those vents, miniature jungles form. Aerial scavengers ride updrafts between cliff systems, barely flapping for hours. There are also dense invertebrate blooms whenever thaw pulses hit—brief, furious lives synchronized to moisture windows.

Human presence is sparse and old-in-the-making rather than ancient. Scattered structures appear where routes cross: hide-roofed ledge shelters, woven root platforms anchored to living trunks, stone windbreak circles, smoke-blackened overhangs, beacon masts made from stripped stalks and bone pins. Tools are simple—flaked stone, antler wedges, fiber cordage, resin glue, occasional cold-worked native metal when found—but expertly adapted. People here do not build against the landscape; they graft into it. A hut may be partly grown, partly woven, partly stacked rock, then abandoned to be reabsorbed by climbing vinewood in a season or two.

The rules of survival are practical and almost ritualized:

- Follow sound in clear weather, avoid sound in storms.  
- Sleep where condensation forms, not where runoff carves.  
- Never camp beneath singing reeds; wind shear will take them down.  
- If frost-smoke rises from ground vents, predators are near (prey gathers there).  
- Trust green glow for direction only at close range; at distance it bends with fog.

What makes the place unusual is not one impossible thing, but the way all processes run at once. Uplift is still shaping the land. Freeze-thaw is cutting it. Plants are engineering it. Animals are redistributing nutrients fast enough to alter whole corridors in a season. Weather forms locally around vegetated basins, spills over ridges, and collapses into sleet in the same afternoon. Nothing is settled. Nothing is fully bare either.

So the landscape feels young and already ancient in behavior: a cold, high, dark world where life has learned to occupy every opportunity, but only in patches, leaving great voids between green strongholds. A near-empty continent, loud with motion.
