The place is called a town, but it feels more like the inside of a damp machine.

It sits in a shallow basin barely above tide level, no wider than a long walk across. The land is young here—recently exposed mudstone and shelly clay, still deciding whether to be ground or water. Edges are sharp where runoff has cut narrow, clean gullies into the banks, and each rain redraws them. Nothing has had time to soften. Even the stones look newly broken, their faces raw and pale, weeping beads of cold water that gather, swell, and drop into the channels below.

Water is everywhere, but rarely dramatic. It rises through floorboards, sweats from walls, condenses on rope, drips from lintels, beads on skin. You hear it more than you see it: a patient tapping from thousands of points. The basin holds a mesh of hand-cut runnels and natural seams, all linked. At dawn they are shallow mirrors; by noon they are slow brown threads; by evening, fed by inland seep and a breathing tide, they fill enough to carry small flat boats. No one says “flood” unless the walkways disappear entirely. Most days the water is simply *present*.

Because the ground cannot be trusted, the settlement packs upward and inward. Timber platforms on driven piles, clay-plastered walls, reed matting, stacked storage lofts, ladders, gantries, fish-drying frames, rain screens, cistern pots, net racks—every cubic handspan has a function. There are almost no open squares. Passages are shoulder-tight and layered: a lower level for water and waste, a middle web for people, an upper fringe of roof-bridges where smoke and mist braid together. From outside, it looks like a low, tangled hive; from inside, like moving through the ribs of something alive.

Vegetation is sparse despite all the wet. A few rope-thin reeds cling to channel mouths. Moss grows only where smoke doesn’t blacken the plaster. In brighter patches, liverwort spreads in coin-sized fans, then disappears when trampling finds it. The dominant green is algae on wet stone, a skin rather than a plant. Fuel and fiber are imported from drier ground upriver, so every stalk and twig is counted, dried, reused, and eventually burned.

Animals are uncommon and oddly ceremonial when they appear. A pair of long-legged marsh birds visits after storms, stalking the runnels in silence. Eels thread the deeper channels during certain moons, and entire households wake to catch them in woven cone traps. Pale crabs live under platform shadows, rarely seen except by children with lamps. Dogs are impractical here; hoofed animals sink. Most protein comes from fish, shell scrape, and traded grain rather than local herds.

The tools are simple but adapted to wetness: bone awls, stone adzes, fired-clay pipes, wood pegs swollen tight by moisture, cordage thick with tar and resin. Little metal survives long in this water; iron pits, copper skins over, hinges seize. So construction assumes decay and replacement. Buildings are not monuments but habits—panels unlaced, piles reset, walkways shifted each season as channels migrate. The city is dense because it is always being repaired while occupied.

Sound is surprisingly restrained for such activity. No broad market roar, no wind across trees. Instead: paddle-knock against a post, rope under tension, distant mallet on wet wood, murmured barter, drip on ceramic, drip on hide, drip on water. In fog, voices travel around corners before bodies do. Quiet here is not emptiness; it is absorption. Soft materials, wet air, and low ceilings drink the noise.

The unusual part is this: the basin has rules, and everyone lives by them as if by law. No structure may block a primary runnel. No hearth at ground level. Nothing heavy stored on fresh-cut banks. Every roof must harvest condensation before rain. At first these read like engineering wisdom, but people speak of them with the gravity of taboos, because when ignored, the town answers quickly—banks slump overnight, floors tilt, a whole row of platforms settles by morning.

So the landscape is never still, though it is seldom loud. It is a compact wet world of near-ground clouds, raw edges, and constant maintenance: cool, crowded, and alive with small movements. A place that has only recently become habitable, and might decide, with little warning, to become water again.
