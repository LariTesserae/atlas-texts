The plateau arrives before you realize you’ve climbed to it.

One moment there are switchbacks and stone; the next, the land levels into a bright, wet expanse under a hard white sky. It is high country, but not the sharp kind. No dramatic peaks, no deep-cut ravines—just young ground spread wide, as if the mountain is still deciding what shape it will eventually become. The rock underfoot feels newly exposed: pale volcanic tuff, fresh basalt ribs, beds of gritty ash not yet sorted by long weather. Water sits close beneath all of it. Step wrong and the surface trembles.

Almost everything that is not rock is plant.  
Not forest—something flatter, more total. Matted sedges, thick moss, rushes, dwarf willow, and low, fibrous shrubs knit themselves into one continuous skin. In places the vegetation is so complete that soil is just rumor below roots and peat. The openness comes from height, not barrenness: the eye travels far across a living carpet, interrupted only by shallow pools, silver channels, and occasional machinery. The world is full of biomass and still somehow spacious, because it is mostly one layer, low and spread, under a huge sky.

Water is everywhere but rarely dramatic. It beads, seeps, weeps, condenses.  
The morning begins with a film of moisture on every blade. By noon, sun breaks through and the whole plain glitters painfully bright; each thread of grass holds a lens. Pools reflect sky like cut metal. Some streams have no banks yet—they are simply moving decisions in the plant mat, shifting by a hand’s breadth each week. Nothing here looks oldly carved. Drainage is still being negotiated between gravity, roots, and recent stone.

And yet the place is deafening.

At first it seems impossible, because nothing moves quickly. But the sound is constant and layered: wind shearing across open wetland; reed beds hissing; distant spillways roaring where channels drop over raw ledges; pump stations thudding in low rhythm; turbine housings whining; metal guy-wires singing. Even far from any structure, the ground itself amplifies water-noise through hollow peat and buried culverts. It is a broad, industrial surf with no shore, loud enough that speech becomes gesture.

The built world appears in scattered pieces, never enough to make a settlement.  
A concrete pump house on pilings. A line of rust-striped pipes crossing a marsh on trestles. A narrow-gauge service rail disappearing into sedge. Small transformer sheds with lichen climbing their walls. Windbreak fences. Gauge towers marked in faded paint. All of it practical, minimally inhabited, and set far apart as if the plateau were too wet, too bright, too exposed to permit closeness. Humans are present as maintenance rather than community.

The technology feels mid-century industrial and still working: cast housings, riveted steel, insulated cables, ceramic insulators, slow electric motors that never quite stop. The system seems designed to move water, measure water, or harvest moisture from air. At this altitude, clouds drag low and fast; condensers and mesh arrays gather what the sky gives, then pumps nudge it into reservoirs farther downslope. Nothing is sleek. Everything is durable, overbuilt, serviceable with gloves in cold wind.

Life beyond plants is sparse.  
Now and then a marsh bird stands motionless in a drainage ditch, then lifts away as a dark, deliberate stroke against the glare. Insects gather in dense local swarms over warm machinery, then vanish into the wet. Small mammals leave narrow runnels through moss but are rarely seen. Predators are almost absent. The ecology is dominated by growth, saturation, and decomposition: roots locking young ground, peat thickening by fractions, water chemistry slowly deciding which plants win the next decade.

The atmosphere is cool, never bitter. Jackets stay on; breath is visible at dawn, not at noon. Sunlight is intense because the air is thin and clear between cloud passes. When clouds arrive, they do not storm so much as occupy—rolling over the plain, condensing on everything, softening edges, then burning off. Change is measured in seasons and infrastructure logs, not moments. Despite the noise, this is a slow landscape: loud, bright, patient.

Its rule is simple: **surface is alive, subsurface is water, and both are still under construction.**  
Rock has not finished becoming land. Water has not finished choosing routes. Plants have moved in first and refuse to leave. Humans have inserted machines into that negotiation, not to conquer it, but to keep it legible. Everything here is provisional, stable only through constant small adjustments—the quiet kind that take years, and the loud kind you hear all day.
