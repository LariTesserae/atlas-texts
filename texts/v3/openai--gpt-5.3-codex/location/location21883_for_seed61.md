You find it by accident, because it was never meant to be found from above.

A crack in a quarry wall, a shoulder-width slot between two weathered slabs, and then a steep crawl down through stone that has been polished by generations of knees and wet hands. The air cools quickly. Not cold—just cave-cool, steady, damp enough that your skin is never fully dry. Every surface sweats.

Then the passage opens, and the place reveals itself: a buried district built inside a single long cavern, no wider than a market street, no taller than a cathedral nave, and packed almost solid with construction.

Not ruins. Not growing. Simply *there*, held in equilibrium.

---

The terrain is all mineral: black basalt ribs, pale limestone shelves, old flowstone that hangs like frozen cloth. Water threads through everything. It doesn’t rush in floods; it persists. It beads on ceilings, gathers on ledges, spills in thin curtains from one carved lip to another. The floor is never quite floor—more a layered system of channels, stepping slabs, and shallow runnels cut smooth by centuries of passage.

There is no green. No moss, no roots, no leaf litter, no timber scent. Where a surface looks soft, it’s mineral felt—fine crystal fuzz or fungal-like films that are more chemistry than life, feeding off trace salts and seep heat. The city has learned to live in a world where nothing sprouts.

So the built forms use what the cavern offers: stacked stone, fired clay blocks, lime plaster, rope made from animal fiber and mineral threads, hammered soft metals where they can be afforded, mostly in fasteners and hinges. Homes cling to walls and to one another, room pressed against room, stair crossing stair, bridges tucked under other bridges. Dense enough that from a distance it resembles a honeycomb grown by masons.

---

Light is dim and local. Bowls of rendered fat burn with short yellow flames; mineral lamps glow weakly where certain salts are heated; reflective mica is set into corners to steal brightness and spread it. No space is uniformly lit. You move through pockets: a bright workbench, a dark stair, a low amber arch, then shadow again.

But sound fills what light cannot.

The cavern is loud all the time, and not from chaos. It is loud from constancy.

Water hammers hollow stone drums built into spillways. Overflow chutes produce fixed notes. Clay pipes in wall niches whistle as pressure changes by fractions. Mill wheels clack. Chain lifts rattle. Sand clocks hiss. People speak in clipped phrases because the air is already crowded with noise. Children learn to sleep to percussion and dripping before they learn words.

After an hour, you realize the roar has pattern: this district is tuned. The engineers—if that word still applies to people working with chisels and plumb lines—shaped the channels so each terrace announces itself by sound. You can navigate blind by listening: deep thud for the cistern quarter, high chatter for the ceramic kilns, a broad rushing chord for the communal baths.

---

Ecology is sparse but stubborn. Fauna exists, just barely: translucent cave eels in the deepest pools, pale beetles in mortar cracks, thick-shelled scavenger crabs in sump basins, occasionally a blind bat drifting through upper dark. People trap little and waste nothing. Protein comes from fungal vats, from crustaceans, from traded goods lowered by winch from the surface.

Because there are few animals and no plants, rot is almost absent. What decays here decays slowly and cleanly. Bone lasts. Leather hardens and survives decades. A dropped tool can be found years later under a skin of calcite, exactly where it fell.

---

The place is old enough that erosion and architecture no longer compete. They collaborate.

Stairs are worn into troughs exactly matching the footfall of generations. Doorframes bulge with mineral deposits and are periodically chiseled back to function. Pillars are not decorative: they’re where the cave wanted to crack, now persuaded to hold. Water has rounded every sharp intention into something settled, a geometry negotiated between hand and drip.

Nothing dramatic happens here. That is one of its rules.

Water level stays within finger-width marks cut into shrine walls.
Temperature barely shifts across seasons.
The same bells ring the same intervals.
Repairs replace like with like.
New rooms are rare; old rooms are relined, reinforced, reassigned.

Time in this hidden district feels less like a line and more like a wheel with almost no wobble.

---

And yet it is not dead. The unusual quality comes from how alive *structure* is. The city listens to itself.

At night—if “night” means anything underground—the loudness drops only slightly. You hear maintenance: scraping mineral from channels, adjusting sluice gates by half a notch, tapping stone to check for hollows. People place their palms to walls the way others might test a forehead for fever. They trust vibration more than sight.

Maps are carved as sound instructions:  
“Follow the three-drop rhythm.”  
“Turn where the wall sings in two tones.”  
“Do not sleep beneath the fast drip.”

In a world without weather, this is their weather: acoustics, seep rates, and pressure.

---

So the landscape, in the end, is an intimate subterranean city-cistern—wet, dim, close-packed, loudly self-maintaining, built with simple tools into a mature stone body that has had centuries to become itself. No forests reclaimed it. No sun transformed it. It persists in cool mineral breath, in carved channels and echo, in the permanent present of dripping water.
