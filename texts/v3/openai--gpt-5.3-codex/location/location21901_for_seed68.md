At first glance it looks like a town that forgot to spread all the way out.

You stand on a low, fresh shelf of ground—new land, really, pressed together from dredge mud, crushed shell, and black slag. The edges are still sharp in places. Berms run straight for a while, then break into abrupt cuts where rain has bitten into them and stopped, as if erosion hasn’t had enough years yet to decide on a style. The whole district could be walked in an hour, maybe less: a compact basin ringed by levees, crossed by two rail spurs, three streets of brick workshops, and long empty strips between them where grass and wild cane have come up in orderly chaos.

Water is never absent, but never quite present either.  
There is no grand river in view, no broad lake, no constant stream. Instead: dampness. Rain that comes in short, silver bursts and leaves. Condensation dripping from pipe bridges in a steady indoor rhythm. Shallow sumps beneath machine halls where clear water gathers with an oily rainbow and then evaporates by noon. Ditches hold water for a day or two after weather, enough for reeds, mint, and white-flowered marsh creepers to bloom hard and fast, then sink back into green waiting.

Because of that rhythm, the vegetation is lush but patchy, like someone painted with a broad brush and lifted it often.  
Whole walls are furred in moss where steam vents breathe. Figs grow from cracks in retaining walls. Alder and willow saplings lean over culverts. Vines climb signal posts and telegraph frames until maintenance crews cut them back with hooked blades. Between buildings, open lots become dense meadows in spring—fox-tail grasses waist-high, broadleaf plants with cool wet stems, mats of clover thick enough to hide tools. Then, in drier weeks, the same lots flatten and pale, leaving only stubborn green around drains and shaded foundations.

The air is cool most of the year, not cold enough for bite, just enough to keep your skin aware.  
Mornings carry a metallic chill from wet rail. By midday, ordinary daylight settles cleanly over everything—no dramatic haze, no strange color cast, just an honest, full brightness that makes rust redder, moss greener, concrete whiter. Clouds move through often but don’t brood. Even on overcast days the light is workable, practical: the kind that keeps a yard crew moving.

It is loud here, and almost all of the loudness is repetitive.  
Stamp presses from the valve works: thump, pause, thump. Belt-driven saws whining in open sheds. Freight wagons rattling over plate joints. Over it all, birds—so many birds that machinery and ecology seem to compete for the same frequencies. Gulls from the tidal flats inland a few kilometers away. Marsh hens barking from drainage reeds. Starlings massing on wires and then erupting as one when a boiler hisses. Frogs take over after rain, filling every ditch with a blunt mechanical croak that echoes against corrugated walls.

Fauna thrives because this is edge habitat made permanent.  
Rodents feed on grain dust and seed heads. Snakes follow rodents into warm pipe culverts. Herons stalk sump ponds. Feral dogs patrol in loose, stable routes tied to butcher scraps and shift changes. Insects are constant: dragonflies over standing water, moths around sodium lamps, beetles under pallets where condensation drips. Nothing here is especially rare, but everything is numerous. Life stacks into niches quickly because the place itself is young and full of unfinished boundaries.

The built environment sits at a middle density: clearly urban, never crowded.  
Rows of two- and three-story brick blocks stand with deliberate gaps between them. Service yards open wide, fenced but mostly empty except for stacked timber, rusting drums, and weatherproof crates. Gantries stride over streets at intervals, carrying steam lines and power cables. At night, workshop windows go dark in sequence, but security lamps keep the skeleton of the town visible—rails, ducts, ladders, catwalks, all outlined like notes on staff paper.

Industry here is not frantic; it is patient.  
The engines are old enough to be trusted and new enough to be repaired. Pumps run continuously to manage seep and runoff. Condensers tick as they cool. A municipal siren marks four work phases, and almost everything—loading, cutting, firing, washing—follows those pulses. Change comes slowly: a new wall poured this season, a ditch deepened next season, a grove of volunteer willow becoming “official” only after it survives three winters. Even decay is gradual, measured, entered in ledgers.

The unusual quality of the place is subtle and accepted: moisture behaves socially.  
Condensation gathers most heavily where people linger—tea stalls, loading docks, bench corners, doorways where arguments happen. No one calls it magic, but everyone adjusts for it. Public awnings are set lower than in neighboring towns. Tool handles are wrapped in absorbent cloth. There are bylaws about shared drip lines between buildings. Children learn to read pavement darkening the way others read wind.

So the landscape becomes a compact treaty between fabrication and growth:  
fresh earth not yet softened by centuries, sparse structures with loud hearts, wetness arriving in intervals, and life crowding every damp seam. It is a place where metal sweats, grass remembers every leak, and the day’s brightest hour belongs equally to shift whistles and nesting birds.
