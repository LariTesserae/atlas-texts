From far away, it looks like a continent that forgot how to be rock.

The highlands are there—the altitude is undeniable, the air carries that thin-bellied openness of elevated country—but no sharp peaks survive. Everything has been worn patient and round: mile-wide domes of stone, long-backed ridges like sleeping animals, broad basins that hold cloud the way bowls hold milk. Erosion has had ages enough to smooth every argument out of the land. Cliffs still exist, but they are damp and softened, hanging gardens more than faces of stone, weeping from a thousand seams.

Between the domes lies water, almost everywhere. Not lakes in the simple sense, but saturated ground: peat-dark meadows, reed-thick hollows, shelves of moss that float when stepped on, slow channels braided through rootwork. The water table sits so near the surface that the earth never quite dries. Even when rain pauses, the place keeps sweating. Warm mist rises from sunless hollows. Leaves bead and drip. Lichened boulders gleam as if oiled. At dawn and dusk, whole valleys condense out of the air and then retreat.

And over all of it: plants, plants, plants—total occupation.

Trees are only one layer. Above, an uneven canopy of broadleaf crowns and rope-vines catches the dim light. Middle levels are packed with fern-fans, branch orchids, laddered creepers, and giant epiphyte bowls full of rainwater and tadpoles. Ground level is mostly not ground at all but a composite skin: moss over roots over fallen trunks over peat over old stone. You can walk for hours without touching mineral soil. In places, the vegetation has become architecture: buttress roots fuse into vaulted corridors, vine curtains make rooms, and old windthrows become living bridges as neighboring trunks root through them and knit them into the forest body.

Because the scale is so large, weather is local and self-made. Warm air rolls uphill from low countries and meets the cool high basins, and every afternoon ridges disappear into their own cloudbanks. One plateau can be damp and dripping while the next, only a few kilometers away, sits in silver haze with no rain at all. Storms are rarely violent here; they are cumulative. Week after week of soft rainfall, seepage, and fog does the work that elsewhere is done by floods.

It is mostly quiet. Not silent—never silent—but the soundscape is absorbed, softened by wet leaves and sponge-thick ground. You hear drops first: individual drips from canopy to understory, then the delayed answers below. Distant water moving through hidden stone. The dry click of beetle shells. The papery lift of wings. Occasionally a sudden, carrying call from something unseen, answered once and then not again for hours. Large animals are present in abundance, but many move with uncanny gentleness: broad-backed grazers that crop floating mats, long-legged marsh browsers threading reeds, furred climbers that feed upside down beneath boughs. Even predators seem shaped by the acoustics; ambush works better than chase in a place that muffles everything.

The fauna here is rich partly because the plants are rich, and partly because water is never far. Every hollow breeds life. Leaf axils hold micro-ponds. Rot pockets house entire food webs. Amphibians are everywhere, though rarely noticed until twilight when the understory fills with glistening eyes and soft throat-notes. Insects come in dense but localized blooms, rising from one bog and vanishing before reaching the next ridge, as if each basin keeps its own calendar.

Strangeness appears slowly, then all at once.

Some groves are arranged in near-perfect spirals kilometers wide, visible only from high domes. Certain vine species only flower along lines that correspond to buried fault fractures, tracing geology in luminous threads. There are “listening trees” with hollow buttresses that resonate at infrasonic frequencies when wind crosses particular saddles; animals gather near them before weather shifts. In the oldest basins, mats of liverwort and filament moss form patterned plains that react to touch hours later, closing tiny pores in expanding rings—like thought spreading through green tissue. Nothing behaves theatrically strange; rather, everything is just slightly off from expectation, as if biology had a few extra rules here.

There are built things, but sparse and half-claimed. On some ridge shoulders stand single stone rings or low walls, made of locally split slabs now rounded by age and quilted in roots. Here and there, narrow causeway lines cross marsh fingers—old raised paths of packed gravel and woven branchwork, mostly sunk but still legible as straight intentions in a curved world. No metal, no glass, no active machinery. Whatever made these traces belonged to patience and hand-work, and left long ago or became indistinguishable from the ecology. In many places, you cannot tell where construction ended and growth began.

Time in this landscape is slow, but not still. Change comes by accretion: one more season of peat, one more trunk laid down and inhabited, one more seep carving a new silver thread through old stone. Whole valleys migrate from open wet meadow to thicket to forested bog over centuries. Ridges slump by centimeters. Species ranges slide upslope and downslope with long climate breaths. Nothing hurries except brief lives—mayflies, fungal blooms, hatchlings—and even those feel embedded in a larger patience.

If the place has a governing rule, it is this: nothing remains bare for long, and almost nothing is ever fully dry. Rock becomes sponge, sponge becomes soil, soil becomes root, root becomes structure, structure becomes habitat, habitat becomes weather, weather returns as water, and the cycle thickens. At this elevation, under this dim warm sky, the world has not stopped changing—it has simply learned to change by layering instead of breaking.
