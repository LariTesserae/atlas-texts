From a distance, it looks like a continent of old stone bowls stacked into the sky.

The land is high—so high that clouds don’t pass over it so much as lean against it. Long escarpments, worn smooth by ages of runoff, step upward into broad terraces where whole districts have been built. Nothing here is young in shape. Ridges are rounded, canyon walls are grooved and polished, and every ledge has the softened profile of something water has touched for a very long time. The erosion is not dramatic anymore; it is settled, mature, almost architectural. Ravines know exactly where they are going.

Water is never far away, but it rarely appears as dramatic rivers. It beads on iron rails at dawn, seeps out of dark seams in retaining walls, gathers in shallow stone pans, and threads through gutter-canals that line every street. After any rain—and rain comes often enough to matter—the whole city glistens for days. Rock faces weep in slow, regular drips. Cistern towers sweat. Mist hangs under bridges. The ground is mostly paved, tiled, or naturally vitrified, yet if you pry up a slab, moisture is right there, cold and metallic-smelling, close under the surface.

There is almost no plant life. No grass in cracks, no moss, no vine reclaiming ruins. Walls remain bare unless stained by minerals. Open lots don’t green; they crust. The living film of the place is thinner and stranger: bacterial sheens in drainage channels, fungal lace in steam tunnels, pale chemotrophic mats in warm spillways where mineral-rich water flows constantly. Life here begins with stone, heat, and dissolved metals, not sunlight and leaves.

And yet the fauna are overwhelming.

Everything moves with animals. The sky is thick with wheeling flocks of slate-winged gliders that nest in vent chimneys. Drain mouths pulse with slick-bodied scavengers the size of dogs, blind until they surface and their eyes clear. Long-legged waders pick through sediment basins, clicking their beaks on ceramic edges. Armored burrowers tunnel under foundations, guided by vibration, emerging at night to scrape mineral crust and hunt anything smaller than themselves. In the upper districts, domesticated pack-beasts with bony feet climb stairways cut directly into basalt, carrying tools and ore between foundries.

Because there are no forests and no fields, the city itself is the habitat. Fauna feed on fungal growth in heat ducts, on industrial byproducts, on each other, on the constant rain of scraps from markets and processing yards. Whole food webs are mapped to infrastructure: turbine runoff supports filter colonies; filter colonies feed crustacean swarms; swarms feed the birds; dead birds feed canal scavengers; scavengers feed rendering plants. The line between “wild” and “urban” doesn’t hold for long here.

Built space is dense enough that streets feel like channels in a reef. Buildings interlock, bridge, stack, and buttress each other across centuries of additions. Older stone viaducts carry new steel conveyors; ceramic stormways run beneath electric tram-lines; workshops hang from cliff walls on anchor-bolts thicker than tree trunks (if trees existed here). The industrial layer is everywhere but weathered into belonging: smokestacks patched with old brick, turbines housed in carved caverns, pump stations decorated by mineral runoff into accidental murals of copper green and sulfur yellow.

The sound is loud, but not frantic. A constant loudness: pump rhythms, tram brakes, flock cries, warning bells at locks, far hammering from forge districts, water slapping culvert mouths. Even the animals contribute steady noise—chittering colonies under grates, drumming feet on rooftop tiles, deep mating calls echoing through transit tunnels at dusk. You don’t get silence, but you do get patterns. People here tell time by recurring sound the way others use shadows.

Light is ordinary, almost mercifully so. No alien skies, no permanent gloom. Days are bright, often diffused by wet air; nights are clear enough between cloud banks to show sharp stars. The unusualness of the place is in combinations, not impossibilities: marsh humidity at altitude, abundant animal life without visible flora, heavy industry integrated into a hydrological landscape so old it has stopped resisting and started collaborating.

On the scale of years, little changes quickly. District lines move by meters, not miles. New channels are cut only after decades of planning. Species populations rise and fall in long pulses tied to monsoon cycles and maintenance schedules. Stone settles. Metal rusts and is replaced. Reservoir levels breathe with the seasons. Catastrophe is rare; adjustment is constant. The world runs on slow gradients.

If this place has a rule, it is this: **nothing is abandoned, everything is absorbed**. Water absorbs architecture, architecture absorbs industry, industry absorbs animals, animals absorb waste, waste feeds the underlife, and underlife returns minerals to water. The city is not built on the landscape so much as folded into an old, wet, elevated geology that had already learned patience before anyone laid the first road.
