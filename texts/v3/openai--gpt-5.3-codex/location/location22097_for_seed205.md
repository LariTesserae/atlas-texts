From far away, it looks empty.

That is the first trick of the high green plateau: distance erases detail, and detail is everything here. You can stand on one wind-rounded ridge and see eighty kilometers of ochre and green, a continent of open ground under ordinary white daylight, and think: sparse, survivable, maybe quiet.

Then the wind shifts, and the place reveals what it really is.

---

The land sits high enough that horizons are sharp and storms can be seen being born. Old stone shoulders push up through the surface in long, weathered ribs, not jagged mountain peaks but patient uplands worn into broad backs and shallow basins. Erosion has done its work and stopped hurrying. Slopes are stable. Gullies are old. Nothing looks newly broken.

But almost no bare ground is truly dead ground.

The “soil” is mostly plant body: layered root felt, fibrous mats, bulbous water-storing knots, and plate-like crusts of living tissue that harden in heat and soften at night. In places it has the spring of peat, in others the brittle ring of fired clay. The color map shifts with moisture—gray-green in dry weeks, black-green after rain, rust-red where the plant mats bleed tannins through mineral dust.

Trees exist, but not as forests in the ordinary sense. They rise in scattered giants, each one with a territory around it: columnar trunks forty meters high, bark like braided rope, crowns exploded into broad parasol canopies. Between them stretch wide intervals of open plant plain—low succulents, whip-grasses, crawling vines that bury themselves by day and unfurl at dusk. Vegetation is total, yet crowded only in local bursts; the vastness between major forms gives the whole plateau its sparse feel.

Water is stingy but not absent. Rain comes hard and brief, then vanishes into root lattices and stone cisterns. Most moisture arrives sideways: dawn fog snagged by leaf combs, condensation wrung from nighttime temperature drops, cloud-shadow drizzles that wet one valley and skip the next. Every basin has pockets of damp, and every pocket has life stacked in layers.

---

Sound is the second trick.

At noon, under clear light, the plateau can be painfully loud. Not from rivers or human industry, but from animals and plant structures that have turned wind into instrument.

The giant trees are hollow in sections; as hot air climbs through them, they moan and flute. Seed pods rattle like dry bones. Strip-leaves slap each other in the gusts like cloth snapped on a line. Insects—if “insects” is still the right word for meter-long chitinous gliders and ground-boring drummers—keep continuous rhythm. Herds of six-legged grazers with keratin beaks grind and call and stamp. Predatory flyers scream overhead in spirals. Somewhere, always, thousands of throats pulse in mating choruses from hidden wet hollows.

It adds up to a pressure more than a noise. Travelers learn to read each band of sound: buzz-fields of nectar swarms, low booming from territorial root-boars, the sudden silence that means a shadow-hunter is passing.

Silence does happen, but mostly as omen.

---

Fauna dominates motion here.

Plants are the architecture, but animals are the weather inside that architecture. Migrations cross the plateau in belts tens of kilometers wide, following bloom fronts that move after isolated rains. Burrow colonies rework entire slopes, aerating root mats and exposing mineral pockets that certain vines require. Pollinators are not small: furred, warm-blooded fliers with translucent sails drift from canopy to canopy at dusk, carrying fist-sized pollen clumps. Carrion disappears in minutes into layered scavenger guilds.

Even the “small” life is overwhelming by number. Ant-like cutters strip a giant crown in one afternoon and leave it alive, because the tree responds by dropping narcotic sap that paralyzes only their predators. Mite-clouds feed on condensation films and rise in shimmering veils each morning, prey for net-winged hunters that can bank in place against cliff winds.

Predator-prey relations are old and negotiated, not chaotic. Warning colors are respected. Certain paths are ceded by common agreement of species at certain hours: ridge lanes at dawn for horned runners, low basins at night for blind grazers that navigate by vibration. Break these rhythms and the plateau punishes confusion quickly.

---

Human presence is intermittent but legible.

You find it where the land already offers structure: under overhanging root-arches, on stone knuckles above seasonal mist pools, at the bases of the largest canopy columns. Built forms are small—woven towers, hide roofs, clay-lined pits, ladders stitched from vine fiber. Nothing tries to dominate the landscape; everything hooks into it temporarily.

Technology is hand-scaled and clever: stone blades for cutting resin, bone awls for piercing bark-skin, fired ceramic condensers set overnight to gather drinking water from fog. Some groups work early copper beaten thin for hooks and rivets, but metal is rare and precious compared to fiber, shell, and hardened sap composites.

People mark routes with sound as much as sight. Hanging seed-rattles tuned to specific winds identify clan territories. Hollow gourds buried at trail forks boom differently depending on direction. A traveler can navigate in dust or darkness by listening for the right tone in the right gust.

Permanent cities do not take hold on this plateau. The living substrate shifts subtly over seasons, and heavy foundations are swallowed or cracked. Settlements stay light, migratory, and conversational with the plant body beneath them.

---

The strange part is not that life is abundant. It is how coordinated it feels.

Many plants here respond to vibration thresholds. A herd’s footfall can trigger nectar release kilometers ahead, opening flowers before pollinators arrive. Certain canopy trees “decide” storm posture collectively—leaves folding in waves from ridge to ridge, reducing wind drag before squalls hit. Root mats in damp basins redistribute stored water toward damaged zones after fire, as if pressure messages travel through them.

Outsiders call it intelligence and mean something mystical. Residents call it etiquette and mean something practical: everything listens, so move with care.

There are rules, and most are acoustic:

- Do not shout near spawning pools; the larvae surface to low tones and die in dry air.
- Do not drum after sunset on eastern ridges; it draws cliff gliders that hunt by resonance.
- Do not sleep beneath singing trunks in storm season; pressure reversals split them without warning.
- If the insects stop and the leaves begin to hiss in unison, climb. Ground predators are moving.

---

At evening, heat lifts but never fully leaves. The sky remains bright longer at elevation, and then drops quickly into violet. Moisture beads along every edge. The plant crust softens. Night-bloomers uncurl and release heavy, fermented perfumes that carry for kilometers. The deafening day chorus gives way to an even denser night pulse—lower, throatier, full of clicks and tremors.

From a high ridge, fires appear sometimes: not wildfires, but human signal braziers in protected bowls, smoky and brief. Around them, tiny compared to the continental spread of living terrain, people scrape resin, mend fiber armor, set out condensation nets, and wait for the next dawn fog.

In ordinary light, from a distance, it still looks empty.

Up close, it is one of the loudest, busiest, most inhabited places on the planet—an old high world where plants are ground and wall and weather memory, where water is hunted from air, where animals write the daily map, and where survival depends less on conquering the land than on hearing it in time.
