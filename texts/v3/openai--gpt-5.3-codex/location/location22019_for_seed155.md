From a distance, the place looks like a high country that forgot whether it was a city or a forest and became both permanently.

It spreads across an old uplifted basin near the top of a mountain province: broad, wind-planed shelves of stone, shallow valleys polished into long curves by extinct ice, ridgelines worn down to patient backs. The geology feels ancient in the way old bones feel ancient—nothing sharp unless it was recently broken, everything settled into a shape that has lasted too long to argue with. Elevation keeps the air cool and thin. The sky is clear more often than not, light ordinary in color but hard in clarity, as if distances are overpronounced.

And yet bare ground is almost absent. Vegetation has taken total possession—not as lush jungle, but as infrastructure-scale botany. The “soil” is mostly rootfelt: meters-thick interwoven mats of woody rhizomes, moss fiber, and plate-like leaves hardened into load-bearing strata. Streets are cut through living tissue that keeps healing at the edges. Retaining walls are braided trunks. Bridges are trained vine-bundles anchored to old pylons. What would be vacant lots in another city are here low plains of breathing green membrane, flexing faintly with sap pressure.

Density is strange: total plant coverage, but sparse presences. Buildings and major growth-columns stand far apart, each one commanding a lot of space around it, connected by long causeways and cable lines. Between districts are open wind courts, broad terraces, and kilometers of cultivated canopy with almost no people visible. It feels urban at regional scale rather than block scale: a city stretched out until each neighborhood has weather of its own.

Moisture arrives in episodes. Rain comes in brief cold sheets, then vanishes for days. Most water is harvested from air: condenser towers, mesh sails strung between spires, copper gutters that sweat at dawn. In hollows, water gathers in dark basins lined with black reed and pale fungal bark. Every surface beads with intermittent condensation—railings, leaves, the sides of boilers. Dampness is local, pocketed, never lush enough to blur the edges of things.

Industry is grafted directly into the plant body. Steam plants sit in trunk-citadels where sap is tapped, fermented, and cracked into fuel oils and polymer resins. Conveyor belts run through green tunnels. Piston houses thump under pergolas. Turbines are mounted on ridge crowns where crosswinds are permanent. The city smells of wet bark, hot metal, ozone, and tannin. At all hours you hear it: fan-hum, valve scream, chain clatter, pressure releases, tram cables singing, and beneath that the continuous leaf-noise of high-altitude wind. Loud, never chaotic—an organized roar.

Fauna is surprisingly scarce. A few tough species persist: slate-wing swifts nesting in vent shafts, slow six-legged grazers in protected terraces, translucent moths that feed on condenser algae. No great herds, no predator chorus. Pollination is partly mechanical: drifting clockwork kites dusted with spores, released at intervals from municipal towers.

The weirdness is not decorative; it’s infrastructural. Reality here has thinned along old fault grids. Distance can buckle: two towers seen a kilometer apart may touch by footbridge if approached in fog. Echoes sometimes arrive before their source. Rain falls in vertical curtains that stop at property lines. Certain intersections repeat one block unless crossed while carrying a living branch. The city has regulations for this: painted orientation glyphs, daily bell tones to re-synchronize public clocks, right-of-way laws for “phase overlap events.” People treat ontological instability the way coastal towns treat tides—dangerous, mundane, managed.

At dusk, normal sunlight drains quickly from the high basin and the vegetation begins to glow faintly with bioluminescent stress proteins, green-white seams outlining roads and root walls. Industrial lamps come on in grids. Condenser mists creep uphill. Shift sirens call across kilometers. You can stand on an old eroded parapet and see the whole open organism-city breathing: scattered towers, long dark intervals between them, moving weather, moving machinery, and the plant substrate underneath all of it, alive enough to count as ground and architecture at once.
