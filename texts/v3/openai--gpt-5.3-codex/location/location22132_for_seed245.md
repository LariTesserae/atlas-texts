At first it looks like a floodplain after a storm—flat, wet, heat-shimmering—but the longer you stand in it, the less “plain” and “after” apply.

This is a low world-basin the size of a country, all of it made by hand-scale logic and continental ambition: fired brick laid in endless fields, packed clay embankments, mortared stone ribs, ceramic channels stitched together with pegs and lashings of spun mineral fiber. There is no native ground beneath it. When a slab cracks, you don’t find soil; you find another layer of construction, older and rougher, then another, then another, as if the world is a stack of attempts.

Water is everywhere but rarely deep. It sweats out of joints. It beads on vertical walls. It crawls along seams and disappears into vented culverts that exhale warmth like breath. After each convective burst overhead, the whole basin glistens—not with rain puddles in dirt, but with thin mirrored sheets spread across tile, plaster, and black slag. In places, condensation hangs in the air as a moving veil, and droplets gather on the undersides of arches, falling upward for a few heartbeats before choosing a new down.

The heat is constant and bodily: kiln heat, bathhouse heat, the heat of stones that were fired recently and never fully cooled. The light is dim despite all this warmth. The sky is a lid of mineral haze, copper-gray and bruised violet, lit from nowhere in particular. At noon, shadows are faint and close to their objects. At night—if “night” is the right word—the basin is outlined by dull red seams where expansion joints glow, and by occasional pale flashes inside distant towers as if lightning is being made indoors.

Because erosion is barely begun, almost everything has first-edged sharpness. Stair corners are crisp; tool marks are readable at arm’s length and from miles away through field lenses. Channels cut last week still look newborn. Even wet surfaces don’t soften into age here. Turbulence arrives before weathering can. Whole sections of embankment heave, settle, and re-lock in new alignments. Reservoir walls split and are somehow already buttressed by rough fresh masonry, as though invisible crews with stone mauls and plumb lines never stop working.

And yet the place is mostly quiet.

No birds, no insects, no grazing, no human chatter—no animals at all. The loudest things are intermittent: a far-off collapse like a drum struck under blankets; the hiss of steam venting from a cracked manifold; the clack of ceramic gate teeth ratcheting one notch at a time. Between these, silence spreads wide and damp. Mist absorbs echoes. Even rushing water often moves under covers, below grates, inside walls.

Life, in the familiar sense, never took hold. No roots pry the masonry apart. No moss softens the shade. Instead there are process-ecologies: rust fans blooming overnight on iron clamps and flaking away by afternoon; white mineral whiskers growing from salt joints; translucent films assembling on warm stones and dissolving when the pressure shifts. Nothing with eyes. Nothing with hunger. But patterns recur, compete, vanish—chemistries acting like neighborhoods.

The built forms are primitive in method and colossal in result. You can read rope abrasion on lifting posts taller than citadels. Counterweight pits descend like wells to darkness, lined with hand-set brick. Sluice doors are slabs moved by treadwheel arrays big enough to be villages, though no village stands beside them now. Every solution is mechanical, tactile, pre-precision: wedges, pins, gravity, leverage, heat. No seamless alloys, no humming circuits. Just craft multiplied to impossible scale.

Reality frays most at the margins of the floodworks. Distances fail politely first—an obelisk that seems two hours away is reached in fifteen minutes, while a nearby culvert retreats as you approach. Then it fails rudely: canals loop into themselves but carry water onward; reflected structures in shallow sheets are not mirrored but alternative, with different buttress counts and door placements; once in a while, a new wall appears already old in one direction and freshly laid in the other. Survey stakes disagree from dawn to dusk. Maps are written in pencil and revised constantly, then become wrong again before drying.

Storm-state here doesn’t always mean wind and rain. Sometimes it means geometry convulsing. Whole sectors of platform tilt a few degrees, enough to redirect sheets of warm runoff into basins that did not exist yesterday. Sometimes it means thermal uprush: columns of hot damp air rising in silence until the haze condenses into sudden vertical rain, a rain that threads through lattice roofs and collects beneath them rather than above. Sometimes it means construction events: blocks extruded from mud presses begin to stack themselves in rough spirals, then stop, leaving unfinished towers like thoughts interrupted.

Stand long enough and the place resolves into a single rule: this world is not ruined, and it is not complete. It is perpetually in the act of being made—using old hands, simple tools, vast plans, and unstable laws. A marsh of architecture. A kiln-wet continent of beginnings.
