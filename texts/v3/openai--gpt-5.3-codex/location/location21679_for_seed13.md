The city sits on a high shoulder of new stone, where the mountains look less carved than *assembled*—slabs tilted up, edges still sharp, gullies too clean to be old. Nothing here has had time to round off. Cliffs break in straight faces of dark basalt and pale ashstone, and the valley floors are broad enough to feel open, almost spare, with long distances between one cluster of life and the next.

From far away, it doesn’t look like one city. It looks like many small settlements that agreed to share a plateau: districts of low buildings, then empty ground, then another district; walled orchards separated by bare lava flats; cistern yards, then silence. Streets are wide and often unoccupied. Space is treated as a material, as deliberately placed as stone.

The air is cool all day, cool enough that breath occasionally shows at dawn and at the edges of shade. The light is ordinary and clear—no dramatic perpetual dusk, no colored sky—just a stable, honest daylight that never seems to tilt toward evening. You do not get weather in the usual sense. No storms roll in, no fronts arrive. At fixed intervals, cloud fingers from the lower valleys climb the escarpments, spill across certain ridges, and condense on everything left out to receive them: nets, comb-walls, ceramic spines, ropes of rough fiber. The moisture gathers, drips, and is led away through channels cut by hand. Then it stops. Then clear air again. Always the same sequence. Always the same volume.

Because of that, water exists in pockets and plans. There are no broad rivers crossing the city, only stone-lined runnels, cistern courts, and narrow terraced basins sunk into the lee of walls. Some neighborhoods are dry underfoot except for morning beading on every surface; others are perpetually damp where condensation drains. Mosses map these microclimates precisely—thick green on north walls and cistern rims, almost none on exposed paving. Ferns rise from cracks where driplines persist. You can walk two blocks and pass from dusty pumice to a lush sunken garden.

Vegetation is rich where it can be, but never continuous. This is not a forested highland; it is a patchwork of deliberate fertility. Fig-like trees with silver undersides stand in enclosed courts. Reeds and broadleaf herbs crowd the margins of catchponds. Vines are trained up lattice towers, not for beauty alone but to comb moisture from the air. Between these productive islands are open expanses: stony plazas, bare terraces, old lava fields used as transit ground.

The built fabric is sophisticated without being technologically dense. No steel, no glass walls, no engines. Construction is dry-laid block, mortared rubble, timber beams, fired clay pipe, hammered copper fittings where needed and treasured. Roofs are shallow and wide, made to harvest condensation rather than shed rain. Every district has raised walkways of wood and rope over the damp channels, and stair-routes etched directly into bedrock. Infrastructure is visible and tactile: you can trace water from fog net to gutter to settling jar to public basin with your finger.

Animal life is everywhere, and mostly unhurried. Long-legged cliff goats move along impossible ledges above the city and descend at set times to mineral licks near the reservoirs. Ground birds with fan tails forage in the dry squares, then vanish into wet courtyards when mist comes. Pale amphibians thrive in storage cisterns and are considered signs of water purity. In the sky, broad-winged gliders circle on thermal habits that never vary. Insects are abundant around green pockets—bees, hover moths, water gnats—but even they seem to follow fixed rhythms instead of outbreaks.

It is a quiet place, not because it is empty, but because nothing needs to shout over change. You hear sandals on pumice. Rope creaking. The thin plink of drops into jars. Wingbeats overhead. Animal calls are brief and repeated at dependable times. Work sounds are hand sounds: stone scraping, wooden mallets, grinding grain, knife on reed. Even in market courts, voices stay low, as if everyone has learned that the plateau carries noise farther than intention.

The unusual rule of this landscape is not magic in the flashy sense—it is invariance. Things grow, hatch, heal, and are built, but the *conditions* do not drift. The mist arrives exactly as it did generations ago. The damp zones do not migrate. The clear sky never surprises. Erosion is stalled, so escarpments remain fresh-faced and debris fans stay neatly angular. Ecologies settle into narrow bands and remain there. The city’s planning philosophy comes directly from this: don’t fight flux, because there is none; instead, tune each craft, field, and courtyard to a known, permanent pattern.

So the place feels both young and old at once. Young in its geology—raw edges, uncompromised forms. Old in its habits—techniques repeated until they become architecture, architecture repeated until it becomes law. It is a high, cool urban archipelago in stone: sparse in footprint, lush in pockets, damp by design, alive with animals, and held in a calm that never breaks.
