The city is under the crust, but it does not feel buried. It feels *hollowed*—a regional basin of caverns linked by broad tunnels, each chamber wide enough to hold weather, districts, and migration routes. The ceiling is high and dark with mineral ribs, and the floor is mostly flat, powdery, and pale with crushed salts and dry clays. No streams cut it, no dampness gathers in corners. Even the air seems to have had its wetness filtered out.

And yet almost everything that matters here is plant.

Not leafy forest-plant, but structural plant: columns of segmented stalkstone, mats of gray-green filament, fan-shaped fronds stiff as sheet metal, root-bridges that span transit cuts, and vast low “lawns” of succulent scales that never bead with dew because there is no dew to bead. The vegetation is total in reach but sparse in placement. It does not crowd; it occupies. Between one living mass and the next are long corridors of open ground where dust drifts and carts can pass side by side for kilometers.

The plants drink other chemistries. Their tissues wick nitrates, sulfates, and vapor-phase solvents from the mine air. Their sap is oily, amber, and flammable in thin films. Their leaves are matte-black on one side and silver on the other, flipping slowly with convection to regulate heat. Most of them root directly into unfinished geology—newly exposed seams, crumbly ashstone, fresh-cut walls. This is a young underland, still becoming itself: not yet worn smooth by age, still angular, still provisional.

Light is ordinary in brightness but not in source. There are municipal light-rails strung along the ceiling, banks of broad-spectrum lamps powered by turbine rows deeper in the works. There are also plant lanterns: bulb clusters that glow softly when charged by induction towers. Day and night are imposed by schedule, not sun; every district keeps phase-time so growth cycles and shift cycles overlap efficiently. When “dawn” comes, entire terraces of leaf-metal unfurl in sequence, reflecting white light into side streets.

The built environment has the logic of an industrial town and the biology of a greenhouse that forgot water. Foundries sit beside seed-refineries. Conveyor belts run through living trellises. Rail spines thread the chambers, carrying ore one direction and biomass bricks the other. Habitation blocks are grafted into giant trunkworks—apartments grown as cavities inside woody tissue, then lined with ceramic and wire. Ventilation towers rise like chimneys and like trees, because they are both.

It is mostly quiet. That is the first surprise. In an active place you expect roar, but sound is absorbed by fibrous bark, porous stone, dust drapes, and hanging root-curtains. You hear distance: a stamp press thudding three chambers away, chain haulers rattling beneath grates, the thin electric whine of tramlines, the dry hiss of pollen harvesters. Nearby, footsteps are soft. Voices carry only if spoken upward.

Fauna is abundant and practical. Herds of six-legged grazers scrape mineral lichens from support columns. Long-necked tunnel birds nest in cable trays and feed on luminous mites. Blind fox-sized scavengers follow freight lines for seed-cake spills. Pollinator swarms move like weather fronts through plantation wards, guided by low-frequency hum emitters. Predators exist, but they are more ambush than chase—stillness is easier than speed on dry powder.

The strangeness is not monstrous; it is procedural. Every organism is part of infrastructure, and every piece of infrastructure is expected to live, molt, seed, or rot. Streets are replanted, not resurfaced. Bridges are pruned. Zoning maps include flowering periods and predation windows. Fire codes account for resin storms. Transit halts for root heave. Civic law distinguishes between cuttable tissue, protected growth, and “thinking groves” that have accumulated enough sensory mesh to qualify as legal neighborhoods.

Nothing here feels anciently settled. Survey stakes still mark future districts. Excavation fronts advance weekly. New light wells are drilled, new seed-lines tested, new fauna introduced and monitored. The undercity is in a state of organized emergence: raw rock becoming field, field becoming factory, factory becoming habitat.

So the landscape holds a paradox comfortably: dry but verdant, urban but spacious, industrial but hushed, subterranean but lit like day. It is a place where life learned to build without rain, and where a city decided that concrete was too dead to scale.
