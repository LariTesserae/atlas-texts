At this altitude, the world is all edge and plane.

You arrive on a **manufactured high basin**—not carved by rivers, but poured, riveted, and tensioned into existence: a valley-sized arrangement of terraces, retaining walls, suspended decks, and sloped service lanes that imitate geology without ever being soil. Every surface is made: cast ceramic aggregate, oxidizing steel mesh, translucent polymer slabs, enamel conduit, glass brick. Even the “cliffs” are layered infrastructure—power trunks, coolant galleries, freight shafts—sheathed in structural skin.

It feels open in reach but dense in body. You can see far—across kilometers of platforms, cranes, chimney arrays, cable bridges, and stacked habitation blocks—but wherever you stand, something is always near: a parapet, a vent stack, a rail, an inspection ladder, a nest, a line of pipes. The place extends like a regional landscape yet behaves like a crowded room.

Moisture is intermittent, patient, and everywhere in traces. There are no broad lakes, no true streams; instead, water appears as **condensation economies**. Fog collectors comb the thin air. Cool rain arrives in short, precise weather windows, then drains along chamfered channels into cistern slots. Seep-lines bead on north-facing walls. Under catwalks, droplets gather and let go at regular intervals, making tiny ticking falls. In service courtyards, shallow reflective pans hold water just long enough for life to claim their edges before pumps reclaim it. Dampness is less an event than a persistent condition of joints, corners, undersides.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. You see cable-moss in insulation seams, pale frond mats in gutter shadows, rust-root vines that follow bolt lines, and occasional engineered shrubs in ballast boxes where maintenance crews once tried to “green” the district and mostly failed. The plants grow as annotations rather than cover—small, bright statements in a mostly mineral sentence.

Despite the sparse flora, fauna is abundant—because this ecosystem feeds on heat, residue, shelter, and each other more than on leaves. Flocks of narrow-wing glide birds wheel between turbine housings and perch in thermal exhaust. Rodent-sized six-legged scavengers patrol conveyor troughs for organics and lubricant gels. Quiet ungulates with silicone-like hoof pads graze lichen off warm pipe jackets at dawn. In upper gantries, nesting colonies of filter-feeders spread membranous fans into vent outflow, harvesting airborne particulates as if they were plankton in a current. Predators exist, but ambush here is architectural: a shadow behind louvers, a still shape in a maintenance alcove.

The light is strikingly bright. At this elevation the sky is hard, clear, and thin, and the built surfaces throw light back at each other until even shaded alleys glow. Add to that process illumination—arc strips, warning beacons, inspection floods—and the whole district is luminous without feeling warm. Colors skew cool: white glare, steel blue, sodium amber, caution red. At noon, edges are razor-sharp. At dusk, the lights don’t come on so much as reveal they were always there.

And yet the soundscape is mostly quiet. Not silent—never silent—but subdued. Industry here was designed for acoustic discipline. Motors sit on magnetic bearings, turbines are baffled deep in honeycomb housings, freight runs on soft-contact tracks, and pneumatic pulses happen behind thick dampers. What you hear: a low continental hum through your boots, distant relay clicks, thin wind across tension cables, water ticks from condensation lines, the occasional brief animal cry. In a landscape this crowded, the restraint of noise feels uncanny, almost ceremonial.

The strangeness arrives gradually, then all at once. Perspectives misbehave. Two parallel corridors may converge into a shared distance, then separate again when you blink. Rain sometimes falls upward along particular facades, returning to the cloud net. Shadows detach from their objects during high-output electrical cycles and lag by a second or two. Entire blocks occasionally appear “newer than now,” as if one construction phase overlays another. Locals treat this as weather. They mark safe paths with moving paint that chooses stable geometry each morning.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to learn them:

- Do not trust any horizon line that crosses more than three tower gaps.
- Where condensation is warm, machinery is thinking.
- Animals avoid phase-unstable joints before instruments can detect them.
- If a stair repeats the same landing number twice, go back immediately.
- The quietest districts are the most active; loud places are usually broken.

Change happens slowly here. Corrosion blooms by millimeter. New decks are added in seasonal campaigns. Ecological niches settle over years around a single leaking flange. Even the reality glitches have cycles, strengthening under certain pressure systems, easing under others. The whole place feels young not because it is unfinished, but because it is still deciding what kind of world it is: factory, city, cliffland, aviary, machine, weather—none exclusive, all true at once.
