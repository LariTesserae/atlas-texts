At this height, the world should have been rock, wind, and lichen.  
Instead, it is a bowl of cold water suspended in the mountains.

The basin is an old caldera, its rim dark and stable, worn down into rounded teeth by ages of rain and freeze. Water fills it to the lip and keeps filling: cloudbanks drag across the rim, condense, and pour themselves in. There is no shoreline in the ordinary sense. Stone steps, terraces, and old streets descend from the crater wall and vanish under green-blue water, as if a town were gently lowered and left there. You can still follow its plan by the straightness of things: causeways, marker-pillars, gate-arches, all softened by moss and root, but undeniably made.

Vegetation has taken the role of ground. Thick rafts of interlocked stems float in districts, each raft anchored by rope-like roots to masonry below. Some are reed-thatch islands, some are mats of broad, waxy leaves, some are forests of hollow, buoyant trunks that rise through the water like bundles of pipes. Underneath, vine curtains and root lattices create rooms, alleys, courtyards. If you push down through one layer you enter another: bright leaf-canopy above, shadowed stem-city below, then the old stone quarter lower still where everything is furred with water fern and pale algae. In places the plants have grown so thoroughly through walls and windows that the architecture now seems carved *out of* living tissue.

The built parts are extensive but not crowded. This is an urban place laid out in separate neighborhoods, each with open channels between them wide enough for current, fish schools, and transport canoes. Platforms of lashed timber, baked clay floats, and stitched bark pontoons form intersections. Towers are primitive in method—stacked stone cores, timber ribs, cord lashings, woven screens—but sophisticated in arrangement: they rise as mooring masts, watch perches, and communal dry pockets where air is trapped under leaf domes. Ladders descend into water instead of up from it. Doors are round and gasketed with moss. Storage hangs in nets from beams to keep it out of mouths, molds, and wandering claws.

Sound is constant and layered, far louder than the still surface suggests. Waterfalls feed the basin at three points along the rim, each a permanent roar. Rain drumming on broad leaves adds a higher percussion. Beneath that: the groan of soaked timbers, knocking float-bridges, rope under strain, fish clicking, shell-creatures rasping stone, frog-throats pulsing from root hollows, and the deep, occasional boom of trapped air escaping from the old submerged halls. Nothing here is silent, not even night. At dusk the fauna shift registers rather than stopping.

Life is abundant to the point of choreography. Long-bodied trout and silver shoals thread the street channels; soft-shelled crabs graze algae off carved lintels; waterbirds nest on the upper reed towers and spear downward through roof vents; salamanders with fern-colored backs cling to wet masonry. In the root rooms, snail colonies the size of shields polish surfaces clean. Small grazing mammals—semi-aquatic, sleek, whiskered—sleep in woven baskets above waterline and feed in the shallows at dawn. Predators exist, but this is a mature system: territories are old, routes are known, panic is rare. Even hunting feels procedural, part of a schedule older than memory.

Light is workable, human light, though filtered. Midday enters as pale gold at the surface, green in the upper districts, and blue-gray in the lower streets. You can read a carved sign in the top tiers; below, you read by contrast and movement. Nights are never black. Cloud glow, moon diffusion through mist, and the faint bioluminescent threads in certain root colonies keep edges visible. The weirdness of the place is mild but persistent: plants that hold pockets of sweet air in their stems; stone lions on old stairheads now serving as nesting sites for eels; rain that falls so steadily it feels less like weather than gravity made visible.

Change comes slowly. Districts migrate by handspans each year as anchors are reset and roots thicken. A collapsed platform is repaired with the same knot patterns used generations ago. Erosion is no longer destruction here; it is maintenance, smoothing sharp intent into durable form. The basin has been in this state a long time—flooded, inhabited, grown-through, loud, cool, and patient. A small world with clear boundaries: crater rim above, drowned foundations below, and between them a city that learned to float without hurrying.
