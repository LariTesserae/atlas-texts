The land sits low and warm, a broad basin that feels less like ground and more like a single breathing organism spread to the horizon.

From a distance it reads as a city: districts, corridors, plazas, towers. Up close, nothing is built from stone or fired clay or metal. Every wall is braided stem. Every column is trunk. Every bridge is a root lifted into an arch by growth-pressure and then thickened by a hundred smaller vines that accepted the shape as law. It is urban without tools, planned without planners, dense enough that “outside” barely exists.

There is almost no bare soil. Where the surface opens, it opens briefly—fresh cuts in dark loam, sharp little escarpments where yesterday’s runoff peeled away a layer of root felt, clean-sided channels that did not exist in the morning and are already being netted over by pale new tendrils. The terrain is young in the way a wound is young: edges crisp, geometry temporary, everything actively closing.

Water is never absent, but rarely obvious. It arrives as breath, as sweating bark, as fog that condenses beneath leaves and falls in sudden local showers. Whole streets go dry and papery for hours, then a cloudless rain will pour from the canopy because some high reservoir of cupped leaves tipped at once. Pools gather in knot-hollows, reflect green light, then vanish into capillary roots by afternoon. You are always damp; you are almost never standing in open water.

The light itself is ordinary sun, but handled strangely. Bright noon reaches down in long shafts through gaps that open and close as giant fronds rotate. Everything is lit, but seldom evenly. A plaza can be day on one side and deep chlorophyll dusk on the other. Colors stay warm—amber bark, bruised purple stems, the yellow-white of new growth—but all of it filtered through layers of living translucent tissue, so even shadow seems botanical.

And the sound—deafening, continuous, inhuman.

No birds, no insects, no footfalls, no voices. The absence of animal sound is total and uncanny, but not quiet. The place roars with plant mechanics. Trunks crack as they torque a few degrees and lock in a new orientation. Seed vessels burst like percussion lines. Vast leaf-fans slap each other in gust fronts. Gas vents pulse from swampy cavities under the root mats with subsonic whomps you feel in your ribs. Fibers under tension pluck and scream when they snap. Rain moving through layered canopy becomes a thousand overlapping drumskins. Standing still is like standing inside a factory made of forests, except nothing here was manufactured.

Because the world is near-breaking, the city-forest doesn’t keep fixed geometry. A corridor walked at dawn may fork into three by noon; one fork leads to a district you already passed, another to a basin that should be kilometers away, and the third returns you to the same fork from a different angle. Distances buckle. Vertical trunks occasionally continue upward into cloud, then curve and re-enter the ground without crossing the intervening air. Shadows sometimes root: a dark shape cast by a tower-vine will linger after the light moves on, thickening into a cool stain where moss can grow despite no moisture source. In heavy turbulence, growth events happen visibly—whole facades swelling outward in minutes, knotting themselves into balconies, then shedding those balconies in shearing mats that become new streets below.

Despite the chaos, there are rules:

- Anything rigid is temporary; anything flexible persists.  
- Surfaces that touch will graft if left together long enough.  
- Voids are invitations and will be filled by dusk.  
- Boundaries are seasonal, not structural.  
- Maps expire in hours.  

So it is open at regional scale—district after district, ridge after ridge of vegetative architecture—yet claustrophobically dense at arm’s length. Warm air, wet skin, no animals, no machines, no stillness. A metropolis of pure botany in permanent rearrangement, loud as weather, alive as infrastructure, never finished, never truly stable, and never, for even a minute, at rest.
