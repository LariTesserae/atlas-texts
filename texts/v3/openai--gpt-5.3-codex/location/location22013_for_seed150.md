At this altitude, the world has learned to build in close quarters.

The place sits in a shallow volcanic bowl no wider than a few streets across, cradled between dark ridgelines that still look newly broken—stone faces sharp, ledges clean-edged, scree pale and raw where frost has only recently started to soften them. You get the feeling the basin was opened not long ago in geological terms: a collapse, a tear, a young wound in the mountain. Water keeps finding those fresh fractures. It beads out of seams, runs in threadlike streams, and disappears under mats of roots before reappearing as cold mirror-pools in low corners.

Everything vertical is either plant or being turned into plant.

What might once have been masonry walls now serve as ribs for living facades: trunks trained flat against stone, layered branch lattices, thick quilts of moss and liverwort that swallow corners and steps. Streets still exist, but as narrow green corridors under woven canopies. The city pattern remains—blocks, courts, terraces, stairways—but every boundary is softened by growth so dense it behaves like architecture. Hedges are load-bearing. Vine bundles are railings. Root vaults hold up whole walkways. You don’t see “buildings” and “gardens” separately; you see a single packed organism occupying an urban plan.

It is cold enough that wetness never feels lush. Water here is metallic, near-freezing, and persistent. After any weather shift, droplets gather on needles and leaf tips, then fall in a constant fine rhythm. The air is dim not because it is dark underground, but because light is filtered repeatedly: first by cloud, then by high canopy, then by hanging curtains of fern and vine. Even at midday, the brightest surfaces are only dull silver—wet bark, slick stone, thin skin of ice forming at shaded edges. Dawn and dusk look almost the same. Colors stay deep: fir-black, moss-green, iron-gray, occasional rust-orange where young soils are exposed.

The vegetation is not decorative; it is substrate, pavement, wall, and weather system.

Ground level is a layered compression of root felt, decomposing stems, peat-dark soil, and flat stones locked in place by rhizomes. You walk on resilience rather than solidity. Some plazas have become floating lawns over saturated ground, trembling faintly under weight. In colder pockets, low marsh plants occupy old courtyards, their stiff blades rimed white in the mornings. Along former roofs and upper terraces, dwarf conifers and wind-pruned shrubs knit together into dense, interlocking crowns that shed meltwater into channels intentionally guided by old gutters and newer root grooves. Even drainage has become botanical.

Despite the density, it is mostly quiet.  
No broad chorus of animals, no insect thunder. Fauna is sparse: a few pale mountain birds that nest in sheltered eaves of branchwork, occasional long-legged amphibians in seep channels, tiny furtive mammals leaving tracks in frost-soft mud. Their scarcity makes each sign feel important. Sound comes from process rather than population: drip, trickle, leaf-rub, the gentle crack of ice releasing from stone, and at intervals the soft groan of loaded branches settling under meltwater.

The place is active in slow, overlapping cycles. Freeze-thaw opens rock; roots follow; water occupies the new space; moss seals the surface; then a cold snap tightens everything again. Fog drifts in from the outer slopes and condenses directly onto leaves, effectively raining upward into cistern-branches and bark hollows. Wind rarely blows hard in the basin, but when it does, whole green facades sway as single units, redistributing weight through woven stems like living suspension bridges. Nothing is static, yet nothing is frantic. Change is continual and incremental.

There are rules here, plain once you notice them:

- Open channels are kept clear by growth patterns themselves; block one and water will overtop somewhere inconvenient by morning.  
- Surfaces that look dead are often structural—old trunks carrying new load. Cut them and adjacent walls slump.  
- The highest terraces are colder and barer; the lowest are wetter and less stable. Safe travel is a middle band of rooted walkways.  
- Light wells—small vertical shafts where canopy thins—function as communal “squares” for the ecosystem. Most flowering and seed exchange happens there.

The slight strangeness is in how complete the integration is. It does not feel like ruins reclaimed by nature, nor a planned garden-city, but a third state: urban geometry and alpine wetland merged into one compact, self-thickening habitat. You can still read intention in the layout—aligned paths, repeated arcs, terraced gradients—but intention has been taken over by growth logic. The city no longer belongs to builders, and it has not been erased. It has been metabolized.

Stand still long enough and the basin seems to breathe: mist gathering, droplets forming, tiny runoff increasing, then tapering; branches taking weight, then releasing it. In the dim cold, surrounded by wet green structures packed almost wall-to-wall, you feel less like a visitor to a landscape than a brief moving gap inside a living district.
