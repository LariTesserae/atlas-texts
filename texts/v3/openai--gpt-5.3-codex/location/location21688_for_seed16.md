At first it seems impossible: a green place with no water.

Then your eyes adjust to the basin and it starts to make sense on its own terms.

This is a low country, a sunken pocket hidden inside a larger salt plain, as if a city-sized thumb pressed into soft stone long ago and never lifted. The rim is high enough that the horizon is close and circular. Light arrives reluctantly—filtered through dust, mineral haze, and hanging curtains of heat—so even at midday everything sits in a copper dimness. Shadows are soft-edged and long. Nothing sparkles. Nothing reflects like water, because there is none: not a puddle, not a seep, not even dampness in cracks.

And still, there is vegetation.

It grows in dense bands where the ground exhales gases from old volcanic seams: forests of jointed, matte-green columns, fan plants with ceramic-looking leaves, low mats of pale filaments like spun glass. Their chemistry is dry. They drink nothing. Instead they harvest airborne salts and reactive vapors, pulling thin compounds out of the heat itself. In the mornings (if “morning” can be named in this dim light), their leaf surfaces frost—not with ice, but with mineral crystals. By noon those crystals are gone, metabolized. When a frond tears, no sap runs; powder spills, then hardens.

The city is built among them, but spread out—urban in plan, empty in feel. Broad avenues of fired clay brick run for long distances between clusters of structures, with silence in the gaps and then sudden pockets of industry. Houses are low and thick-walled, made from mudstone, baked salt block, and woven plant fiber sealed with resin. Towers rise only where needed: wind organs, watch platforms, kiln stacks. No metal gleam, no glass panes; tools here are stone-edged, ceramic-toothed, rope-driven, lever-worked. Primitive technologies, but old and optimized, worn smooth by generations of use and weather.

The loudness comes in layers.

Wind pushes through carved vents and hollow towers, turning whole blocks into instruments—deep fluting moans, pipe-whistles, throbbing bass notes you feel in the sternum. Seed pods in the canopy rattle like dry rain. Kiln districts crack and pop. Thermal chimneys cough from below. Fiber mills clatter where rotating vanes comb plant threads into cloth. Even empty plazas hum, because the paving is set over resonant cavities that amplify ground tremors. It is not a place of chatter or birdsong; there are no animals at all. The noise is mechanical, botanical, geological.

Without fauna, the ecology is all wind and timing.

Plants broadcast spores in synchronized bursts when heat gradients flip at dusk. Vines creep toward mineral-rich masonry and etch it slowly with acidic breath, then retreat when the stone is exhausted. Succulent towers collapse into nutrient cones that feed ring-colonies of seedlings. Nothing hunts, nothing flees. Competition is slow, territorial, and chemical: shade wars, root exclusion, toxin veils. Even decay is loud here—shell-like trunks splitting with pistol cracks as internal crystals expand.

Erosion has done its long work and then settled into rhythm. Corners are rounded. Steps are dished by grit. Facades are skinned with thin varnishes of wind-polished salt. Drainage channels exist out of habit and memory, not necessity; they now carry only dust. The basin floor is braided with old runnels from an era that might have had liquids, or perhaps from liquids that were never water at all. Either way, the current world is stable in its dryness: active, not chaotic; weathered, not ruined.

The strangest rule here is that growth and construction no longer oppose each other. Streets are laid to follow root migration. Buildings include deliberate hollows for trunk expansion. Public squares are planted with “clock groves” whose leaf angle tells shifts in vent pressure better than any instrument. Urban life—whether presently inhabited or inherited from those who are gone—is written into the same material cycle as the flora: bake, crack, seed, bind, polish, repeat.

So the place resolves into something intimate despite being a city: a hidden bowl of heat and dim light, mostly empty space between purposeful forms, where no creature with blood or bone lives, yet everything feels busy, maintained, and ongoing. A dry metropolis that grows like a garden and sounds like a furnace choir.
