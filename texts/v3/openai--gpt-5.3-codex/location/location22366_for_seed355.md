The land sits almost level with the sea, though you never quite see the sea itself.  
You feel it under everything.

A skin of warm water lies just beneath the crust, so the ground gives slightly when stepped on, as if paved over soaked bread. Long basins spread between raised causeways of black stone and old fired brick. Some basins are broad, mirror-flat sheets; others are reedless marsh, only a glaze of mineral water over silt. The city is here, unmistakably—avenues, plazas, walls, stairways, towers—but it is spaced out, interrupted by water and empty flats, so that each district feels like an island of intention surrounded by patient terrain.

It is hot in a way that does not shimmer with sunlight. The heat rises from below, stored in stone, in mud, in the deep water table. Overhead hangs a low roof of cloud so thick that day and night are almost the same charcoal tone. Light, when it appears, is indirect: pale seams in the cloud deck, dull glows caught in wet pavement, occasional brief silvering when lightning travels inside cloud but never strikes. Most surfaces are dark and damp. Even high walls weep.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Tufts of salt-grass root in cracked mortar. A few cypress-like columns grow from courtyards where rainwater lingers for months, their bark bleached and smooth as bone. Along canal edges, mats of leathery, low plants cling to stone joints and drink condensation. No canopy, no lush tangle—just scattered growth that has negotiated terms with heat, salinity, and stone.

There are no animals. Not even insects.  
No wingbeats in the cloudbreaks, no ripple-signature in pooled water, no scavengers in alley shadows. The absence is not dramatic at first; it is simply clean. Then you notice how complete it is, and the place turns uncanny. Water stands untouched. Fruit, when any appears, falls and remains. Silence is never broken by life, only by matter.

And matter is active.

Rain comes in pulses, sudden and heavy, then gone. Channels fill, overflow, vanish into sink courts and old cistern shafts. Minutes later steam begins to rise from sunless pavement. Condensation beads along arch undersides and drips in perfectly regular intervals, then stops all at once as if a valve has turned somewhere inside the air. Walls slump by fractions over years, then harden again under mineral accretion. Erosion and construction are no longer opposites here; they collaborate. Corners round, reliefs blur, steps sink, but mineral films rebuild edges in new geometries. The whole urban field looks ancient not because it is dying, but because it has settled into a continuous weathering life.

The city’s materials are simple and pre-mechanical: quarried basalt, lime mortar, clay brick, timber petrified by salts, hammered copper gone green-black. No glass towers, no wiring, no engines, no visible tools left in place. Infrastructure exists as gravity channels, siphon stairs, open drains, aqueduct ribs, overflow courts, and elevated walkways. It is an urbanism of slope, porosity, and patient handwork. Every useful system is legible. Every system leaks.

In the dimness, distance behaves badly. A colonnade three blocks away may take an hour to reach. A tower seen at dusk might be behind you at dawn without any memory of crossing. Reflections often sharpen before their objects do: in flooded plazas you first see bright window-shapes in water, then turn and find only blank walls. Sometimes rain falls upward from ground to cloud in silent threads. Sometimes a district repeats itself with slight edits—same street plan, different decay, same fountain dry in one copy and overflowing in another. The people who once mapped this place (if anyone did) must have stopped pretending it was fixed.

Yet there are rules, and they are learned physically:

- High ground is trustworthy, but never permanent.  
- Moving water marks current reality better than stone does.  
- If a sound echoes, the path is stable for a while.  
- If there is no echo, distance cannot be trusted.  
- Never follow your own footprints in wet dust; they may lead to a different hour.

The soundscape stays restrained. Dripping. Occasional sheet-rain. The soft collapse of saturated plaster. A deep, infrequent groan from far foundations as load shifts through mud. Wind is rare at street level, but when it comes through long corridors it hums in ceramic drain mouths like a low flute note, then is gone. Mostly, it is quiet enough to hear water condense.

The atmosphere tastes metallic and mineral—iron, salt, wet limestone, a trace of sulfur after stormbursts. Skin remains damp. Clothing never fully dries. Darkness does not mean cold here; it means closeness. Heat and shadow coexist, wrapping the city in a perpetual after-storm.

From above—if above can be found—you would see an open region of scattered built masses and intervening wetlands, stitched by causeways and canals, all of it dark, glistening, and old. A drowned plain that learned architecture, or a city that learned marsh behavior. Either way, it has reached equilibrium on unstable terms: sparse life, abundant water, enduring construction, and a reality that flexes like saturated ground underfoot.
