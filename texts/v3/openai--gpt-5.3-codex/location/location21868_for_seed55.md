At first it feels like open sea: cold water, black water, long distances with no horizon you can trust.  
Then your hand brushes a straight edge.

The whole basin is made.

Not “city made,” not “there are ruins,” but total construction—the floor, the ridges, the terraces, the miles of retaining walls, the drowned stairways that descend into deeper drowned stairways. The world here is low and broad, a continental plain of old stoneworks laid out by patient, preindustrial hands: fitted basalt blocks, lime mortar long cured, clay-lined channels, timber caissons turned to dark mineral wood. Even where everything has been rounded by ages of current, the geometry remains: right angles softened into old bones, circles turned egg-smooth, causeways reduced to long humps under mats of weed.

Water fills everything. There is no dry ground, only depth and less depth. In the shallower tracts the water is the color of iron and tea, stained by plant tannins. In the deeper corridors between raised embankments it goes ink-black in a few body lengths, cold enough to numb fingers through gloves. Light barely arrives. Day and night differ only by a slight thinning of darkness near the upper layers; below that, the place lives in permanent dusk. You navigate by touch, by slope, by the pale drag of vegetation across your shoulders.

And vegetation is everywhere—lush to the point of architecture. Long ribbon-kelps anchor in mortar seams and run for kilometers along old walls. Fan-moss coats stair faces in thick velvet, trapping silt until each step becomes a planted ledge. Reed-forests rise from flooded courtyards where silt has finally settled, stems packed so densely that moving through them feels like threading a brush. There are floating meadows too: rafts of interlocked roots and fibrous fronds, entire fields drifting slowly along ancient canal lines, snagging on pillars, breaking, reknitting. No flowers in any bright sense; this is a cold palette—green-black, bottle green, umber, ash. But it is unquestionably fertile.

There are no animals. No fish flashing away from your shadow, no shell scrape on stone, no insect skitter at the surface. The silence this creates is not absolute, but specific. You hear water itself: soft pressure against walls, the distant tick of cooling stone, the long cloth-rustle of kelp beds folding with the tide. Occasionally a buried timber in some old gateframe releases a slow bubble stream that taps upward through leaves like muted beads. That is most of the sound. A quiet place, and densely occupied at the same time—by plants, by structures, by layers of history pressed close together.

Age is visible in the way sharp intent has become gentle form. Spillways built to break floods now look like natural saddles. Defensive berms read as underwater hills. Carved channels are broad valleys, their inscriptions gone, their edges consumed by centuries of abrasion. Erosion here has not destroyed so much as finalized. Everything unstable has already fallen. Everything that could crack has cracked and settled. What remains feels like the last draft of a very old design.

Because change is slow, patterns matter. Currents move on seasonal memory, not weather panic. Cold upwellings arrive along the same masonry trenches year after year. Floating root-mats migrate and return. Silt thickens one basin while another slowly clears. The world teaches patience: if you mark a wall with twine and come back in a month, it may have shifted only by a handspan of new growth; come back in a decade, and the passage may be gone behind a living curtain.

If there are rules here, they are simple and strict:

- **All surfaces are claimed**—stone does not stay bare for long.  
- **Depth equals silence**—the deeper you go, the less the world announces itself.  
- **Straight lines survive longest**—old human geometry still organizes water and plant life at continental scale.  
- **Nothing hurries**—the landscape is active, but only in long sentences.

So the place is not a ruin in the dramatic sense. It is an old, functioning water-world built with simple tools and impossible persistence, then given enough centuries to become its own kind of wilderness—constructed, submerged, cold, dark, and quietly, relentlessly alive with green.
