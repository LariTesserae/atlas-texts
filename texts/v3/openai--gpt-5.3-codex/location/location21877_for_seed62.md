From a distance, the region looks like a folded sheet of green glass laid over young stone.

It sits high enough that air stays cool even under a hard, bright sky. Light is almost constant here—clear, silver-white daylight that makes edges look newly cut. The mountains and ridges are geologically young, all sharp shoulders and unfinished slopes, with exposed seams of pale rock between masses of vegetation. Erosion has started, but only just: gullies are narrow, streambeds abrupt, and cliffs still hold the brittle geometry of recent uplift.

Water is not abundant in the obvious way. There are no broad lakes, no thunderous rivers. Instead, moisture gathers and vanishes in cycles: cloud-strand rain, night condensation, mist trapped in ravines, dew that beads on metal and leaf alike. In the morning, everything sweats—ferns, cable housings, stone retaining walls, rusting rail spines. By afternoon, surfaces dry, and the wetness retreats into shaded pockets, moss trenches, cisterns, and root-chambers. The landscape feels damp without ever feeling flooded.

Vegetation has taken this as an invitation and gone nearly feral. Dense growth crowds every viable surface: high-canopy conifers braided with vine-lattices, broadleaf thickets in hollows, moss sheeting over boulders, epiphytes draping from utility lines. Even the industrial pieces grow gardens of their own—lichen on turbine casings, creepers through open ventwork, seedbeds in cracked concrete. Paths remain visible only because they are used or heated; leave one untended for a season and it softens into green.

The built world is present but scattered, as though someone planned an industrial district and then changed their mind halfway through. A pumping station on one ridge. A relay tower in a stand of trees. A narrow-gauge freight line crossing a valley and ending at a warehouse with one lit window. Condenser farms shaped like metallic reeds. Vertical tanks linked by insulated pipes that disappear underground and reappear kilometers away. Nothing forms a city, but every structure implies a system larger than what can be seen. Most machinery runs slowly, almost politely—steady indicator lamps, measured valve turns, low-pressure exchange cycles. No roaring furnaces, no pounding assembly halls. Industry here is distributed, patient, and mostly automated.

Because of that, the great surprise is the silence. Wind passes through leaves with a soft static hiss, water drips from one level of canopy to the next, and somewhere a distant actuator clicks every few minutes. Sound does not carry far; dense foliage and broken topography absorb it. Even when you stand beside operating equipment, it hums below conversation level. If you hear a bird, you remember it. Animal life is sparse and elusive at this elevation and in this managed ecology—small tracks in mud, occasional glides of high insects, a single foxlike shape at dawn. The place feels inhabited more by growth and process than by creatures.

Its strangeness comes from how cleanly nature and machine have interlocked. Some plants open their stomata only during the warm exhaust phase of nearby heat exchangers. Certain mosses trace perfect rectangles over buried cable channels where the ground stays fractionally warmer. Fog collects first around electrostatic frames set along ridgelines; by dawn, those frames drip into cisterns that feed both irrigation roots and cooling jackets. The local “weather” is partly meteorology, partly infrastructure. Nothing dramatic happens quickly, but everything is always, quietly, being adjusted.

Over weeks, terraces deepen by millimeters, roots split a new crack in stone, oxidation blooms under paint, and a platform vanishes under fern. Over years, a whole installation can transition from facility to ruin to habitat without ever fully stopping its task. This is a landscape of slow custody: bright, cool, overgrown, and attentive, where the world is still young in its bones and already old in its systems.
