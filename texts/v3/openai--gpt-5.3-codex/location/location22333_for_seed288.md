It feels like standing inside the husk of an old continent.

The land is **below the surface**, but not cramped. A roof of stone hangs far overhead—so high and so dark that distance eats it. You are in a sunken region the size of a small province: collapsed basins joined by broad tunnels, terraces, and old fault corridors. The ground is mostly pale mineral—chalky marl, gypsum crust, old volcanic tuff—powdered by ages of wind and feet into fine dust that dulls every color.

There is almost **no open water**. No streams, no reflective pools, no mud. Moisture exists only as breath in the air and a nightly skin of condensation on cold stone. People here talk about dampness the way coastal people talk about rain: as something seasonal, minor, precious.

And yet the place is unexpectedly green.

Not wet-green—never that—but dense with drought-adapted growth. Whole groves of hard, ribbed plants rise from cracks where mineral salts collect. Their leaves are narrow, gray, and furred to trap vapor. Vines drape old walls, not because the walls are damp, but because porous masonry sweats trace moisture just before dawn. Mats of lichen and pale moss grow in dim zones under vents, drinking fog that never falls as droplets. In wider basins, low forest spreads out in interrupted patches, each tree spaced far from the next, each trunk swollen with stored fluid like living ceramic jars.

The climate sits on the cool side of temperate: jacket weather, always. The stone acts as a thermal governor. Days and nights exist mostly as changes in light, not heat. The air is mineral-clean, slightly alkaline, with a tang of dust and dry leaf oil.

---

The **city** is not a single city but a belt of settlements strung along the old basin edges. Built density is high where people are, but those built zones are separated by long open tracts of empty ground, fields of scrub growth, and animal migration lanes.

Construction is all low-tech, hand-scale, and old-fashioned:  
- load-bearing stone walls,  
- packed-earth courtyards,  
- timber from the tougher drywood groves,  
- lime plaster burnished smooth by generations.

No engines, no glowing grids, no machine hum. Infrastructure exists, but it is physical and legible: stepped roads, gravity chutes, stone cisterns for captured dew, elevated walkways to keep hoofed herds from trampling market lanes. Everything has been repaired so many times that straight lines have softened into negotiated curves.

You can read age here in rounding. Erosion has had forever to work. Corners have become shoulders; stairs have become shallow ramps; inscriptions have become texture. Even abandoned districts have not collapsed dramatically—they have simply settled into the same language as the cliffs around them.

---

The light is dim most of the time. It arrives from skylight shafts, broken roof-faults, and long slits where the upper crust thinned. By midday those openings throw pale columns into suspended dust. By late afternoon the whole region returns to a blue-gray half-dark.

That dimness makes sound bigger.

And sound here is **enormous**.

Wind drives through vertical vents and tunnel throats in a constant organ-tone that never fully stops. Animal calls ricochet off hard surfaces and overlap into continuous roar: herd bellowing, wingbeat thunder from cave rookeries, chittering colonies in the walls, the scrape of claws, the hard clack of hooves on stone roads. In market districts, voices must be projected from the diaphragm or not heard at all. People communicate with whistles and hand signals at distance because spoken words get shredded by echo.

It is a deafening place, but not a frantic one. The loudness is steady, expected, infrastructural—like surf in a coastal town.

---

Fauna is abundant and integrated into daily life. Large browsing animals move between cultivated dry-gardens and wild scrub belts. Burrowing mammals aerate the dusty terraces. Insect life is thick in the vegetated bands, feeding birds and bats in huge numbers. Predators follow the herds along known routes, and settlements are designed with raised sleeping floors and thorn corridors for this reason.

Because change is slow, rhythms matter more than events.  
- Roof shifts happen over decades.  
- Groves expand or fail over generations.  
- Trade routes move when a ramp finally wears too thin, not when policy changes.

The governing rule of the landscape is simple: **capture and keep what passes briefly**—moisture, shade, soil, seeds, animal movement, human labor. Nothing here is abundant in the immediate moment except stone, wind, and sound. Everything else is harvested from small cycles and held carefully.

So the place feels old, practical, and alive: a dry underworld where cities and ecosystems have learned to live loudly, patiently, and very close to their limits.
