At first it looks like a contradiction: a low, warm basin with almost no standing water, and yet so much life that the ground is hard to see.

The land is young. You can feel it in the edges of everything. Broad flats of pale mineral stretch for hundreds of kilometers, interrupted by low hummocks of fresh stone and unfinished ridges that seem poured rather than carved. There are no old valleys here, no deeply cut canyons, no patient riverwork. The surface is still in its first draft—plates of clay-salt crust, fields of powdery gypsum, black seams of recent basalt, all lying close to sea level in a continental depression where heat gathers and lingers.

And still, it is green—just not in the way wet countries are green.  
Vegetation grows dense, shoulder-to-shoulder, in layered canopies of drought-built forms: waxy column shrubs, broad-leafed succulents, reed-thick thickets of jointed stems, mat-plants that knit whole districts together. Leaves are heavy, glossy, and tight-veined; trunks are ribbed storage. Most plants open to the air at dawn and dusk, drinking humidity rather than rain. Roots go down absurd distances into mineral faults where trace moisture and cool gases collect. The landscape’s “lushness” is a packed architecture of water-saving bodies, not soft grassland abundance.

Over this, settlement rises in the same logic.  
No machines, no visible advanced devices—only labor, habit, and biological intelligence. Towns are made from tamped earth, salt brick, fired clay, woven fiber, and living hedges trained into walls. At vast scale these towns merge into corridors, then belts, then near-continuous urban ecologies: courtyards sunk for shade, wind alleys, roof gardens of thornfruit and night-blooming vines, cisternless plazas where condensation nets hang like cloth sails and drip into communal basins before sunrise. “Urban” here means density of arrangement, not industry: every surface occupied, every gap assigned a use—food, shelter, storage, nesting, passage.

Fauna is everywhere and mostly quiet about it.  
Small hoofed grazers browse the succulent understory in endless movement. Burrowing colonies churn the young soils from below. Pollinators move in low clouds at dusk, not loud like bees but papery and soft, making a dry brushing sound. Predators are lean, long-legged, and patient, hunting by shadow and stillness. Birds favor gliding over calling; reptiles communicate with posture and color more than voice. Even in abundance, animals here have evolved toward low-noise living—sound carries too far on open mineral flats, and quiet is safety.

The weather has its own large-scale pulse.  
Days are bright, warm, and hard-edged. Afternoons bring shimmering uplift winds that move dust in long transparent sheets. Nights cool quickly enough for condensation to appear on stone, leaf, rope, and skin; before dawn, whole neighborhoods are active collecting it. Seasonal shifts arrive not as rainstorms but as humidity fronts and flowering waves: entire districts bloom within three days, feed a migration, then return to muted greens and silvers. The place is active all the time—growth, grazing, building, breeding, drying, storing—yet rarely noisy. From a hill, what you hear is mostly cloth snapping lightly in the wind, feet in dust, and the small click of cooling stone after sunset.

The rules of this world are simple and strict:

- Surface water is rare enough to be ceremonial.  
- Life survives by storage, timing, and crowding.  
- Shade is infrastructure.  
- Density is resilience: plant against plant, house against house, species against species in mutual shelter.  
- Nothing is wasted, because nothing is easy.

So the landscape resolves into its true character: not barren and not fertile in the old sense, but **efficiently alive**—a vast, low, sunlit basin where geology is still settling, civilization is pre-industrial but highly organized, and nearly every living thing has agreed to thrive in the thin margin between heat and thirst.
