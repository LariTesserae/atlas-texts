From a distance, the place looks less like a city on land and more like masonry grown out of a wet mineral skin.

It sits in a low basin where the ground never quite dries and never quite becomes mud. Underfoot is young stone—dark, smooth, barely weathered—split by a thousand shallow channels of warm water. The rock still has the look of something recently cooled: broad plates, rounded blisters, seams that haven’t yet learned how to crumble. There are no old cliffs, no deep gullies, no patient shaping by wind. Edges are blunt and new. Even broken surfaces look fresh.

Across this young plain, people have built densely and close, as if construction was another kind of sediment. Walls of stacked basalt, packed-clay towers, shell-lime plaster, timber scavenged from drift and bone. Everything is hand-cut, hand-carried, hand-fitted. No grand machines, no polished alloys, no glass skyline—just rope, lever, kiln, chisel, basket, muscle. Primitive tools, urban scale.

And packed into that scale: lane over lane, stair over stair, bridges crossing watercourses every few body-lengths, terraces loaded with coops, pens, fish vats, drying racks, workshops, shrines, cistern mouths. The density is physical, immediate; nothing stands alone. Buildings touch, almost lean on each other. Water touches everything.

---

There is no vegetation in the sense most travelers expect. No trees, no grass, no reed beds softening edges, no climbing vines swallowing walls. The absence is startling at first: no green break in the stone and clay geometry. Color comes from mineral and animal life instead—black rock, gray plaster, rusted iron stains, white shell heaps, ochre mud, slick films of sulfur-yellow and blue-black microbial sheen in warm channels.

Those films are the base of the food web. In the shallows where hot seep-water meets cooler rainwater, mats of simple life spread over stone like living paint. Snails rasp them. Crabs and soft-bodied grazers feed on the snails. Fish feed on them all. Birds and people feed on the fish, crabs, eggs, and each other’s leftovers. Nothing here depends on leaf or seed. Life runs on mineral water, rot, and constant moisture.

Fauna is everywhere. Eels thread through culverts. Frogs pulse in cistern throats. Crabs crowd steps in red-brown drifts. Insects bloom in clouds above warm drains and are hunted in turn by lean, long-winged marsh birds that nest in wall niches and roof trusses. Domesticated animals—short-haired, wide-footed, tolerant of damp—shuffle through alleys hauling sledges. Dogs with slick coats and broad muzzles patrol fish yards. The city breathes through animal bodies.

---

The light never fully arrives.

The sky is a permanent lid of low cloud and vapor. Not storm-dark, not night—just a persistent dimness, like late evening stretched across the whole day. Warmth rises from below as much as from above. Seeping geothermal water and crowded bodies keep the air thick and humid. Stone weeps. Metal beads with condensation. Cloth never feels fully dry. Breath is visible only in the coolest corners, and those corners are rare.

Because the light is subdued, surfaces matter. People polish shell inlays into doorframes to catch what little brightness there is. White limewash is prized. Pale fish-skin screens and bone-char lamps make interiors usable, but outdoors the world stays in muted tones: damp charcoal, clay red, salt white, skin, hide, and water-gloss.

---

And always, always, the sound.

The basin is deafening in a way that becomes architecture. Water runs continuously through engineered steps and spillways; each district has its own roar where channels drop from terrace to terrace. Vents hiss. Hammering from stone yards carries through wet air. Animal colonies scream, croak, trill, bark, and beat wing against timber. Market calls are short and close because no one can shout over distance and be understood. Children learn hand signals before they learn full speech.

The noise does not peak and fade. It is constant, a full-pressure field. Newcomers feel it in their ribs, in their teeth. Residents sleep inside it like fish inside current. Silence is a superstition here, something imagined, not experienced.

---

For all that activity, the place is strangely unchanging.

Not dead—busy, crowded, fertile with animal motion—but climatically and geologically locked into equilibrium. The same warm seep rates. The same cloud ceiling. The same water levels within a handspan. No dramatic floods, no deep drought cycles, no bright summers, no freezing turns. Even the city’s growth has slowed into infill rather than expansion: repair, patch, add a room, raise a parapet, deepen a channel by a palm’s width.

The rules of the world are simple and obeyed because they are visible:

- **Water is near everywhere**; build for wet feet, not dry foundations.  
- **Nothing wooden lasts long**; stone and fired clay are the real wealth.  
- **Food comes from channels, pens, and microbial flats**, not fields.  
- **Sound is unavoidable**; communication is touch-close, visual, ritualized.  
- **Brightness is precious**; pale surfaces and reflective materials are civic assets.  
- **Rot is quick**; maintenance is not a trade but a daily law.

So the city persists: dense, warm, dim, loud, and wet, spread across young stone that has not yet learned age. A place with no gardens, but with life in excess. A place where geology still feels newborn while human habit is already old.
