At this altitude, you expect thin dryness, but the place refuses that logic.

It is a **hanging basin** no larger than a village green, cupped into the shoulder of a mountain and ringed on three sides by weathered stone. The fourth side opens to sky and far blue drop—layer upon layer of ridges fading into haze—yet inside the basin the ground stays dark and wet. Water does not run in one obvious stream. It appears in many small ways: sweating out of the cliff face, collecting in root hollows, shining in black soil under a skin of leaf litter, hanging in the air as mist before noon and again just before dusk.

The stone is old volcanic tuff and dark basalt, softened by time. Nothing here looks freshly broken. Every edge is rounded, every ledge undercut to a smooth lip by centuries of seepage. Even the boulders look as if they have settled into their own names. Thin channels, now mostly moss-filled, show where heavier rains once carved through. They no longer cut deeper; they are part of the basin’s memory now, not its ambition.

Despite the wetness, the space is not crowded. Vegetation is **lush but spaced**—a scatter of broad-leaf trees, each standing apart like deliberate thoughts, with open ground between them: slick rock, fern mats, shallow pools, low grasses that bow under droplets. Vines hang in curtains from one wall, but only in patches. Clumps of giant horsetail rise where water seeps strongest. A few orchids root directly in cracks and flower briefly, bright as tongues of paint, then disappear back into green.

Sound arrives before detail. The basin is loud in a way that has little to do with animals. Water ticks, drips, rattles, and occasionally thunders when a hidden pocket overflows. Wind comes over the lip and turns the hanging vines into brushwork against stone. Leaves clap. Hollow roots amplify drops into drumbeats. In the morning, cloud passes low enough to make the whole basin hiss as condensation forms and falls. At night, there is a periodic booming from inside the cliff—air shifting through old lava tubes—slow and resonant, as if the mountain is turning over in sleep.

Fauna is scarce. You can spend an hour and see only one lizard, pale and motionless on warm rock, and perhaps a pair of long-winged insects hovering over a pool. Bird calls are uncommon and brief, usually from far downslope. Yet life is clearly present in subtler forms: leech-thin worms in the mud, tiny crabs in freshwater pockets, translucent snails grazing moss, fungal fans opening and closing with humidity. The basin feels inhabited without feeling populated.

There are no built things. No walls, no paths, no shelters. But there are hints of **primitive use** at the edges: a branch sharpened and abandoned near a root tangle, stones cracked in a way that suggests deliberate striking, stripped bark from a medicinal tree. Whoever comes here comes lightly, gathers, and leaves. The basin keeps no permanent marks from them.

Its strangeness is gentle and persistent. Metal brought into the basin fogs faster than expected, as if the air recognizes it and wants it gone. At midday, when sunlight reaches directly over the rim, droplets on certain mosses bead into perfect lines that point uphill, not down. Pools can look shallow and then take your hand to the wrist. Sounds echo from the wrong directions, especially near the weeping wall.

Change is slow. This place does not lurch from season to season; it shifts by emphasis. Wet months deepen the pools and wake new fern spirals. Drier spells tighten the moss and expose old stone scars. Trees widen by a finger-width in a year. The cliff weeps less, then more, then less again. Over long time the basin grows not outward but inward—more humus, thicker roots, deeper dark under the leaves—an old high place that has learned how to hold water, warmth, and silence between the noises.
