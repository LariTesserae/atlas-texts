From orbit, it looks like a broken crown: a continent of uplifted shelves and knife-edge plateaus, all high enough to cast their own weather shadows. There are almost no lowlands. The world has been pushed upward and left there for ages, so everything is carved rather than chaotic—long escarpments, dry gullies, amphitheaters of stone, terraces worn smooth by wind loaded with mineral dust instead of rain.

There is no open water anywhere. No rivers, no marshes, no ice caps glittering in pockets. The basins are filled with powder, salt-glass, and old evaporite crusts that crack underfoot like thin porcelain. The air is warm, breathable only with adaptation, and thin on the upper ledges. In daylight the color palette is pale and hard: oxidized reds, sulfur yellows, chalk whites, and dark basalt seams where ancient lava cooled into columns. Under normal sun, shadows are sharp and practical; nothing here glows mystically unless industry makes it glow.

The cities are sparse but enormous in intent. You can travel a hundred kilometers through emptiness and then find a vertical settlement bolted into a canyon wall—freight lifts crawling up black rails, pressure foundries set in old lava tubes, condenser towers pulling trace volatiles from the air. Infrastructure follows geology: bridges from mesa to mesa, rack-rail lines along ridges, pipelines suspended above dust plains on articulated legs. It’s urban in nodes, not carpets: each city is a machine-oasis, then vast silence again.

Industry is old enough to look geological. Smelter stacks are encrusted with decades of mineral bloom; conveyor routes have become permanent scars visible from the stratosphere. The factories do not roar constantly. They pulse on long cycles—days of inactivity, then a shift of grinding, pumping, stamping, then stillness again. Energy is drawn from thermal gradients, piezoelectric bedrock, and high-altitude wind arrays. Nothing wastes momentum; this is a civilization designed for scarcity and distance.

And yet fauna is abundant.

Without plant life, the food web starts in chemistry: mineral films, airborne microbes, oxidizer blooms on warm rock, and engineered nutrient exudates that leak from industrial infrastructure. Animals graze rust, not grass. Herds of six-legged varnishers move across metal fields, scraping thin electrochemical layers with ceramic mouths. Cliff-nesters with translucent sail-skins ride updrafts between towers, folding like tools when they land. Burrowers tunnel in saltstone and emerge at dusk to feed on condenser sludge. In the upper air, threadlike floaters drift for weeks, harvesting charge differentials between dust strata. Predators are elegant and patient, built for low-energy chases over open terrain.

The strangeness is not frantic; it is systemic. Some canyons hold static fields strong enough to lift loose grit into hovering curtains. Certain cliffs “grow” metallic whiskers overnight as trace vapors plate onto exposed crystal faces. There are migration routes that track not temperature, but electromagnetic weather. Entire species time reproduction to refinery maintenance schedules because nutrient runoff peaks then. Life has braided itself into industry so deeply that shutting down a city can collapse a local ecosystem.

Sound carries far because there is so little of it. Mostly you hear wind brushing stone, the clicking of distant herds, occasional wing membrane snaps, a chain lift clanking once every few minutes. In the cities: low transformer hum, pressure valves sighing, the soft thunder of ore dropped into hoppers. At night, the quiet expands until single calls from ridge to ridge feel architectural, like signals bounced through a cathedral.

Change comes slowly. Dunes creep a few meters per year. Rail pylons are re-leveled every generation as bedrock relaxes. Migration maps are revised in decades, not days. Even storms are deliberate: broad dust fronts that take a week to cross a province, sanding everything a little smoother. Erosion and construction are in a long truce here—stone wears down, steel gets replaced, cities settle deeper into the shapes the land already wanted.

It is a warm, high, dry world of distances and patience: mostly empty at first glance, densely alive once you learn where to look, and governed by a simple rule everyone obeys—nothing arrives quickly, and nothing survives alone.
