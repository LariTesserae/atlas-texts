From orbit it looks like a pale-green continent skinned over a mountain range and then left in a wind tunnel for a million years.

There is almost no free water anywhere. No rivers, no lakes, no dew beading on surfaces. The air is dry enough to sting, and every exposed mineral is dusted with crystalline salts and oxalate powders, as if the ground is forever mid-evaporation. Yet the world is overwhelmingly vegetal. Not “forest” vegetal—there are very few trunks, no soft mossy shade, no leafy comfort—but an endless spread of living structures: latticed fronds of silica-rich tissue, cable-thick vines that root in cracks and hang between ridges, low mats of silver thallus that cover scree fields, and towering column-plants that look like heat exchangers.

At this elevation, the light is hard and nearly white. The sky is a thin, bright cyan during calm periods, bleaching toward silver when storms roll through. Shadows are sharp and short at midday. Even in cool temperatures, exposed surfaces can flash hot under direct sun and then plunge toward frost when cloud bands pass. Every organism here is built for that swing: reflective skins, segmented tissues, collapsible pores, chemical reservoirs instead of watery sap. The “plants” run on oils, alcohols, and dense ionic gels. If cut, they bleed amber vapor and granular resin, not liquid.

The terrain is old—ancient old. It has stopped trying to become anything else. Ranges are rounded by eons of grit-blast rather than rain-cutting. Valleys are broad troughs full of powder and root-web. Plateaus are armored in living crust, cracked like pottery but flexing microscopically as root pressure shifts below. Erosion is ongoing but in equilibrium: what wind strips from one face is trapped by comb-plants on the next ridge and slowly turned back into substrate. Whole escarpments breathe dust in and out seasonally.

And always: sound.

Not occasional sound—continuous, saturating sound. The landscape is deafening even when no obvious storm is present. The dominant note is wind, but wind filtered through billions of resonant bodies. Hollow stems boom like organ pipes. Ribbed fronds whistle in stacked harmonics. Seed-rattles clatter in dense sheets. Dry pods crack explosively as pressure changes. Root-plates grind under soil creep with a bass rumble you feel through your feet. During major fronts, it becomes industrial in character: piston-thumps from flexing trunk-columns, turbine-like shrieks from spiral canopies, percussive snaps traveling kilometer-long vine lines. It sounds like a factory district, but there are no factories—only biology that has converged on mechanical form because mechanics are what survive here.

That is where the “industrial” quality comes from. No built structures, no roads, no machines manufactured by hands—yet the ecology functions as one vast extraction-and-processing complex. Wind is mined. Light is fractionated. Dust is sorted by electrostatic cilia fields. Metallic aerosols are captured on conductive leaves and plated into support tissues. Thermal gradients are harvested in stacked membranes. Entire hillsides act like conveyor systems: upper-slope combs trap airborne grit, mid-slope grinders macerate it, basin mats ferment and chelate it, then capillary ropes haul concentrated minerals back upslope to reproductive towers. Standing in one of these basins, you can watch synchronized pulses move through the vegetation like current through cables.

Because density is low, all this activity is separated by immense empty spans. You can cross ten kilometers of bare, bright mineral flats between two “processing groves.” In those flats, the scale becomes obvious: weather systems are local but huge, and they interact with plant architecture the way surf interacts with reefs. Gust fronts hit one ridge and split, vortices shedding downwind in stable trains; dust-devils pick up pale spores and carry them for hours; pressure waves trigger coordinated seed release across entire provinces. From high points, you see patches of active vegetation flickering and roaring in the distance, each one a node in a continental network.

Fauna is abundant but not crowding. Life here distributes itself along corridors: glider-herds riding slope winds between feeding towers, six-limbed grazers scraping nutrient films from root-bark, burrow packs tunneling through cool subsoil where volatile-rich tubers form. Many animals are auditory specialists—crest-eared, chest-sinus resonant, using low-frequency calls that ride the ground. Others are electroreceptive, following the weak fields generated by mineral-pumping root masses. Predators often hunt by vibration silhouette: in the noise, vision is less useful than timing.

They have adapted to the roar. Some carry bony shutters over ear openings that ratchet closed during storm peaks. Some communicate in flashes—bioluminescent flank bands visible even in daylight because they strobe at impossible intensity. Scavengers follow rupture events when overloaded stems burst and shower nutrient resin over the flats. Pollinators are less bees and more kites: membrane-winged animals that anchor themselves to sonic nodes where flowers open only at specific frequencies.

The weirdness is subtle until you stay long enough. Then patterns emerge that feel almost intentional. Certain valleys “start” at dawn with a sequence of tones as different plant guilds vent and expand. There are migration routes that track not food but harmonic corridors, as if animals travel where the landscape is in key. Some ridgelines act like memory: damage one section, and distant sections alter growth angles days later, maintaining aerodynamic performance across kilometers. In rare calm windows, when the wind drops below threshold, the whole biome pauses—fronds folded, vents sealed, fauna crouched—as if waiting for the engine of weather to restart.

At night, brightness does not fully disappear. The sky is clear and star-thick at this altitude; mineral dust in upper air scatters moonlight into a persistent glow. Many plants expose reflective underlayers after dusk, turning slopes into dim silver fields. Cold deepens quickly. Frost forms not from humidity but from outgassed volatiles crystallizing along spine edges in intricate geometric films. By morning, the first gusts strip them away in glittering plumes.

There are no ruins here, no sign that anyone built and left. The place is untouched by construction and still unmistakably technological in effect: a planet-scale, self-maintaining, wind-driven biotic industry, loud as war, old as stone, and alive in every direction.
