At high altitude, where you would expect bare rock and wind, there is instead a **made basin** the size of a temple district—bounded, enclosed, complete. Nothing here is natural, not in any visible way. The ground is paved in fitted slabs of fired clay and dark stone; the retaining walls are dry-laid blocks rubbed so long by grit that their seams have become almost calligraphy. Every surface bears tool marks from hands, wedges, cords, levers—simple means, repeated for centuries until the work outlived its makers.

The place is warm in a stubborn, body-level way: not blazing, but heat-soaked. The air is dry enough to chap lips in minutes. No moss in cracks, no grass in mortar joints, no insect stitch in the dust. There are no tracks because there is nothing that leaves tracks. Even the dust is reluctant—heavy mineral flour that settles quickly and stays where it lands. If there is chemistry of life here, it does not resemble life as movement; it resembles varnish, tarnish, thin crusts of salts that bloom and stop, bloom and stop, but never spread into anything like growth.

Above, the light is dim and old. A permanent haze of suspended powder turns daylight into late-evening amber. Shadows exist, but softly, as if every edge has been handled too many times. The basin sits high enough that clouds pass beneath the outer parapets on some horizons, yet no moisture reaches the interior; whatever weather exists outside is filtered away by layered screens of masonry, baffles, and vent towers. The sky appears through narrow cuts, like strips of tarnished metal.

The density is overwhelming. There is no open plaza, no resting emptiness. The whole basin is packed with structures: stacked buttresses, low vaults, chimney-throats, ribbed ducts, belfry-like frames, hanging lattices of ceramic tubes, stone throats drilled through columns, and drum chambers capped by stretched hides that have long since mineralized into rigid membranes. Every handspan of space has been assigned to some part of one gigantic apparatus. Walking through it would mean turning sideways, climbing, ducking, squeezing through intentional gaps.

And the sound—constant, monumental, inescapable—is the ruling climate.

Wind is caught at the rim and driven through thousands of tuned passages: reed slots, whistle pipes, resonant cavities, slit-gongs, vibrating panels. The system is entirely passive and entirely relentless. No gears, no engines, no visible fuel. Just pressure, channel, resonance. The result is a deafening, layered roar that never drops to silence and almost never varies. Deep tones hold like mountains. Midrange howls braid and unbraid. Needle-high shrieks stitch the top of hearing. You feel it in jawbones, in teeth, in sternum before you identify it as sound. Conversation is impossible. Thought becomes rhythmic, forced to march with the vibration.

Age is everywhere. Corners have been rounded not by rain, but by centuries of airborne grit and acoustic tremor. Ceramics are glazed smooth by abrasion. Stone lips at pipe mouths have worn into soft ellipses. Some channels have narrowed by mineral deposition; others have widened by erosion, yet the whole system has settled into a stable equilibrium: each deviation compensated by another, until the same vast chord reasserted itself. It feels less like decay than like final form—the state reached after all alternatives were exhausted.

There is no fauna to nest in the cavities, no plants to split joints, no water to rust and rot. So the place persists. Its ecology is mechanical-mineral: dust circulates, crystals accrete, flakes detach, powders drift, and the architecture absorbs all of it without changing character. In this world, sound is weather, erosion is timekeeping, and construction is geology. The basin does not ruin. It does not renew. It simply continues, full and warm and dim, forever singing one unending note large enough to stand inside.
