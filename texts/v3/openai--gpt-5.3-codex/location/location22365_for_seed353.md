From a distance, it looks like a mountain city made of charcoal coral: a broad highland basin packed edge to edge with black-green towers, ribs, lattices, and stacked terraces, every surface grown and then machined, then regrown over the machining. There is no bare ground anywhere. Even what passes for pavement is alive.

The basin sits high enough that the air is thin and hot at once—heat without comfort, a dry thermal pressure that seems to come up from below and down from above together. The sky is almost never visible. A permanent lid of soot-colored cloud hangs low, lit only by dull industrial glow from beneath. Light does not fall here; it leaks. It comes out of seams, vent grilles, translucent vascular tubes, furnace mouths, and the occasional strip of electroluminescent mold woven through support trusses. Most of the world stays in deep near-black, with shapes recognized by sheen and silhouette more than color.

As your eyes adjust, the “forest” reveals itself as a complete built system.

Each trunk is also a chimney. Each canopy is also a heat exchanger. Each vine is also a conduit carrying syrup-thick hydrocarbons, reactive salts, and gaseous feedstock instead of water. Leaves are narrow metal-hard blades, mineralized with silica and copper, angled to condense trace vapors from the air—every molecule scavenged, nothing wasted. Root-mats have no soil to penetrate; they grip stacked ceramic plates and old composite foundations, clamping onto infrastructure laid down so long ago that the original architects are only inferred from repeating patterns buried twenty growth-cycles deep.

This place is old in the way slag is old: not untouched, but reworked until stable.

You can see erosion written into the forms, though there is almost no rain and little free liquid. Age comes from abrasion, thermal cycling, and chemical wind. Hot drafts loaded with fine crystal dust have shaved corners off entire districts. Buttresses are fluted into knife-thin fins; exposed growth rings have become hard as stone; once-ornate facades have softened into identical windward curves. Ancient conveyor gorges run through the basin, not carved by rivers but by centuries of moving biomass and ash. Their walls are polished by traffic and storms to a dark satin.

Nothing here is “natural” in the old sense, yet nothing is inert.

The vegetation is engineered into architecture, and architecture is pruned into vegetation. Apartment-like hollows are grown inside giant lignin columns. Bridgeways are braided from living cable-roots that tighten or relax depending on thermal load. Factory blocks are wrapped in photosynthetic skin despite the darkness; the skin feeds mostly on furnace light, chemical gradients, and stored energy from older bright ages. Entire neighborhoods migrate by growth—slowly lifting from old anchors, extending into adjacent voids, and abandoning petrified sections that become load-bearing fossils.

No animals move through this world. No birds, no insects, no scavengers in cracks. Pollination and maintenance are done by pressure pulses, static charges, and timed spore releases. Reproduction is industrial: seed-capsules stamped in foundry orchards, sorted by heat tolerance, fired through pneumatic trunks into vacant scaffolds. When a structure fails, it is not eaten; it is dismantled by enzyme fog, then rebuilt in place with corrected geometry.

Despite the turbulence, the soundscape is restrained. This is not a roaring factory hell. It is mostly hush and interval.

You hear long breaths of venting gas. The tick of contracting beams. A distant chain drag. Dry leaves ringing against one another like thin metal. Low transformer hum buried under everything, felt more in the ribs than heard. During storm shifts—common, abrupt, and dangerous—the basin fills with rushing particulate wind that turns all those blade-leaves into a single vast whisper, like sand being poured across a million drums. Then it quiets again, almost immediately, as if muted by command.

And there are rules, clear enough that even a newcomer can learn them quickly:

- **Do not trust stillness.** Apparent stability often means a growth phase is about to invert.
- **Stay off pale surfaces.** Light-colored crusts are fresh mineral blooms over heat vents; they collapse without warning.
- **Follow the warm pulse-lines.** Faint periodic glows in trunk-veins mark safe pressure corridors and breathable zones.
- **Never shelter in deadwood.** Fossilized districts look solid but delaminate in thermal gusts.
- **When the basin goes silent, find an anchor.** Full silence means charge buildup before electrostatic spore storms.

At regional scale, the highland is a dense mosaic of named sectors—Kiln Groves, Siphon Rows, the Old Rack, Lantern Hollows—each specialized but physically interlocked. Nothing has room, yet everything expands. Turbulence is not random destruction; it is the operating mode. Growth surges, pruning collapses, vent storms, rerouted flows, sudden blackout, sudden bloom. The whole basin behaves like one organism undergoing industrial metabolism.

In the darkest hours, when only maintenance mycelia are lit, the landscape resembles a starfield turned inside out: constellations threaded through trunks, running along catwalk roots, descending into exhaust wells. You realize then that this hot, dry, crowded plateau is less a place *with* plants than a place *made of* plant—an ancient manufactured ecology that replaced ground, weather, and city alike, and has been quietly, relentlessly refining itself ever since.
