The basin sits lower than everything around it, a broad cold bowl cut into young stone, as if a glacier left yesterday and forgot to take its meltwater with it. From the ridges above, it looks almost level—an inland sea of reeds, roofs, channels, and pale mist—but once you descend, you realize it is all folded layers: flooded streets, raised causeways, sunken gardens, peat islands, and fresh-cut drainage veins still sharp at the edges.

Water is never absent. It beads on basalt faces, seeps up through black soil, runs in thin bright threads under plank bridges, and gathers in shallow mirrors between structures. The ground is not truly ground; it is sponge, root mat, mud, and stone in negotiation. After rain, the whole settlement seems to sweat. Even on clear days, cold vapor drifts from warmer channels into the air and hangs low among willow crowns.

It is lush in a way that ignores latitude. The temperature stays near freezing for long stretches, but the basin’s wetness and shelter keep it green: sedges shoulder-high, moss as thick as felt over every wall, dwarf alder leaning over canals, berry scrub tucked into any dry seam, lichens painting the cut stone in silver and citron. Vegetation doesn’t frame the built space; it occupies it. Posts sprout ferns. Roofs host mats of grass. The banks of every lane are braided roots and woven stems, maintained, trimmed, and replanted so often they read like architecture.

And there is architecture—dense, intentional, almost urban in pattern, but made with hand, bone, hide, reed, peat brick, and split timber. No metal glints anywhere. No glass. No wires, no engines, no kiln-fired regularity. Yet the organization is unmistakable: districts on slightly raised knolls, radial footways, storage mounds sealed in clay, communal halls sunk partly into earth for warmth, sluice gates made from interlocked logs and stone weights. Everything is close-packed. There is no empty lot, no spare margin. If a place is dry enough to stand on, something grows there, shelters there, nests there, or is built there.

The city and marsh are not opposing categories here; they are one system. Canals are both street and fish run. Reed fences break wind and trap silt. Waste channels feed fungal beds. Boardwalks are lifted and relaid each thaw season because the basin is still changing—banks calve, new springs open, old channels choke with debris. The land is young and unsettled, and the people who shaped this place built for revision, not permanence.

Despite all that activity, the soundscape is restrained. Water speaks quietly: drip, wick, lap, the soft clack of current against poles. Footsteps are dull on wet timber. Voices carry only a little before fog and moss absorb them. Fauna is abundant but mostly low-voiced—wading birds, marsh hare, trout turning in dark water, otters slipping under platforms, frogs pulsing at dusk, clouded swarms of insects over warm outflows, foxes moving along embankments. Predation happens constantly, but at this temperature and in this soft ground, even urgency is hushed.

Light is ordinary daylight, just filtered and redistributed. The basin catches long, slanted sun; cloud cover turns everything pearled and diffuse; standing water throws light upward into undersides of leaves and bridge decks. In winter, thin ice plates over side pools, and the sky feels very high above a low world. In summer, the day is longer than the warmth, and green persists under cold rain.

The governing rule of the place is saturation: nothing remains separate for long. Wood becomes mossed, moss becomes soil, soil becomes channel, channel becomes road, road becomes reedbed, reedbed becomes wall. Another rule is proximity: every life-form exists within arm’s reach of another, stacked in vertical layers from root to roof to branch. And a final rule is adaptation without machinery. The basin is undeniably engineered, but by repeated labor, memory, and material intelligence rather than devices. It is a wet, cold, crowded underland that keeps moving, keeps growing, and keeps remaking itself in quiet.
