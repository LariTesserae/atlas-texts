The place is roughly the size of a village square, but it feels bigger because there is almost no open ground to measure it by.

You come into it along a raised strip of old concrete, and within a few steps that strip is swallowed by growth: roots buckling slab, vines glazing railings, fern trunks erupting through what used to be drainage grates. The whole basin sits low, only a little above a broad estuary somewhere beyond the trees, and water is always near—if not standing, then beading, sweating, hanging in the air. The soil never fully dries. Even the metal perspires.

### First impression: a green machine that forgot which part was machine

Vegetation is not “in” this place; it is the place.  
Leaves layer over leaves in vertical stacks. Reeds rise through lattices of cable. Moss pads over rusted housings. Huge rope-thick climbers tie tree to tower to tree until each structure becomes a rib inside a living body.

There are scattered built elements, but each one is partial, absorbed:

- a pump house with no walls left, just a frame of girders wrapped in strangler fig;
- three cylindrical tanks, split and open like seed pods, holding black rainwater and floating mats of duckweed;
- a line of ceramic insulators still strung between poles, now used as anchor points by climbing orchids;
- concrete plinths with bolts where machines were removed long ago, now topped by bromeliad rosettes full of frogless water.

Nothing dominates. Artifacts persist like bones in peat.

### Terrain and materials

The ground alternates between:

1. **spongy hummocks** of root and leaf mold, springing underfoot;
2. **shallow runnels** of tea-colored water, slow enough to reflect every stem;
3. **hard islands** of old industrial surface—brick, poured slab, grating—slick with algae.

Because erosion has had time to settle into balance, edges are rounded. Nothing is freshly broken. Concrete corners have become curves. Metal plates are scalloped by rust and plant acids, then stabilized under mats of lichen. Timber, where it survives, is mineral-dark and water-heavy.

Everything carries a film: pollen paste, condensation, fine silt, green dust. If you touch anything, your fingers come away marked.

### Light and atmosphere

Light is ordinary daylight, but filtered through layers of green. At noon it is diffuse and humid, not dim, just softened. Shadows are shallow because leaves catch and re-emit light from all angles. After rain, the air turns reflective; tiny droplets hover and make distant shapes look doubled, as if there are two versions of each tree offset by a breath.

Warmth is constant. Not tropical blaze—more like a permanent late-morning heat held under a wet blanket. Sweat appears quickly and evaporates slowly. The place smells of chlorophyll, iron oxide, anaerobic mud, bruised stems, and the faint ozonic tang of old electrics that still occasionally arc somewhere unseen.

### Sound: louder than it looks

From a distance it seems still. Inside, it is loud in layers:

- continuous drip from leaf tips to metal to water;
- the friction-hiss of broad leaves rubbing in slow wind;
- periodic hollow *thunk* when swollen seed capsules drop onto tank roofs;
- a low mechanical hum from a surviving pump deep under root mass;
- belts or chains somewhere turning at minimal speed, barely useful, never quite stopping;
- occasional steam-like exhalations from vents clogged with moss.

There are very few animals to punctuate it. No chorus of birds. No mammal movement in the underbrush. Sometimes one insect buzzes past with absurd volume, then nothing again but water and machinery. The absence of fauna makes the plant-and-metal noises feel intentional, almost conversational.

### Ecology: plant-major, animal-minor

Food webs here are short and plant-heavy.  
Primary productivity is overwhelming; consumption is sparse. Fallen matter accumulates, compresses, ferments, and is taken back by fungi and roots. You can see nutrient loops physically: roots dive into old sumps, emerge coated black, then feed new leaf canopies overhead.

Some plants have specialized around the built remnants:

- **coil-vines** that preferentially wrap warm pipes, using residual heat;
- **rust lilies** whose roots concentrate iron and stain their petals orange;
- **condenser moss** growing thickest on insulated tanks, dripping harvested moisture into miniature channels it has grown for itself;
- **fan palms** that orient around rotating shafts to maximize intermittent airflow.

Fauna exists mostly as traces: shed skins, snail rasp marks, tiny fish in isolated puddles during flood phases, a rare pale heron that arrives at dusk and stands among pipes like a misplaced survey instrument.

### Weirdness and world-rules

The strange part is not that nature reclaimed industry; it’s that the merger reached a stable operating state.

This place appears to follow a few local rules:

1. **Any moving part eventually becomes a trellis.**  
   If it turns, a plant species evolves to use that motion—spreading spores, grinding seed coats, polishing leaves.

2. **Any surface that condenses water becomes alive within weeks.**  
   Moisture is the real infrastructure. Pipes and tanks are now climate organs.

3. **Growth favors circuits.**  
   Vines connect conductive remnants in closed loops. During wet afternoons, faint bioluminescent pulses run along some of these green circuits, slow as sap flow, as though electrical memory persists in tissue.

4. **Silence attracts collapse.**  
   Fully quiet zones—where drip and hum cease—tend to sink into peat. Sound, however mechanical, marks active ground.

None of this changes quickly. The dynamic is patient: roots thicken by millimeters, puddles migrate by centimeters, gears lose one tooth every few seasons, then are bypassed by tendrils and kept in motion anyway. Over years, not days, pathways close and reopen.

### Scale and boundary

The landscape has clear edges. On three sides, embankments of packed clay and old retaining wall hold it like a bowl. On the fourth side, a sluice gate—half-open forever—lets excess water ooze out into reed flats. You can cross the entire site in ten minutes if you force a line; it takes hours if you follow the actual navigable spaces, ducking under stems, stepping across old catwalk remnants, waiting for a platform of roots to settle before committing weight.

It feels like a single organism with rooms:

- tank court,
- pump hollow,
- vine nave,
- settling pond,
- cable grove.

“Cathedral scale” is right, but not because it is huge—because vertical layers and acoustics make each chamber feel deliberate.

### If you stayed

After a while you’d stop looking for wildlife and start reading plant posture and machine tone instead. A louder hum means rising groundwater. A certain mint-metal scent means a relay box is warming under moss. Shiny new leaves over a vent mean warm air still flows there. The place teaches attention to slow systems: not events, but conditions.

By evening, normal light goes gold, and every wet surface answers back. The entire basin seems to glow from thousands of tiny reflections. Nothing dramatic happens. No sudden migration, no stormbreak, no collapse. Just the long, wet continuation of an old agreement between chlorophyll and steel.
