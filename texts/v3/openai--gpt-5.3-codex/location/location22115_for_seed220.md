The place is a city, but it behaves like a cave.

Not metaphorically—a literal cavern, shallow under the crust, where the rock ceiling hangs low enough to feel present and high enough to swallow whole neighborhoods. The basin is roughly the size of an old cathedral district: broad nave-like void, side chambers, service corridors branching off like crypt aisles. The ground is mostly stone worn smooth by centuries of redirected runoff, with long polished troughs where water still chooses the same routes it always has. Erosion has finished arguing with architecture here. The walls, the channels, the foundations—everything is rounded at the edges, settled into agreement.

It is warm in a way that never feels like sunlight. Heat leaks from below: geothermal veins, power conduits, old reactors no one fully understands anymore. The air carries moisture in pulses. For hours it can be dry enough for dust to whisper under your boots, then a pressure shift opens vent-shafts and the whole district fogs. Condensation pearls along cables, handrails, and ceramic skins of machinery. Drips gather, fall, and are caught by gutter-grids that feed black cisterns sunk in the floor. There are no rivers here, just episodes of water: sweating walls, brief interior rain, pooled mirrors in depressions where broken pavement has sagged.

The built environment is dense by intention and empty by occupancy. Towers only five or six stories high are stacked on plinths and linked by enclosed bridges, but half of them are dark and sealed. Transit tubes run overhead, transparent but unlit, arcing through the cavern like frozen tendons. Service gantries lace the ceiling in geometric layers. The planning is unmistakably urban: zones, utility trunks, numbered sectors, civic plazas. Yet between one active block and the next there can be a hundred meters of open stone and shadow. Whole streets stand ready and unused, lane lights dead, signage still updating in languages no one now speaks natively.

Light is scarce, and mostly functional. Thin violet maintenance strips, emergency glyphs, occasional scanning beams that move and vanish. Most surfaces are matte and drink what little light there is; only wet metal and mineral crust give back a sheen. Your eyes learn edges first: rail, curb, stair lip, then forms emerge—an arch, a crane arm, a stand of pillars grown from both concrete and calcite. In the darkest spans, orientation is done by sound.

Sound is constant and big. Not chatter—there are too few people for that—but infrastructure at work: intake fans, pressure doors cycling, distant mag-tractors, periodic valve thunder, warning tones, sluice release, the hiss of steam through needle vents. Drips become percussion in vaulted spaces. A single maintenance drone passing overhead can fill the chamber with rotor echo for thirty seconds after it’s gone. Somewhere, always, a pump starts up with a whale-deep note that rattles chest bone. Silence exists only in sealed rooms.

Vegetation survives as a strategy, not a landscape. Sparse mats of heat-moss cling near vent condensers where mineral water is warm and regular. Pale wire-vines run through cracks in insulation foam and down into damp mortar, leafless but alive. In one quadrant, old bioremediation planters have gone mostly dormant; a few tough reed-analogs still stand in shallow runoff trays, their stalks translucent, moving faintly with air currents. Green is rare enough to be mapped.

Fauna is rarer, but not absent. Small blind skitterers—more cartilage than bone—feed on fungal films in drain mouths. Long-winged cave swifts nest in turbine housings and hunt insects drawn to diagnostic LEDs. Once in a while something larger is heard in utility shafts: padded movement, then nothing. People speak of maintenance organisms released generations ago—self-limiting, nonaggressive, mostly—though no one agrees which are still biological and which are machine systems that learned hunger.

The technology here is beyond modern in the way old miracles become municipal. Matter-repair foam climbs cracks overnight and hardens to the texture of stone. Structural fields hold impossible spans where columns should be. Air is sorted by invisible membranes stretched across alleys. Data moves through mineral veins doped long ago; walls themselves are circuitry. Some doors open only when they detect the humidity profile of a known neighborhood. Some lamps light in anticipation, as if they remember traffic patterns from years when the city was full.

And that is one of the rules of this place: systems assume continuity even when people vanish. Another: water has right of way—always. Streets dip around runoff channels; elevators lock during condensation storms; power reroutes to pump stacks before anything else. Another: noise means life. If a sector goes quiet, crews go in fast.

Nothing here is ruin, exactly. It is active, maintained, still in use—just underused, like a theater running machinery for an audience of twenty in a hall built for thousands. The cavern breathes in cycles: warm surge, mistfall, fan-roar, drain, dim lull, then motion again. The city keeps itself ready in the dark, expecting return.
