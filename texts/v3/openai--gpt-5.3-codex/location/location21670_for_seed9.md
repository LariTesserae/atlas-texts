At this altitude, the air should be thin and stern, but this place has learned warmth by manufacture.

It is a **small upland basin**, roughly the size of a village square, cupped inside a ring of newly raised buttresses. Nothing here is bedrock in the old sense. The ground is poured, riveted, printed, tiled: matte basalt-composite plates, ribbed ceramic channels, gritted polymer aggregate still showing the texture of their molds. Even the “cliffs” are cast in segmented lifts, each seam labeled in enamel numerals. The mountain is not ancient; it is **under construction as a landscape**. Its faces are too sharp, too clean, too uninterpreted by time. Corners have not yet rounded. No talus. No patient chewing by wind.

And yet it is wet—constantly, luxuriously wet.

Clouds snag on the high ringwall and unravel into condensate. Warm mist beads on steel handrails, on vine leaves, on cable sleeves, then gathers and falls in steady punctuation into narrow runnels. Water sits close under every surface: press a boot to a panel and you feel the hollow coolness beneath, cisterns and pressure chambers and capillary mats. The basin floor is a marsh made of engineering—floating decks of latticework over shallow treatment pools where roots lace through mesh and nutrient foam. Water moves everywhere but rarely rushes; it travels by overflow lips, siphon loops, and slow gravitational sheets. After rain (and there is often rain), the whole place weeps for hours.

Vegetation has taken this invitation and become almost indecently lush. Fern towers, glossy and thick-stemmed, crowd around vent stacks. Climbing gourds wrap scaffold poles in green corkscrews. Moss lines expansion joints in plush seams. Broadleaf canopies stretch from gantry to gantry until the pathways below feel tunnelled, room-like, near-touching. Orchids bloom from bolt heads. Reeds grow in fabricated marsh pans, their roots threading through stainless grates into warm runoff below. You can’t tell, at a glance, what is horticulture and what is opportunistic takeover; likely both. The density is intimate—leaf to pipe, vine to ladder, frond against warning placard.

The industrial layer is present but oddly polite. There are pumps, belt housings, condensers, valve forests, maintenance cranes on short rails. Indicator lamps blink amber in plant shade. A low electric hum never quite disappears, but this is mostly a **quiet** place: drip, hiss, leaf-brush, the occasional thump of a pressure gate equalizing. When a larger machine starts—a mist fan rotating open, a recirculation engine engaging—it feels like a throat clearing in a library. Motion is constant, noise restrained.

Life here is active without being crowded by animals. Fauna is sparse. A few pale frogs live in overflow boxes, appearing only at dusk. Needle-thin dragonflies patrol the warmer channels. Sometimes a single mountain swallow spirals in through the cloud gap, astonished by the humidity, and leaves again. The abundance belongs to plants, fungi, microbes in biofilm skins, and to systems: water cycling, nutrient dosing, heat exchange, growth and pruning schedules. The ecosystem is a choreography of valves and roots.

There is strangeness in the rules.

- Water is given right-of-way over everything; paths bend around drainage lines, not the reverse.  
- Any surface that stays dry for more than a day sprouts irrigation nozzles by design.  
- “Soil” is a recipe, mixed in batches and swapped out by crane, so fertility comes in production lots.  
- Erosion has not begun in earnest because wear is repaired faster than weather can write itself. The landscape is always on its **first day**, repeatedly.  
- New topography appears overnight: a fresh berm of cast stone, a just-installed retaining rib, a stair where yesterday there was only moss and mist.

In normal daylight—no dramatic eclipse, no alien glow—the place looks almost believable, which makes it stranger. Sunlight lands on leaves like any tropical morning, while beneath them run numbered conduits and service tags. Shadows of fronds stripe caution-yellow paint. A kingfisher-blue flower opens beside a pressure gauge. High above the cloudline and still soaked through, the basin feels like a prototype for a mountain climate: a warm, wet room built at the edge of the sky, trying to teach itself how to become a wilderness before age arrives.
