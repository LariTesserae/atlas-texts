The place is called a city, but at first it looks like weather trapped in stone.

You enter a broad underland just beneath the skin of the world: not a deep abyss, not a mine, but a collapsed plateau of caverns and open chambers where old ceilings fell away and left a country of roofed valleys. Daylight gets in through long cracks and sinkholes above, and the rock does the rest. White gypsum, pale limestone, mica dust—everything catches light and returns it. So although you are below ground, it is startlingly bright, like walking inside a cloud made of bone.

Nothing green grows here. No vines, no moss, no grass softening the edges. The terrain is mineral all the way down: smooth domes, fluted walls, old channels worn into the floor where water used to run far harder than it does now. Erosion has had ages to work. Corners are rare. Pillars stand like old teeth, rounded by vanished rivers. Whole districts sit on shelves carved by ancient flow, and the shelves are so regular they look cut by hand, though they predate the city by eras.

The city itself is spread thinly across this open basin. It is urban in reach, not in crowding. Built places come in clusters: stone courts around cistern pits, rows of mud-plastered rooms tucked against warm walls, rope bridges linking ledges, kiln yards, animal pens, towers for watching the water. Between neighborhoods: long stretches of emptiness, pale flats, echoing ramps, abandoned channels. You can walk half a day and pass only one guard post and a line of clay markers.

Technology is simple and close to the hand. Stone, fired clay, woven fiber, bone pegs, leather valves, polished shell, simple copper where they can trade for it. Water-lifts are tread-powered. Gates are counterweighted with rock baskets. Drainage is solved with slope and patience, not engines. Yet infrastructure is everywhere if you learn to see it: gutter grooves carved into floors, condensation cloths strung high where cold air settles, settling basins, spill stairs, flood marks painted in mineral red.

Moisture comes in episodes. Most days are cool and dry enough to keep dust loose underfoot. Then rain arrives from above through the cracks—brief, slanting, local. One district can be wet while the next remains powder-dry. Condensation forms nightly on certain ceilings and drips into waiting jars. In lower channels, water appears as chains of shallow pools, silver in the light, then vanishes again into porous stone. The whole settlement lives by timing these pulses: collect, store, filter, ration, wait.

Despite the scarcity of plants, life is abundant—just not the kind you expect. Fauna dominate every niche. Pale six-legged grazers lick mineral films from wet walls. Slick-backed tunnel fish survive in temporary pools and bury themselves in silt when they shrink. Beetles the size of fists move in patient lines toward salt deposits. Flocks of membrane-winged cave gliders wheel silently through light shafts, feeding on smaller insects stirred up by wind. Herds are moved through the city like weather fronts, and every structure is built with animal movement in mind: ramps wide enough for horned backs, low arches that discourage predators, roost lattices above market courts.

The strangest thing is how turbulent the place is while remaining quiet. Pressure shifts between chambers can turn still air into sudden gales; dust spirals race through streets, overturning baskets but making only a soft rushing sound. Flash runoff can thread through dry channels in minutes, forcing whole neighborhoods to haul goods uphill, then disappear before night. Migratory swarms pass overhead in dense ribbons, shadowing the bright floor, yet their wings are nearly noiseless. Even busy hours feel hushed—tools knocking, rope creaking, low calls, hoof-scrape, drip. The stone seems to absorb anger and thunder alike.

Rules of living here are old and strictly practical:

- Build above the third flood line, never below.
- Keep one roof open to catch light, one roof sealed to catch water.
- Leave corridors for herd movement, or the herds will make their own.
- Mark every new crack in white paint; daylight is both blessing and warning.
- In wind-rise, tie everything down and speak with hand signs.

Because this underland is ancient, people do not imagine they will tame it. They negotiate with it, season by season, gust by gust. Districts migrate a little over generations, following new drips, abandoning exhausted cisterns, reusing old stone. Streets are less like permanent lines and more like remembered routes between stable points: the Salt Court, the East Spill Stair, the Three Pillars, the Quiet Kilns.

From a high ledge, the whole landscape reads as a pale, open map: bright fractures above, dry flats below, scattered built clusters, moving animal bands, sudden wet gleams where water has briefly returned. It feels old enough to have forgotten forests, but not dead—restless, adaptive, and continually rearranging itself in near silence.
