The land lies almost level with the sea, as if time has been sanding it down for ages and simply forgot to stop.  
Nothing rises sharply. Instead there are long, low shelves of dark stone, shallow basins full of brackish water, and old channels that split and rejoin like scars. The ground is mostly mineral: slate-gray mud, polished black rock, salt crust, and pale bands of clay that shine when wet. Everywhere is wet. Even where there is no standing pool, water sits just under the surface; a heel-step draws it up.

There are no trees, no grass, no green roofs of anything rooted. The closest thing to “growth” is film: slick microbial skins across stones, pearl-dull fungal veils in shaded cracks, and thready white colonies that cling to the undersides of ledges where spray keeps them fed. Life here does not rise upward; it spreads thinly across surfaces, then gets eaten.

The sky is usually a low, luminous gray, more glow than daylight. Light arrives diffused through mist and marine cloud, so distance is visible but color is muted—iron, bone, wet charcoal, tarnished silver. At dawn and dusk the horizon does not brighten much; it merely shifts from cold blue-gray to warmer smoke-gray. Nights are dark but not fully black, because water and cloud hold a dim sheen.

What fills the world is sound.  
The coast breathes in broad tidal surges that rush through carved channels and explode against hollow rock chambers, turning cliffs into giant flutes and drums. Mud vents belch gas with deep, percussive thumps. Vast colonies of shore-beasts—seal-like grazers, hard-shelled crawlers, hooked beaks cracking mineral crusts—call constantly: barks, whistles, metallic clacks, and long siren cries. Even still air isn’t quiet; droplets condense and fall from overhangs in relentless patter. You shout to be heard at arm’s length.

Fauna is abundant because the water is rich. Each tide brings suspended nutrients, planktonic swarms, and drifting spawn. Filter-creatures line the channels in moving bands, opening and closing in sync with current pulses. Predators run the flats in cycles: low-bodied runners at ebb, gliding hunters in floodwater, aerial scavengers in fog breaks. Migration is rhythmic and visible from miles away—dark moving fronts crossing pale mud. With little vertical cover, everything depends on timing, camouflage, and speed.

Signs of people appear only here and there: low ring-walls of stacked stone above flood marks, hide-covered lean-tos on basalt knobs, fish weirs made of sharpened stakes and woven cord, smoke-blackened caves with shell heaps at their mouths. Tools are simple and stubbornly practical—bone hooks, chipped stone blades, net weights, hammered soft-metal pins traded from elsewhere. Nothing is built to dominate the land; everything is built to be abandoned quickly when channels shift or storms erase a season’s work overnight.

This place follows a few hard rules:

1. **Water decides borders.** Maps change monthly. Paths are agreements with yesterday.
2. **Height is safety but never permanence.** Even “high ground” is only less wet.
3. **If it is quiet, something is wrong.** Silence means pressure drop, predator passage, or a tide behaving strangely.
4. **Food comes in pulses.** Feast-famine timing matters more than territory.
5. **Stone remembers.** Erosion has smoothed nearly everything, but old flow-lines predict new ones.

From a distance, the region looks empty—flat, dim, spare. Up close it is crowded with motion: ripples, tracks, feeding swarms, shifting fog, dripping ledges, calling throats. It is an ancient low world still actively being written over by water, one loud tide at a time.
