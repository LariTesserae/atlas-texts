At first it looks like a mistake in the earth: a shallow, warm basin sunk below a white mineral plain, too bright to look at for long. The ground outside is crusted gypsum and saltstone, flaked and wind-planed into old, settled forms—nothing jagged, nothing fresh, everything weathered into confidence. No streams, no mud, no scent of rain. The air tastes alkaline, dry enough to chap lips in minutes.

Then, as your eyes adjust, the basin reveals its secret scale: not vast, but hidden—an enclosed pocket tucked inside larger barrenness. A city has been threaded into it.

It is an urban place built for emptiness. Most of the volume is open air: a single huge hollow, like the inside of a bell. Along the inner walls, neighborhoods cling in terraces and ledges—stone steps, fired-clay galleries, timber braces blackened by age, rope lifts, lattice bridges. Primitive tools shaped it: chisels, adzes, hand-drills, wedges, pulleys. You can see the hand in every surface. No smooth machine finish, only repeated labor and repair. The city is dense in planning, sparse in matter.

And it is lush.

Not with wet leaves, but with dry-garden abundance: forests of jointed glass-reeds, thick waxy paddles, braided vine-columns whose skins are mineral and fibrous, green only at certain angles. They do not drink water. They harvest vapor from the warm vents that breathe up through fissures—sulfurous breath, carbon-rich gases, metallic mists—and fix it into oil, resin, and sugar-like crystals. Their roots spread through porous stone, extracting trace salts and heat. At dawn the groves click as tissues expand; at noon they exhale sharp, bitter fragrances; at night they fold and hum faintly with static. Lushness here is volume, complexity, and layered canopies—not moisture.

The light is relentless. Sun enters from above and ricochets off pale walls, shell-plaster facades, and mirror-panels made from polished mica. Shadows are hard-edged and blue. People hang woven screens across streets to make moving shade, so whole districts flicker like underwater places despite there being almost no water in the world. Even indoors, brightness leaks through vents and slit windows, drawing bright bars across dust.

Sound is the first law of this basin. It is deafening almost all day. Wind passes through carved throats in the cliff and turns them into organ pipes. Seed-pods burst with rifle-cracks. Stone mills grind resin-cake. Hammer crews shape brick in synchrony. Signal drums answer from terrace to terrace. The hollow geometry amplifies everything and returns it doubled; a dropped bowl can ring like a bell tower. Conversation is done close, mouth to ear, or by sign. Children learn direction by echo before they learn letters.

Fauna exists at the margins: a few heat-loving skitterers in wall cracks, pale glider-bats nesting in the upper ducts, lean six-legged grazers brought in and carefully bred to browse tough vine bark. Predators are nearly absent. Pollination is mostly wind and human hands with dusting brushes. Animal life feels precious and oddly ceremonial—named, tracked, never numerous.

Nothing here is static. Vents open and seal over seasons. Canopy-walks are re-tensioned weekly as vines gain weight. Salt blooms creep over masonry and get chipped away. Districts migrate upward or inward depending on which fissures are active and which groves are fruiting. The basin looks ancient because it is balanced, not because it is still.

The strangest rule is this: water is treated as a dangerous luxury. A skin of imported water, when it appears, is used for medicine, ritual adhesion, and finishing fine plaster—not for crops. Too much moisture rots the native plants and destabilizes walls. So this warm, bright, green city thrives by staying dry. It has learned abundance without wetness, density without clutter, and noise without chaos—a hidden urban garden built inside a resonant bowl of stone, alive in ways a rain-fed landscape would not recognize.
