From orbit it looks like frost on metal: a pale, bright continent of interlocked growth, all of it geometric at first glance, all of it alive when you stare longer.

There is almost no free water here. No rivers, no rain in the way you would mean it, no wet soil. The ground is a lattice of ceramic rootwork and manufactured loam, built layer over built layer across a shallow basin that spans half a hemisphere. Moisture exists only as breath-thin films pulled from the air at dawn, then vacuumed inward by capillary nets before the sun rises high. Every surface is designed to catch that film. Every surface is also vegetal.

The “plants” are not soft. They are pale green, silver, and chalk-white, with tissues reinforced by silica, carbon foam, and conductive veins. They grow as infrastructure: buttressed columns, ribbed canopies, load-bearing mats, corridor walls, pump housings, wind baffles. In many places you cannot tell where architecture ends and botany begins because there is no natural substrate beneath either; there are only older generations of growth-cities, cut, plated, grafted, and regrown. The landscape is built the way a reef is built, except by planners with foundries.

It is cool, always a little cooler than expected under such brightness. The sky is clear enough to hurt the eyes, but the air near the canopy floor is tempered by millions of transpiration exchangers—organs bred to release refrigerant gases instead of water vapor. So the place shines like noon and feels like morning. Low ridges roll out for thousands of kilometers, never truly mountainous, just immense shelves and depressions packed with dense vertical growth. Distance is hard to read because every horizon is cluttered with repeating trunks, towers, and conveyor-vines.

Industry is continuous but quiet. No roaring smokestacks, no clanging yards. Motion here is distributed: belts of living phloem carrying powdered minerals, piston-flowers opening and closing in synchronized fields, silent rail crawlers threading between root pylons. You hear tick-clicks of valve leaves, faint electrostatic hiss, the papery rasp of fronds turning to follow mirrors. At night—if it can be called night in a world so luminous—there is a low choir of harmonic hum from nutrient distilleries embedded in the canopy, like distant glass singing.

Erosion has done its long work, but not on stone. Old districts have sagged, compacted, and petrified into terraces of dead fiber and polymer bark. New growth climbs over them, honoring their shape. Wind has polished exposed ribs smooth; mineral dust has filled old conduits; abandoned scaffold-vines have become wind organs. Nothing feels raw or newly made. Even the newest construction carries the logic of a place that has been revising itself for ages.

Fauna exists, barely. A few glider organisms drift between canopy strata, feeding on pollen-conductive gels. Needle-legged grazers appear at dawn to scrape nutrient crust from cooling fins, then vanish into maintenance hollows. Predators are mostly microbial swarms triggered by chemical trespass. The dominant life is botanical-engineered mass, and what little animal life survives has adapted to be small, quiet, and opportunistic.

The weirdness is structural, not decorative. Space behaves oddly within mature growth districts: paths curve back with no measurable turn, shadows detach from their casters and pool under unrelated structures, and mapped distances vary by season as if the geometry swells and contracts with metabolic load. Entire avenues can “fold” during high-production cycles, not physically collapsing but reindexing—doorways leading to different sectors, airflow routing sound away so footsteps vanish. Locals navigate by scent codes and polarization patterns in leaf-skin, not by sightlines.

Its weather is internal. Above the upper canopy, jet streams drive bright dust around the basin in continental spirals. Below, thermal tides move on a six-hour pulse as industrial metabolism ramps and rests. Massive stomatal fields open in sequence, pulling air through kilometers of porous growth, generating steady directional winds that pollinate, cool machinery, and power turbine groves. The whole landscape breathes in phases, active everywhere, yet so well synchronized that it feels ceremonially calm.

The basic rule of the world is simple: nothing is inert unless it is dead, and nothing dead remains unused for long.  
Second rule: dryness is sacred capital. Every molecule is counted, borrowed, and returned through living infrastructure.  
Third rule: if a structure looks decorative, it is probably doing three jobs you cannot see.

Stand still long enough and the place reveals itself as one planetary organism pretending to be a city, or one city pretending to be a forest. Bright, cool, immense, and almost silent—except for the endless small sounds of things growing on purpose.
