The place is called a **sea** by the people who live at its edges, though it has never held a drop of water.

It lies under a continent-thick roof of stone: a buried basin so large that weather forms inside it, with its own prevailing winds, its own cloud-equivalent of mineral dust, its own horizons that fade into black. Imagine a cavern system, then scale it up until “cavern” fails — an underworld plain ribbed with fresh fault lines, knife-edged ridges, collapsed vaults, and fields of black glass where heat once surged through and froze quickly. Erosion has not had time to soften much. Corners are still corners. Breaks are still bright and raw. Walls keep their fracture patterns like recent scars.

The ground is dry in the strictest sense. Not merely parched — chemically unwilling. Salts crust in pale fans. Sulfur beads in yellow seams. Iron-rich dust lies in drifts that flow like powder snow under your steps. Water exists only as rumor, locked in crystal lattices or spoken of in myths as a dissolving force that would melt this world’s logic. Here, nothing swells, nothing rots wetly, nothing drinks. Things react, sublime, crack, and shed.

Despite the darkness, it is warm. Not furnace-warm, but body-warm and mineral-warm, heat rising steadily from below through vent fields and long seams in the basin floor. The air carries that warmth in layers. Stand still and you feel invisible rivers passing across your skin: thin currents of hot, sulfurous breath moving from deep cracks to higher domes. There is weather, but it is old and repetitive, so stable it feels like a law rather than an event. The wind always comes downslope at one watch, always climbs again at another. Dust mists gather in the same corridors each cycle. Nothing surprises anyone who has lived here long.

Light is scarce and local. There is no skyglow. Most of the basin is black enough to erase distance. Illumination comes from three things: ember-red vent mouths, brief static flickers when dust storms rub against basalt, and the bodies of animals. The living are the lamps of this place — not bright, but numerous. A thousand dim glows moving in different heights and rhythms can make an entire plain seem to breathe. Some creatures pulse cold blue to coordinate movement. Others drag pale bioceramic tails that phosphoresce only when scraped. Predators often stay dark.

There are no plants, no forests, no mosses softening stone. The base of the food web is chemical: films of metabolizing microbes that colonize hot mineral faces, chalky “reefs” of non-plant growth built from sulfur and metal compounds, and drifting sheets of spores from fungus-like colonies hidden in fissures. Grazers rasp these films with silica plates. Scavengers follow grazers. Burrowers follow everyone. The density of life is startling not because anything is lush, but because every ledge, crack, overhang, and thermal seam is occupied. Shell against shell. Antennae tapping antennae. Nest pits dug almost touching. Migration routes stacked in vertical layers like roads in a crowded city.

Most animals are built for touch and vibration rather than sight. They are low, armored, articulated things: six-legged dust-runners with whisker fans; ribbon-bodied tunnel eels that “swim” through talc-like sediment; broad-backed grazers that carry entire micro-ecosystems of parasites and hitchhikers in their joint folds. Larger hunters move slowly to preserve silence, tracking by thermal gradients and electrostatic fields. Flight exists, but only in short glides between stone spires — membrane-winged drifters catching steady convective updrafts from vents.

For all this abundance, the basin is mostly quiet. Noise is expensive here; sound travels oddly through dense, warm air and fractured rock, and anything loud is quickly located. So life has adapted toward hush. You hear granular sounds: the sift of dust, the click of claw on obsidian, distant pebblefalls, a single chitin plate flexing. At great distance, a constant sub-audible murmur rises from vent fields — not a roar, more like a held breath.

Built things appear in intervals, never in clusters big enough to become towns. Scattered works: stacked basalt waymarkers capped with reflective mica; low windbreak walls mortared with ash; bone-and-ceramic cages around safe vent mouths; rope ladders braided from tendon and fungal fiber descending into hunting pits. Primitive metal is rare but present in treasured forms — hammered copper hooks, soft bronze awls, bells that are never rung except in funerary caves. Most tools are stone, horn, fired clay, and patient handwork. The architecture follows the same principle as the ecology: occupy existing seams, do not fight the basin.

The people who move through this undersea of stone keep maps as songs of pressure and scent rather than lines on hide. They navigate by warmth, by draft direction, by the texture underfoot. They build little because the landscape itself is stable, and stable things become infrastructure. A ridge that is safe this generation will be safe in ten more. A vent that emits today will emit tomorrow. Paths become tradition because the world almost never changes enough to demand invention.

That may be the strangest rule here: on a vast scale, in deep darkness, amid crowded life, time feels nearly static. Birth and death are constant, but pattern is fixed. The same herds pass the same choke points. The same hunters wait at the same thermal bends. Dust settles, lifts, and settles again in familiar basins. Even ruins do not collapse quickly; they stand in dry air for centuries, half-buried, edges still sharp.

It is a young-looking world that has already settled into an old rhythm — a warm, air-filled abyss where stone is fresh, life is thick, and history repeats quietly in the dark.
