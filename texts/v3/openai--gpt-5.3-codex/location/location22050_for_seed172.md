It sits above the weather, but not above the cold.

You reach it through a hatch in the spine of a mountain-sized structure, and suddenly the world narrows to a single engineered basin: a high ledge no larger than a courtyard, hidden inside a ring of steel buttresses and vent-stacks. Nothing here is natural. The ground is plated in ribbed alloy and ceramic slabs, their edges still sharp from recent machining. Bolts are clean. Weld seams are bright. Corners haven’t had time to round off. Even the “soil” is a spread of crushed refractory grit poured into containment channels, as if someone wanted the idea of earth without allowing real earth in.

Water is almost absent, reduced to rumor. There are no puddles, no melt lines, no dampness underfoot. Moisture exists only as a brittle skin of rime on shadowed pipes, and if you scrape it with a glove it powders instead of smearing. The air is thin and mineral-dry, tasting faintly of iron filings and cold ozone. Breath flashes white once and disappears. Anything exposed long enough becomes static, glassy, and hard.

Light is ordinary in color and intensity, almost offensively normal against the strangeness of everything else. Banks of broad-spectrum lamps hang overhead and make clean, noon-like shadows. Beyond a grated perimeter you can glimpse open sky, pale and bright, far below and farther above at the same time, as if altitude has folded perspective into a shallow box.

And always, the sound.

Not loud in bursts—loud in total. A permanent, full-body industrial roar: turbines in sealed housings, compressor throats, pressure valves whispering at the edge of scream, harmonic vibrations traveling through every beam. It is deafening but stable, a single unbroken chord of machinery. Nothing starts. Nothing stops. The volume never rises, never falls. Even silence, here, would have to be manufactured.

Life appears only as sparse, stubborn vegetation in failure points. In hairline cracks where two plates don’t quite meet, pale cable-vines have rooted in insulation dust, their stems translucent and rigid as drawn glass. Low mats of black lichen-like growth cling to warm conduit collars, feeding on trace heat and metal salts rather than moisture. A few red needle-shoots stand alone in drainage grates, evenly spaced as if printed there. No insects visit them. No birds nest in the girders. No tracks cross the frost. The plants persist in isolation, each one its own tiny republic.

The place feels young, but not in the way of saplings and soft soil—young like a new blade, new concrete, a facility only recently brought to operating pressure. Erosion has barely begun. Time has not yet learned where to bite.

Its rules are simple and absolute:  
- Everything is fabricated.  
- Nothing is wet.  
- Noise is constant enough to become architecture.  
- Change, if it happens at all, happens elsewhere.

So this pocket remains: a cold, high industrial terrace, hidden in plain structure, where machinery performs the same instant forever and sparse plants write their quiet, improbable script in the seams.
