At this altitude, the world is not wild ground so much as **old workmanship**.

You come onto a high basin made entirely of human surfaces: terraces, retaining walls, stair-stepped streets, courtyards, gutters, cistern mouths, rooflines collapsed into one another and then settled in place. Stone, fired brick, lime plaster, old cedar beams gone silver and hard. Even where things have broken, they have broken into architecture—no open soil, no raw cliff, just rubble on paving, moss on walls, roots threading mortar joints. The land is a town-sized bowl of construction that has existed so long it now behaves like terrain.

It is warm here, though thin-aired. Not tropical heat, more a high, breathing warmth: skin damp in still air, cool where shadows pool. Light is scarce. Not because of night alone, but because the basin is narrow and deep-rimmed; sunrise arrives late, sunset early, and most of the day is a blue-gray dimness under cloud and overgrowth. Vines and fig canopies bridge alleys from building to building. Arcades swallow what daylight does enter. Interiors open to interiors. You move through bands of near-dark and darker.

Water comes intermittently and never wastes itself. Rain passes in short spells, then gone. Mist slides over the rim in the early hours and condenses on stone faces. Every course of masonry has learned to catch it: carved channels, drip lips, scalloped steps leading to cisterns black with depth. Pockets of standing water collect in roof pans and sunken courts. Most are shallow, skin-warm, green-edged. In drier weeks they shrink but rarely disappear; condensation and shade keep them alive. The whole basin is a damp mechanism without any moving parts.

Vegetation is lush but disciplined by the built forms. Trees grow from stairwells where lintels failed. Ferns line drainage cuts. Reeds rise in abandoned baths. Moss turns entire north walls velvet. Climbing plants rope across balconies and lock one façade to the next. Nothing reads as untouched wilderness; everything reads as succession on masonry, life choosing cracks, ledges, gutters, parapets. The green is thick, layered, and close—never broad meadow, always surfaces occupied.

And everywhere, animals. Not in occasional sightings—**in numbers**. Swifts spin through open halls. Pigeons and cliff doves mass on cornices. Bats pack vaulted ceilings by the thousand. Frogs own every cistern lip. Lizards skitter in sheets over warm brick. Beetles pulse in dark drains. At dusk, macaques (or their equivalent in this climate) travel the wall-lines like old residents using remembered streets. Packs of semi-feral dogs patrol lower courts. Goats graze vertical gardens of scrub on broken upper terraces. Insects provide the constant base note; birds and mammals ride over it in waves.

The sound is startlingly loud for a place with no machines. It is a **biological roar**: wingbeats in enclosed spaces, colony calls ricocheting off stone, mating choruses from water pockets, monkeys barking, hooves clattering stairs, dogs answering, frogs drilling from every damp cavity. In narrow lanes the echoes stack until direction disappears. You hear life before you see any opening, and silence never fully returns—only thins near noon, then swells again.

Erosion has done its long work and mostly stopped arguing. Edges are rounded, inscriptions softened, corners melted to touch. Stair treads are dished from centuries of feet and then of paws. Drain channels are polished where seasonal flow repeats the same path year after year. Collapse happened long ago in predictable places; now those slopes are stable, buttressed by root mats and fallen stone. The basin feels ancient but not fragile—weathered into equilibrium.

The pace of change is slow, almost ceremonial. Daily cycles matter (mistfall, chorus, dim noon, dusk surge), but decade to decade the place keeps its arrangement. A wall leans another inch. A fig takes ten years to split a parapet. A cistern silts by finger-widths. Animal populations pulse seasonally, yet the overall abundance persists because the architecture makes niches by the thousands. Nothing here is rushing toward climax or ruin. It is already in its long middle age.

So the rule of this landscape is simple: **construction became habitat, and habitat kept the construction legible**. Every surface is made; every made thing is occupied. High, warm, damp, dark, crowded, and loud—an old built world that has handed itself over to animals without ever ceasing to be a city.
