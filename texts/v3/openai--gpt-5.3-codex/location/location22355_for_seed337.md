At first it looks like a valley, then like a quarry, and only after a while like a city.

The whole place sits in a broad, shallow depression below the surrounding plain—perhaps thirty or forty meters down, enough that you descend by ramps and stairs and feel the temperature drop as you go. The light is ordinary daylight, not dim or magical, but it arrives at an angle. Morning sun spills over one rim first and crawls across everything in bands; afternoons are brighter and flatter; evenings gather cool quickly because the walls hold shadow. From above, the settlement reads as a tight weave of roofs, courtyards, scaffolds, cistern mouths, kiln domes, and drying frames, all packed edge to edge across the basin floor and climbing the lower slopes in stepped rings.

Nothing is old enough to be softened.  
Stone faces are still sharp from recent cutting. Clay banks show clean tool marks. Timber posts are straight and pale, not yet silvered by years. Even the streets feel provisional—hard-tamped earth with stone edging that ends abruptly where a new channel is being dug. It is a landscape in the middle of becoming itself: drainage ditches half-finished, retaining walls still receiving baskets of rubble, fresh terraces waiting for their first season of use. You can see the decisions happening in real time: where water should run, where people should sleep, where refuse should go, where the next stair should bite into the slope.

Water is present, but never abundant.  
Rain comes in short, cold spells, and the basin catches it. Overnight condensation beads on stone and canvas; dawn finds people wiping moisture from stretched cloth into jars. There are pooled pockets in shaded hollows, lined with clay to keep them from vanishing too fast, and narrow runnels that only carry flow after storms. Cisterns are everywhere—lidded, counted, guarded—not monumental, just necessary. The ground is damp in places, dusty in others, and everyone reads the difference with their feet.

Vegetation is sparse and practical.  
No forests, no lush fringe. Instead: mats of moss on north-facing stone, thin reeds around water pits, low bitter herbs in walled plots, a few knotty shrubs clinging to ledges where runoff lingers. People cultivate what survives cool air and unreliable wetness—leafy greens in shaded beds, tubers in imported soil, climbing beans trained up rope lattices beside warm walls. Every plant looks placed, protected, and slightly stressed, as if it has to justify the space it occupies.

Despite the tight density, the soundscape is restrained.  
This is an active place, but not a loud one. Damp surfaces swallow echoes. Work is mostly handwork: scraping, tying, levering, hauling, tamping. You hear rope strain, wooden wheels creak, shovels bite, water drip into jars, low voices carrying instructions across short distances. Even the market hours sound muted—barter in close conversation rather than shouted pitches. At night, wind moves along the rim and down the ramps; somewhere a gate beam settles; then mostly quiet.

Fauna is scarce.  
A few hardy birds circle the upper edges and occasionally drop into the settlement for scraps. Small burrowing animals appear near grain stores unless chased off. In wetter corners, pale frogs or slick-skinned insects emerge after rain, then vanish again. There are no great herds, no dense wild populations—animal life here is opportunistic and thin, following water and human presence.

The rules of the place are simple and visible:

- **Water outranks everything.** Streets, homes, workshops, and fields are arranged by catchment logic before comfort.  
- **Space is never empty for long.** If a ledge is stable, it becomes a platform. If a wall stands, someone leans a roof against it.  
- **Nothing is finished.** Every season adds another layer: another stair, another drain, another ring of dwellings.  
- **Coolth is an asset.** Food keeps longer below grade; workshops use shaded air; sleeping quarters tuck into earth for steadier temperature.  
- **Labor is continuous but measured.** The city grows by hands and simple tools, not by sudden transformation.

So the landscape feels both raw and fully inhabited: an emerging geology occupied at maximum intensity. A sunken, cool, damp-leaning urban basin where nearly every surface is spoken for, every motion is purposeful, and the future is visible as chalk lines and stacked stone waiting for tomorrow’s work.
