High above the tree line, where the mountain is mostly wind and stone, there is a **constructed hollow** tucked inside the summit works: a maintenance basin no larger than a chapel, hidden behind insulated doors and a curtain of hanging hoses. From the outside it is just another numbered panel on an industrial wall. Inside, it is its own climate.

Everything is made.

The floor is ribbed steel and poured composite, still too new to have softened. Edges are crisp. Bolt heads keep their hex corners. Guardrails shine under mineral film instead of rust, because the water here is constantly cycled, stripped, and returned. The place feels not old, but **permanently unfinished**—as if assembled yesterday and then held in that just-built state indefinitely. Even the grime looks applied in even coats, like primer.

And yet it is wet, deeply wet.  
Water lives in the air first: cold vapor that thickens against every surface, beads along conduit seams, gathers on cable jackets, then lets go in regular drops. Rock is almost absent, only occasional inspection windows revealing the mountain skin outside; even there the stone looks freshly cut, unweathered, like raw bone. Most of what you touch sweats. Most of what you hear is water striking metal in a million precise impacts.

The basin itself is shallow and black, a rectangle sunk into the floor grating. No ripples travel far—too many bodies interrupt them.

Because fauna dominates this room.

Not large animals: **small, relentless life** in numbers that feel architectural. Pale sump-fish bred for filtration, their whiskers combing intake meshes. Blind pipe-crabs with transparent shells clinging in clusters beneath overflow lips. Soot-winged moths the size of fingernails packed by the hundreds along warm relay housings. Thread-thin salamanders braided through cable trays, feeding on the moth larvae. A constant turnover of motion without any true change in pattern: each species locked into a route, a rhythm, a depth. They don’t migrate, don’t disperse, don’t boom or crash. They occupy, continuously.

Sparse vegetation survives only where machinery accidentally permits it: a strip of iron-gray moss at a failed seal, a few glassy stalks in the shadow of a drip manifold, one flattened mat of lichen around a lamp housing that never quite dries. Plant life here is not absent, just outnumbered to insignificance.

The air is cold enough that breath ghosts immediately, but not clean-cold. It tastes of wet concrete, hot insulation, fish oil, ozone. Altitude thins your lungs; humidity fills them anyway. Light comes from caged tubes and maintenance LEDs, all dimmed by condensation halos. Nothing has a hard outline. Every surface appears doubled: itself, and the water sheen over it.

Sound is the true weather.

Fans throb behind walls. Pumps hammer through the floor. Valves chatter at fixed intervals. Rain from condensation patters on grates, pans, backs, wings. Moth-clouds rasp against mesh. Fish strike the intake screens with soft, incessant ticks. Somewhere a warning klaxon hums at low power—not alarming, just present, folded into the roar like a note in a chord. It is deafening not as a single blast but as saturation: no empty frequency, no silent corner, no pause to reset your hearing. After a minute, you stop trying to separate sources. After ten, the noise feels load-bearing, as if silence would collapse the room.

Despite all this activity, the place is nearly time-invariant.

That is its strangeness.  
Not frozen, not dead—**equilibrated**.

Every drip is answered by a pump draw. Every hatchling count is matched by predation in another tray. Heat bled from motors warms exactly the conduits that keep one insect brood viable. Overflow levels rise to the same mark, stop, fall to the same mark, stop. Even the hanging chains sway to a fixed amplitude in the fan wash. If you marked the waterline, came back in a year, you would find it precisely there. If you recorded one hour of sound, it would overlay next week’s hour almost perfectly, phase for phase.

Rules of this pocket world become obvious after you stand in it long enough:

1. **Nothing here is allowed to become landscape.** Surfaces do not weather; they are replaced before they can.
2. **Water may gather but never escape.** It circulates, condenses, falls, is reclaimed.
3. **Life may multiply but only within assigned volumes.** Too many fish trigger grates; too many moths trigger heat shifts; balance is mechanical.
4. **Change is treated as fault.** Drifting from baseline calls maintenance, not adaptation.

So the place remains: high, cold, wet, packed tight with machine and creature, dim as an underwater tunnel, loud as a foundry drum, and hidden in plain sight inside a mountain facility. A tiny world that behaves like a closed equation—solved once, then held there forever.
