The place is not a cave in the usual sense.  
It is a **country under rock**—broad enough to have weather, districts, migration routes, and local names—yet still unmistakably below the surface.

You enter through a fracture where the upper stone has peeled away in slabs. After that, the ceiling lifts and lifts until it stops feeling like a ceiling at all: a high vault of pale mineral ribs, cracked through with translucent seams that leak daylight down in broad, usable brightness. Not dim bioluminescent gloom—real working light, enough to cast shadows, enough to see faces at distance. The whole underland is lit like a cloudy afternoon.

### Terrain and materials

Nothing here looks old.  
The ground has not been softened into familiar shapes by centuries of water or wind. It is sharp-edged, angular, in the middle of becoming itself. Long fields of fractured shale stand on end like books. Bulging pillows of half-cooled basalt have split, exposing glittering cores. Sulfur-yellow crusts overlap black glass, then abruptly give way to gray clay flats that tremble when something heavy runs over them.

There are channels everywhere, but most are empty most of the time. They wait.

When moisture comes, it comes in pulses: condensation rains, sudden sheetflow from sweating walls, warm fog that gathers into dripping curtains and then vanishes. Pools appear in hollows for a day or an hour, then retreat into cracks. The damp is intermittent, never absent. Surfaces gleam in patches. Air beads on skin. Stone "breathes"—you can watch dry planes darken as humidity rises, then pale again.

Because erosion is just beginning, water does not yet own the land. It only tests it.  
Runnels form, collapse, reroute, and leave behind delicate mineral lace like the first strokes of a map not yet committed.

### Atmosphere and sound

It is warm enough that you stop noticing cold stone as an idea. Warm ground, warm breath from vents, warm mist. Some basins are almost tropical in feel without a leaf in sight; others are kiln-hot around fumaroles and then suddenly temperate ten steps away.

The noise is constant and layered:

- hooflike clicks of chitin on stone,
- wing-rattle in dense swarms,
- wet percussive drips from a thousand overhead points,
- grinding of shifting scree,
- sudden collective hisses when colonies are disturbed,
- and, underneath all of it, low tectonic thumps as new cracks open.

Silence never fully arrives. Even at rest, the underland murmurs and scrapes and chatters like an enormous workshop.

### Ecology without plants

There are no trees, no grasses, no moss banks, no vines. No green at all.

Life is everywhere anyway.

The base of the food web is mineral-fed: slick bacterial films in damp seams, sulfur-eating mats around vents, iron-oxidizing veils that tint seeps red-orange. These are not scenic; they are infrastructure. The animals graze, rasp, sieve, and harvest them constantly.

Fauna dominates every scale:

- fist-sized shell-borers packed along wet walls in near-touching rows,
- herds of six-legged blind runners crossing dry flats in synchronized waves,
- floating drifter-jellies that ride warm updrafts from vents and settle to feed in condensation zones,
- predatory ribbonforms threading through cracks like living whips,
- carrion mounds alive with cleaning species before a body has fully stilled.

Density is the rule. Stand still and things pass over, under, around, and above you in uninterrupted traffic. Paths are not paths because someone built them; they are paths because millions of repeated bodies carved preference into stone dust.

### Built traces (scattered, primitive)

People—or something close enough to call people—have marked the place, but lightly.

Here and there: low dry-stone rings around stable drip points, clay cisterns sealed with waxy resin, bone-and-hide windbreaks at vent mouths, rope ladders slung into vertical fissures, stacks of fired tablets weighted with rocks. There are no cities underground, no grand machine halls. Just practical nodes: water capture, shelter, butchery platforms, watch perches above migration corridors.

Tools are simple and repaired often: chipped stone blades set into antler handles, hammered soft-metal hooks, woven mineral-fiber nets stiffened with salt. Everything is hand-made, hand-carried, hand-fixed.

You get the sense of communities that move with cycles rather than claim territory permanently. They return to the same stations when the moisture pulse and animal tide line up, then leave them to dust and dripping until next turn.

### Rules of this world

A few local truths emerge quickly:

1. **Water is event, not background.**  
   You survive by reading condensation before it falls and by knowing which basins hold water longest.

2. **Brightness does not mean surface.**  
   The light is trustworthy for sight, not for direction. Upward can still mean kilometers of rock.

3. **Nothing stays empty.**  
   Any cleared space is recolonized by animals within minutes to hours.

4. **New ground is dangerous ground.**  
   Fresh fractures look stable and are not. The underland is still assembling itself.

5. **Noise carries life.**  
   Loudness here is information—migration fronts, vent awakenings, predator blooms, rock failure.

---

So the landscape feels less like a ruin and more like an **unfinished organ** inside a planet: warm, wet in pulses, overfull of moving life, structurally young, and busy beyond comfort.  
Not dead underworld. Not lush subterranean forest.  
A mineral savanna beneath stone, mid-creation, already crowded.
