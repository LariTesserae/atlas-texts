At low tide in the air—not the sea, but the air—you can see the shape of the place.

It is a shallow basin, hamlet-sized, ringed by old, softened embankments that were once walls and are now roots wearing the memory of masonry. Nothing here rises very high. The horizon is close, curved by hedges of giant reed and banyan-lattice. You can walk from one edge to the other in an hour if the paths agree to stay put.

They often don’t.

The ground is mostly dry underfoot, but never truly dry. Warmth sits on everything, and moisture arrives in episodes: noon condensation that pearls along leaf-ribs, brief rainbursts that hiss and vanish, fog that forms only in doorways and then drips like a leaking roof. Water gathers in pockets—cistern hollows in old courtyards, heel-shaped puddles in root-paving, narrow runnels that appear after a cloud passes and disappear before you return with a cup.

What looks like a city is, now, almost entirely botanical. Streets are root corridors. Arches are woven cane and strangler fig. “Buildings” stand as living blocks: mangrove-like buttresses fused into walls, canopies trimmed by grazing animals into level roofs, vine curtains hanging where shutters once were. There are still traces of deliberate layout—grids, plazas, drainage lines, neighborhood boundaries—but construction is done by growth, not tools. No metal hum. No lamps. No wires. The infrastructure is biological: capillary channels, fungal exchange mats, pollinator routes, territorial songs.

And despite this abundance, the place feels empty.

Not barren—empty in volume. Huge chambers of air between masses of green. Courtyards the size of ponds with only one tree in the middle. Long covered walks where nothing moves for minutes and then everything moves at once. You hear life everywhere, yet often see only open space, sunstruck and waiting.

The sound is the first law of this basin, and it is deafening.

Cicada clouds pulse from one district to another like weather fronts. Frogs call from hidden cisterns in polyrhythms so dense they become a single metallic roar. Wingbeats in the canopy make a rolling surf noise. Hollow stems act as organ pipes when wind crosses them, and entire streets moan in chords. Fruit bats scream overhead at dusk; ground birds rattle seed pods; monkeys drum buttress roots. Even the rain is loud here, each drop amplified by leaves broad as doors.

People who stay long stop speaking in sentences. They point, touch, sing short bursts. Language gets timed to openings in the noise, like stepping stones in floodwater.

The second law is that shape follows sound. Not metaphorically—physically, somehow. Vines thicken along frequently sung routes. Moss retreats from corridors where predator calls recur. A plaza where thousands of mating insects chorus each evening has become a perfect amphitheater, its walls curved by growth toward resonance. If a species vanishes, its old acoustic niche collapses: branches sag, arches split, paths fill in. The city’s map is rewritten by what is loud enough to belong.

Reality goes soft at the edges of this rule. Echoes arrive early. A call can round a corner before the caller does. Some alleys repeat a footstep three days later at dawn. There is a well in the eastern quarter where sounds drop in dry and come back wet; afterward, condensation forms on your arms in the shape of words you didn’t say. Distances stretch or compress depending on chorus density. Two plazas that should be fifty paces apart may be adjacent during the evening roar and separated by a long, vine-dark corridor at noon silence.

Fauna thrives in this instability. Pollinators in impossible numbers. Nectar birds with iridescent throat sacs that inflate like little bells. Blind fish in seasonal basins that remember routes between disconnected pools. Pale geckos that live inside rolled leaves and emerge only during thunder. Grazers that trim pathways by habit, keeping certain districts open. Predators that hunt by phase-locking to insect rhythms, striking in the tiny fractions of a second when the whole basin inhales.

Everything is weathered, but not ruined. Erosion has done its work and then stopped arguing. Edges are rounded. Channels are established. Collapse points have become gardens. The place feels old in the way a face feels old: not broken, only thoroughly used.

By late afternoon, normal sunlight slants through leaf architecture and turns the basin into rooms of gold-green haze. Steam lifts from warm stone-root intersections. Drips begin under every arch. The noise climbs toward its nightly peak until the air itself seems textured, almost visible.

Then for a few breaths—never predictable—the entire basin goes quiet.

In that impossible quiet, you hear the smallest things: water threading through capillaries in a wall of vine, a seedpod splitting, the click of a beetle turning over. The city holds still, as if listening to what it is becoming next.

Then the chorus returns, and the streets rearrange around it.
