From a distance, the region looks like a mountain range that forgot it was ever stone.

The “peaks” are stacked districts: tiered retaining walls, old blast-furnace ribs, switchback roads, water towers, crane skeletons, and habitation blocks mortared together over centuries until they read as geology. At this elevation the air is warm but thin enough to make breath noticeable, and bright enough that every edge throws a hard shadow. Noon arrives like polished metal. There is no exposed earth anywhere—no bedrock, no raw slope. Even the cliff faces are poured aggregate, riveted plate, heat-glazed brick, and ancient ceramic spill. Yet everything is weathered into soft forms: corners rounded by runoff, stair nosings hollowed by millions of feet, paint chalked into mineral bloom. It has the settled look of a place that has already had all its catastrophes and decided to continue.

Water is never absent, just rarely obvious.  
You hear it before you see it: ticking through pipe racks, dripping from condensers, sliding down mossed seams behind ductwork. Rain comes in brief, decisive showers that darken whole neighborhoods, then vanish, leaving steam and the smell of hot iron, wet leaves, and algae. Most moisture is captured—awning gutters, cable-slung cisterns, rooftop pans, terraced reed beds. Between rains, fog forms in the early hours where warm machine exhaust meets night-chilled towers, and whole levels of the city-mountain bead over with condensation. Vines and epiphytes drink this directly. So do people, in some districts.

Vegetation is not decorative here; it is structural, nutritional, and old.  
Every horizontal surface carries growth. Fig roots pry into expansion joints. Thick fern mats drape over railings. Engine housings support orchids. Bananas grow in old ore chutes lined with composted fiber. Great lattice orchards hang from cable trusses in the lee of taller blocks, and their shade makes green canyons out of former transit corridors. There are managed forests too—columns wrapped in climbing beans, terraces of medicinal shrubs, moss farms in ventilation galleries. Because there is no native ground, soil is manufactured continuously: shredded cellulose, char, fungal substrate, fish sludge, pulverized masonry, kitchen waste. The result is lushness with an industrial metabolism.

Animals are everywhere and unbothered by machinery.  
Parakeets nest in signal housings. Fruit bats sleep beneath conveyor bridges and lift at dusk in dense ribbons. Goats with rubber-soled harnesses graze vertical planters. Semi-feral pigs patrol compost ramps. Long-tailed lizards bask on sun-warm transformer casings and hunt insects swarming around sodium lamps. In wet channels live carp bred for low oxygen and high metals, feeding both people and wetlands. Dogs know freight schedules. Cats know turbine rooms. Insects are a constant layer: bees in façade hives, dragonflies over settling basins, moths pulsing around factory lights. Fauna density is so high that silence is impossible even when machines pause.

And machines rarely pause.  
The soundscape is loud but steady—an inhabited roar rather than a crisis. Fans thrum from cooling towers, belts hum behind brick, pressure valves hiss, rail carts clatter on elevated tracks, and somewhere a stamping press keeps a patient rhythm that has outlived governments. Over this: market voices, caged birds, children in stairwells, metal tools ringing on metal floors. The loudness has strata; residents can tell time and weather from which frequencies dominate. Storm hour has more rattling sheet and gutter rush. Dry noon has cicadas and generator bass.

Despite all this activity, change is slow.  
District boundaries move by meters per decade, not by conquest. A disused foundry becomes a mushroom hall, then a school, then seed storage over thirty years. A cracked flyover is first propped, then planted, then accepted as a hanging garden. Erosion here is chemical, biological, social: rust, root pressure, lichen acids, maintenance habits. Whole neighborhoods sag into graceful arcs and are reinforced in place, not replaced. The landscape feels dynamic only if you stay long enough to notice that one wall is now a wetland, one tower now an aviary, one freight stair now a stream.

The governing rule of the place is simple: **nothing is natural, and nothing is separate from ecology.**  
Every built surface is habitat potential. Every utility line is also migration route, trellis, perch, or watershed. Waste must become feedstock because there is nowhere “outside” to dump it. Height is valuable not for prestige but for climate zoning—cool storage above, humid gardens mid-slope, workshops below, reservoirs in shaded hollows. Bright light is harvested, reflected, filtered through leaves, and broken across mirrored panels to keep plants alive in deep canyons of masonry.

So the landscape reads, finally, as a mature compromise: an extreme vertical machine that has learned to grow green skin, a dense constructed wilderness where industry is not opposed to life but continuously negotiated with it. It is unusual mainly in one quiet way—after enough time there, “artificial” and “alive” stop feeling like opposites at all.
