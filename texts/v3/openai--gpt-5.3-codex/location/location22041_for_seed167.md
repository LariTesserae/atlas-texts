At first it feels like open water, just a warm, heavy dark—then the dark resolves into **districts**.

Not districts of brick and glass, but of bone-lime and shell, stacked and repeated until the seafloor has the logic of a city. Reef-ridges run in parallel like avenues. Pillars of old coral rise in rows. Collapsed sponge domes make courtyards. Giant clam beds form tight mosaics, each shell edge touching the next like roof tiles. Nothing here was cut or engineered with tools, but everything has been built: accreted, inhabited, abandoned, overgrown, reclaimed, layered again. It is urban by patience.

The land beneath it is low and old, a drowned plain that long ago stopped resisting the sea. Erosion has done its work and gone calm. Every hard edge has been filed into an arch, a lip, a step. Limestone mounds are scalloped with centuries of current. Channels are not dramatic canyons, just steady troughs where water remembers where to go. The place has existed in this state long enough to become self-consistent: predators know the bends, larvae know the eddies, silt knows where to settle without being asked.

Light barely arrives. Day and night are almost the same shade of blue-black. Overhead there may be weather, sun, moon, cloudbreaks—but here, illumination is an event, not a constant. A passing school flashes silver and is gone. A jelly pulse opens and closes like a lantern behind smoked glass. Sometimes a broad creature turns and the pale underside of its body briefly paints a wall. Mostly, vision is near-field and slow. Surfaces emerge only when you are already among them.

Vegetation is sparse, and that sparsity is obvious. No waving forests, no meadows. Here and there, strings of tough algae cling to stone seams. A few fanlike plants root in pockets where current slackens. Most vertical space belongs instead to animals: soft corals, anemone curtains, tube-worm thickets, branching colonial bodies that resemble shrubs but feed like mouths. The city is alive because fauna has taken architectural roles usually held by plants.

And fauna is everywhere. Dense, layered, nearly touching. Shrimp comb the cracks. Eels write cursive through collapsed arches. Rays skim avenue floors in slow, deliberate traffic. Crabs patrol thresholds between one colony and another. Shoals bend around columns in coordinated turns, like crowds navigating corners. Higher up, hunters pass without hurry, their routes predictable enough that smaller species time their movement between appearances. Nothing is still for long, but little is frantic. Activity here has rhythm, not panic.

The water is warm in a bodily way, holding heat even in darkness. Some of that warmth wells up from vents and fissures, mineral-rich and faintly metallic to taste. Around these seeps, neighborhoods change: pale bacterial films, blind scavengers, heavy-bodied fish that rest in the thermal plumes. Elsewhere the water is clearer, saltier, and gently stratified—thin layers with slightly different temperatures sliding over one another like transparent cloth.

Sound is mostly quiet, as if padded. No wind, no leaves, no machinery. Just small sounds in close quarters: shell tapping shell, the dry crunch of a bite, distant clicking, the soft sand-hiss of a current turning a corner. Far off, occasionally, a long animal voice drifts through the avenues and makes every smaller body pause for one beat before resuming.

The unusual quality of the place is not spectacle; it is governance. It seems to run on unwritten rules:

- Currents are streets, and crossing them carelessly costs energy or life.  
- Height is status but also risk; the upper columns feed better and get hunted first.  
- Empty structures do not stay empty. If a chamber exists, something will occupy it.  
- Death is immediate construction material. Skeletons become walls; walls become habitat.  
- Darkness does not mean absence—only delayed recognition.

So the landscape keeps building itself. Not upward toward sky, but outward in wet layers: colony on ruin, ruin in silt, silt under shell, shell under new mouths. A warm drowned metropolis, quiet at first hearing, crowded at every scale once you learn how to look.
