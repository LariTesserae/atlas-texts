From a distance, it looks like a dark-green weather system that has settled onto the mountain and never left.

The land sits high enough that the air is thin and sharp, but not barren. Instead, every surface is occupied by plants—if there is stone, it wears moss; if there is moss, it carries liverwort; if there is bark, it is itself clothed in smaller barkless things: pale threads, black cups, silver films that hold cold dew through midday. The ground is almost never exposed. What you step on is root-mat over peat over old root-mat over shattered schist, all of it sprung and wet in pockets, crisp and frozen in others. The terrain rises and folds in long glacial shoulders and troughs, weathered for ages into rounded ridgelines, with occasional broken escarpments where old freeze-thaw has bitten through.

Water is not a governing river here; it is episodic and distributed. Mist condenses on needles and broad fern-fronds, then drops in delayed rain hours after the cloud has passed. Snow comes thin and frequent, melts unevenly, vanishes into sponge-like vegetation, and reappears downslope as black seep-lines. Pools form in root basins, reflect the sky for a day, then drain into hidden channels. In colder shadows, translucent ice sheaths whole plant colonies, preserving their shape like botanical casts.

Though there are no buildings, no roads, no visible tools, there is an industrial feeling to the place—an ecology organized like machinery. Vast stands of reed-stemmed shrubs flex in coordinated waves, driven by pressure changes and katabatic winds that slide off higher snowfields. Dense vine lattices ratchet themselves across gullies as if on conveyor timing: one growth front collapses under frost, another surges from below. Seed pods burst in synchronized pulses. Spores vent from hillside mats in dull, brown plumes. At night, sap rises audibly in frozen trunks and then locks solid before dawn. Nothing is “made,” but everything operates.

That operation is turbulent. Not violent in the sense of explosions—more constant reconfiguration. Slopes creep under the mass of wet vegetation. Entire curtains of root-bound soil peel free and slump ten meters, remaining alive as they settle, immediately recolonized by hanging growth. Windstorms comb one valley flat while the next remains still. Frost heave lifts stones the size of carts and tips them downslope. Then, within weeks, stems rethread through cracks, lichens varnish new faces, and the old equilibrium returns in a new arrangement. It feels less like disturbance and recovery, more like ongoing manufacture.

And yet the sound is minimal. The wind is usually above you, moving in upper canopies you cannot see. At ground level: drip, slight fiber-creak, the soft hiss of fine ice crystals sliding from leaves. No bird chorus, almost no insects, no herd movement. Fauna exists, but sparsely and briefly—white hares passing like misplaced snow, a narrow fox track that appears and disappears at bog margins, a single dark raptor circling once and gone. The silence is not emptiness; it is absorption. Plant mass catches sound and keeps it.

In ordinary daylight—pearl mornings, flat noon, quick blue dusk—the weirdness becomes clear in details rather than spectacle. Some trunks are hollow but still photosynthetic, their inner walls lined with pale green tissue that glows softly when cloud breaks. Certain ground-plants orient not to sun but to temperature gradients, all their leaves angled toward cold air drainage. On exposed ridges, low woody mats carry metallic salts from bedrock; frost on them plates into mirrored scales, making whole hillsides flash like sheet metal at sunrise. You may walk for hours and never leave living ground.

The rules of the place seem simple and strict:

- Bare surface is temporary.
- Water moves by pause and release, not by channel alone.
- Cold is continuous, but never final.
- Collapse is a growth phase.
- Noise is wasted energy.

So the landscape persists as a quiet engine on a high, weathered plateau: open in extent, dense in texture, almost animal-free, always in motion, and wholly, relentlessly vegetal.
