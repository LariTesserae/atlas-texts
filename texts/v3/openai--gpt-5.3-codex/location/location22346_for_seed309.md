The place is called a **town**, but it behaves more like an organ.

It lies in a shallow bowl beneath the surface—an old collapse chamber where the ceiling never fully caved in. Above, a skin of stone and packed soil hangs like a dark lid, split by hairline fissures. Through those cracks, weather leaks in: a little rain, now and then, sifted down as cold threads; dust in dry weeks; occasional shafts of pale day that never reach the floor intact. Most of the time the light is low and greenish, as if everything is seen through leaf-water.

You enter along cut ramps in weathered rock, and the first thing you notice is that **the walls are alive**. Not moss-on-stone alive—stone-replaced-by-plant alive. Trunks flattened into buttresses. Root lattices grown into stairways. Fiber-wood ribs arcing overhead in planned spans like old rail sheds. Every surface is vegetal, but not crowded in the way a jungle is crowded. The chamber itself has room in it. Open pockets. Corridors of air. Plant mass forms the architecture, then leaves voids deliberately, so movement and industry can happen inside it.

The settlement occupies only part of the cavity, about the size of a few city blocks. It is dense where it exists—catwalks, hoists, pumps, cable towers, vent stacks—but beyond those clustered works are stretches of dim undergrove: root-pillars, fern plains, fungal courts, and black channels where moisture gathers. The whole place feels old enough to have stopped expanding. Edges are rounded. Surfaces worn by decades of hands, claws, and runoff. Metal has gone matte. Timber has become something between wood and cartilage. Nothing is pristine, but nothing is collapsing either. It has found equilibrium.

Water is never abundant, always present. It appears as condensation on the undersides of leaves the size of sails, as sweat on pipes, as thin streams running only after seep-rain overhead. In dry intervals, the town drinks fog. Industrial condensers—big finned engines set in pits—pull damp out of the warm air and feed it into cistern roots, where plant tissue filters and stores it. When the chambers cool at shift-change, mist forms and rolls low along the walkways, ankle-high and luminous with spores.

The sound is constant and loud enough to feel in your sternum:  
belt drives rattling, piston pumps coughing, chain conveyors slapping, warning gongs from tram crossings, fans beating humid air through duct-thorns. Over that mechanical layer comes life-noise: clicking swarms, deep throat-calls from tunnel grazers, wing-shears of colony bats, chittering arguments in the rafters, sudden mass movement when something predatory passes. Silence here is not an absence, it is a brief mechanical fault.

Fauna is not incidental; fauna is governance by pressure.

There are too many species to keep still in memory. Blind, warm-bodied crawler packs that follow heat plumes from turbines. Long-legged nectar thieves that walk cable lines in groups, each animal touching the next with antennae. Thick-scaled burrow ungulates bred half-domestic to prune aggressive root growth. Amphibious scavengers that emerge after drip-rain and strip dead matter to clean white fibers by dawn. And above them all, the **vault kites**—pale membrane predators with sonar organs tuned to machinery frequencies. They dive only when engines stall; everyone keeps the engines running.

People (or people-equivalents, if you want the truth of it broader) have built rules around these ecologies:

1. **Keep rhythm.** Machines must pulse on a predictable cycle. Irregular vibration attracts hunters.  
2. **Never seal all leaks.** Dry air makes the green-architecture brittle and enrages spore blooms.  
3. **Leave crossing space.** Every walkway includes animal lanes; every market deck has ceiling clearance for wing traffic.  
4. **Do not overlight.** Bright lamps trigger mating swarms and territorial fights. Illumination stays low, amber, screened through leaf baffles.  
5. **Harvest only the outer growth.** Core roots are structural and communal; cutting them is treated like sabotage.

Industry here is strange by surface standards: less mining, more **processing of life** at scale. Fiber is extruded from root pulp into belts and insulation. Alkaloids are refined in still-towers for medicine and for tranquilizing bulk fauna. Chitin from molted swarm shells is pressed into paneling. Gas from digesters powers turbines. Waste heat is routed into hatch galleries for domesticated insects. It is industrial not because it is metallic, but because it is continuous, synchronized, and hungry.

At shift peaks, the settlement glows in patches—inspection lamps, furnace mouths, bioluminescent culture vats—while most of the cavern remains dim and thick with moving silhouettes. You see distance in layers: near cables beaded with condensation, midground platforms wrapped in vines, far-off root columns disappearing into haze, then occasional glittering eyeshine and the pulse of vent lights. Space feels both limited and deep, like a room that keeps finding new rooms inside itself.

And every few days comes the **ceiling weather**: rain from above-ground storms filtering through cracks in sudden curtains. Sirens sound. Drip channels open. Creatures surge from burrows and ledges. Leaves uncurl, cisterns overflow, tram lines hiss, children run with collection frames, predators patrol the flood edges, and the whole town shifts from grinding industry to hydrological choreography for an hour, maybe two. Then it passes. The chamber steams. Work resumes.

So the landscape is this paradox held stable:  
a warm underworld that is mostly plant, never truly wet, never truly dry;  
a small urban machine nested in living architecture;  
a loud, dim ecosystem where animals outnumber plans;  
a place eroded into permanence, yet always in motion.
