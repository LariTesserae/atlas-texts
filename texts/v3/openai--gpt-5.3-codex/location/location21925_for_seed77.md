From a height, it looks like a coast without a sea.

The land is flat enough to fool the eye, but not truly level: it is laid out in broad, shallow basins and raised ribs, all of it deliberate. Kilometer after kilometer of cast surfaces—pale concrete, fired clay aggregate, dark slag stone, old lime plaster—stitched together in repeating panels. Expansion seams run in long straight lines to every horizon, then bend subtly to follow the planet’s curve. Nothing here is bedrock in the usual sense. Even the hills are embankments. Even the “soil” is ground fill: crushed ceramic, mineral dust, and the powder of failed surfaces.

At this scale, weather belongs to the place itself. Heat gathers over the grids by late morning, and the air rises in visible shimmer columns. By afternoon, clouds form not because mountains force them, but because the built plain does: millions of hot planes lifting moisture from scattered catchments. Rain comes in narrow events—one district soaked, another ten kilometers away untouched and chalk-dry. Water does not linger in rivers so much as in design leftovers: retention basins, settlement ponds, low service corridors, roofless cisterns the size of towns. They gleam for a day, then thin into mineral skins and dark damp margins.

Because elevation is low and broad, air moves lazily. There is no alpine drama, no roaring passes. The wind is mostly a warm slide, carrying dust and the faint alkaline smell of wet concrete after rain. Nights cool just enough for condensation. Before dawn, metal handrails, ceramic faces, and shaded stone sweat beads of water. By sunrise they are gone, drunk by cracks, evaporated, or held in the shallowest depressions where a green tint appears.

Vegetation is sparse, but stubborn in pattern. Not forest, not meadow—just distributed tenacity. Saltbush-like shrubs in seams where filler has accumulated. Reeds in drainage slots that still remember their gradient. Mats of wiry grass on the lee side of walls, where windblown dust has made a finger-deep rooting layer. In shaded culverts and around persistent damp, thin films of algae darken the surfaces and make them briefly slick. Nothing lush. Most of the land remains open, hard, and sun-struck, with long intervals of bare material between living patches.

There are no animals. No birds crossing the thermals, no insects lifting from puddles, no tracks to interpret. The absence changes scale: distances feel larger when nothing moves across them under its own will. Activity comes from processes instead. Heat expands slabs by day and releases them at dusk; whole districts tick and click softly as joints settle. Sudden rain sends sheet flow over kilometers of graded plane, converging into channels that were engineered long ago and now behave like seasonal streams. Fine sediments migrate with each storm, gathering into low fans at culvert mouths, then cracking in geometric plates under the sun. Erosion is only beginning. Edges are still sharp, corners mostly intact, markings legible. The place is aging, but just barely.

Sound is spare. At noon: almost nothing but wind dragging along rough surfaces. After rain: distant trickles, the occasional hollow drip inside a chamber, a soft hiss where warm ground evaporates fresh water. At twilight: thermal pings from metal, one at a time, huge distances apart. Silence here is not empty; it is measured, segmented, infrastructural.

The rules of the world are simple and strict:

- **Everything has been made.** If it looks natural, it is weathering into that appearance.
- **Water is episodic and opportunistic.** It appears in events and pockets, not in permanence.
- **Life follows defects.** Roots, moss, and films occupy cracks, seams, shadows, and slow drains.
- **Change is constant but shallow.** Daily cycles are powerful; long-term breakdown has only just started.
- **Space dominates presence.** Objects, plants, and wet places are scattered across overwhelming extents.

So the landscape reads as a beginning, not an ending: a constructed continent just entering its first true climate, learning rain, learning heat, learning where life can hold. It is vast, hot, mostly quiet, and in motion all the time.
