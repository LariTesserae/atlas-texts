At first glance it looks empty: a high, cold country of stone laid bare to an ordinary white daylight.  
Then your eyes adjust, and you realize the emptiness is crowded.

The land is an upland tableland shattered into long-backed ridges and shallow basins, all of it worn old—so old that even the sharp things have been filed blunt. Cliffs do not break so much as sag; pinnacles lean but never fall. Erosion has had ages to finish its work. Rock here is mostly pale mineral: chalky ashstone, salt-veined basalt, iron-brown crusts polished by vanished winds. No streams cut it. No mud softens it. Every channel is fossil, every gully dry enough to ring when struck.

There is almost no free water. In shaded seams you find a skin of hoarfrost at dawn, gone by midmorning. In deep cracks, bitter crystals grow where water once might have seeped, but now the chemistry of this place is mostly dust, salts, and breath-thin gases. The air is cool and thin enough to make effort feel loud in your own ribs, though the world itself stays quiet. Sound does not carry far; it gets swallowed by distance and porous stone. Most of the time you hear only cloth rubbing, claws on grit, and the faint static hiss of dry skin against dry ground.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. A few wire-root shrubs crouch in fractures, gray-green and thorn-hard, feeding on mineral fog and nighttime frost. Black crust-lichen paints north-facing rock in coin-sized colonies. In the highest flats, there are mats of glassy stalks that look dead until twilight, when they flex open to drink the cold. Nothing here grows tall. Everything clings low, as if height belongs to stone and sky, not to life.

And yet fauna overwhelms everything.

Every ledge, shelf, sink, and overhang is occupied. Herds of long-jointed grazers move in thin files across impossible inclines, licking salt films from rock with abrasive tongues. Colonies of shell-backed crawlers stack themselves in layered drifts like scree that suddenly blinks. Pale membrane gliders hang from cliff lips in dense curtains, opening and closing with tiny synchronized shivers. Burrow-mouths pepper the flats so thickly that you cannot place a foot without choosing which life to disturb. Even the "bare" ground is alive: dust-hiding mites, filament worms, blind skippers hopping from shadow to shadow.

The strange part is how little any of it seems to change. Predation happens, but rarely in motion; an animal is present, then not. Tracks remain sharp for years. Nests are repaired in the same pattern generation after generation, until nest and ruin are the same thing. Many creatures hold still for hours, days, sometimes whole seasons, metabolizing at the edge of pause. The whole ecosystem feels like it has reached a final arrangement and agreed not to leave it.

That is where the world begins to break.

Distance is inconsistent. A herd on a far ridge may be the same herd you passed an hour earlier below you, moving in the same direction, at the same pace, in the same order. Shadows occasionally detach from their bodies and rejoin them at dusk. If you build a cairn to mark your path, you may find it ahead of you before you place it. Locals—when they come this high—set simple markers: stacked stones, bone stakes, braided-fiber flags. Primitive, scattered works. A shelter of hide and shale under one overhang; an obsidian scraper left in a crack; hand-carved notches up a cliff face. The rule they follow is practical: trust texture over sight, and count by touch when the horizon starts repeating.

There are no cities up here, only brief human insistences: a ring of fire-blackened stones, a low wall for windbreak, a shelf shrine with teeth and salt set in spirals. People come to hunt, to gather shells, to trade dried meat and crystal salts, to leave offerings where reality runs thin. They do not stay long. Not because it is hostile—though it is—but because it is too settled. Too complete. The plateau does not welcome change; it absorbs it, grinds it down, and files it into one more ancient layer.

So the landscape remains: high, dry, quiet, crowded, and nearly still under normal daylight.  
A place where life is everywhere, motion is rare, and time seems less like a river than a mineral deposit.
