At first glance, it looks abandoned.

The land is flat enough that the horizon feels like a drawn line, and everything sits low: long levees, broad concrete pads, rusted rail spurs, pumping stations on pilings, neighborhoods of boxy housing raised half a story above flood marks. Water is never far. It lies in drainage canals, in subsidence bowls, in old extraction pits gone black and still; it beads on handrails; it sweats out of retaining walls before noon. After rain, shallow sheets connect everything into one reflective skin, and you can read the whole district by where the puddles linger longest.

There are almost no plants. No grass in the cracks, no trees for shade, no vines reclaiming anything. Surfaces stay mineral: salt-streaked concrete, ceramic brick, corrugated steel, basalt ballast, lime scale. Where life clings, it does so as film and crust—algae in warm runoff, bacterial iridescence in culverts, pale fungal fans under dock plates. The city never turns green; it just oxidizes.

It is hot in a persistent, low-elevation way—heavy air, slow evaporation, heat rising from slab and pipe even after sunset. But the light is dim more often than bright. Clouds hang low off the coast, industrial haze mutes the sun, and the sky tends to stay pewter-white by day, charcoal by evening. Shadows are soft and short. At night, sodium yard lights and mast lamps blur halos into moisture, so distance dissolves into amber fog.

Despite all that emptiness, animals are everywhere.

Not in dense herds crossing roads, but in total count, in relentless presence. The canals are thick with fish and eels feeding on nutrient outflow. Crabs own every riprap edge. Insects hatch in impossible numbers after each warm rain and fold themselves into door seams, vent grilles, and lamp housings. Gulls, cormorants, kites, pigeons, feral ducks, and night herons occupy roofs and gantries in shifts. Rats and feral dogs patrol service corridors. Stray cats sleep on transformer housings. Frogs call from storm sumps. The place has almost no flora, but it has abundance because heat, water, waste, and shelter are enough.

Sound carries oddly here. For an industrial city, it is mostly quiet: no downtown roar, no crowd noise, long gaps between machines cycling on. You hear drips, wingbeats, distant pump thrum, a steel sheet ticking as it cools, a buoy bell from somewhere unseen. When activity comes, it comes in pulses—freight crawler passing, floodgate motors engaging, a flock lifting all at once, rain hammering roofs for ten minutes and then gone.

The terrain is old in its current shape. Erosion has done its work and now mostly maintains: rounded embankments, undercut canal corners armored by riprap, concrete edges worn satin-smooth by decades of wet grit. Nothing feels raw or newly broken. Even damaged structures have settled into function—collapsed warehouse becoming roost, cracked settling basin becoming nursery pond, disused pipe rack becoming migration route for birds.

The rules of this place are practical and legible:
- Water always returns, by rain, tide, seep, or condensation.
- Anything uncoated corrodes.
- Anything shaded and damp grows a film.
- Any warm cavity becomes habitat.
- Open space does not mean lifeless space.

So the landscape is neither ruin nor flourishing wetland, neither fully city nor fully marsh. It is an established hot-water industrial plain, sparse in structure but crowded in biology, quiet at human scale and busy at every other one. It feels ordinary once you stay long enough: not strange, not symbolic—just a place that found equilibrium without leaves.
