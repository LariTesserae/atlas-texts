The city begins as a watershed.

From far away, at the scale where mountain systems are usually read as single shapes, you can see that this high country has been organized by water rather than roads. It is a broad, cold upland—too high for forests, too wet for dust, too old to be dramatic in the young sense. The ridges are rounded, the valleys wide-shouldered, everything weathered into long decisions. Rain arrives often, not as violent storms but as patient, repeating weather: passing veils, steady sheets, drifting cloud that settles onto stone and turns to beads.

Across this plateau, people built with the grain of that climate. Not towers first, but channels. Not walls first, but ledges. Over centuries, those channels and ledges thickened into districts, and those districts into a continuous highland urban fabric: causeways over boggy basins, stepped retaining walls along shallow ravines, stone courts set around shared cisterns, long aqueduct-spines that now function as streets. It is urban in density and extent, but pre-mechanical in method—no engines, no metal lattice, no lit windows of circuitry. Just masonry, timber where it can be spared, packed earth, lime, slate, rope, labor, and time.

At ground level, the place feels close-packed. Surfaces are near to each other: wall to wall, wall to channel, channel to stair, stair to terrace, terrace to another wall. Even in open squares, there are gutters, low parapets, stacked cistern mouths, drying racks, covered wells, spillways, and stone posts polished by hands. Nothing is empty, yet little is lush. Vegetation is sparse and practical: sedges in wet seams, moss in mortar lines, tough grasses on roof edges, occasional willow thickets where groundwater rises, kitchen plots in sheltered pockets. Beyond the built bands, heather and lichen spread thinly across rock.

The materials are almost all local and almost all damp. Dark basalt in lower districts, pale granite up-ridge, shale flags where paths need grip. In constant moisture these stones hold different sheens: matte where rough-cut, glossy where footsteps and water share a line. Wood is used carefully—doors, beams, footbridges—then patched and repatched. Rope grows green with algae. Clay plasters breathe and flake and get renewed each season. The whole city looks less “constructed” than sedimented: a human layer laid into geological time.

Despite the wetness, this is cool country, and the air keeps a high-altitude edge. Breath is visible at dawn most days. In winter the channels skin over in the early morning and are running again by noon. In summer, sunlight is clear and honest, never tropical; shadows stay blue. Light is ordinary, not mystical, but the frequent cloud makes it mobile—districts brighten and dim in sequence as weather crosses the plateau. You can watch rain move toward you by the darkening of roofs miles away.

Sound is surprisingly restrained. Water moves everywhere, but mostly in soft registers: trickle, thread, drip, hush over stone lips. Wind arrives as pressure before it arrives as noise. Footsteps are dulled by damp surfaces. Voices travel poorly in mist and are kept low by habit. This is an active landscape that rarely becomes loud. Its busyness is hydraulic, not mechanical.

Ecology follows those limits. Large fauna are uncommon; the upland does not offer much shelter or biomass. You might see small herds of hardy goats on outer slopes, marsh birds in seasonal pools, and fish only in the deepest cold channels where flow stays constant. Frogs and insects come and go with rain cycles, but even they are not overwhelming. Predation exists, but at low densities; nothing here swarms for long. Most life clings, persists, and returns.

Because the system is old and tuned, the “rules” of the place are visible in its form:

- **Never block a flow line.** Every path is also overflow infrastructure in heavy rain.  
- **Build heavy, build low, build repairable.** High winds and wet freeze-thaw punish light ambition.  
- **Share elevation.** Upper districts depend on lower storage; lower districts depend on upper catchment maintenance.  
- **Leave breathing gaps.** Narrow courts and vents prevent fog-stagnation and wall rot.  
- **Expect movement.** Slopes creep, joints open, channels silt. Nothing is final; maintenance is the true architecture.

So the landscape is neither wild marsh nor tidy mountain city, but a merger of the two at continental scale: a vast high wet urban watershed, quiet in the ear, dense in the hand, active in every hidden gradient. It feels established because it is—an equilibrium not of stillness, but of repeated adjustment. Water falls, gathers, passes; stone receives, shifts, is reset; people continue the pattern.
