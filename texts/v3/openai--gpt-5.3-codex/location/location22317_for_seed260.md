At first it looks like a mountain country seen in bad light: high shelves, long ridges, cold air, a horizon that keeps unfolding. Then you notice what is missing.

There are no rivers. No snowmelt threads. No marsh gleam in the low places. Even the idea of damp feels foreign here. The ground is dry all the way down—powdered salts, fresh black glass, pale mineral plates that ring when struck. The valleys are not carved; they are opened, as if pulled apart recently. Edges stay sharp. Scree doesn’t soften into soil. Rock here has not had time, or permission, to become old.

The elevation is severe. Whole districts sit on uplifted tables above deeper, darker troughs. Some cliffs run for kilometers in straight faces, too clean to be natural by ordinary standards, but not built either—more like geology in draft form. In places, slopes stop halfway and continue several meters away, offset, the land failing to agree with itself. You can see three versions of a ridgeline at once depending on where you stand.

Light is dim but not absent: a cold, diluted glow, as if the sun were distant and filtered through metal dust. Colors flatten in it. Greens turn bluish. Stone loses warmth. At dusk—if this place has true dusk—the sky shows thin luminous fractures like cracked glaze, and those fractures cast shadows in directions that don’t match the ground.

And still, for all this dryness, there is vegetation—lush in bursts, sparse in distribution. Not forests everywhere, but broad colonies where conditions align. Giant frond-mats cling to slopes, each blade stiff as hammered copper and veined with translucent salts. Pillar-groves rise from mineral flats: segmented trunks, matte jade to ash-green, packed with fibrous tissue that drinks vapor, light, and static charge instead of water. Carpet forms spread over basalt benches like moss from a distance, but up close they are braided crystal threads dusted with chlorophyll-dark films. Growth is exuberant where it occurs, then abruptly absent for long stretches, so the land alternates between abundance and bare silence.

No animals move among it. No wings, no burrows, no scavengers, no insects in the fronds. Reproduction happens by wind-thrown spores, electrostatic puffs, and occasional cracking events where mature stalks split and root themselves downslope. Pollination is weather. Decay is mostly mineralization.

The weather is the loud thing.

The place is deafening almost all the time, though little appears to be happening. Wind crosses the elevation in immense laminar sheets and drives through hollow plant-columns, cliff tubes, and fault chimneys. Everything resonates. The mountains behave like organ pipes. Notes hold for hours. Subharmonics travel through your bones before your ears register them. Sometimes the sound resolves into chords so stable they feel architectural, like hearing the outline of invisible walls. Conversation is impossible in the open. Even alone, you feel shouted at by the air.

Human marks are scattered and small compared to the land: stacked-stone windbreaks, low dry shelters tucked against lee faces, ladders pegged into soft tuff, ropeways made from split plant fiber, kiln circles lined with reflective shale. Primitive craft, competent and patient. Knapped glass blades, hammered native copper, woven mats lacquered with resin from the pillar-groves. No large settlements. Just stations, watch huts, seed caches, shrines marked by suspended chimes that are never still in the roar.

The people who cross this region (if “cross” is the right word) build lightly because permanence is unreliable. Reality here drifts at geologic speed. Not fast enough to watch, but fast enough that maps inherit errors within a generation. Distances lengthen by a few steps each year. Ravines duplicate. A path can arrive at itself from two directions. Shadows sometimes remain after the object has moved, then fade minutes later. Cause and echo are slightly misaligned.

So the rules are practical:

- Build where the stone hum is steady; unstable harmonics mean unstable ground.
- Trust fixed stars over ridgelines.
- Mark routes in sound, not sight—hanging resonators tuned to known pitches.
- Never sleep in a valley that has no morning wind; stillness means the basin is about to shift.

Despite all of this, the landscape does not feel violent. It feels unfinished. Slow, cold, and enormous, like a world still curing in its mold—already beautiful, already habitable in pockets, but not yet fully committed to a final shape.
