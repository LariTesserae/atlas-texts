At first it feels like a floodplain after rain, only stretched wider than weather should allow.  
The land is low and newly made—broad shelves of silt and black clay that still remember being slurry. Fresh cuts run through it where water has only recently decided to flow: narrow channels with crisp edges, little collapse, no old rounding. Everywhere, the ground is close to saturation. Step on a rise and it trembles; step in a hollow and water lifts around your boots without ever becoming a proper pool.

It is warm in the way enclosed places are warm, though the sky is open. Moisture hangs in layers a meter or two apart, so moving through the plain feels like crossing invisible curtains. Rock outcrops—young, iron-rich, still jagged—weep steadily from seams, making soft vertical veils. There are not many plants, but what exists is tenacious: reed-clumps with blade-thick leaves, mats of pale moss on concrete, thorny shrubs rooted in rusted grates, isolated trees with broad crowns and almost no undergrowth beneath them. Sparse vegetation does not mean empty; it means each patch is sharply defined, an island with its own small weather.

The darkness is total in principle, not just night. Light exists, but only as events. A valve wheel turns in the distance and throws three seconds of blue arc. Gas stacks breathe and briefly ember-red their own vapor. Insects blink in synchronized waves over the marsh channels, drawing temporary maps across the black. The eye learns to navigate by wet reflections, by heat, by motion, by soundless contrasts: matte mud against slick steel, still water against slow current, dead machinery against live machinery.

Scattered industrial structures stand across the plain as if dropped there between tides: pump houses on pilings, short conveyor runs that stop in midair, crane skeletons, round settling tanks, fenced substations with no visible supply lines. None of it forms a single plant, yet all of it works. Pistons rise and fall very slowly. Belts creep. Pressure gauges twitch once every few minutes. Thick hoses run from nowhere into the marsh and return full. The sense is not of active production but of long-duration processing—decades-long cycles measured in corrosion depth and sediment chemistry.

Despite the quiet, the place is crowded with life. Fish-like bodies move through ankle-deep channels that should be too shallow to hide anything. Amphibians the size of dogs rest in warm runoff, skin banded like caution tape. Flocks of low-flying birds pass in near-silence, wings adapted for damp air, their feathers collecting phosphorescent spores. There are arthropods everywhere: on girder legs, beneath floating mats, inside valve housings. Some species nest in machine warmth and feed on mineral films, others on each other. Predation is constant but subdued, more like harvesting than pursuit.

The strangest part takes longer to notice because it happens slowly. Distances do not stay fixed. A tower seen on the horizon at dusk may be beside you by dawn, not because it moved, but because the intervening marsh folded into itself like fabric settling. Water sometimes flows uphill for an hour, then resumes normal drainage. Echoes arrive before their source. Footprints fill not with water but with a copy of the sky—darker than surrounding darkness, as if each print opens briefly into deeper night. None of this is violent. Reality here does not shatter; it softens and creeps, like wet paper losing stiffness.

There are rules, and locals—human or otherwise—seem to follow them:
- Never trust a straight line after midnight; pipelines and channels may re-route through folded space.
- Keep one continuous sound when walking (a chain dragged, a hum, spoken numbers). Silence invites positional drift.
- Avoid newly dry ground; it is usually “upside water,” a thin skin over suspended depth.
- If lights appear in a perfect grid, do not approach. Grids indicate active recalibration zones where scale changes.

What makes the landscape feel alive is its patience. Nothing rushes. Flood marks climb millimeter by millimeter. Rust blooms, then hardens into protective crust. Fauna migrate in loops timed to pressure cycles, not sunrise. Structures sink by degrees and are jacked back up on long intervals by pumps no one sees maintained. Even the weirdness has inertia: folds open, remain, and close over seasons.

So the place resolves into this: a warm, water-heavy industrial marsh in engineered darkness, young in its geology, old in its operations, biologically saturated, and governed by slow distortions rather than sudden disasters. It is less a ruin than a process zone where land, machine, and ecology are still negotiating what counts as stable ground.
