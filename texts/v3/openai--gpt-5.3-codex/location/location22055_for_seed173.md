The place is called the **Lower Intake**, though no one remembers what it intakes anymore.

It sits below the city’s old floodline, a single bounded complex of chambers and tunnels that were engineered to move water, store it, filter it, and force it back out. Now the whole thing is almost entirely submerged: not a ruin in open sea, but a sealed industrial volume filled with moving cold water. No bedrock is visible. No sand flats. Every surface you could touch was poured, welded, cast, riveted, or bolted by someone long dead.

At first glance it feels like one room the size of a chapel nave, then your eyes adjust and it keeps unfolding: side galleries, intake throats, maintenance alcoves, a collapsed catwalk hanging at an angle, gridded floors disappearing into green-black distance. The scale stays intimate compared to an ocean trench—you can always find a wall if you swim long enough—but it still has that interior vastness built spaces get when they were made for machines larger than people.

### Materials and age

The complex is ancient in the way infrastructure gets ancient: not romantic collapse, but long use worn into a final stable shape.  
Concrete has softened at corners and gone silky under centuries of flow. Steel beams are furred with rust and calcareous growth, narrowed to knife-thin flanges in places, thickened in others where mineral crust rebuilt them. Paint survives only in ghosts: hazard yellow turned to dull mustard, warning stripes barely readable under lime and algae film.

Erosion has done its patient work. Sharp edges are rare. Bolts have become rounded knobs. Steps have become ramps. Drain channels widened themselves into smooth troughs. Nothing here is fresh enough to cut you quickly; everything is old enough to abrade you eventually.

### Water, light, and air

Water is the atmosphere. Cool, heavy, always in motion.

There are no broad currents like open coast, but the machinery geometry creates constant local flow: fast jets through sluice slots, gentle gyres in dead-end bays, upward pull near intake mouths, pulsing reversals with each tidal phase. Every six hours the whole complex seems to inhale and exhale.

Light arrives dim and green from two sources: distant surface shafts where access hatches failed, and a sparse chain of maintenance lamps still powered by a stubborn municipal grid. The lamps are industrial sodium converted to modern LED housings over decades, and now they glow tea-colored through fouled glass. They don’t illuminate so much as mark islands of certainty in a mostly murky volume.

In some pockets, trapped air still exists above inverted beams and vaults, but it is stale and metallic, and the ceiling drips constantly. Most life ignores these pockets.

### Sound

Even underwater, it is loud.

You hear the place through your bones before your ears:  
- the low rotary thrum of pumps still operating somewhere behind locked housings,  
- periodic gate impacts, deep and blunt,  
- cable vibration like giant instrument strings,  
- pressure surges banging loose panels,  
- surface storm-noise transmitted down shafts as a distant rolling roar.

On top of the mechanical bass sits the living crackle of the ecosystem: shrimp snaps, mussel beds filtering, fish schools turning as one body with soft percussion against mesh and rail.

Silence exists only in brief lulls between cycles, and even then the structure hums.

### Ecology

Vegetation is sparse. This is not a kelp forest or weed-choked canal. Light is too limited, substrate too vertical, flow too changeable. What grows here grows in patches: short ropes of eelgrass near skylit breaks, dark mats of filament algae along lamp halos, mossy bacterial films in warm outflow seams.

Fauna, though, is abundant.

The built geometry creates habitat gradients every few meters, and animals fill them:
- Mussels and tube worms line calmer pillars.
- Crabs patrol bolt-lines and cable trays.
- Eels and conger hide in conduit mouths.
- Schools of silver fish hold in the lee of buttresses, pivoting with each pulse.
- Predatory fish cruise the transit corridors timed to current changes.
- Octopus dens occupy old valve casings, each ringed with shell debris.

Because the space is bounded and patterned, behavior here is rhythmic. Feeding follows flow schedules. Spawning uses light cues from the lamps. Predators stake out repeatable choke points where corridors narrow between structural ribs.

### Rules of the place

The Lower Intake has practical rules, learned quickly:

1. **Flow beats force.** Nothing muscular can outswim a gate surge; you move diagonally, use handholds, wait for reversals.
2. **Edges are maps.** Center volumes are empty and disorienting; walls, rails, and conduit runs are how animals and divers navigate.
3. **Warmth means machinery.** Slightly warmer plumes mark active systems and denser life.
4. **Light means competition.** Any lit patch is immediately occupied—by algae, by grazers, by hunters waiting for grazers.
5. **Rust is structure now.** Corrosion isn’t only decay; it has become reef, shelter, and food substrate.

Nothing supernatural governs it. It is a normal ecosystem, just one written in concrete and steel instead of stone and reef.

And that is what defines the landscape most: it is fully made, fully flooded, deeply old, and still working—less a drowned ruin than an industrial organ that never stopped moving water long enough for nature to ignore it.
