At first it seems like a mountain city after rain.  
Then you realize it is always after rain.

The land is a high, cold shelf lifted so far above sea level that clouds move through it like slow freight, not sky. Peaks are new here—sharp, unweathered, dark as if they were poured yesterday. Their ridgelines are serrated and clean, with very little of the softened roundness old ranges acquire. Between them, broad basins hold shallow water that never quite drains: tarns, black marsh flats, mirror-channels laid in geometric cuts by engineers who learned to work with saturation instead of against it.

Everything is wet. Not flooded—wet in the way metal hulls are wet from breath. Rock sweats. Railings bead. Glass wears a film. Water stands just beneath every surface, waiting for pressure changes to call it up. Walkways are built on pilings, grates, and suspended ribs because solid ground behaves like a soaked sponge. In colder pockets, condensation freezes to translucent fur on beams and cable trays; in warmer exhaust corridors, it drips steadily into collection gutters.

Vegetation is unexpectedly abundant for the altitude and cold. It comes in masses rather than individuals: thick mats of silver-green moss, ropey reed-forests in standing water, low broadleaf growth with wax-black surfaces that shrug off ice. Whole cliff faces are upholstered in vascular lichen that climbs over masonry as readily as basalt. There are no flowers in the familiar sense, no bright invitations—just layered, efficient green and charcoal biomass drinking fog, runoff, and mineral-rich seepage. In sheltered cuts, tree-forms exist: pale trunks, branch fans flattened by prevailing vapor flow, leaves like overlapping slate tiles.

No animals move through any of it. No birds on the girders, no insects over the marsh skin, no fish rings in the retention lakes. The vegetation grows, decays, and reconstitutes through microbial and fungal engines alone. Pollination, where needed, is done by wind ducts and periodic electrostatic pulses from the city grid. Seed dispersal happens by steam vent, conveyor, and rain-channel. Ecology here is vast, lush, and almost silent biologically—except the place is not silent at all.

It is loud enough to become weather.

The city is spread thin across an enormous plateau system, less a dense metropolis than an archipelago of industrial districts separated by kilometers of wet green and raw stone. Towers stand in disciplined clusters: condenser spires, pump houses, electrolysis halls, cable bridges, vertical foundries with open sides to vent heat into cloud. Between clusters run elevated rail lines and pipe avenues, each carrying water, slurry, current, and pressurized vapor. The spaces between structures are huge—empty enough that distance itself feels engineered.

From every district comes a constant register of sound: turbine hum, piston thud, steam bleed, chain drag, harmonic whining from high-voltage lines under persistent moisture load. Waterfall noise from overflow chutes merges with fan arrays and the drumbeat of condensate hammers. The remarkable part is not just the volume but its invariance. The same note at dawn, at noon, at whatever passes for night under this pale light. No shift changes in the acoustic profile, no seasonal hush, no migratory crescendos. It is a fixed chord, planet-wide.

That fixedness is one of the world’s rules.

Another is this: gradients are shallow and self-correcting. Heat dumped by industry is immediately captured by fog fields and spread; cold drains into reservoirs and returns through exchanger towers; water lifted to high cisterns comes down in measured curtains that recharge marshes feeding intake galleries that lift it again. Storms exist only as texture, not events. Rain does not begin or end; it thickens and thins around a stable mean. River levels stay within hand-width tolerances decade after decade. Landslides are rare despite the youth of the terrain because retaining roots and meshworks are everywhere, woven into slopes as soon as they emerge.

The light is ordinary in spectrum and intensity, yet strange in behavior. Cloud and vapor flatten shadows. Surfaces gleam without glitter. Distance washes into layered gray-blue planes where mountains, chimneys, and reedbeds overlap until scale becomes hard to judge. A near railing drips in perfect detail while a ten-kilometer refinery appears as a soft graphite block suspended in mist.

Materials tell the same story as the climate: basalt, fresh and dark; concrete blended with volcanic ash; enameled steel gone matte under mineral films; copper streaked green-black by perpetual wet; glass thick enough to withstand thermal cycling; bioceramic tiles colonized deliberately by moisture-loving plants. Nothing here seeks dryness. Infrastructure embraces seepage, capillary rise, and condensation as primary utilities.

If you stand on one of the high transit viaducts and listen past the immediate machinery, you can hear the continental mechanism under it all: millions of synchronized valves, pumps, and vents keeping a wet cold equilibrium alive over an immense young roof of stone. Lush growth fills the gaps. Empty distances hold the pattern intact. The city does not sprawl outward so much as repeat at intervals, each node tuned to the same steady-state equation.

A strange place, but not chaotic.  
Alien, but methodical.  
A high industrial marsh world where nothing hunts, nothing flees, and almost nothing changes—except mist shape, droplet path, and the patient thickening of green over steel.
