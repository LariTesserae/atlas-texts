At first it looks empty: a high, cold tableland running beyond the horizon, gray-green and silver under an ordinary sky. Then your eyes adjust, and the place resolves into layers.

The ground is young. Not “newly paved” young—tectonic young. Great sheets of dark stone have been lifted and laid here within living memory by crustal engineering, and they have not yet learned softness. Ridges are blunt, not carved; gullies are shallow scratches where water has only recently begun to choose its routes. The rock still holds manufacturing textures in places—hexagonal cooling fields, faint seam-lines where mineral foams were extruded and fused. Erosion is just beginning to write on it.

Water sits close to the surface almost everywhere. Not deep lakes, mostly: a continent of seepage, tarns, braided rills, and marsh-edge flats. After any weather shift, the whole plateau weeps. Condensation pearls on retaining walls, on the undersides of transit bridges, on bare stone ledges. In low pockets, peat is just forming; in higher bands, shallow pools skin over at dusk and thaw by noon. The wetness keeps the cold intimate. Even when the air is clear, it feels like breathing through thin wool.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. You get wide reaches of bare mineral ground, then sudden strips of life where water lingers: sedge combs, rust-red moss, low knotty shrubs with leaves like metal filings, lichen maps painted across concrete and basalt alike. No forests, no lush cover—just scattered growth tracking microclimates with mathematical precision. The city planners long ago stopped fighting that pattern and began designing around it.

Because this is urban, but in the scale of weather. Settlements are distributed in long arcs along thermal faults and transport spines, each district separated by kilometers of open stone and wetland reserve. From above, the built zones look like necklaces laid across a giant’s collarbone: compact, bright nodes connected by elevated maglev ribbons, service causeways, and buried heat conduits. Buildings are tall where needed, but more often low and dense, wind-shaped, with skins that harvest fog and dump excess heat into ground batteries. The technology is far beyond modern engineering, yet nothing feels magical here—just precise. Roads repair themselves overnight. Drainage channels regrade in response to storm forecasts. Transit pylons tune their vibration signatures so migrating animals aren’t driven off-course.

And animals are everywhere. Not in crowds that fill the frame, but in continual presence. Herds of long-legged grazers moving between thaw zones. Marsh birds in impossible numbers during season change, their calls thin and carrying in cold air. Thick-bodied amphibians in geothermal ponds. Burrowing mammals using utility embankments as winter shelter. Predator tracks cross service corridors each morning, and the city’s traffic AI simply routes around them. This is an inhabited highland in both senses: human infrastructure and nonhuman abundance, interleaved.

The soundscape stays mostly quiet despite all that motion. Wind, always. Drip from condenser fins. Distant wingbeats like cloth snapped once and gone. Occasionally a low infrastructural hum from deep power trunks beneath the stone, felt more than heard. During storms, rain rattles membrane roofs and then passes, leaving a silence so broad you can pick out individual rivulets threading rock.

Light is ordinary and therefore beautiful: pale mornings, hard noon reflections off wet black stone, long blue evenings. Nothing uncanny, nothing surreal. Just a vast, active, still-forming plateau-city where climate, geology, and engineering are in ongoing negotiation. A place not finished, not ruined—mid-sentence.
