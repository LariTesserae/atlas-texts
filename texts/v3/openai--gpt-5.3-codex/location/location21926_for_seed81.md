The city sits where a mountain should have been, as if the peak was carved out from within and replaced with scaffolding, reservoirs, chimneys, and terraces of black stone. At this altitude the air is thin but never dry. Heat rises constantly from fissures under the foundations, and warm vapor gathers under a low iron-colored sky. Nothing grows in the cracks. No grass, no moss, no stubborn alpine bloom. Just wet mineral faces—basalt, slag brick, oxidized steel—sheened with condensation that beads, runs, and vanishes into channels cut centuries ago.

From a distance, it looks crowded: towers, gantries, elevated conduits, long ribbed roofs, cranes frozen mid-gesture. Up close, it feels empty. The structures are immense and spaced with deliberate severity. Streets are broad enough for vanished convoys. Courtyards could hold whole markets and now hold only rainwater and echoes. Bridges span gaps no one crosses at your hour of arrival. The architecture assumes population; the atmosphere confirms absence. Even the lights are sparse—amber maintenance lamps, weak arc strips under eaves, signal buoys blinking in mist. Illumination does not banish shadow here; it simply punctures it.

Water is everywhere, but almost never in the form of rivers. It sweats out of retaining walls. It condenses on cables and drips in perfect rhythms from suspended pipes. It collects in shallow mirror-basins across plazas paved with dark volcanic glass. During the frequent bursts of rain, drainage gates open and the whole district becomes a temporary marsh of engineered intent: sluices hiss, overflow stairs become cascades, and runnels braided into every street feed cistern stacks sunk deep into the mountain’s hollow core. When the rain ends, steam rises off the ground in pale folds, and visibility shortens to a few blocks.

The heat is not sunlight-driven—there is sunlight, somewhere above the cloud canopy, but down in the city it arrives filtered and fatigued, as though borrowed secondhand. The warmth comes from below and within. Boiler houses still pulse. Thermal exchangers breathe. Old foundry floors glow faintly behind slatted walls. Even abandoned districts stay warm enough to shimmer. You can stand in drizzle while your boots rest on stone hot enough to dry them from the sole upward. The weather and the infrastructure argue constantly, and neither side wins.

Erosion has had time to negotiate with construction. Corners are rounded. Stair edges slump into smooth saddles. Acidic condensation has etched maps into metal cladding. Stalactites of mineral salts hang from viaduct undersides, each drop laying down another translucent ring. Concrete has exfoliated into layered fans that resemble sedimentary cliffs. The place is not decaying so much as settling into a mature shape that includes weather, chemistry, and neglect as permanent planners. It feels old in a stable way: not ruin, not renewal, but equilibrium with abrasion.

Life, in recognizable forms, is scarce. You may see a handful of pale, kite-winged scavengers circling thermal updrafts, never landing where you can watch. In flooded service tunnels there are transparent things the size of fingernails that pulse once and disappear when disturbed. Most “ecology” here is film-thin and mineral-fed: iridescent bacterial veils in warm runoff, sulfur-loving mats in vent chambers, black lace colonies crystallizing along condenser fins. They are not green, not soft, not inviting. They spread by geometry and heat gradients, as if solving equations on the surfaces of machines.

The strangeness of the city reveals itself through its rules. Doors are numbered by pressure zone instead of street. Public clocks track condensation cycles and reservoir head, not hours. Certain avenues are marked with high ceramic pylons; when fog thickens and static builds, those corridors hum and become no-go channels where discharged electricity crawls low along the wet stone like sheet lightning in slow motion. In some districts, voices carry too far—as if the vapor itself is a duct—while footsteps vanish entirely on porous paving designed to swallow vibration. You learn quickly that orientation by sound fails here.

And yet the place is active. Not bustling—active. Pumps engage and idle in coordinated waves. Floodgates test themselves at dawn-equivalent. Heat plumes shift with subterranean load changes. Freight lifts descend, empty, and return with sealed tanks beaded in cold sweat. Somewhere far inside, turbines start for seven minutes every cycle, then stop. The city behaves like an organism in low metabolism, still alive, still maintaining pressure, temperature, circulation. It does not need crowds to prove function.

In its quiet hours, what you hear most is water deciding where to go next: ticking from cables, whispering through grated throats, drumming softly in hollow steel. Beneath that, a bass note of machinery too large to identify. Occasionally a long metallic call rings out from one of the high suspension trusses, not intentional, just thermal expansion flexing old joints. Then silence returns—thick, damp, and warm—while dim light spreads across terraces and disappears into steam.

If you stay long enough, the emptiness stops feeling like abandonment and starts feeling like spacing: this world is built with distances in mind—between towers, between sounds, between living things, between one visible certainty and the next. Here, water writes on stone, heat edits the weather, and the city keeps itself company.
