From orbit it looks like a pale, hammered-metal planet with one impossible feature: a **high ocean**.

Not sea level—high. The water sits across uplifted continental crowns and drowned mountain tables, a cold inland world-ocean spread miles above the old lowlands. The horizons are huge enough to curve under their own weather. You can stand on a basalt tooth that was once a summit and look out over thousands of kilometers of near-freezing gray-blue water, interrupted only by other teeth, black and newly torn, as if the crust cracked yesterday and never had time to soften.

### Terrain and material

Everything is sharp.  
The erosion is young enough that cliffs still look quarried by tectonics rather than rounded by age: knife-ridges, fresh fault faces, glassy scree slopes descending into water clear enough to show drowning walls far below.

The ground—where there is ground—is mostly igneous: dark basalt, iron-rich gabbro, plates of volcanic glass, and seams of pale salts precipitated by spray and freeze. Sediment hasn’t had time to build soil. There is almost no mud, no loam, no green dust. Just rock, salt, rust, and ice.

At this altitude the air is thin and cold, but the light is ordinary daylight: hard noon glare, white cloud banks, long amber evenings. Nothing mystical about the illumination. The strangeness comes from what it falls on.

### Water and sky

The water is nearly everywhere. Not tranquil, not stormy—**constant**.  
A permanent wind belt drives a fixed set of swells around the basin-world. Same direction, same period, same roar, day after day, year after year. The climate found an equilibrium and refuses to leave it.

Rain walls move in broad lanes, but even they are stable lanes, as if painted onto the atmosphere. In some districts you pass from clear air into a vertical curtain of rain that never migrates. Mariners and aviators chart these as if they are geography.

The sea itself is stratified and cold. Near-surface brine hovers just above freezing; deeper layers are denser, darker, mineral-heavy. In places, geothermal vents rise through fracture fields, and the water there clouds with metallic plumes.

### Sound

The defining fact of the place is that it is **deafening**.

Not because it is chaotic, but because the same huge sounds never stop:

- the endless surf detonating against fresh basalt walls,
- pressure-horn calls from industrial towers,
- turbine farms whining through salt wind,
- millions of animals drumming, clicking, and booming through water and air.

There are no true silences. Even “quiet” means your bones still carry vibration. People here sleep to regular thunder, not peace.

### Life without greenery

There are no forests, no kelp beds, no grasses, no moss. You can travel for weeks and never see a leaf-shaped thing.

Yet life is abundant.

The base of the food web is non-plant: mineral-bacterial films, sulfur and iron chemotroph colonies in fracture zones, floating mats of simple translucent organisms that are not quite algae and not quite jelly. They bloom in cold upwellings, then shred and sink, feeding everything larger.

Fauna fills every layer:

- shoals of pale, armor-scaled swimmers that flash like hammered foil,
- long filter-ribbons that hang beneath wave troughs like living nets,
- cliff-nesting cartilage flyers that skim spray lines and scream into the wind,
- heavy-shelled crawlers clustered around warm vent outcrops,
- leviathan grazers that harvest microbial veils from submerged stone.

They are numerous, migratory, loud, and adapted to mineral diets and low temperatures. Color is sparse—mostly whites, grays, iron reds, and the occasional bioceramic blue.

### Built traces

Civilization is present, but scattered thin across immense emptiness.

You find solitary pump stations on sea stacks; chain-linked buoy factories that refine salts and metals from brine; intake towers anchored to submerged ridges; rail-straight catwalks ending in crane heads above black water. Some are active, some abandoned but still humming from autonomous maintenance cycles that no one shut down.

Industrial technology here is practical and oversized:

- piston desalters,
- pressure forges,
- current turbines in fixed straits,
- fog condensers high on exposed rock,
- sonar beacons as common as lighthouses.

Settlements are small and far apart, usually bolted to stone like barnacles. Between them: vast nothing. Open water, open sky, open cold.

### Rules of this world

A few local truths emerge quickly:

1. **Constancy is more dangerous than storms.**  
   Because conditions barely change, systems scale up: wave trains, predator routes, machine cycles. If you drift into one, it will still be there tomorrow.

2. **Anything loose is eventually taken by resonance.**  
   The unending noise has structure. Cables, hull plates, even bone can fatigue to the world’s persistent frequencies.

3. **Biology follows minerals, not sunlight.**  
   Productivity maps to fractures, vent fields, and iron upwellings. “Fertile” means chemically rich, not green.

4. **Distance is the main weather.**  
   With so little per volume—so few structures in so much space—travel is exposure. Most of your journey is empty brightness, cold spray, and unbroken roaring.

---

So the landscape resolves into a paradox: a nearly all-water world that feels austere; a cold place full of life; an industrial frontier where human marks are tiny; an environment so loud and so stable it seems almost mechanical.  
It is less a changing wilderness than a permanent engine—ocean, stone, fauna, and machines locked in one enormous, steady-state thunder.
