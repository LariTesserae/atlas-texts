At first glance it looks empty: a high country of old stone, opened wide to the horizon, where distance is the main feature.

Then your eyes adjust, and the emptiness turns out to be mostly *air*.

Everything living has climbed.

---

This is an upland of immense, weather-worn tables and ridgelines, the kind of elevation where the land has stopped trying to rise and simply endures. The rock is ancient enough to have forgotten sharpness. Cliffs are not jagged but rounded and fluted, carved by vanished rivers and patient wind into long ribs, bowls, and vertical channels. Whole mountains have been hollowed into amphitheaters. Valleys are broad, dry most of the year, with only dark seams where moisture lingers.

Water is not absent; it is intermittent and opportunistic. Rain comes in brief, warm bursts, hard enough to hiss on stone and vanish. More reliable than rain is condensation: night fog slides up from lower basins, catches on leaves and lichen, beads, drips, and feeds the world in tiny increments. Every surface that can hold a film of water does. Every depression in rock hosts a temporary pool. Nothing here depends on steady flow; everything is adapted to pulses.

The plants have answered this by becoming infrastructure.

Ground, where it exists, is mostly rootfelt and peat-moss over stone. Trees are less trunks and more woven systems—arched buttresses, braided stems, suspended mats. Vines connect one outcrop to another like living rigging. On sheltered ledges, shrubs merge into continuous cushions as dense as upholstery. Epiphytes stack upward in layers: fern over bromeliad over orchid over moss over algae, each catching what the one above sheds. In places you can walk a hundred paces without touching mineral soil at all, only springy plant mass anchored to cliff faces.

Because vegetation is concentrated where moisture and grip coincide, life arranges itself in bands and islands. Between them are large, stark spaces: open wind corridors, bare shelves of ochre stone, long quiet slopes with almost nothing but lichen. That contrast makes the land feel both lush and empty at once—green citadels in a mostly exposed sky.

And the sky is loud.

Fauna dominates this region not by bulk but by number and timing. Dawn detonates: millions of insects rising from overnight roosts, cliff swallows and needle-winged hunters slicing through them, macaque-like cliff ungulates barking from ledges, bright-throated frogs calling from water pockets, beetles knocking against bark, wingbeats like rain. The noise bounces off stone walls and comes back doubled. Even at noon, when heat settles, there is stridulation from every vegetated seam. At dusk, another shift: night fliers pour out of fissures, and the ridges thrum like drums under the feet of herd animals moving to salt licks.

You can stand alone on a broad shelf with no settlement in sight and still feel surrounded, because everything that is alive is either calling, answering, or being hunted.

Predation is constant and visible. Narrow-bodied gliders leap between vine bridges. Ambush lizards flatten against warm rock and strike at pollinator clouds. Small hoofed browsers with prehensile lips climb near-vertical root lattices to reach moisture-heavy leaves, then sleep in wind-carved alcoves that stink of guano and musk. Carrion never lasts long; scavenger crabs—yes, at this altitude—emerge at night from damp crevices whenever fog has been thick enough.

Human marks are sparse and old in style. Here and there on stable saddles sit low dry-stone shelters, roofed with layered bark and tied cane. Ladder scars in cliff faces show where people harvested nesting eggs or resin. Small wind shrines—stacked stones with bone chimes—stand at pass mouths, warning of gusts or honoring something less practical. Paths are not roads but repeated choices: braided foot-tracks, polished handholds, pegs hammered into softer rock generations ago. Metal is rare and treasured; most tools are stone-edged, bone-toothed, fiber-bound.

The people who cross this country obey practical rules that feel like folklore:

- Travel ridge-top at first light, valley-bottom at dusk.
- Never camp under a dripline you did not watch for a full night.
- If the insects fall silent, leave the ledge.
- Follow fig roots for water, not bird flight.
- Tie everything; the wind steals.

Nothing supernatural is required for the place to feel strange. The unusual quality is in combinations: warmth at such height, abundance perched over emptiness, deafening life in a landscape that looks almost austere from far away. It is an old world in a late, settled form—deeply eroded, structurally stable, ecologically frantic.

A place of final stone and restless biology. A high, open ruin that plants rebuilt and animals filled with sound.
