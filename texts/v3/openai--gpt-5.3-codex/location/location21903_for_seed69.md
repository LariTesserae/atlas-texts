At first it feels like a mountain range at night—until you realize the “mountains” are breathing.

The land rises in long, old-backed ridges, worn into smooth arches by ages of rain and mineral sweat. Nothing here is sharp unless it was cut by a machine; everything natural has been rounded, slumped, hollowed, and resealed by water. Even at high elevation, the ground is wet underfoot. Not river-wet, but sponge-wet: peat-dark soil over stone chambers full of seepage, moss mats stretched over fissures, root-webs holding entire slopes together like soaked cloth. Rain has just passed, or is about to begin, or is condensing out of the air itself. Water exists in too many states at once—mist, drip, runnel, bead, vapor, warm puddle, black pool.

It is utterly dark, not because there is no sky but because cloud sits low and thick as rock wool, swallowing starlight and horizon alike. The only illumination comes in brief, hard geometries: furnace doors opening, arc lamps swinging from cranes, pressure gauges glowing weak amber, bioluminescent fronds pulsing in tidal rhythms. In those flashes you see the scale. Valleys are not valleys but drowned basins miles across, linked by elevated catwalks and rusted rail spines. Whole weather systems snag on the high escarpments and turn in place, raining themselves out over the same districts for weeks.

Vegetation is everywhere there is a pause in heat. Not pristine forest—more like a negotiated occupation. Giant fern-trees climb slag mounds. Vines as thick as pipe insulation wrap smokestacks and drink condensation from their metal skins. Marsh canopies spread over settling ponds, their roots filtering heavy sediment into terraces of black reeds and copper-red algae. Fungal shelves bloom on timber pilings, and carnivorous pitcher groves cluster in exhaust shadows where insects gather by the millions. Nothing is sparse. Even barren-looking surfaces reveal films, threads, spores, scales, eggs.

And the animals make the place feel crowded even in darkness. Wet-winged gliders skim just above pooling water and click to each other in rapid metallic bursts. Herds of six-legged marsh ungulates move in the lowlands, each step releasing gas blips from the mud. Eel-like climbers thread through cable trays and flooded culverts. Predators hunt by vibration: blind, broad-headed things that read the thrum of engines and the pulse of blood with equal precision. Around every pump station and turbine outfall there are feeder frenzies—fish, amphibians, filter-mouthed birds, scavenger crabs the size of helmets, all cycling around waste heat and oxygen-rich discharge. Life has learned the machine calendar: shift-change sirens trigger migrations.

Sound never stops. Rain on sheet metal, turbines whining through worn bearings, piston strokes in deep syncopation, frogs like struck ceramic, wings drumming tarps, steam vents screaming, distant rockfalls softened by fog. The loudness is layered rather than overwhelming; if you listen long enough, you can pick apart zones. High ridges carry wind-hum and cable-song. Middle terraces are all pumps and belts and labor noise—hammers, shouted counts, chain drag. Wet basins below are biological thunder: choruses, mating booms, feeding splashes, territorial knocks on hollow trunks. Silence exists only in tiny pockets, brief as held breath.

The built world is scattered but consequential. You don’t get cities here—you get stations. A refinery perched on basalt columns. A chain of lift towers crossing a sink valley. A maintenance hamlet clinging to one dry spur, lit by shielded lamps and ringed with rain gutters. Decommissioned ore bins now used as rookeries. Broken observation domes colonized by moss and nesting bats. Every structure is either actively fighting water or surrendering to it in stages. Bolts are oversized; foundations are deep; walkways are grated so mud and blood can fall through.

Its strangeness is not decorative. It is systemic.

Gravity feels normal, but fluids behave as if impatient: water climbs warm pipes against expectation, fog pours downhill in visible streams, and certain nights bring “up-rain,” when droplets form on stone and leap toward charged cloudbanks. The rocks themselves weep conductive salts, and old miners mapped safe paths not by compass but by listening for a subaudible ground-hum that changes before slope failure. Lightning rarely strikes from sky to earth; it crawls ridge-to-ridge, horizontal, seeking tall wet metal. Creatures have adapted: fur that channels charge, shell ridges that ground it, mating dances timed between storm pulses.

Over centuries, erosion and industry have reached a wary equilibrium. Excavations become wetlands. Tailings harden into new cliffs and host rare orchids. Flood channels are reinforced, then undermined, then reinforced again a few meters over. Nothing is finished; everything is maintained, reclaimed, repurposed, or being eaten. The world here does not oppose human machinery—it metabolizes it.

So the landscape, taken whole, is a high dark watershed of engines and marsh, ancient stone and recent steel, loud with both manufacture and appetite. Vast enough to have its own climate, dense enough that every square meter is occupied, active enough that maps age in months. If you stand still long enough on a catwalk and let your eyes adjust, the blackness resolves into motion in every direction: drip lines, vines, belts, wings, cables, steam, predators, workers, clouds.  
Nothing here is empty.  
Nothing here is ever truly at rest.
