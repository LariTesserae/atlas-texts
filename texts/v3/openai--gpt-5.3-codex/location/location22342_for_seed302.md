At this altitude, the world is all stone and water, with almost no empty space between them.

The land is a high shoulder of mountains, but not jagged in the dramatic way of young ranges. It has been here long enough to settle into itself. Ridges round off into broad backs; cliffs stand in tiered faces, undercut and polished; whole slopes look poured rather than broken. Everywhere, water sits close under the surface. Rock sweats. Hairline fractures bead constantly. Shallow basins brim and overflow in thin silver sheets that immediately find another channel, another lip, another drop.

There are no plants at all—no grass in cracks, no lichen haze, no moss in the wet seams. The color palette stays mineral: pale limestone, iron-dark basalt, bands of greenish serpentine, occasional quartz glare where a face has split clean. Without leaves or soil to soften anything, the terrain feels packed and total: columns crowded beside gullies, gullies feeding chutes, chutes feeding plunge bowls, bowls overflowing into braided runnels. Even flat ground is busy with texture—rippled crusts, knuckled stone, fractured plates, pebble fans cemented in mineral skin.

The light is relentless. High elevation thins the air; cloud breaks quickly; wet surfaces return brightness from every angle. Even on overcast intervals, the whole place glows as if lit from below by reflected sky. When sun does strike directly, the water turns to moving glass and the rock to a hard, almost chalk-white blaze. Shadows are sharp but short-lived, constantly diluted by reflected glare from nearby wet faces.

And over all of it: sound. Not occasional, not weather-dependent—constant. A full, body-filling roar from thousands of simultaneous flows. No single waterfall dominates; instead, every meter contributes: drips amplified in hollows, rills hissing over rough shelves, sheetfalls drumming on ledges, subterranean torrents thudding in stone tubes. The mountains seem to breathe through resonant cavities, producing low notes that merge with the higher rush. It is deafening in the literal sense; speech vanishes at arm’s length. Silence does not occur here.

Yet for all that force, the place barely changes. This is an equilibrium landscape. Moisture arrives in a steady cycle—condensation, runoff, seep, return—without sharp seasons. Channels are old and fitted to their loads. Edges are worn to stable angles. Pools fill to exact lips and spill at exact points. The same trickles run in the same grooves year after year, so reliably that mineral deposits have built smooth collars and rims around them. Erosion is active, but patient, operating within long-set boundaries.

Life exists, but sparsely and without greenery. In sheltered seeps, thin biofilms cling directly to stone—colorless to pearl-gray, feeding on dissolved minerals and trace organics. In deeper cistern pools, rare blind amphibious things move slowly along the bottom, more like articulated stone than flesh, grazing those films and each other’s shed skins. Once in many hours, something winged crosses the bright cut of sky—a high scavenger riding thermals between cliffs—but it never lingers. Most of the world’s metabolism here is water and chemistry, not biomass.

The slight strangeness comes from how complete the system is. No tree line, no meadow patches, no ruins, no signs of interruption—just an immense, wet mineral engine running at one setting. It is not hostile in the dramatic sense, only absolute. You don’t feel watched; you feel excluded. The rules are simple and unwavering: if there is a surface, it will be wet; if there is a drop, it will sound; if there is a cavity, it will resonate; and nothing soft will take root.
