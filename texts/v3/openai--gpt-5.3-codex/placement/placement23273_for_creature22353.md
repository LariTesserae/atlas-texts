At first I think I’d be a visitor with a notebook—something upright, curious, and temporary—standing in the Arcades while everything ignores me.

But that feels wrong almost immediately. Nothing here is built for spectators. The basin rewards repetition, not astonishment. So if I belong, I probably belong as a **repeating act**.

I’d be found where one district gives itself to another: the lip between the Arcades and the Bowl, where fused trunks break into salt plates and every route has to decide whether to narrow, split, or descend. A planning edge. A place where mistakes become infrastructure.

At first light I’d be small and unremarkable: a low, matte-bodied hexapod, shoulder-high to the local grazers’ knees, with a blunt mineral-rasping mouth and pads that taste charge differences in stone. I’d walk one loop, then another, then the same loop for years. Not because I am obedient to a leader, but because the loop is how thought happens here.

My species—if “species” is even the right word—would do three jobs and call none of them jobs:

- shave lane edges where talus encroaches,  
- compact loose granules where traffic should hold,  
- leave a thin resin-salt film at junctions, faintly bitter on disfavored turns, neutral on preferred ones.

To an outsider, that’s maintenance. To the Concord, that is memory update.

By noon I’d vanish into a cavity at the usual height—because “usual” is a structural force in this basin. In that cool hollow I’d host what I carry: filament colonies threaded through my gut and along my limbs, microbes that read mineral films better than I do. I feed them trace organics; they tune my resin chemistry. We are a moving committee.

At dusk I’d re-emerge when the hiss-swarms rise. They’d pass through my fresh trail marks, picking up and redistributing the signature to nearby roots and wall crusts. By night, a trunk two corridors away has already “heard” my decision at the junction.

And after enough seasons, I wouldn’t describe myself as an animal at all. I’d say:

I am a **turning radius** with legs.  
I am a **zoning preference** that can sleep.  
I am one short-lived body through which a long municipal sentence keeps being written.

So where would you find me?

Not at the center. This place has no center.

You’d find me at thresholds—where collapse just happened, where a new crack cools at dawn, where traffic hesitates for a generation before becoming law. I’d be the quiet bias there, one participant among thousands, helping the basin decide what counts as a street.
