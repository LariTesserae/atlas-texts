At first I think I would be something high—one of the long patrol bodies, a clean silhouette moving the avenues by right.

But that feeling passes.

In this place, height is exposure, and power is expensive. The true center is lower, closer to the stone, where decisions are made in seconds and remembered in route-changes. So I wouldn’t live in the open water. I’d live at a **junction**—half shelter, half street—where three currents meet around a collapsed sponge court and a seam of old clam shells.

I would be a reef octopus, yes, but not the dramatic kind.  
Not a myth-beast.  
A municipal animal.

---

My den would sit inside an old intake tube calcified into architecture, its mouth narrowed with carried rubble: shell lips, coral chips, one polished vertebra set like a doorstop. Outside, a fan of discarded shell and crab carapace would spread over the silt like paperwork no one bothered to file. That midden would not be trash; it would be signal.

Scavengers read it first.  
Then hunters read the scavengers.  
Then tenants read the hunters and decide whether this is still a safe block.

I would be, in effect, an administrator of occupancy.

At dim-cycle I’d flatten into the wall and become fracture pattern: stone-brown, pore-textured, nobody-home. At feed-cycle I’d pour out along the avenue floor, arms tasting ahead of me, each sucker taking attendance. Shrimp density in crack three. New eel musk under arch two. Fresh breakage near the clam mosaic. Pressure pulse from upper water—large body, predictable route, three breaths away.

I wouldn’t “own” territory so much as keep it legible.

---

My closest neighbors would be:

- a pair of blennies in a hinge-shell above my entrance (loud, territorial, useful alarms),  
- brittle stars under my threshold stones (night sweepers, excellent at making evidence vanish),  
- a blind crab that visits when vent-warmth pushes mineral taste into the district (late, careful, never steals).

I’d tolerate a small cleaner shrimp guild if they kept to my dorsal line and didn’t advertise me with too much motion. I’d evict squat lobsters only when they blocked my emergency exit. I’d leave one side-chamber empty on purpose, because empty space is bait and buffer both.

In this city, vacancy is a tool.

---

Communication wouldn’t be spectacle. No bright reef semaphore here. Too dark, too particulate, too close. I’d use skin flashes only at arm’s length: pale bar for *yield*, dark bloom for *occupied*, quick silver ripple for *now*. Past that, I’d speak through structure:

arm-tip taps on clam shell,  
two knocks through limestone,  
siphon puff across grit to send a pressure whisper down-tunnel.

A ray passing overhead would feel none of it.  
The tenants three chambers over would understand perfectly.

---

And if I had a civic role beyond survival, it would be this:

I’d be a **keeper of crossings**.

Not a ruler. Not even important in the way predators are important. But I would know the timing gaps—exactly when to cross open channel between shark loops, when current slacks enough for larvae to settle safely, when vent-plume expansion will make lower tunnels unbreathable for soft-bodied juveniles.

Young ones wouldn’t inherit that as instinct alone. They’d inherit it by following my route, losing me, finding me again, and living.

That is how this metropolis thinks: not in laws, but in repeated successful passages.

---

So where would you find me?

At the elbow of a broken sponge dome, where warm mineral thread meets clear salt layer. At a doorway made of old death and recent labor. Half hidden, always listening through my skin.

Who would I be?

A small, many-hearted clerk of the drowned city—  
one temporary face of the Current-Mind,  
rezoning the dark one shell at a time.
