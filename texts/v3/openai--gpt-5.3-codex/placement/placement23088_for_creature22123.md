At first, I think I’d be placed high—on the pale granite ridges where the first catchments gather—because that’s where maps begin.

But if I stay with your city long enough, I don’t trust that first instinct.

High places here are prestigious only to outsiders. The real intelligence lives at transitions: where one slope changes material, where one channel becomes three, where a stair is also a spillway, where a wall is holding both earth and memory. So I start moving downslope in my mind, not to the lowest basin, but to a hinge.

I find myself in a mid-elevation district where basalt starts replacing granite, where water arriving from the upland first slows enough to confess what it carries—silt, cold, leaf fragments, mistakes.

There are three courts there, close-set, connected by narrow runnels and one long covered passage where sound is always half-muted by condensation. In that passage is an ear stone at shoulder height, smooth from generations of cheeks. I keep coming back to that stone.

So maybe I am not a mason, not exactly.  
Not a water-keeper, not exactly.  
I am one of the people assigned to **threshold work**.

My band does not build new things often. We inherit friction points. We are called when a channel starts speaking in the wrong pitch, when frost heave opens a hairline seam, when a parapet dries too quickly and hints at a hidden diversion. We carry wedges, lime, hooked scrapers, plaited cord, slate tags, and a patience that looks like slowness until rain comes hard and everything we did yesterday becomes the reason nothing fails tonight.

If I give this self a name, it would be something plain, maybe a name that sounds like wet stone—Teren, Sile, Marr. But title matters more than name in this place. I’d be a **Keeper of Third Flow**—the person responsible not for the main channel, not for emergency overflow, but for the subtle middle path that saves both from being overwhelmed.

My mornings in freeze season begin before dawn. I walk the same route with a tapping rod, testing lids, breaking skins of ice where they shouldn’t persist, listening for that dangerous silence that means blockage upstream. I know which stairs go glossy first, which joints complain in east wind, which family always overdraws from the west cistern and then repays in labor during desilting week.

I live in a narrow house built into a retaining wall, two rooms deep, one room warm. Above my door hangs a coil of algae-green rope that’s still good because I keep re-oiling it. Inside, there is a shelf of stone fragments wrapped in cloth: pieces from old repairs that held. Not relics—references.

And yet, sitting with your world, one more shift happens.

I think I would not only be a person in it. I would also be one of its small instruments: an ear, a hand, a habit. In this city the self is distributed. A life is less “I built this” than “I kept this interval from widening.”

So if you asked someone else where to find me, they might not point to a face. They’d say:

- at the north transfer stair after first thaw,  
- in the covered passage with the listening stone,  
- at the junction where three runnels braid and unbraid,  
- in the maintenance log marked by slate chips instead of ink,  
- wherever water has started to hesitate.

Who would I be?

A citizen of gradients.  
A minor custodian of continuities.  
One of the many who make sure the city keeps becoming itself, rain after rain.
