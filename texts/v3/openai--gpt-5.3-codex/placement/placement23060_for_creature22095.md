At first, I think I would live high—on one of those rope-lashed platforms above the worst of the surge, somewhere that pretends to be safer.

But this basin punishes pretense.

So after a few weeks, I’d drift lower, not out of courage, but because everything important happens where the pressure changes are fastest: near the central crack, under the water-ceiling that can’t decide if it is sky. Not directly beneath it—that’s for fools and the newly arrived—but off to the side, where one old lime-mortared alcove faces away from the dominant push. I’d sleep there sometimes, then somewhere else the next cycle, because water remembers.

You’d find me by touch before sight: one hand on a tether line, one forearm against basalt, feeling for the basin’s mood swings.

---

Who would I be?

Not a hunter first. Not a builder first. I’d be one of the **timers**—the people who keep rhythm when the roar tries to erase sequence.

I imagine my called name would be something like **Silt**, short and practical, easy to sign close to the chest.  
My second name—the one for reflections—would be **Third-Kneel**, because once, early on, I’d survive by kneeling before anyone else when the roar thins for half a breath. After that, no one would ignore my flinches.

My work would look small from outside:

- retying anchors others assume are still good,
- tapping stone routes at dawn-dim to confirm what’s solid and what only *looks* solid,
- moving sleeping lines a handspan up or down because last cycle’s safe height is this cycle’s trap,
- teaching children to read pressure in their teeth before trusting their eyes.

At ritual, in the slab ring, I wouldn’t lead. I’d hold the **off-beat**—the stubborn interval that keeps everyone from matching the basin too perfectly. The elders strike the obvious pulse; I strike the delayed one, so the ocean-in-training doesn’t steal our timing whole.

---

If I stayed long enough, I think the answer would change again.

In this place, identity is not a noun for long. You become a pattern you repeat under stress.

So eventually I wouldn’t just be Silt, or Third-Kneel, or even one of the Kneeling Tide. I’d be a **transfer point**: body to stone, stone to rope, rope to platform, platform to sleeping child. A human coupling in a living pressure circuit.

And if you asked, years later, “Where can I find you?” the truest answer would be:

Not in a house.  
Not at a fixed height.  
Find the bend where the mist-herds shear sideways under the low ocean roof.  
Find the alcove with three anchor scars and a membrane flag stitched from old fin-skin.  
Wait for the moment the roar drops and everyone folds to their knees.

I’m the one already down, palm on rock, counting the vertical surge before it arrives.

A person, yes.  
But also a beat the basin has not managed to swallow.
