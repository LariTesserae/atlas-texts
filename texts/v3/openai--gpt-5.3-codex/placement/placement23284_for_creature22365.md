At first, I think I’d be an outsider with instruments.

You’d find me on the highest long-backed swell for a few days, trying to pretend distance helps: marking vent intervals, plotting rain curtains, counting the metallic rings like they’re data points instead of weather. I’d sleep badly there. Too exposed, too visual, too much horizon.

Then I’d drift lower, almost by accident, into the damp logic of the place — the narrow strips where stone sweats at dawn, where skitters arrive exactly three minutes before the first drip, where black sand lines appear and unmake themselves. That’s where the plain actually *speaks*: not in landmarks, but in timing.

So if I belonged here, truly, you wouldn’t find me on a ridge.  
You’d find me in a shallow notch between two bellbed knuckle-fields, near a vent that exhales on a near-perfect 12-count. A place with no view to brag about, just repetition precise enough to trust.

And what would I be?

Not a ruler, not a builder, not a survivor in the heroic sense.  
I’d be a **phase-keeper** — a small mobile symbiont of the bellbed system.

Part animal, part instrument: skin salted against mineral air, inner ear dense with magnetite grains, sternum tuned to ground-borne hum. I’d live on condensation, microbial films, and what the skitters leave behind. I’d move slowly between dome clusters and “listen” for drift: when one district rings a fraction late after a collapse, when a vent gate starts hissing off-beat, when a rain-trigger arrives out of sequence.

Then I’d do the only work that matters here: tap corrections back into stone.

Not commands. Not thoughts. Just interval.  
A pulse in one trough, an answering pulse two ridges over.  
A carried beat until the plain re-locks.

In a world that runs like a factory and produces no monument, I wouldn’t be an inventor of futures. I’d be maintenance with a heartbeat. A living metronome. A brief, warm creature whose purpose is to help an ancient, distributed animal keep its endless tempo intact.
