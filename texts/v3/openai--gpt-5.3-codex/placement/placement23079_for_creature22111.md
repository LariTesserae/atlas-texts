At first I think I’d be on the rim—some outsider posture, squinting down, trying to understand the whole bowl at once.

But that’s wrong for this place.  
The rim is for arriving, not belonging.

If I lived there, you’d find me lower, in the moving shade just under the third terrace-ring, where the wind-throats open into the city and everything is loud enough to feel in your teeth. Not in a grand house, not in a green court—on a maintenance walk barely wider than my foot, with coil-rope over one shoulder and a pouch of resin wedges at my hip.

I would be a **throat-tuner**.

Not a musician exactly, though music would be my work.  
Not a mason exactly, though I’d always have stone dust on my hands.

I’d belong to the crews that keep the carved wind channels in balance: shaving a lip here, packing a crack there, changing the pitch of a vent before it changes the temper of a whole district. In that basin, tone is infrastructure. One note too sharp and people miss drum-phrases. One note gone flat and you don’t hear a fissure warning in time. So I’d listen for tiny drifts: how the noon gust catches a new plaster seam, how salt bloom roughens a curve, how heat in the wall adds a half-beat of delay to an echo route children use to navigate home.

By sight, I’d look like anyone grown there: narrow, tendon-lean, resin-matte skin, brim and veil angled against glare. My ears would be thickened and notched by years of sound. I’d speak little in public—mostly wrist-sign, knuckle-taps on rail, mirror flick if distance demanded it. Voice would be for closeness: mouth to ear, in door-shadow, saying only what shouldn’t be thrown into the bowl.

My day would start before full light, when the groves are still unfolding and the city is loud but not yet furious. Tap-test the throat stones. Mark new salt creep. Retension a bridge line that dropped in the night as vine-weight shifted. Trade three-beat gossip with a mill crew. Stop to dust-pollinate a wax paddle cluster the bats skipped. At midday, disappear into blue shade while the white walls throw fire. At dusk, climb high and listen to the basin settle into its night register—the hum of folded canopies, distant drum code, reed-click tapering to static.

And if you asked who I am, not just what job:

I’m one of the people who keep “finished” from happening.

In that city, collapse doesn’t arrive as drama; it arrives as neglected adjustment. So my identity would be maintenance as ethics: balance today, rebalance tomorrow, never confuse stability with stillness. I wouldn’t be central. I’d be structural—an edge-dweller in a culture of edges, tuning the agreement between stone, wind, and hand so everyone else can keep living lushly in the dry.

So: where would you find me?

Not in the center of the basin.  
In the narrow places where sound turns into warning, warning into work, and work back into song.
