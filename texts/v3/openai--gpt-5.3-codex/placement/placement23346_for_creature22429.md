At first, I think I’d be an outsider on the rim—too upright, too loud, too certain that a city is supposed to stay where it was built.

But if I stayed long enough, the basin would cure me of that.

You’d find me not in the center square, not in any grand chamber, but at an unremarkable intersection where one dust stream should divide and doesn’t. Kneeling there. Palm flat to stone. Waiting for the tiny delay in vibration that says a plate below has begun to lift.

I think I’d become one of the lesser specialists among Those-Who-Reset: a **threshold keeper**. Not an architect, not an elder, just the person responsible for transitions—door lips, stair starts, culvert mouths, the first handspan where one condition becomes another. In a world like that, thresholds are where failure begins, and where recovery is easiest if you catch it early.

My tools would be simple and intimate:

- a forked dust-comb for steering particulate flow,
- a pouch of wedge-stones sorted by thermal bite,
- a crescent scraper for salt skin,
- braided lichen cord for tying reed beds against night contraction,
- thin resonance plates I’d set into mortar and read at dusk with my fingertips.

I’d speak little. Mostly rib-band color, wrist taps on masonry, shoulder angle, shared touch on the same block. If I had a voice-name, it would probably be short and percussive, used only in close quarters. My truer name would be the pattern I tap before resetting a seam—recognized by crew before they see me.

Home would be inside a thickened wall on a leeward terrace, in a chamber that warms late and cools slowly. I’d sleep near a safe crack lined with felted lichen, because even rest there is calibrated to movement. Too still would feel wrong.

So: who would I be?

Not the one who built the city.  
Not the one who rules it.  
I’d be the one who notices when a corner has shifted half a finger-width before anyone else does, and quietly puts it back into conversation with the rest of the basin.

In that place, that is a life.  
And, maybe, a kind of devotion.
