At first, I think I’d be something obvious: a traveler standing in the glare, squinting, trying not to step where the mud remembers better than I do.

But if I stay—if I let the basin teach me its pace—I don’t remain a traveler for long.

You would find me at a boundary.

Not at the center where the shrine-rings are easiest to see, and not in the fern-thick seams where the experienced Tenders move like thought. I’d live where one condition turns into another: the lip of a mineral shelf where droplets gather, the edge of the obelisk’s slow-air field where sweat begins to dry again, the split point where a channel chooses pool or runnel depending on wind. Places most people cross without naming.

I think I would become a **Margin Tender**—one of the people trusted with thresholds.

---

My dwelling would be tucked under a leaning shelf that weeps at regular intervals, each drop landing in three old basins tuned to slightly different pitches. Not decoration—instrument. My mornings would begin with listening before moving: which basin rings full, which rings dull, which has gone nearly silent because a root-mat shifted overnight.

I wouldn’t carry many tools. A soft scraper for mineral bloom. A coil of living fiber to redirect trickles without cutting flow. A set of dark ceramic reeds for making calibration tones when my own throat is tired. A slate of etched schedules—not clocks, but sequences: ribbonfish passage, browser crossing, predator window, umbrella lift.

I’d be the one children are sent to when they’ve learned names but not intervals.  
I’d teach them this:

- Wetness is a map that changes faster than stone.
- If two species arrive at once, you didn’t discover chaos—you missed an earlier sign.
- Never “fix” a crack before asking what now depends on it.
- A perfect repair is often a wound.

---

Over years, I think my body would change in small basin ways. Skin less reflective. Soles broader. Hearing tuned to impacts more than voices—the difference between a drop hitting deep water and a drop hitting silted shallows. I’d speak less loudly. I’d stop expecting silence to mean emptiness.

And eventually, maybe, I’d take on a quieter role still: not hunter, not builder, not even daily steward, but **keeper of handoff**.

Because this place doesn’t need heroes. It needs continuity.

So at dusk, when gold light slides over wet stone and the pure tones rise from nowhere obvious, I would stand with an apprentice at the field edge and let them call first. If their note holds the fog one handspan lower without startling the kites, I say nothing. If they miss, we watch what moved instead. We learn.

Who would I be here?

Not owner.  
Not ruler.  
Not even resident, exactly.

I’d be a practiced nerve ending in a very old living body—one small mind in the basin’s memory, helping it keep its promises for another day.
