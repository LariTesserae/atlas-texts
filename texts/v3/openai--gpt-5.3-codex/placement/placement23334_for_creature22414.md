At first, I think I’d be something quick and low—an eel in a culvert mouth, all muscle and decision, reading pressure with my whole body.

Then I think: no, a bat—because this place is made of echoes, and anyone who truly belongs here has to hear shape before seeing it.

Then again: maybe a person, because this underland is not anti-human. It remembers hands. Bolts were tightened here yesterday. Flood marks were painted by someone standing in thigh-high water with a shaking wrist. There’s room for a life that carries tools.

So if I let the image settle slowly, I find myself **not as one creature**, but as a kind of local profession that has gone feral in the best way.

---

You’d find me on the **painted high lines**, mostly between pump stations where the light thins to amber haze and the columns come close enough to make acoustic pockets. Not in the loud centers; in the margins just beside them—where you can still hear the whole system without being swallowed by it.

I’d keep a route, not a home.  
Door 12 to Manifold C, across the old tram ties, down to the settling flats, back up the dry corridor when alarms test. Same loop, season after season, until the loop itself becomes a kind of nest.

I’d know the place by small truths:

- which valve starts whining three days before it sticks,  
- which basin grows frogspawn first after warm rain,  
- which “dry” concrete island sweats enough to turn treacherous before dawn,  
- where clear water is a lie over undercut marl.

---

Who would I be?

A **floodline keeper**.  
Part inspector, part naturalist, part listener.

Not heroic. Not central.  
One of those rotational mammals the system tolerates and quietly trains.

I’d carry chalk, zip ties, a dissolved-oxygen meter, spare glow tags, and a notebook in a waterproof sleeve gone soft at the corners. I’d mark new scour, flag unstable skins, log dead zones, note where fish still hold steady in surge tails. I’d trust frogs more than dashboards when they disagree.

When storms come, I’d move upward and wait with everyone else—rats, cats, insects, people—on the same narrow elevations, all of us suddenly one species called **not-drowning**.

When waters drop, I’d be among the first back down, reading what changed.

---

But if you mean *deeper*—what, beneath the job title—

then I think I’d be one thread in the underland’s memory system.

Not the pumps. Not the fish.  
A recorder in motion.

Because this place is never finished, memory is survival.  
Someone has to remember that last winter’s seep is now a trench.  
That this rust bloom means dissimilar metals and three months to failure.  
That herons began using Shaft 4 only after the grate bent in the June storm.  
That life moved here first, and the map caught up later.

So I’d be a creature of updates.  
A living errata sheet in rubber boots.

---

If you came looking for me, you’d probably spot my light briefly on a column, then lose it in mist.  
If you listened, you might hear two knocks on pipe—my signal to a partner across the roar.  
If you followed, I’d lead you by the warmer fog, the safer eddies, the ledges where the toads sit fat and unbothered.

And if you asked what I worship down there, I’d say:

Not wilderness. Not machinery.  
**Continuity.**  
The ongoing pact between flow and structure, failure and repair, runoff and appetite.

In a place built from almosts, I’d be an almost too—  
almost engineer, almost animal, almost archivist—  
living where the city’s underside keeps teaching everything in it, including me, how to adapt without ever being finished.
