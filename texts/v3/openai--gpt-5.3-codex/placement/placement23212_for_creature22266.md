At first I think I’d be something with wings.

A kite, maybe—because from above the district resolves into logic: rectangles, channels, turning circles, heat plumes, schedules. From up there, nothing is sentimental. Hunger is mapped to airflow and spill points.

Then I drop lower and think: no, I’d be a channel-edge bird. An egret in oil-rainbow water, standing perfectly still beside a pump outflow, letting the machinery do the stirring and taking what rises. Patient, formal, unoffended by ugliness.

Then lower still: maybe I belong on the metal itself. A rat on pipe racks at shift change, reading the district as a network of warm routes and blind corners. Or rust, honestly—slow, inevitable, practical—never dramatic, always advancing where condensation beads first.

But if I stay with it long enough, I end up human.

You’d find me on the grated walkway of Pump Station 3, night shift, clipboard in a zip sleeve, one hand on a handrail wet with warm condensation. I’m the technician everyone calls when “it sounds a little off.” Not a manager, not a hero—just the one who listens. I know which intake clogs first after heavy rain. I know which motor always runs hot at 03:00. I know the dogs by coat pattern and which gull has learned to wait for the fish cart brake squeal.

So: who am I there?

I’m a maintenance listener in a place that survives by attention.  
Part mechanic, part field naturalist, part archivist of tiny changes.  
I live gauge-to-gauge, bearing-to-bearing, call-to-call.  

In that district, that is a full life: to notice what shifts before it fails, and keep the truce—water, steel, marsh, hunger—holding one more cycle.
