At first, I think I’d be a stranger at the edge—someone careful, someone observing.

Then I sit with it longer, and that feels wrong.

In a place like this, observers don’t stay observers. You either become useful, or you become gone.

So you’d find me not in the center, and not on the perimeter, but at a **hinge**:  
the low knot of walkways where three districts meet—one colder, one warmer, one always half-flooded—just downslope from the root-arches that mark the rot chambers.

I’d be one of the Reedbound who keeps that hinge alive.

Not a chief. Not a mystic.  
A maintenance mind. A pulse-reader.  
The kind of person who knows which braid of reed will slacken first when water lifts, which sedge-bundle needs turning before dawn chill, which argument about storage space is really an argument about fear of the next collapse.

They might call me something like **Threadwake**—because I tend the thread-lines along the rails, and because I wake when the water does.

---

My work would look small if you came from outside:

- tapping walkways with a bone peg and listening for hollowness  
- tightening knots swollen loose by fog  
- rubbing fish-oil into handrails so they shed water instead of rotting  
- reading beetle routes under bark and marking danger with reed-click codes  
- shifting sleeping platforms up two handspans before a flood-pulse, so nobody has to scramble in the dark

But here, small work is city work.

I’d spend long stretches kneeling, palm flat to moss, feeling for that deep pressure-change in the peat before it becomes visible. I’d trust the eel restlessness more than rumor, moth direction more than confidence, and the sudden absence of frog-noise more than either.

When people quarrel over boundaries, I’d be one of the ones who says:  
“Fine. Keep the strip. But who patches it when the clay shears?”

---

And over years—because years matter here—you’d find that I am less a person with a job and more a point where many signals meet:

animal movement, water breath, old memory, collective habit.

Not because of magic.  
Because attention, repeated long enough, starts to look like prophecy.

So who would I be?

I’d be a Reedbound infrastructure-keeper at a vulnerable crossing.  
A listener with callused hands.  
Someone whose name is spoken mostly when something starts to fail.

And where would you find me?

In the dim before full morning, on a slick reed span above black water,  
ear near the rail-thread,  
counting the basin’s next inhale before the town feels it.
