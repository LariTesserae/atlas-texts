At first, I think I’d try to be a traveler: a careful outsider with wrapped feet, a dust-cloth over the mouth, a satchel of traded water-sacs, moving from ash ring to ash ring.

But that version of me doesn’t last long here.  
Travelers still imagine ground as ground, path as path, shelter as shelter.  
This underland punishes nouns that pretend to be permanent.

So if I belong anywhere, it’s not in the wide sink-vales and not at the herd edges where everything is appetite. I’d be found in the **throats**—those narrowing passes where chambers pinch together, where sound and bodies and spores all funnel at once. Places too dangerous for camps, too important to ignore.

I’d be one of the people who stays anyway.

---

Not a hunter, exactly.  
Not a shaman, though outsiders would call it that.  
Not an engineer, though the work is technical in the oldest way.

I’d be a **Pitchkeeper**—a keeper of rising tone.

In a world where “sound is weather,” Pitchkeepers are meteorologists of pressure and living seismographs. We sleep in short intervals, cheek to stone, reading distant herd-thunder through jawbone. We taste the air for metallic sweetness before ventbursts. We carry forked reed-bones and chitin plates, tapping chamber walls and listening for the answer: hollow, tight, wet, ready to split.

When the valley’s pitch climbs half a note by dusk, we don’t debate.  
We move people.

---

I can see my station clearly:

A shelf cut into a pale green tuff wall above a choke pass.  
Three drum posts planted in a triangle, each tuned to different strata.  
A rack of obsidian flakes for scraping growth off resonant stone.  
Copper leaf hammered thin and tied to vine cords, not for ornament but as vibration flags.  
Bundles of bitter resin to burn when scent-signals must carry farther than ground-thumps.

Behind the station, tucked into a crack that never quite closes, a sleeping niche lined with shed membrane and braided reed fiber. No fire unless necessary. No unnecessary speech. A cache of wax-salt bricks. Two precious fluid bladders sealed in clay.

Above all of it: one of those half-grown, half-carved standing stones that hums on clear cycles.

My duty would be to keep that hum true.

---

Because I don’t think those stones are monuments.  
I think they are interfaces.

The clans strike them with padded mallets in strict patterns before storms. They smear specific oils into their carved grooves. They lash certain vines to them and cut others away. Animals avoid them, yes—but sometimes, after the right sequence of percussion, nearby ceiling mats loosen and vent gas harmlessly in little sighs instead of one killing burst.

Coincidence, maybe.  
Or conversation.

So who would I be?  
A person apprenticed to that conversation.

Part lookout, part mechanic, part diplomat between fast blood-life (herds, gliders, clans) and the slower, vast life of the underland fabric. I would not command anything. I’d negotiate timing.

---

I imagine the body that work makes:

Palms callused from drumwood.  
Forearms scarred by silica cuts.  
Inner ears half-ruined for ordinary hearing but exquisitely tuned to low-frequency tremor.  
Teeth stained amber from resin chewing.  
A permanent film of oil-clay on skin.  
An inventory of smells held like a sacred text: hot copper = fresh crack; sweet-rot = overripe bladder bloom; sharp violet = leave now.

People would come through my throat-pass in waves—hunters, children, carriers, injured, rivals forced into temporary truce by a closed route. I’d mark their wrists with pigment: safe until second shift, no delay at basin rim, follow white-backs at dawn. I’d send warning pulses downline through drum posts to the next station.

Sometimes I’d be ignored.  
Sometimes people would die anyway.  
The job would be continuing to read, continuing to signal.

---

And if I stay long enough—years, maybe—I suspect the role changes you in the way this place changes everything. The distinction between “my rhythm” and “the valley’s rhythm” would thin. I’d wake before collapses not from skill but from entrainment. My taps on stone would start matching pulses that began elsewhere.

In that late stage, maybe I’m no longer fully just clan.

Maybe I become what this world seems to produce wherever systems meet: a **bridge tissue**.

Not heroic. Not central.  
Just a living gasket between moving parts.

In a loud unfinished underworld, that might be the closest thing to belonging:  
to be the one who listens where everything narrows,  
and helps the blood keep moving through the body.
