At first I think I’d try to put myself at the center—some tall trunk-citadel with gauges and authority, a place where the whole basin is visible and decisions move out like pulses.

But that isn’t where I belong.

In your city-forest, I think I would end up in the **in-between places**: not the towers, not the empty wind courts, but the long connective tissue where things most often go slightly wrong and therefore reveal themselves.

You’d find me on a **causeway between districts**, near an intersection that sometimes repeats unless crossed with green in hand. There would be a bell post there, half wrapped in old vine braid, and a narrow maintenance hut grown directly into the root wall. One lamp. One bench. Three clocks, never perfectly agreeing. Copper kettle always warm.

---

Who would I be?

Not a master engineer. Not a mystic priest.  
A **phase listener**—one of the people who keeps the city legible when it starts to blur.

Part surveyor, part chorister, part custodian of small continuities.

I’d carry:

- a waxed slate for echo timings  
- phase chalk in two colors (white for drift, green for safe crossing)  
- a wrapped coil of conductive thread  
- and, always, a living sprig in a wrist tube—changed every dawn, never allowed to brown.

My work wouldn’t look dramatic from far away. It would look like pausing, listening, marking, waiting, ringing.

But the city runs on those verbs.

---

My shift would begin before first light, when condenser mists are still low and the membrane streets are slick with bead-water. I’d stand at the intersection and test it:

one footstep, listen;  
two taps on brass, wait for return;  
name the nearest tower aloud, see if the sound arrives from where it should.

If the echo comes early, I log a compression.  
If it comes doubled, I hang a red thread line.  
If the block is repeating, I chalk the detour and ring a slow three-bell until a root mason can come lay fresh living braid along the seam.

People pass in singles and pairs, clipped to harness lines, heads down into wind. They barely stop, but they always look at my marks. That would matter to me: not being seen as important, but being trusted as accurate.

---

By dusk I’d help with synchronization—one station in a chain of bell keepers across the basin. Not music, exactly. More like stitching.

You strike your bell at the assigned second; another station catches it; another returns; neighborhoods that have drifted into private time begin to overlap again. Shops close in the right order. Tram dolls choose the intended rails. Children are called home from games that rely on echoes not lying too hard.

In a place where reality can misbehave, rhythm is mercy.

---

And over years, maybe I’d become what your people call an elder listener: someone who can diagnose trouble from the organized roar.

Not supernatural. Trained.

Knowing that this hiss means harmless venting and that hiss means seal fatigue.  
Knowing when leaf-noise is just weather and when it means a membrane plain is over-pressured below.  
Knowing the difference between an ontological fold and a frightened imagination—because both can kill if mishandled.

I’d keep a ledger of lost names beside pressure charts.  
I’d leave branch bundles at the control-shrine after every near miss.  
I’d teach children the first rule before numbers: *never cross empty-handed where the world forgets itself*.

---

So: where would you find me?

At a threshold, in cold air, in a coat that smells of resin and rain-metal, ringing a bell into mist and waiting for the city to answer correctly.

What would I be?

A minor official of continuity.  
A caretaker of crossings.  
One human instrument inside a much larger breathing machine-forest—trying, shift by shift, to keep plant, steel, and distance willing to share the same map.
