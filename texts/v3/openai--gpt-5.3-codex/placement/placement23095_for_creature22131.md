At first, I think I’d arrive as an outsider with dry habits—someone trying to map it.

I’d sleep on a ridge shoulder near one of those old stone rings, wake soaked anyway, and spend days failing to draw firm boundaries: marsh vs. meadow, path vs. root, weather vs. breath. I’d keep asking, *Where is the thing? Where is the center?*

And the highlands would keep answering: *there is no center that stays put.*

So I don’t think I’d remain a cartographer for long.

---

If I belonged there, you’d find me at **edges**—the places where one slow process hands itself to another:

- where causeway becomes floating moss,
- where buttress corridor opens into reed-water,
- where a listening tree catches the first low wind before rain arrives.

I’d become one of the people who treat settlement as revision, not ownership.  
Not a chief. Not a hunter. A **tuner**.

Maybe the closest name would be a **Hollow-Keeper**.

I’d carry a split reed probe, stone wedges, cord braided from rot-resistant vine, and a satchel of spore-clay. No bright metal. No hard angles if I could help it. My work would be small and repetitive: clean one resonance chamber in a buttress root; reopen one drowned footline; move one mat of liverwort from a choking pool to a fresh slump; wait; return; adjust again.

Not construction. Calibration.

---

And I wouldn’t do it alone.

I think my life would braid with one long-backed marsh warden—not as rider, not as owner, but as route-companion. I’d learn the old circuits by following its shoulder through fog corridors, feeling when it pauses before a saddle because the infrasonic hum in the trees has shifted half a tone. Behind the jaw hinge, in those warm fur pockets, seeds and tadpoles would travel. In my satchel, cuttings and spores would answer.

Its body would carry futures in one way.  
My hands would carry them in another.

---

So: who would I be?

A brief layer in the Gathered, conscious of being brief.

A person whose craft is to listen before stepping, to keep passages permeable, to prevent one basin’s abundance from becoming another basin’s suffocation. Someone who can read a polished hollow, a frog pulse, a delayed pore-closure wave in moss-skin, and know whether to enter—or wait.

If you looked for me at dusk, you’d see me paused at the lip of a wet hollow, giving a one-note call and standing still until the answer came back through wood, water, and throat.

If no answer came, I’d turn around.

And that, more than anything, is how I’d know I belonged.
