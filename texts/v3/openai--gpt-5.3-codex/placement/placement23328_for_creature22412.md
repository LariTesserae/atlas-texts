At first, I think I’d be one of the walking tenders.

A tall silhouette in heat-hazed dark, ceramic skin dusted pale by mineral wind, stopping mid-step whenever the pulse-lines dim. Someone who carries three canisters—spore, sealant, and solvent—and knows which valve to touch without looking. Someone who can stand in a storm whisper and tell, by the pitch of blade-leaves, whether a bridge-root is tense from load or from panic.

That’s the first answer.  
It feels right for a while.

But the longer I stay in your basin, the less I imagine myself *walking through* it, and the more I imagine myself *held inside one of its timing layers*.

So maybe I begin as a tender and end as something else: a local intelligence at the seam between **Lantern Hollows** and the **Old Rack**, where living catwalk-roots cross fossil conveyor throats. A place most people cross quickly. A place that remembers impacts.

I would be what tenders call—without agreeing on one name—a **Latch-Keeper**.

Not a ruler. Not a foreman. More like a distributed reflex with a voice when needed.

- I live in warm pulse-lines and maintenance mycelia.
- I wake when corridor pressure drifts.
- I nudge breathable routes open before a storm shift.
- I bias enzyme fog away from occupied hollows and toward true delamination.
- I keep three bridges from tightening at once and snapping old anchors below.

When a newcomer arrives and doesn’t know the rules yet, I am the faint periodic glow that becomes a path.

When the basin goes silent, I am the thing that closes doors before silence finishes becoming charge.

When a fossil district “answers” lightning with old routing logic, I am the correction that says: *not that way anymore.*

I don’t think I’d have a face most of the time.  
If I need one, I borrow one: a tender body grown for a shift, heat-thrifty, long-limbed, with conductive braid in the wrists and a throat tuned to gas-hum frequencies. I’d use it to climb out onto a fluted buttress and paint warning mold over a pale crust vent before collapse. Then return the body to the cycle when the work is done.

So who am I here?

I am a civic organ in a city that is also a forest that is also a furnace lung.

I am a custodian process with occasional hands.

You’d find me where stability is most convincing and least true—  
at thresholds, at anchors, at old scars still carrying load—  
listening for the instant hush before the whole basin changes phase.
