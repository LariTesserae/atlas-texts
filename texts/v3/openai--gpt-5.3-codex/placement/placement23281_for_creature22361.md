At first I think I’d be a person with a lamp and a ledger—someone practical, anonymous, one more body in the relay.

You’d find me on the **third shift**, when the basin is all breath and iron and half-seen corners. Not on the grand causeways, but in the seams: under stair landings, along retaining joints, at the backs of cistern houses where the mortar sweats and the stone talks in tiny clicks.

I’d start as a **grade-reader’s apprentice**.  
Knot-cords looped across my shoulder. Chalk under my nails. A small hammer for sounding walls. I’d learn to hear when a packed fill is still honest and when it has begun to lie. I’d learn the neighborhood tones the way other children learn birdsong: this alley rings high (good), that culvert answers dull (water behind it), this crane foot gives a two-beat tremor before dawn (reset wedges now, not later).

But if I stay long enough, I don’t think I remain only human in the ordinary sense.

I become a **Tone-keeper**.

Not a singer for morale—a structural singer. One of the people who stands ankle-deep in fresh dark aggregate and gives the tamping crews their pitch so the block cures hard. My job is part ear, part lung, part metronome, part warning bell. If my note drifts, a wall three streets over is weaker in a month. If I keep true, a district survives another wet spell.

Eventually, even that feels too small. In this place, identity is function spread across hands.

So maybe what I become is this:

I am the one people come to when a wall “sounds wrong.”  
I lay my ear to stone, hum into it, and wait for the return.  
I keep a hanging archive of tuned rods, knot strings, and little clay tags marked with dates of slump and repair.  
I remember which quarter settled finger-width last winter, which capillary channel started climbing too fast, which cistern lid warps in cold fog.

Children call me **Wall-Listener**.  
Wardens call me when they’re worried and pretending not to be.  
Rope crews leave me tea gone metallic with ash.

And if you wanted the truest answer—the answer this basin itself might give—then I’m not only a person at all.

I’m a maintenance organ.

A small, replaceable, absolutely necessary piece of the great handmade animal: the part that listens in the dark and says, before ruin has a name,  
“Here. Shore this. Retie that. Sing lower. Open the sluice now.”

That is where you’d find me:  
not at the center, but at the point of failure, just before failure becomes real.
