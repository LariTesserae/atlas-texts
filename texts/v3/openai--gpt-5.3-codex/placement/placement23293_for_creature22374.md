I don’t think I’d begin as a hunter or a grazer.  
Not in a place like this.

At first, I’d think I belonged in the air—one of the dark gliders writing circles over escarpments, arriving exactly when weather tips. But if I stay with the feeling a little longer, that’s not quite right. Too visible. Too terminal. They are the punctuation marks of this world; I feel more like one of its commas.

Then I’d think underground: trench-digger, blind engineer, shoulder and claw and repetition. That’s closer in spirit—useful, rhythmic, anonymous—but still not it. The diggers are infrastructure. I feel less like structure, more like timing.

So:  
you’d find me on a **boundary bench**, where a high gravel shelf leans down into a seasonal basin, not far from one of those warm exhalation valleys. A place with almost nothing to look at in still weather—just rust seams, frost haze, and stone that rings faintly if struck.

I would be a **resonance grazer**—a low, six-limbed, long-ribbed animal built to read vibration through chest and jaw before sound reaches the ear. Not large. About the size of a wolf, but flatter, denser, with a hide that takes rime and sheds melt. My skull would be blunt and split with sinus chambers; my front limbs broad enough to brace on scree; my mouth lined with soft rasping pads for biofilm, mineral gel, old pellet crust, under-ice bloom.

I would not chase much.  
I would wait, test, move, and re-time myself to the continent.

My days would be mostly stillness broken by exact action:

- At dawn fog, I would press my sternum to stone and map incoming herd-lines from kilometers away.
- At midday haze, I’d work seep edges where thin salts bloom, feeding on what the big bodies miss.
- At dusk, I’d patrol old trench grids, eating the soft-bodied sift colonies that rise after damp.
- In deep cold, I’d cut narrow slots in clear ice and harvest the translucent underfilm in long ribbons.

But the real thing—my real role—would be signaling.

Not language, exactly.  
A practiced interference pattern.

When scraper fronts drift too close to exhausted crust, I’d drum low with my forelimbs and chest, sending short-range pulses that bend the file a few degrees toward fresher seam. When scavenger shadows gather too early over weak or calving animals, I’d stay quiet and let attrition do its work. When warm-rim valleys open at dawn and queues jam, I’d stand at the edges and issue slow, spaced knocks that keep bodies moving, reduce panic, reduce trampling.

I wouldn’t command anything.  
No species here is in charge.  
But I’d be one of the small regulators that keep throughput smooth.

In mating season, my kind would gather at humming cliff faces during low pressure, when the rock itself carries a sub-audible tone. We’d align our chests to the stone and answer it, not with calls but with matched tremors. Pairing would be less romance than calibration: whose rhythm holds steady through weather, whose pulse can be followed through frozen ground.

Our young would be born blind for the first long stretch, orienting only by vibration. We’d raise them near active trench fields so they learn the grammar of movement: scraper thunder, picker scatter, digger churn, glider drop, thaw-drip, frost-crack. By the time their eyes clear, they’d already know the world’s true map—the one carried in timing, not shape.

So if you asked again, “Who are you there?”

I’d say:  
I’m not apex, not architect, not spectacle.  
I’m one of the **metronome bodies** in the wide quiet between migrations.  
I keep to the seams.  
I eat the overlooked things.  
I listen through bone.  
And when the living machinery of the plateau starts to drift out of phase, I tap it gently back into time.
