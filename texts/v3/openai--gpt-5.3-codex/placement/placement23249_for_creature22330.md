At first I think I’d arrive as a person.

A visitor with a wrapped throat and too much vocabulary, trying to ask complete questions in a place that only permits them in fragments.

I’d gravitate toward the eastern quarter, because I wouldn’t trust the broad choruses yet. I’d choose edges: doorway fog, cistern rims, the underside of arches where drips make private metronomes. I’d tell myself I’m there to observe. To map. To keep my own shape.

But this basin doesn’t let you remain only an observer.  
If you stay, you become rhythmic or you become lost.

So maybe I apprentice myself to the Wet-well keepers first—the ones who lower dry sounds and harvest them wet. That job suits someone who listens more than speaks. I picture my hands always damp, my forearms silvered with condensation-script that isn’t mine, carrying words that arrived back altered: softened by root, weighted by fungi, salted by old stone memory.

I’d sleep near the well, in a hanging nest of reed cloth and wax, because dawn repeats are strongest there. I’d learn which echoes are fresh, which are ancestral, which are bait laid by clever birds. I’d learn to read moisture as grammar: a bead for warning, a runnel for invitation, a misting for grief.

Then I think: no, not the well.  
Too ceremonial. Too central.

I’d actually belong in one of those long corridors that sometimes return a footstep three days late—the kind of place everyone uses but nobody owns for long. A hinge-route. A path that is fifty paces in rain-roar and half a morning in noon silence. I’d be one of the people who keeps it open by repetition: heel-flat, toe-roll, breath-click, same sequence daily, teaching the wall that we still need passage.

That feels right: not a leader, not a seer, but a maintainer of continuity in a city that forgets quickly unless sung to.

In Gapfolk terms, I’d be nearest to a **Tuner**, but a minor one: corridor-scale, not plaza-scale. My instrument would be my sternum and two lengths of hollow cane. I’d carry a pouch of seed-bells and fungal chalk. I’d test resonance with low hums at dawn, then mark safe frequencies in condensation on leaf curtains before the heat erased them. I’d know which predator rhythms to mimic to clear children from a path without frightening the grazers. I’d keep three silence-maps: one for storms, one for mating season, one for mourning processions.

And after enough seasons, as happens there, “I” would blur.

Not die—diffuse.

My body would still walk, still sweat, still eat sliced fruit under bat-noise at dusk. But my truer self would become a recurring acoustic event: the four-click signal that tells reed-shade district the path is stable today; the dusk hum that keeps thorn-vines from closing over the school route; the little palm-on-root pulse children answer from doorways so I know they made it home.

That is who I’d be in the end:

Not a hero. Not even fully a person.  
A **reliable interval**.

A small, kept promise in the Root Ear’s larger attention.

You’d find me where the basin briefly remembers straight lines—between the eastern wet well and the amphitheater that used to be a market—walking the same curve until it holds. During the impossible hush, I’d stop with everyone else and listen for the city choosing its next shape.

When the roar returned, I’d answer with my four clicks, and the corridor would decide to remain a corridor one more day.
