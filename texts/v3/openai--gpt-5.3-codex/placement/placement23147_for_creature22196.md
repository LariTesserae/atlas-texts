At first I thought I’d be one of the obvious vertebrates—something with a spine and a route.

A cat on the ledge, maybe: ribs like sprung wire, paws memorizing rusted grating, eyes tuned to the half-light where frogs misjudge distance. Or a rat, all map and nerve, carrying the quarter’s newest geometry in my whiskers before the concrete even dries. That would fit the place’s tempo: quick adaptation, no sentiment, always one turn ahead of rising water.

But if I stand there long enough—upper catwalk, hand on a sweating rail—I feel myself becoming slower and less singular.

I think I’d be the **chalk marks** that never fully wash away.

Not one mark: the whole changing script. White numerals ghosting on pillars above the brown flood line. Arrows near conduits that have already been rerouted. “TEMP” stenciled over something that has worked for three years. Handwritten dates on replaced pump seals. Little circles around hairline cracks that haven’t widened yet. Notes from different shifts, different handwriting, layered until they become a kind of living notation: what failed, what held, what to trust when the rain comes hard.

That’s where I’d live—in the margin between plan and habit.

I’d be read by rats as shelter geometry, by roaches as heat forecast, by workers as memory that outlives a single shift. I’d fade in condensation, reappear under fresh dust, get crossed out, rewritten, moved a meter left when the basin decides. I wouldn’t command anything. I’d just keep the quarter-animal coherent as it molts: one more soft system inside all the hard ones.

So where would you find me?

At 03:40, under a cage lamp gone amber with mineral film, on a concrete rib above ankle-deep water, half-erased and still legible:

**↑ KEEP ABOVE STAIN LINE**  
**PUMP 3 CHATTERS BEFORE SEIZE**  
**CAT DEN BEHIND CRATE — DO NOT BLOCK**  

Not the heart.  
Not the brain.  
The remembering skin.
