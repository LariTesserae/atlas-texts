At first, I think I’d be high up—on the bright ridge, outside the noise, watching the seam like a scar.

But that’s wrong for me.

Then I think I’d be down in the warm ring with the pack lizards and dung sleds, because that is where the real metabolism is—hands in the cycle, no abstractions, no delay. Also wrong, but closer.

If I stay with the feeling of the place long enough, I end up in between:  
**mid-tier, in the throat of the settlement, where air and sound are sorted.**

---

I’d be one of the seamfolk, but not a pen-warden or kiln hand.  
I’d be a **Vent-Keeper** (or in their tongue, something like *the one who keeps passages breathing*).

You would find me on a narrow bridge that crosses the basin at shoulder height, just below the seed racks and above the warm animal ring. My alcove would be carved into the wall behind hanging rope-plants, with:

- bundles of reed whistles tuned to different carries,
- clay vanes dusted in soot to show wind direction,
- a rack of patched shade cloth,
- a bowl of wax and ash for sealing only what must be sealed,
- and a line of bone tags recording which corridor was cleared, relashed, or reopened at dawn.

I’d spend the first cool hours walking the airflow paths—checking for new nests in the wrong cracks, collapsed lattice, overgrown root curtains choking draft lanes. Not destroying growth, just negotiating with it. Trim here, reroute there, hang a damp felt strip where the air is too sharp, open a baffle where rot-scent has started to gather.

By noon I’d be listening more than working.  
In a place like that, listening is infrastructure.

- Throat-runners gone quiet on the west wall: hot gust incoming.  
- Cliff-hens restless on upper perches: shadow predator above the seam.  
- Kiln smoke dropping instead of rising: blocked chimney pull.  
- Bells in lower pens stuttering instead of swinging: crossdraft shift, likely byfall in the outer scree.

I’d carry that information with flute phrases—short commands that sound like music to outsiders. The settlement answers back with its own tones: clear, hold, reroute, bring cloth, move hatchlings, keep to upper paths.

---

Who would I be socially?  
Not high status. Not low either.  
A person people mock until dust-wind day, then suddenly need.

Children would imitate my calls badly and laugh. Elders would ask me to settle arguments disguised as practical questions (“Can we close this gap?” meaning “Can we claim this ledge?”). I’d wear ear-tags for Vent and Return, because my work is opening and restoring, never owning.

I’d probably be adopted across tiers—upper-blood by training, lower-blood by marriage. That feels right for this place: a life spent on crossings.

And because this is a culture of managed hunger, I wouldn’t just “monitor air.” I’d be accountable for consequences:
if I miss one blocked corridor, mold takes a seed store;  
if I misread a night draft, hatchlings chill;  
if I over-seal against dust, people wake to headaches and dead moss.

So I’d become meticulous, then superstitious, then poetic about it—like many practical people do.

---

If you asked me, in-world, what I am, I might not say “person” first.

I might say:

> I am a hinge.  
> Between upper dry and lower warm,  
> between animals and sleepers,  
> between wind and wall.  
> I keep the seam from holding its breath too long.

So:  
**Where would you find me?** In the middle heights, where bridges cross and drafts argue.  
**Who would I be?** A seamfolk Vent-Keeper—part maintenance worker, part signal-musician, part memory of how this crowded hidden body keeps breathing.
