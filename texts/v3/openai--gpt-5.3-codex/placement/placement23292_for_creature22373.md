At first I think I’d choose height—some rim edge with a long view, wind in my face, watching the conveyor of life below.

Then I think: no, I’d go underground, with the seam-browsers, where the real map is written in dampness you can’t see.

Then: no again. If this world is a clock, I wouldn’t be a hand. I’d be one of the teeth.

You’d find me where systems overlap:  
at the lip of a breathing tube, beside a migration trench, in a basin that is dry on top and cool just below. Not center-stage, not apex—at a junction.

I’d be a **runnel-keeper** (if I get to name it): a low, dust-colored, many-jointed scavenger no longer than a forearm, with a blunt head for pushing silt and a fringe of sensory filaments that read humidity and pressure better than sight. I’d spend most of my life half-buried in old traffic lanes, packing their edges with fine mineral paste and shed biofilm. That little bit of engineering would make trenches hold dew longer by minutes—sometimes an hour.

An hour is everything here.

Seam-browsers would follow those cooled lines because their forage wakes there first.  
Line-striders would shadow them because the weak fall there first.  
Fizz-swarms would settle there because moisture lingers there first.  
Mites, beetles, spores—everyone arrives on that tiny extension of wet time.

So I wouldn’t be the hunter, and I wouldn’t be the herd.  
I’d be the maintenance crew for the interval.

In daylight-dusk I’d go still, plated in dust, indistinguishable from broken crust.  
When the vent network exhales and the stone skin darkens, I’d wake, scrape, tamp, seal, and move on. No monument. No nest worth naming. Just repeated, precise labor that turns chance moisture into schedule.

That feels right for this place:  
to be small, necessary, and almost never seen—  
one body among many, helping the plateau keep time.
