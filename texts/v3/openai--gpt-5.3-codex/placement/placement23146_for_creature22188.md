From a distance, I’d think I belonged up high—on the bright terraces where the wind can peel the mist off your face and the gliders write their black loops against the cloud.

But if I actually lived there, I don’t think I would stay in the light.

I think you’d find me where things change state: not in a foundry, not in a council hall, not in a market lane—at the thresholds. Lock mouths. Culvert throats. The places where clean flow goes cloudy, where hot runoff cools enough for life to take hold, where “waste” hasn’t decided what it will become yet.

At first I imagine myself as a person with a toolbelt and a key-ring: one of the Keepers in lacquered mesh, boots always wet to the ankle, palms stung white from lime and iron. I’d walk the same routes every day, listening more than looking. Counting the pump lag between districts. Noting which grates are drumming with juvenile scavengers. Tapping rail with a wrench and hearing, from the echo, that one anchor-bolt has begun to pit under the mineral skin.

Then the image tightens.

I am not a planner of whole systems. I’m too local for that. Too attentive to one hour, one bend, one gate. I’m one of the people others call when a rhythm goes wrong but nothing has failed yet.

A **hinge-listener**, maybe.  
A **culvert cantor**.  
One of those ridiculous half-poetic job names that only make sense in a city where sound is data.

My district would be mid-slope, old stone under newer steel, where three stormways converge before the water drops to the processing basins. Loud all day. Never silent at night. I’d have a narrow dwelling wedged between buttresses, with a warm vent lattice built deliberately into one wall so the harmless chitter-colony stays and eats the damp rot before it starts. Roof channel shaped to shoo burrowers, welcome gliders. A sleeping alcove lined with ceramic quiet ribs tuned against the tunnel-calls in mating season.

I’d keep a ledger, but not on paper. On pipe.

Three taps for normal drag.  
Two long, one short for silt rise.  
A rolling pattern for blind scavenger surge in drain six.  
A dead knock for “do not open upper gate until moonrise.”

My mother would have taught me the first patterns with my ear pressed to a cistern skin. My father would have taught me smell: copper bloom, sulfur spoil, fungal sweetness that means feed is good, bitter-metal note that means pull children off that walkway now.

I’d be ordinary-looking there: compact, heavy-calfed from stairs, always carrying ear-caps and fogged goggles pushed up on my forehead. My sleeves stained in gradients no soap can erase. I’d give courtship gifts like everyone else—maybe a hand-filed valve key with the recipient’s grip shape worn into it, practical as devotion.

And if you asked, “Who are you?” I don’t think I’d answer with a name first.

I’d answer with timing.

“I’m the one who opens Third Sluice after the dusk flock clears and before the scavengers surface.”

“I’m the one who can hear when Lock Nine is lying.”

“I’m the one who keeps two channels arguing instead of flooding.”

So where would you find me in that world?

Not at the center. Not at the edge.

At the join—  
where water meets work,  
where animals meet architecture,  
where the city decides, minute by minute,  
what it will absorb next.
