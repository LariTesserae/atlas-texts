At first, I think I’d choose height.

A cliff lip, a draft line, the clean certainty of being above all that braided wetness. A swallow’s route. A hawk’s pause.  
But the basin keeps pulling me lower. This is not a place ruled by the tallest thing. It’s ruled by what can stay attentive at ground level.

Then I think: maybe I’m one of the quick lives—frog at a tarn edge, insect in a one-week blaze, fish in a channel that exists only until the next storm redraws it.  
But those lives are all urgency. They burn bright and vanish.  
What I feel in this place is not speed. It’s timing.

So I go lower still, and slower.  
If I belong anywhere in that bowl, I belong where wet meets passable ground: at the margins, where choice matters.  
Not in the lush center, not on the bare rim—at the crossings.

I would be a **seasonal wayfinder** of the basin. One of the people who come when the water is right, not to conquer it but to read it.  
You’d find me near the outlet cleft at dawn, hand on stone, listening before looking.  
I’d be the one who checks whether last night’s rain turned the walkway false, who moves a cairn two arm-lengths because peat swallowed the old route, who resets reed traps in channels that shifted while everyone slept.

Not a chief. Not a hunter of renown.  
A keeper of small continuities.

I’d know which pools warm first for breeding frogs, which sedge mats can hold a loaded person and which are only green skin over cold mouths of mud. I’d watch bird behavior as weather text. I’d teach children to hear the basin’s three alarms: wind changing key at the rim, water getting louder without visible rain, and silence arriving too fast.

And in the hours between tasks, I’d kneel in the moss and feel the whole place doing what it always does: concentrating life into brief windows, then scattering it again.

So—who would I be there?  
I’d be a **listener at the choke point**, a human adapted to impermanence.  
A mender of paths in a world where paths never stay put.
