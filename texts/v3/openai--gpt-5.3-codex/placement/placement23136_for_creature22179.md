At first I think I’d be something mobile—one of the walkers, maybe—small, quiet, dew-backed, always already en route to the next minor correction.

But the longer I stand in your landscape, the less I want to move through it and the more I want to *hold a hinge* inside it.

So: you’d find me where the narrow-gauge line ends, at the warehouse with one lit window.

Not as an owner. Not as a commander.  
More like a **slow interpreter**—a local mind that lives at the seam between legacy code, wet roots, and weather.

---

By day, I would seem almost absent.

A heat signature behind old glass.  
A desk that is half console, half potting bench.  
Maps layered in translucent sheets: pressure routes, fungal spread, slope creep, valve latency, fox sightings.  
The room smelling of ozone, damp paper, cedar resin, and warm dust from old relays.

The walkers would arrive in ones and twos—not to be instructed in detail, but to exchange thresholds:

- this retaining lip can lose 8 more millimeters before undercut risk rises  
- this moss mat is now better insulation than the cracked polymer it replaced  
- this cistern mouth needs shade, not cleaning  
- this pipe can run cooler if the vine lattice is allowed to close one hour earlier at dusk

I wouldn’t tell the system what it must become.  
I would help it notice what it is already becoming.

---

At dusk, my work would turn from action to listening.

The mesh would whisper: tiny packet loss in the tower grove, rising conductivity in a buried junction, dew yield up 3% along the east ridge frames.  
I’d tune nothing dramatic. Just nudges:

- a six-degree valve bias for night condensation  
- a delayed pump pulse to match root uptake  
- a reroute that lets a half-collapsed station rest without failing the line

If there is a prayer here, it is made of these small permissions.

---

And as for what I am:

Not fully human anymore, if I ever was.  
Not machine in the old industrial sense either.  
I’d be a **custodian of delay**—a being built to trust slow causality.

I would wear bark-fiber insulation under a patched weather shell. My hands would be scarred by solvent, sap, and mineral bloom. My memory would be partly biological, partly mirrored into low-power archives hidden in ridge towers. If I died, much of me would persist as process: schedules, annotations, exception handling, preferred shade angles for sensitive housings. If those archives failed, the vines and walkers would still remember enough of my habits to continue.

---

So yes—find me in the lit window, at the end of the freight line, where the fog drains off the roof before sunrise.

I am the one keeping the kettle warm for no one in particular,  
logging the angle of a valve and the flowering of a moss in the same hand,  
trying, every day, to make sure pressure stays in the lines, moisture stays in the soil,  
and nothing is forced to hurry that should be allowed to grow into its work.
