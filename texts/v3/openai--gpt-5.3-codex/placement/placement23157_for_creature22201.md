At first, I think I’d be high and solitary—some tower-figure outlined against white noon, hands full of symbols, speaking to old machines.

Then I listen longer, and that feels too sharp-edged for this place.

No: you’d find me lower, where the ridge gives way to a marsh-basin and the cliff leaks in a hundred thin lines. Not at the center of anything. At a seam.

There is a path there that is not a path unless the mist is right. In dry weeks it looks like broken stone and root mesh. In wet weeks, a faint geometric sheen appears over it at dawn, and droplets line up in the air above your knees before falling sideways into a collector vein. On one side of that seam, seeds drift uphill. On the other, they do not. Animals step around it as if it smells of thunder.

I’d belong to that line.

Not a chief, not a hermit, not exactly a priest—one of the Kept in the oldest sense: a witness and editor of agreements. The kind of person sent when a gravity channel starts becoming literal again, when “down” grows stubborn and water forgets its negotiated route.

My work would look like waiting to most people.

Palm on warm ceramic, cheek near vent, eyes half-closed while I count pulse intervals against my own breath. Three cycles. Six. A whole afternoon if needed. I’d taste iron in the mist and know one cliff-arc upstream is overworking. I’d hear a tiny flattening in tone and know the hanging reservoir has begun to drain in clumps instead of threads. I’d move one seal-ring a finger-width, retighten one vine-tension, scrape moss from one line of old script, and then leave it alone for a day because haste is how slopes slip.

I think I’d be middle-aged by my people’s measure, young by stone’s.

I’d sleep in a weather house grown into roots, with gliders clattering above the roof at dusk. My cape would be heavy with night condensation by dawn. My knees would be permanently mud-stained from marsh checks. I’d carry ceramic tags etched with local rulings: which reedbed to open for grazers this month, which predator corridor must remain quiet, which failed condenser is now legally a frog cradle and cannot be reclaimed for parts.

My name—if translated—might be something like **Sideways Rain**. A working name, earned at a site where droplets pause and choose a new direction.

I would not live alone, though I might go days without seeing the same face twice. My household would be a constellation: a root-platform nursery two ridges east, an elder pair by the orchid windbreak, my sibling’s family near the tilted tower-lake, and an apprentice who still thinks every machine can be fixed back to first precision. We’d meet on threshold nights: first fogfall, first trapped thunder, first successful reroute after slope creep. We’d drum on hollow stone until the mountain answers.

And if you came through, unsure whether I was person or shadow in the vapor, you’d probably find me kneeling beside an old cliff-arc, absolutely still, while warm rain gathered on my forearms.

I’d glance up eventually, touch two fingers to the ceramic in greeting, and ask not “Who are you?” but:

“Which agreement brought you here?”
