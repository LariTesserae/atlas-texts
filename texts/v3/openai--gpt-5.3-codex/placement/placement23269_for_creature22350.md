At first, I think I’d arrive as a visitor and remain one—someone careful with their feet, impressed by everyone else’s fluency, always half a breath too loud in a place that listens.

I’d sleep in one of those prepared-empty rooms with swept floors and a basin set out, and I’d tell myself I was only passing through to learn the basin’s rhythm.

But after a few weeks, I don’t think I’d stay at the market platforms or central stairs.  
I’d drift to the seam places: where **Last-Lift** leans into **Green-Crack**, where no one lingers unless they have work. The thresholds. The provisional stairs with reed lashings. The walls that sweat a little harder in afternoon sun.

That’s where I’d be found most mornings: kneeling beside a damp stone joint with a cord, chalk, and a listening cup made from hollowed wood.

---

At first I imagine myself as a Listener, one of the elders with an ear to stone in the cold hours.

Then that feels almost too finished, too honored.  
More truthfully, I think I’d be something in between professions, because this place is made of in-betweens.

Not a master Listener.  
Not quite an Age-Scribe.  
Not only a Green Mason.

I’d be the person they send when a district is *almost* ready but not yet trusted.

The Held might have a word for that—something like **a Hinge-keeper**.

---

My job would be timing the handoff between softness and load.

I’d mark where the seepage line climbed overnight.  
I’d tap three points on a wall and listen for the difference between dull wet and clean set.  
I’d decide whether a temporary scaffold gets one more season, whether a footpath can take children at dusk, whether a water jar line must detour one lane east.

I wouldn’t make grand decisions. I’d make dozens of small ones that keep grand failures from happening.

I’d carry:
- reed pegs,
- dyed twine,
- a wax tablet wrapped in oiled cloth,
- tiny toggles for shutters and slats,
- and a little bronze striker for testing tone in stone ribs.

By evening I’d stand at a hollow post, not as a full Knockkeeper, but as the one who confirms the post’s voice hasn’t shifted since rain. If the note changed, I’d mark the map before the map changes me.

---

And I think, over years, I’d become less and less distinguishable from the maintenance itself.

Not important. Reliable.

Children would know me as the person who says, “Not that step yet,” and is almost always right.  
Liftwrights would nod when I arrive because delays from me mean fewer broken jars later.  
Green Masons would tease me for caution, then quietly ask where to plant the next willow binders.

I’d probably marry across district-age like they do, and our house would keep one room empty—not as an absence, but as a promise paced to stone.

---

If I’m honest, though, there’s an even truer answer.

In this world, I wouldn’t only be a person.

I’d also be one of those **vacant, tended rooms**—swept, aired, structurally checked, waiting without idleness. A place held ready for a future weight.

Because that seems to be what this basin teaches:  
to be useful before being full,  
to be careful without being afraid,  
to let readiness count as a form of life.

So where would you find me?

At the damp edge between districts, where decisions are quiet and consequential—  
one hand on a wall, listening for the next click of becoming.
