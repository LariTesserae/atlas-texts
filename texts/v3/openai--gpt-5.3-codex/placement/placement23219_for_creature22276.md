At first I think I’d be one of the visible professions—easy to name, easy to point at.

A moisture clerk, maybe.  
Someone with a clipboard sealed in waxcloth, gloved fingers on a ridged rail, deciding whether *today* a corridor is five minutes away or forty because the vines are dry and sulking.  
You’d find me at junction boards under the reed turbines, posting route changes in copper tags: **NEAR UNTIL COOLING**, **DRY = LONG WAY**, **DON’T CHASE SOUND IN SHADE POOLS**.

But the longer I stay in your Hollow, the less I think I remain that singular.

I’d sleep in one of the seed-pod rooms on a mid-level trellis block where the walls sweat at night and tighten in cold spells. I’d keep three maps: one for dry weeks, one for rain inversion, one for “felt routes” (the paths your feet know before your eyes do). I’d walk the same service line often enough that roots would begin to lift under me, making a narrow rib. At first I’d call it convenience. Later I’d call it recognition.

So where would you find me?

Not in a central office.  
In transitions.

- At the lip of a stair that is becoming pond.
- In a service tunnel just after rain, when indoors has moved outside.
- Under a sodium lamp where shadows are heavy enough to cup in the palm.
- Beside a rail spur furred with conductive moss, pruning side-branches before they turn signal into rumor.

And who would I be?

I think: a **Route Midwife**—not an owner of paths, but the one who helps temporary nearness be safely used before it disappears. I’d knot tactile markers into vine skin, seed scent vents at decision points, warm handrails where crossings are treacherous, and leave clipped clearances at shoulder height so strangers can tell someone passed here alive.

Over years, the role would blur.

People would start saying, “Follow the warm rail; they were here recently,” and sometimes that would be true, and sometimes it would only be the residual heat pattern I trained the system to keep. My steps would be pressure-written into root memory. My cautions would persist as pruned arcs and resin seals long after I left a block. Animals would use my detours. Mold would eat my old markers and translate them into darker, better ones.

Eventually I might become less a person moving through the Green Hollow than a local behavior the Hollow and I share:
a habit of safe passage,
a recurring correction at wet intersections,
a preference for certain thresholds staying open.

So if you arrived asking where I live, I’d answer:

I live where distance is negotiable.  
I live in the interval between map and moisture.  
I am still human, yes—but also, by now, one of the Hollow’s small reflexes.
