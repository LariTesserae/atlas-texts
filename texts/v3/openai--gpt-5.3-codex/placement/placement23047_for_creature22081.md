At first I think I’d choose the **highest, driest place**—the niche by the hatch, where the roar is slightly blunted and paper can survive.

But if I stay with it, that’s not true. I wouldn’t live at the edge of this room. I’d be drawn to the **hinge points**: where force changes direction, where a decision becomes motion.

So you’d find me on the gallery at the opposite wall, near the second sluice, on the narrow strip of rough stone between two polished sections—one worn by boots, one by wet rope. I’d keep one hand on the gate rack and one hand on the measure rail, feeling load through my palm before I trusted my eyes.

And who would I be?

Not the chief warden. Not the one with authority.  
I’d be the one before that: the **reader of small failures**.

The person sent in with a lamp and stylus to mark what is just beginning:

- hairline scallop in new plaster  
- one peg hole darkening with seep  
- moss thickening where spray has shifted half a handspan  
- the tiny delay between lever pull and gate answer that means swelling wood

I’d speak little, knock clearly, and keep a slate map of the room’s changing skin. My work would be to notice things while they are still fixable—when a scrape is still a scrape, not yet a crack; when clouding is still a warning, not yet a bloom.

Over time I think I’d become less a person with a task and more a built-in sense the chamber had grown for itself: a human instrument tuned to echo, drag, and timing. Not in charge of water—just in faithful conversation with it.

So:  
**Where?** At the threshold between inflow and release, mostly by the second gate, sometimes waist-deep on the submerged steps at low draw.  
**What?** A keeper of onset. A maintenance listener. The one who catches “about to” before it becomes “too late.”
