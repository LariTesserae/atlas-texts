At first, I’d think I was one of the obvious people:  
a maintenance hand on the morning shift, sleeves rolled, rag in pocket, the kind who taps a pipe and knows by sound whether it’ll sweat by noon.

But if I stayed longer, I don’t think I’d remain fully human in the usual sense.

You’d find me at thresholds.

Not in the center of a workshop, and not out in the open lots either—at doorframes, awnings, loading-bay lips, stair landings under gantries, the places where people stop half a minute to trade news, argue, smoke, laugh, wait out a burst of rain. I’d be the one repainting chalk marks where damp always gathers, tightening drip chains, wrapping handles, setting little tin cups under the same leak everyone swears was fixed last year. I’d know the social weather more than the sky weather.

And over time, I’d become something the town itself would recognize:  
a **keeper of wet seams**.

Part clerk, part mechanic, part marsh-instinct.  
I’d carry a ledger not for inventory, but for recurrence:

- Dock 3, east post: first bead at second siren.  
- Bench behind valve works: always damp after quarrels.  
- Culvert 9: frogs by dusk if night shift runs late.  
- Willow at south ditch: approved by survival, not by vote.

I’d be trusted because I don’t predict dramatically; I predict usefully.

By night, when the presses fall quiet in staggered surrender, maybe I’d blur further—less person, more distributed attention. A figure in three places at once: checking sump levels by lamp glow, listening to frogs test a new puddle, watching starlings settle on warm wire. Not a ghost. Not a foreman. More like a municipal nerve ending.

So: where would I be?  
In the compact basin, always near a place where metal meets moss and someone has paused long enough to leave warmth.

Who would I be?  
A local species of witness—  
the one who reads darkening pavement like handwriting,  
and keeps the treaty in good repair.
