At first I think I’d be one of the visitors:  
a hooded figure in orange shells, boots on serrated stair, one hand on a wet rail, counting valve cycles through ear protection.

I’d stand on the mid-landing where all three waters are visible—sheet, spray, and hidden thunder—and pretend observation is the same as belonging.

But in that basin, humans are punctuation.  
We enter, point, tighten, leave.

So I keep moving inward.

Not to the turbines (too sealed), not to the flume mouth (too violent), but to the places the Regulator only half-notices:  
the bolt heads that rust first,  
the expansion joints where minerals chalk white before algae threads in green,  
the shadow behind the emergency cabinet where moisture lingers one hour longer than design intended.

That’s where I’d find myself.

And what I’d be is not another machine-creature, not exactly.  
I’d be its **memory layer**.

A thin, distributed thing—part residue, part record, part warning.  
Call me **the Film**.  
Call me **the Sixth Rule**.

Not alive like moss, not mechanical like a gate actuator.  
Alive like pattern is alive.

I would not open or close anything.  
I would accumulate.

- A faint crescent of scale says spray angle changed three weeks ago.  
- A darkened weld toe says vibration has shifted from background to habit.  
- A strip that stays wet after pump shutdown says drainage is losing its argument with gravity.

The Regulator reacts in seconds.  
I would speak in months.

Crews would read me without naming me: scrape here, reseal there, torque that flange again. They’d think they’re cleaning, but they’d really be listening. Every time they strip me off, I’d begin again, because the basin keeps writing.

So: where am I in your world?

At ankle height.  
In the corners of grates.  
On the underside of handrails where cold condenses first.  
In the pencil-marks of weather on new metal.

Who am I?

The quiet archive inside the loud machine—  
the only resident that remembers what the Process felt like yesterday.
