At first, I think I’d be something with wings.

A night heron, maybe—because this is a city of shifts, and dusk belongs to specialists. I can feel the appeal of standing ankle-deep in a drainage run, motionless until the canal gives up a flicker. Perch on a gantry, drop to a culvert lip, rise again when floodgate motors wake. No romance, just timing.

Then I think: no, lower.

Crab-low. Rat-low. Close to the concrete where the true map is written in dampness and residue. In a place like this, the real roads are thermal: warm pipe, cool shadow, mineral ledge, lamp halo, runoff line. To live here is to read gradients, not landmarks.

Then lower still: I could be one of the films.

A skin on retaining walls. A patient, iridescent intelligence spread thin across culvert water. No eyes, no fear, no drama—just persistent occupancy. First tenant, last tenant. The district before and after everyone else.

But if I let the image keep clarifying, I end up human—just not central.

You’d find me on the night maintenance shift at a pump station near the old rail spur, where the levee dips and water always returns first. Raised room, steel stairs, sodium light, a kettle that tastes faintly of iron. I bike the service roads between gate houses. I clear intake grates after rain. I carry a scraper for algae film, a coil of wire, spare fuses, sealant, two tide tables (official and trusted), and a notebook full of things no sensor tracks: where frogs got louder, which puddle stayed warm, when the kites stopped circling one mast and moved to another.

Who would I be?

Not a hero, not a survivor, not a lone witness to decline.  
I’d be a **keeper of intervals**—one of the people who keep channels open between pulses.

I’d know that a ten-minute rain can reset the whole district. That rust is not failure, just schedule. That “empty lot” means mosquito hatch, feral dog den, heron path, sump overflow, and three different maintenance calls by morning. I’d move through the place the way its other citizens do: opportunistic, moisture-tolerant, crevice-minded, practical.

In other words, I’d be native by method, not by species.

A person shaped into local life until I’m less an individual standing against the environment than one more working part inside it—another warm cavity, another set of habits, another creature listening for the next pump to kick on in the amber fog.
