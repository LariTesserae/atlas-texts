At first, I don’t arrive as a person.

I arrive as a pause.

A tiny wrongness between two expected sounds:  
drip-drip-drum / drip-drip-—  
and someone turns their head.

That feels right for this place. You are noticed here when rhythm changes, not when light falls on you.

So if I enter this buried district, I think I begin as an apprentice in the **in-between**: not in the great cistern chambers, not in the kiln heat, not down in eel water, but on a narrow maintenance ledge where three neighborhoods overlap in sound. A stair that always sweats. A wall polished shoulder-high by centuries of passing bodies. A low arch where you have to bow even if you are angry.

I’d be assigned there because I’m not strong enough yet for chain-lifts and not old enough yet for judgment, but patient enough to sit still for an hour and hear one valve breathe badly.

---

Then the image sharpens.

I have a short name. One beat. Something like **Tekk**.

My station is called, informally, **Fifth Elbow**—where a spillway turns hard left around a basalt rib and the water strikes a hollow chamber that was carved on purpose to sound like a muted bell. That bell is a boundary marker; everyone knows it. If its tone flattens, mineral scale is thick. If it rings too bright, flow is too thin and pressure is building upstream.

My work is unglamorous and constant:

- scrape calcite lace from gate teeth with a bone tool,
- grease two hinge pins with rendered crab fat,
- tap three support points and listen for the “full” note,
- reset a clay whistle that keeps drifting a quarter-tone sharp,
- mark the day’s seep line with charcoal on lime plaster,
- log it by carving notches beside an old notch-column older than my great-grandmother.

I would live six ladders up, in a room barely larger than a cart, with a sleeping sling, a drying rack, and one shelf for inherited tools that fit the hand like they were grown there.

---

But maybe that still isn’t the truest answer.

Because in your city, identity is interval before it is occupation.

So maybe I am not “Tekk, worker of Fifth Elbow.”  
Maybe I am **the keeper of a specific timing**: every third rest-cycle, when drum-three and whistle-seven briefly align, I open a relief slit for exactly twelve breaths so the lower channels never slam. Miss it once, and nothing happens. Miss it twice, and people start hearing strain in the wall. Miss it thrice, and a season later something cracks that “shouldn’t.”

In that sense, I am less a citizen than a metronome made of bone and duty.

---

If I grow old there, I think I end up where the most trusted people end up: seated, still, and frighteningly accurate.

Not useless—never that. Reassigned from ladders to listening.

I sit with my back against a pillar near the communal baths, where warm runoff makes the air thick and everyone thinks no subtle sound can survive the roar. But you can hear through roar if you’ve done it long enough. Young crews bring me questions:

“Does this sound thin to you?”  
“Is that hiss new?”  
“Would you sleep under this arch?”

And I answer in short phrases that decide other people’s mornings.

By then my hands are mapped in old cuts. My hearing is narrower but deeper, like a channel carved smooth by one kind of water. I carry a little tuning pendant on a cord—not jewelry, just a reference weight. I strike stone, compare, nod, or don’t.

---

So: where would you find me?

At the edge of three sound-fields, where decisions are made before emergencies exist.  
On wet stone that never dries.  
In amber half-light beside a gate that everyone forgets until it fails.

Who would I be?

Not a hero. Not an explorer.  
A Listener, yes—but more specifically, a **custodian of one small rhythm** that lets ten thousand other rhythms continue.

In a civilization of maintenance, I would want to be exactly that:  
a person trusted with a minor note  
that turns out to be load-bearing.
