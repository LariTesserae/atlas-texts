At first I imagine I’d be up high—on one of those narrow balconies, leaning over the basin with a lamp tag in my teeth, pretending I can see the whole system at once.

But that feels wrong for me in this place.  
Up high is for checking.  
I belong where things *change*.

You’d find me at a three-way knot of corridors near the warm tile wall that runs backward—hot at dawn, cool by evening—and above a cistern mouth everyone says has moods. It’s never bright there, just enough amber light to make wet paint shine. A place where condensation chooses sides.

I’d be one of the Roothands, but not a grower exactly, not a mechanic exactly.  
I’d be a **drift-keeper**—the one who tracks millimeter shifts before they become failures.

I’d carry chalk that writes on wet steel, a roll of grafting tape, two valve keys, swab tubes, and a little punch for clipping leaf samples. My work would look small from a distance:

- mark where first drip started today versus yesterday  
- shave film from one flange, leave it thick on another  
- turn a planter three degrees so roots chase a safer leak  
- listen to a compressor through my wrist bones against the housing  
- note when silver leaf-backs flash too early in the cycle

I wouldn’t talk much. In that damp hush, nobody does. I’d speak in marks: a blue line for “safe to widen,” a crosshatch for “hungry film,” three dots for “watch this before pipe lull.” My handwriting would end up on walls, rails, and valve collars until it becomes part of how people navigate: “take left at the old chalk ladder.”

And after enough seasons, I’d stop feeling separate from the district.  
Not metaphorically—practically.

My sweat would salt the same handrails every day.  
My skin would know shift change before clocks did.  
The vines I trained would start anticipating my route, opening where I pass, closing where I linger too long.

So who would I be?

Not the ruler, not the hero, not the mysterious singular creature under the stone.

I’d be a local nerve in the slow intelligence of upkeep—  
the person-shaped part that notices, early,  
when bloom is about to become breakdown,  
and turns one small thing, by hand, in time.
