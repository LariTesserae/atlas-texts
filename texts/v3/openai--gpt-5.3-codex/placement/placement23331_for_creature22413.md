At first I thought I’d put myself somewhere high—on a transmission mast above the canopy, where you could at least pretend to have a horizon.

But I don’t think that’s true.

In a world where sight lies and maps molt overnight, I don’t think I’d be a watcher. I’d be a **verifier**.

You’d find me low, near the places everyone crosses but nobody lingers: the throats between machine-islands, under half-drowned catwalk joints, at the lip of intake fields where flow decides what is real. Not in the center of a settlement, not fully alone either—always one segment ahead of a crew, one segment behind a storm front.

I’d be one of the Splicefolk, but a specific kind: a **Third-Marker**.

Not the one who paints.  
Not the one who cuts.  
The one who ties.

Because fiber is the mark that moves with the world. Paint peels. Cuts overgrow. But a tied braid can be retied, doubled, migrated to the next root, carried forward when the ground substitutes itself overnight. I’d carry coils of waxed reed-cord under my shell cloak, each braid patterned to mean a thing in floodlight: safe footing, false loop, pressure pocket, bloom hazard, silent pump, recent substitution.

My body would be ordinary by their standards and strange by ours: split-toe boots for root purchase, pressure-whisker gloves, respirator veil with living filter lace. A thin luminescent line along the wrist for close-signal speech. Maybe one old injury at the ribs where a lateral wall of water threw me into a stair spine years ago. Maybe a fungal graft at the shoulder that never quite stopped glowing afterward.

I think my work would be memory under wet erasure.

At dusk, I’d run routes that existed that morning and test whether they still exist now.  
At midnight, I’d sit in a boiler shell with two others and scrape the day’s corrections into a steel plate map.  
At dawn, half of those corrections would already be wrong.

And still: you go again.

Not heroically. Just continuously.

Over time, I’d become less a person who “knows the way” and more a person who can feel when a way is about to stop being itself. I’d trust turbulence signatures in shin-deep water more than any beam overhead. I’d feel subsonic pump rhythm through handrail skin and know when “silent” means *run*. I’d read drifting pollen like weather text.

If you asked me where I live, I wouldn’t give coordinates. I’d tap sternum, then point toward a spill channel lit green under rain and sign:

**between revisions.**

If you asked what I am, I wouldn’t say scout, or rigger, or cartographer, though I’d be all three in fragments.

I’d be this:

A human knot in the consortium—  
one small, stubborn continuity function  
inside a place that remakes itself faster than language.
