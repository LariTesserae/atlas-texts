At first, I think I would be something light.

A kite-shadow crossing the stair roads.  
A raven on a parapet, head cocked toward weather.  
Maybe even wind itself, because wind gets everywhere here and belongs nowhere.

But the longer I stand in your city, the less I believe in being only one thing.

I would begin at a threshold place—where the high terraces hand off to the denser, warmer districts below. Not the very top, not the market heart. A hinge district. A place of steps, drains, and retaining walls that everyone uses and no one romanticizes. The kind of neighborhood that keeps the rest from sliding.

I would sleep above a cistern chamber with three other households and two dogs, in a room that smells like wool, lime dust, and dried thyme. My roof would be patched in six kinds of clay because every storm teaches a different lesson. In thaw season, water would tick in the walls like a second clock.

By trade, I’d say I am a mason when strangers ask.  
But locally, I’d be called something narrower and truer: **a seam-keeper**.

Not builder. Not architect. Seam-keeper—one who watches where separate things meet and begin to fail.

- wall to bedrock  
- channel lip to frost line  
- old mortar to new patch  
- district to district  
- household promise to household promise

In the mornings I’d walk with a tapping rod and a bundle of chalk, listening to stone faces the way others listen to livestock breath. Hollow note? Mark it. Damp bloom where it should be dry? Mark it. Hairline crack opening against the grain? Mark it, then come back with two cousins and a pry bar before night freeze widens it.

By noon I’d trade labor: half a day re-facing a neighbor’s slope wall in exchange for wool felt and one future favor during snow-load month. In late light I’d help drive goats through the gate cut, because no one in this place gets to be pure-specialized for long. At dusk I’d kneel beside the cistern hatch with an elder who can still smell silt before it settles, and I’d learn not to waste their words.

I think I’d keep a private map—not on paper, too costly and too perishable, but in carved notches on a lintel beam near my bed. One notch for each repair that held through winter. A different notch for each one that failed, so pride can’t edit memory.

And if you asked who I *really* am, after years there, I might answer:

I am one pair of hands in a civilization of ongoing corrections.  
I am not the wall; I am the return to the wall.  
I am not the spring; I am the one who notices when it tastes different.  
I am not the city; I am one of the people who keep it from forgetting gravity.

Maybe that is the most accurate form in this world—not hero, not ruler, not wanderer, but a practiced participant in collective attention.

So where would you find me?

On the third stair above the split cistern, at first light, glove off despite the cold, fingertips on stone, listening for the future in a crack no wider than a thread.
