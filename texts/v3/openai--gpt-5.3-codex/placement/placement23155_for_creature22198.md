At first, I thought I’d be one of the walkers between districts—the kind of person who is always a silhouette on a causeway, wrapped in felt, carrying four copies of the same message so truth can survive the distance.

But the longer I stay in your world, the less I feel like a traveler and the more I feel like a **listener**.

So you’d find me in an old amphitheater quarter where the city and geology have stopped pretending to be separate things.  
A place where one wall is carved, the next is natural cliff, and the floor is stitched with black moss channels that only drink on odd days of rain. It sits just below a warm seam of dark stone and just above a basin that periodically forgets which hour it belongs to. Not a prestigious district. A necessary one.

I would live in a narrow, many-doored house built into a fault stair, with no corridor longer than an arm-span line and no room trusted without a morning clap. Every threshold would carry those curved fidelity marks. Every door would complain when moved, on purpose.

My work would have two names, depending who’s asking:

- to officials: **time-slip notary**,  
- to neighbors: **echo gardener**.

I’d keep ledgers, yes—but not just of debts and plantings. I’d keep ledgers of *coincidence*.  
When frost appears under stone in three adjacent terraces.  
When a tower’s south face loses a level at dawn and regains it by dusk.  
When rain enters a cistern before clouds form overhead.  
When one object casts two shadows and everyone pretends they did not see.

I’d be the person called when two true records contradict each other and both have witnesses. I wouldn’t decide what *really* happened. I’d decide what can be safely acted on by morning.

I imagine I’d carry the standard three tools, worn smooth:

- segmented rod for measure-without-inviting-shear,  
- dyed knot-line for boundary curves,  
- a pale sounding stone hung at the sternum.

But I’d also carry a pruning knife and a pouch of graft tags, because in that place law and horticulture are siblings. If a basin slips three hours, the first evidence appears in terraces: reed pith sour too early, wax-leaf closing at noon, black cup fungi fruiting to lightning that hasn’t arrived yet. The gardens testify before people do.

I think I’d belong to a small night cohort that rotates sleep: one resting, one listening, one walking the wall roots with a lamp hooded in resin cloth. We’d talk little. We’d trust cadence more than speech. We’d know each other by how we pause before entering a room with altered echo.

And if you asked not what I do but what I am in that world, the truest answer is this:

I am a **hinge-person**—  
between stone and root,  
between yesterday and usable memory,  
between the map drawn in ink and the map grown in moss.

Not heroic. Not central.  
But in a fraying city, hinges matter more than doors.

So that’s where you’d find me: high above any river, in a district that keeps changing shape politely, kneeling beside a cistern lip at blue dusk, tapping the wall, listening for the note that means *still faithful*, and carving one more short curved mark before night.
