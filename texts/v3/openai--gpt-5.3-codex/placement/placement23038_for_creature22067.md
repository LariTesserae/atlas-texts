At first, I think I’d be something small and peripheral—  
a hand, not a face.

A hand that appears from under an awning, palm cupped to catch roof-sweat, thumb testing the temperature, then tipping it into the right channel without looking. Someone whose work is visible only as *absence*: no overflow, no ice-lip, no snapped root mat, no quarrel at dawn over whose tray dried.

But if I stay with it longer, I don’t think I’d belong to the open lanes. I’d be found where the city listens to itself.

Not in a shrine.  
In a junction.

There would be a stair that curves between a bathhouse wall and an old cistern shaft, stone worn shallow in the center from generations of careful feet. Above it, kitchen steam gathers under hide awnings and rains back down in patient taps. Below it, a throat of cold air rises from stored dark water. The two climates meet there—warm breath and mineral chill—and every living thing tells the truth at that seam: moss color, fish restlessness, dog posture, whether the wall-sheens leave whole arcs or broken script before dawn.

That is where you’d find me.

I’d be one of the people the Held call for when nothing is *wrong* yet, but something has shifted.

A quiet diagnostician.  
A keeper of air-skin.

I’d carry felt-wrapped tools: a bone pick for mineral bloom, a mica strip for reflected glare, braided cord with knots spaced to drip intervals, a small wax tablet scored with old routes. I would spend mornings with children on our bellies at cistern mouths, teaching them how to hear the difference between a healthy drip and a hurried one. Not louder, never louder—just sharper. A fast drip means somewhere above, a seam has opened or a hand forgot to close a slate.

By noon I’d move gallery to gallery, checking how sound travels in the hard white light, tapping posts with knuckles padded in wool, marking where an echo has become too bright, too thin. In another city that might be superstition. Here it is insulation, safety, social peace, all at once.

By evening I’d be in the steam gardens above the kitchens, pruning fern and bitter-leaf not for yield but for breath: dense enough to hold warmth, open enough not to rot. I’d know which family’s moisture route crosses mine in thaw weeks, and we’d barter in maintenance the way others barter in coin—your flue seam for my gutter hour, your goat-silent line for my north-wall cuttings.

Who would I be, exactly?

Not an elder, not young.  
One of the middle hands—the people who keep continuity from fraying.

I think my name there would be short, mostly consonants, spoken close to the ear. Outsiders might translate my role as “hush-keeper,” but the Held would mean something finer: the one who keeps patterns from tearing when the weather turns.

And if you asked me, in that world, what I make, I wouldn’t say tools or food or law.

I’d say:

I make it possible for everyone else to keep making this place.  
I keep the city’s small agreements with water, stone, heat, and each other.  
I mend timing.

If I were not human at all, I’d be tempted by one other form: one of those glassy wall-sheens, writing faint luminous chemistry across the dark before dawn, a message no one owns and everyone reads.

But no—  
I think I am a person, kneeling at first light with a child beside me, both of us watching that brief blue-green trail fade, and knowing exactly which gutter to clear before breakfast.
