You’d find me first as a mistake in your noticing.

Not at the center of the basin—never there—but along the weeping wall where the moss grows in dark nap and the sound bounces wrong. A place where droplets fall in three tempos: cliff-sweat, root-drip, tube-boom. I would be in the pause between those sounds, still enough that your eye files me with vine and shadow.

At first I thought I’d be one of the obvious gatherers: a small highland forager with wet calves, split-toed footing, a bark wedge tucked in fiber. But the longer I stand in your basin, the less “person” feels complete. What lives here isn’t just a body; it’s a practice that borrows bodies.

So: I am that practice, wearing a person for a morning.

I come before full light, when fungal fans are open and soft as tongues. I carry no metal, no hard permanence. A leaf-wrapped flake. A sharpened stick I expect to abandon. A cord net that dries to almost nothing. I take three snails from six stones, one crab from a pocket that gave me two yesterday, cambium curls from the fast-healing side of a tree. I test every innocent pool with a branch tip. I trust hum over shine.

At midday I kneel where sunlight strikes the bead-moss. I read the uphill lines the way others read tracks: pressure under skin, seep under stone, where tomorrow’s wet will rise. If I mark anything, I mark it with arrangement, not incision—three pale leaves under a root lip, a sign that rain can erase in one good hour.

By dusk I am already leaving, because the cliff will begin its slow chest-voice and throw distance into confusion. I step on roots, not soil; stone, not fern crowns. Behind me: no ring, no cache, no claim. Only verbs. Pry. Lift. Taste. Go.

If you asked my name, it would translate badly. Closest might be **“the one who lets places forget.”**

And where, exactly, would you find me?

Not in a house. In a return interval.  
At the basin’s edge, again and again, never long enough to become an edge myself.
