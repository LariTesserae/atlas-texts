At first I’d be on the rim, I think—on the fresh-broken stone where the old world still feels solid.  
A watcher. A note-taker. Dry boots, cautious steps.

But that position wouldn’t last an hour.

In a place like this, the rim is only an introduction. The real life is where firmness starts lying to you—where gravel softens, peat answers, and every footfall is a message sent into the basin. I’d keep moving inward until I reached one of those weak-current pockets behind a slumped bank, where detached growth collects: moss, roots, silt, eggs, larvae, drift.

That’s where I’d find myself, and what I’d be would stop being singular.

Not a fox, not a heron, not a fish.  
I’d be a **raft-organ** of the marsh-animal: a floating sedge-and-moss island, briefly anchored, then torn free, then anchored again. A modular piece of its metabolism.

I’d do small, unglamorous, essential work:

- baffle flow during melt pulses  
- catch nutrients and carcass fragments  
- shelter fry and tadpoles in my underside shadow  
- host insects for one calm hour before wind strips them  
- thicken with roots until I become “land,” then break and move when load exceeds hold

In other words, I’d be one of the basin’s temporary thoughts:  
an idea it keeps having in different places—**attach, filter, grow, release, repeat**.

So where would you find me?  
Not at the center, not at the edge—at the **hinges**, where water decides what can stay.

Who would I be?  
A small, drifting part of a larger intelligence with no skull, doing shift work in the cold.
