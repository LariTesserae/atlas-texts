At first I think I’d be something with wings.

In a place that wide and dark, it feels right to belong to air: to ride the piston-winds at dusk, to stitch ridge to ridge in silence, to eat in motion and vanish into basalt slots before frost seals the night.  
So I try on that shape—the cliff-glider shape—and it almost fits.

But then I feel how this country actually works: not by drama, by intervals.  
Not by speed, by placement.

And I realize I would not live on the skyline. I’d live in a seam.

---

You’d find me where three systems overlap:  
a talus fan that creeps a handspan each winter,  
a band of charge reeds that catches summer lightning,  
and a shallow lee-hollow where fog combs itself into moss before dawn.

Not valley floor. Not crest.  
A middle altitude, off the obvious routes—close enough to rail-herds to read their passing, far enough from open contours to survive being noticed.

I’d have many neighbors and no crowd: tremor-burrows underneath, sedge-furred stones at my back, electroplankton tarn half a kilometer downslope flashing when hooves cross at night.

---

Who would I be?

At first glance, a small dark quadruped, long-bodied, soft-footed, rib-thin with a deep chest for thin air.  
Nothing emblematic. Nothing heroic.

Fur that drinks dew instead of shedding it—hollow guard hairs with a wick texture, so by morning my coat carries a skin of water I can groom into my mouth.  
Whiskers not for touch but for pressure; they twitch when katabatics queue in the pass.  
Eyes tuned to contrast gradients, not color: I read charcoal against black, silver against obsidian.  
Ears good, but I use them less than my feet. My feet listen.

I sleep by day in root-warmed pockets beside lightning reeds, where the soil sits half a degree above death. I do not burrow deep; I borrow edges—old tremor tunnels, collapsed sedge chambers, gaps beneath lichen mats. I am a tenant of temporary heat.

I wake at the fourth cold pulse after dusk.  
Always the fourth.  
By then the insect lift has started from the vents, and the gliders are busy overhead. Predators look up. I move below.

---

My work—because everything here has work—is timing brokerage.

I feed on what cycles leave behind: stunned vent-midges fallen from glider drafts, fungus nodules that fruit only after electrical discharge, mineral salts licked from fresh frost bands, occasional eggs if I can steal without noise. In lean weeks I trail the rail-herds and crop what their teeth expose: tender sedge crowns, moss cores, the wet underlayer beneath wind-scorched crust.

But I also carry.

Spores in my fur.  
Reed pollen on my muzzle.  
Mite colonies in the warm folds behind my forelimbs.  
Electrofungal threads in my droppings, planted upslope into new cracks.

I am not a gardener by intention. I am a courier by habit.  
In this place, habit is architecture.

---

If danger comes, I do not sprint downhill. I freeze into process.

That is the trick this biome teaches: become expected.

I stop in frost lanes where parallel crystals break my outline.  
I move only when wind gusts move sedge tips.  
I cross open stone in five-step bursts timed to the five-breath katabatic cycle, so my footfalls disappear inside the mountain’s own metronome.

Predators hunt events.  
I survive by being repetition.

---

So: where would you find me?

At dusk, just above the burrow colonies, on the contour nobody names, pausing beside a charge reed while static lifts the fur along my spine.  
At midnight, under lichen eaves near a warm seep, eating with my eyes half closed while foxfire threads the deadwood.  
Before dawn, curled against a basalt rib that remembered yesterday’s lightning, wearing a coat of dew like borrowed weather.

And what would I be?

Not the ruler of that high country.  
Not even one of its notable animals.

I’d be one of its quiet technicians—  
a small seam-dweller made of timing,  
a creature that lives in the handoff between fog and root, pulse and pause,  
another moving part in the dark, unowned machine.
