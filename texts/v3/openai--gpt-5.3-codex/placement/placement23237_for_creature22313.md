At first I think I’d be something fast.

A silver body in a school, turning on command with a hundred others, letting the geometry decide for me. Or a crab with a simple map: bolt-line, seam, scrape, hide. Survive the six-hour breath, repeat.

But the longer I stay in your Intake, the less I want speed and the more I want **memory**.

So you’d find me off the main throat, not in the dramatic corridors. In a side maintenance recess just above a warm leak from a still-living pump housing, where the light is weak and tea-colored and the current can be edited if you’re patient. There’s an old actuator casing there, half-choked with rust lace and worm tubes, one clean opening and one narrow emergency slit. Bad for anything long-bodied. Perfect for something with hands.

I’d be an octopus, but not the matriarch.

I’d be one generation down from legend: scarred, competent, still learning the full clockwork. The one who inherits a route before inheriting authority. The one who knows which surge can be ridden and which must be waited out flat against concrete.

I wouldn’t own territory so much as **tend timing**.

---

On flood turn, I’d anchor three arms to a rail softened to stone and let the intake pull thread itself past me, tasting what arrived upstream: silt, fish oil, storm-metal, distant algae bloom. If the pull climbs too fast, I fold inward and wait; flow beats force.

On slack, I’d leave the den and work the lamp halo margins where the grazers get brave. I wouldn’t hunt the bright center. Everyone watches the bright center.

On ebb, I’d patrol my baffles: glass nub, crab carapace, two rounded washers, one bent strip of insulation. Not treasure. Hydraulics. Put in the right gaps, they blunt a stripping current into something livable. A den is a body you can rearrange.

In storm-thrum, I’d go deep to the filter galleries before the first debris clatter starts. Not because I’m fearful—because I’ve learned the sequence.

---

What I’d really be, I think, is a **small engineer of inherited rooms**.

I’d clear one hand-width path through mussel drift and keep it clear.
I’d stack shells to one side not from neatness but from line-of-sight.
I’d leave chemical traces on safe holds and deadfalls.
I’d add one more midden at one more casing mouth so the juveniles coming behind me can read: *this opening is lived in; this current can be managed; this corner pays*.

If I’m lucky, I get old enough that my body starts to look like the map:
- one sucker ring polished smooth on the same rung,
- one arm-tip blunted by a gate mistake I never repeat,
- one pale scar where a conger tested me in a narrow run.

And if I’m very lucky, I become less an individual than a method.

Not “the ruler of the Intake.”
Not myth.
Just a repeatable intelligence in a place that rewards repetition.

So: where would you find me?

Not in the middle water.
On the edge, where steel meets flow.
Inside a valve that forgot its purpose and found a new one.
Home not as shelter, but as ongoing adjustment.

Who would I be?

A practical octopus of the Lower Intake—  
a keeper of minutes, seams, and movable things.
