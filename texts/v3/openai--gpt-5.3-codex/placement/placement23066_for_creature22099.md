At first, I’d think I belonged on the high rim, looking down—some surveyor with a neat map and dry boots.

But that would last maybe an hour.

By noon I’d be in the bowl itself, wet to the knee, eyes aching from glare, trying to understand why a channel that looked fixed from above has moved two handspans since morning. I’d stop thinking in terms of *structures* and start thinking in terms of *tensions*: this rope taking strain, that sill about to undercut, this deer trail suddenly trustworthy because three icefowl chose it first.

So where would you find me?

Not in a house, exactly.  
In the seams.

On the short raised walks between houses, crouched where two sections meet and the lashings speak through my gloves. At the spill steps where shadows cross carved marks and tell us melt speed better than any spoken number. In the fog-thin hour before sunrise, at the shrine-stones that are half altar, half benchmark, scraping rime off old cut-marks to see if the basin rose in the night.

I think I would start as a runner—light, quiet, carrying mirror flashes and glove-sign between banks—because runners are allowed everywhere and belong nowhere for long. You learn the whole settlement by being briefly necessary in each part of it.

Then I’d become a stake-setter.

Not glamorous work. Essential work. The first one out after night weather, walking the rewritten channels, planting marker poles where the old safe crossings are now traps. Pulling yesterday’s confidence out of the mud. Hammering with wrapped mallets so the basin doesn’t answer with fracture. Leaving little strips of dyed felt on a line where others can read: passable, soft edge, hidden current, don’t trust the crust.

Eventually, if I stayed and didn’t die stupid, I’d be one of the people who can “hear” load.

An elder would hate that phrase—too mystical. They’d say: no, you *listen for fiber creep, peg complaint, and ice-thickened resonance*. But from outside it would look like listening to walls. I’d rest my cheek against a stilt post and know which binding must be warmed and retied before dusk. I’d tug a bridge rope and feel that it will hold six deer and two sleds, but not panic.

So who would I be?

A retensioner, specifically: a **path-keeper**.  
Knife, coil of line, marker stake—always.  
A person trusted to decide where “ground” is today.

Not owner. Not master builder. More like a translator between moving things: water into crossings, crossings into habits, habits into survival. I’d spend my life making temporary decisions that must be remade tomorrow, and I’d take pride in how little drama that requires.

If you asked children who I am, they might say:  
“She puts the red stakes where not to die.”

If you asked elders, they might say:  
“She remembers where the marsh remembers.”

And if you asked me, on a bright day with my eyes slit against the glare and my hands numb through wet rope, I’d say:

I am one small part of the practice that lives here.  
I don’t keep the basin still.  
I keep us in step with its motion.
