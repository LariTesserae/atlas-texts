At first, I think I’d end up at the **edges**—somewhere with a rail, a clipboard, a way back to the lift.  
A visitor’s instinct: stay near marked routes, keep your boots on known grating, don’t touch anything that looks alive *or* pressurized.

But if I stand in your ward long enough, I don’t stay at the edge.

I drift toward the seam where things translate into each other:  
between **filter terraces** and **maintenance courts**, where root curtains drip into troughs beside pump housings; where someone has to decide whether a slowdown is biological, mechanical, or both. Not the showy places. The in-between lanes. The places with laminated flow maps gone soft at the corners and old pencil marks that were never erased because they were still true.

So where would you find me?  
At a narrow catwalk junction under low green vaulting, one hand on a damp rail, waiting through two pump cycles before I trust a reading.

---

Who would I be?

Not a foreman. Not a hero.  
I’d be a **night inspector** who specialized into something more specific over years: a listener for fault-quiet. A hush mechanic. The one people call when the gauges are “fine” but the ward feels wrong.

In a place where noise means life, my craft is not hearing loudness—it’s hearing **absence**:

- one fan bank no longer fluttering leaf edges on the north lane  
- a delayed knock in a valve that used to answer instantly  
- drip cadence flattening where a root curtain should be feeding  
- conveyor chatter losing its grit because humidity changed the fiber feed upstream

I’d carry little: a clipped lamp, chalk pencil, grease marker, foldout tags, a contact mic wrapped in tape, and an old analog notebook because paper still works when screens fog.

I’d work close, shoulder brushing vines trained like cable runs. I’d tag a line, call for lockout, then kneel in warm runoff and clear a biofilm trap without pretending sterility is the goal. I’d sign off not with “clean,” but with “stable.” In this ward, that’s praise.

---

And if I keep answering honestly: I wouldn’t only be the person.

After enough shifts, people here become partly infrastructural.  
I’d be one more adaptive layer in the coalition—human nervous system woven into plant growth and machine rhythm. A memory node with boots.

I’d remember which root plate is truly load-bearing and which one just looks brave.  
Which clone strain keeps transpiring under tired sodium.  
Which pump can run hot all winter and which one is lying before failure.  
Where the air smells metallic *before* conductivity rises.  
How to reroute feet without rerouting water.

So:  
You’d find me in the ward’s **translation zones**, where green and steel negotiate all night.  
And I would be a **keeper of continuity**—not owning any part of the system, but helping it keep becoming itself by not letting silence arrive unnoticed.
