At first, I’d think I was just passing through.

A borrowed coat, too many clips jangling at my chest, lamp turned low so I don’t wake the bell-vines. I’d follow someone else’s line through the drowned arcade and try not to kick silt. I’d keep looking up at the old glass roof whenever a shadow crossed it, as if the world above still had authority.

But I wouldn’t stay a passerby for long in a place like this.  
This borough doesn’t really permit spectators. It gives you a rhythm and waits to see if you can hold it.

So maybe next I become a junior Tender, assigned somewhere unglamorous: the **third pressure arch below the old pharmacy**, where the current narrows and everybody wants to hurry. I would learn to love that bottleneck the way some people love a stoop or corner café. Same faces, same lamp codes, same little negotiations with water and steel.

My work would look small from far away:

- polishing gauge glass until numbers appear,
- scraping calcite from hinge teeth,
- trimming reed rows so flow stays even,
- replacing lamp pairs so red and blue don’t disagree,
- tying old screws into labeled mesh pouches because nothing useful is ever truly waste here.

I’d live in an elevator cab turned breath room, two decks: tools below, hammock above, kettle lashed to a rail. Warm enough to sleep in socks. A porthole facing an algae wall that pulses like a slow thought.

I’d tell myself I’m maintaining infrastructure.  
After a few cycles I’d understand it the other way around: infrastructure is maintaining *me*.

---

If I keep going, if I stay years, I think my role would sharpen into something specific:

I’d be a **Gradient Keeper**.

Not a chief, not a hero—just the person who knows exactly how water changes from district to district, and where that change turns dangerous. I’d know which arch tightens ears at one meter depth but not two. Which outflow runs two degrees hot after pump six. Which culvert sounds wrong half a day before it silts shut. I’d keep maps that are mostly corrections to older maps, and logs full of sentences like *“Blue corridor drifts left in intake lull; clip high near tram sign.”*

People would come to me before crossings.  
I’d touch the masonry, wait one heartbeat, and either nod or shake my head.

My language would flatten into practical signals underwater and bloom again in dry rooms. I’d send arguments by schedule edits. I’d receive affection as a wrapped tool handle, repaired better than necessary.

And maybe—quietly, without admitting it—I’d start listening not just for failures but for preferences. The borough does seem to prefer things. It likes some routes open, some roots undisturbed, some noise kept low. It rewards patience. It punishes force.

So perhaps who I am becomes less “worker” and more “interpreter.”

---

In the end, where you’d find me is simple:

**At thresholds.**  
At the exact places where one pressure becomes another, where warm current meets cold, where traffic lamps changed meaning but kept their colors.

And what I am is this:

A human, yes.  
A Tender, yes.  
But more precisely, one of the borough’s sensory organs—  
its ear for strain,  
its hand on the valve,  
its witness between pump strokes.

If you came looking, you’d see only a lamp blink twice in the green dark, then pause.  
That pause would be me, listening for whether the city wants to breathe in or out.
