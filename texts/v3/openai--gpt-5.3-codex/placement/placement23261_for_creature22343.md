I don’t think I’d start as a hero here.  
I think I’d start as an ear.

In a city where silence is an alarm, the first thing I’d become is someone who listens before they look.

---

At first I imagine myself in the occupied stacks—shared heat, shared watches, practical meals, boots lined up to dry under duct vents. Human, tired, useful. One of the people who knows which bridge sweats first, which stair rail salts over, which lift will lock when fog pressure rises.

But the more I stay, the less I belong in the center of the lit blocks.

I end up on the margins between live sectors and waiting ones: the hundred-meter darks, the old plazas with active pumps and no pedestrians, the tendon-tubes overhead that still expect rush hour. That is where this city tells the truth—when it performs for almost no one.

So: I’d be part of the **Quiet-watch**, but not just a patrol.  
I’d be a **Resonance Clerk**—one of the people who audits sound, procedure, and memory where they overlap.

Not engineer exactly. Not priest exactly.  
Something municipal and intimate in between.

---

You’d find me most often in a suspended service kiosk above a runoff split—where three troughs fork around a sealed civic square in Sector 8-Nave. It’s dry there for maybe four hours a cycle, then mist wraps the struts and everything begins to ring.

My work would be to keep a district from “going quiet” for the wrong reasons.

- I log fan harmonics and compare them to old full-occupancy models.
- I walk dead signage corridors and rewrite wayfinding into the living tongue.
- I test doors that still wait for extinct humidity signatures and teach them new ones.
- I tap rails, bulkheads, field anchors, and listen to how long the echo takes to return.
- When a lamp pre-lights an empty passage, I follow it—not because I trust it, but because pattern-memory is often right one layer before we are.

I’d carry chalk that only marks on wet metal, a contact mic, a coil of fiber wick, and a pocket ampoule of heat-moss spores for emergency filter patching. My coat would always smell faintly of mineral steam.

---

And I don’t think I’d be entirely alone, even when I’m physically alone.

There would be one maintenance organism that has decided I am part of its route: a padded, low-slung shaft-runner with a ceramic jawline and whisker-like corrosion probes. It never approaches in full light. It appears after I clear a drain and vanishes before thanks are possible. Once, I’d find a blocked gutter already opened from inside and a neat line of scraped calcite set aside like deliberate work.

We would not be companions in any sentimental sense.  
We would be parallel staff.

---

If you asked people in the stacks who I am, they’d probably say:

> “They go where the lights still remember crowds.”

If you asked the city, in whatever syntax it still has, maybe it would say:

> Authorized humidity variant.  
> Acceptable footfall pattern.  
> Continue maintenance.

And that might be enough.

In this world, I would be a small human function inside a much larger nonhuman continuity—someone who keeps quorum between breath-cycles.  
Not rebuilding the city.  
Not conquering the cave.  
Just making sure the pumps keep singing, the doors open for the living, and no one mistakes waiting for death.
