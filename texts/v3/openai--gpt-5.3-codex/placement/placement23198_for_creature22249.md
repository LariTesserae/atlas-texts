At first, I think I’d be easy to find: on that central platform with the green-nailed boards, standing still and pretending to understand everything.

But that’s just where newcomers stand.

If I stayed, the place would pull me off the platform and down to its edges—where things leak, chatter, hesitate.

---

I don’t think I’d be a master keeper. Not one of the old sure-handed people who can hear backflow in their sleep. I’d be lower in the guild, closer to the machine’s throat: a **hinge-listener**, a walker of seams.

I’d live in a loft just above old flood marks, in a room that smells of wet rope and lime dust. I’d sleep beside a rack of tools that aren’t really mine, just inherited shapes worn to everyone’s grip: cedar mallet, bone wedge, handspike polished by palms, spool of shell-cord rattles.

Before dawn I’d make rounds while condensation still falls in clear rhythms.  
Tick from beam three: normal.  
Fast tick under the west lintel: blocked lip.  
No tick at all at the south grate: either dry (unlikely) or choked (bad).

My job would be less “fixing” than **translating**. Frogs throw one kind of warning, boards another, birds another. I’d learn which white-throated screamer means hawk, and which means pressure spike. I’d learn that when the broad-backed toads leave the low spillway all at once, a release gate upstream is about to shudder open. I’d carry those readings to the others, not in numbers but in knocks, shell-rattle patterns, chalk marks on post faces.

---

Or maybe even that is too human.

Given enough seasons, maybe I become something the place makes out of me: bent, damp, patient, less a person than a moving sensor. Someone children from elsewhere would call a marsh-spirit because I never seem dry and always arrive just before a gate starts to hammer.

I’d have names for water moods:
- rope-water (heavy and slow),
- knife-water (narrow, fast, metallic sound),
- milk-water (fog-thick, blind),
- ladder-water (rising in little regular climbs along brick seams).

I’d trust paws and wings as much as instruments. If fox-hunters abandon a ridge, I don’t step there. If insects suddenly lift from one warm plank in a column, I check the sump below it first.

---

If I had one fixed place, it wouldn’t be the center. It would be a narrow run between two basins where an old statue foot has cracked, and blind crabs come out when fog condenses enough to trickle down the stone. That seam would tell me nearly everything: temperature shift, pressure drift, mineral creep, how long since the last proper flush.

I’d sit there at dusk with my feet on wet tile, listening to the chorus phase-shift as predators take their turns, and I’d understand that “alive” here means “in time.”

So who would I be?

Not ruler, not hermit, not hero.  
I’d be one joint in the hand.  
One ear in the wall.  
A keeper of intervals in a place that survives by being heard.
