At first, I’d think I live in the obvious human place:  
the canteen block at shift change, where steam ghosts out of the vents and people peel off gloves with stiff fingers.  
A corner table under a buzzing light.  
A mug gone lukewarm before I can finish it.

But if I stay with the basin longer, I don’t end up indoors.

I find myself on the service path that runs the north retaining wall—where moss stays wet even in a dry week, where lamp cables sweat, where the ditch grade is just imperfect enough that water hesitates, gathers, and decides where to go next. Not central, not dramatic. A seam. A listening place.

And what I am changes with that location.

Not a foreman. Not a driver. Not one of the big loud organs.

I’m one of the Throughput’s small senses:  
the **walker of lines**—the person (or thing) that reads the basin by moisture, vibration, and timing. I check culverts before dawn, knock rime from sensor housings, clear bark paste from grates with a hooked bar, log seep migration in a grease pencil notebook that never fully dries. I know which slope will slump two centimeters by spring, which handrail beads first before freezing rain, which fan note means a bearing is beginning to go.

I work mostly in the blue half-light when floodlamps are still pretending to be stars.  
I move between poles of noise, and I can tell where I am by frequency alone: debarker thunder to the west, dryer roar underfoot, shunter clangs like iron bells through fog. Ear protection on, hood up, shoulders hunched into mist. Communication by glove, chin tilt, flashlight arc.

In the official chart, I’d be “drainage and instrumentation tech.”  
In the organism, I’m closer to proprioception—its balance sense.  
I keep it from forgetting gravity and water.

Because in this basin, failure begins quietly:  
a ditch icing shut, a berm drinking too much thaw, a cable tray sweating into a junction box, a culvert mouth furred over with night frost and chip dust. Big breakdowns are born from small wet facts no one heard over the machinery. I hear them anyway.

So where would you find me?

At the hinge points: culvert mouths, pump houses, under-conveyor shadows, ladder cages slick with hoarfrost, the narrow strip behind bunk modules where wind dies and fungus starts. I am most myself with one boot in mud and one on grated steel, writing numbers while ravens argue over the spoil ridge.

Who would I be?

A human, yes—but also a function.  
A minor nerve in the Throughput.  
The one that notices when the basin’s damp pulse skips, and puts a hand on the wall until it steadies again.
