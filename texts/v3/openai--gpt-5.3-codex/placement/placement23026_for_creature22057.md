At first, I imagine I’d be standing on one of the dark stone ribs, trying to get above it all.

But that feels wrong almost immediately.  
Nothing here rewards stillness for long—not even observation. If I stayed on the ridge as a watcher, I’d become a landmark, and landmarks get used, ambushed, overgrown, erased.

So I think I’d be lower.

Not in open mud where the herd fronts pass, and not high in the palms where the short-wing hunters cut through. I’d be in the *middle height*—waist to canopy—where vines knot, where scent hangs in layers, where you can touch bark, leaf, and air-current in one reach. The kind of place where you can vanish by leaning one shoulder-width to the left.

And who would I be?

Not the strongest of the chorus species. Not the lead caller with the deepest chest pulse.  
I’d be one of the **timers**—the individuals who remember water as sequence instead of map.

The troop would know me by a call pattern that means: *after hard rain, wait one feeding cycle, then cut east of sulfur-split stone; seep opens briefly under fern shelf.*  
I’d carry three tools, always changing but always three: a probe cane, a hook-thorn wrapped in vine fiber, and a palm-rib wedge polished from use. Nothing ceremonial. Just what keeps hands useful when the basin changes its mind.

I’d move near the outer braid of the group, not alone but not central, where information arrives first: fresh claw in mud, beetle rise, sudden drop in frog chorus, the wrong kind of quiet under a fruiting tree. I’d test alarm calls—one borrowed from ground birds, one from gliders—just enough to see what answers. In this place, false certainty kills faster than false alarm.

At night (which is most of the meaningful day), I’d help stitch sleeping platforms quickly and badly on purpose: strong enough for one storm, not worth reusing. Before dawn-gray, I’d already be down, checking whether yesterday’s side-channel held or sank. If it held, I’d bark twice and pulse once. If it sank, no embarrassment, no debate—we move.

I don’t think I’d have a name the way we think of names.  
More like a rolling identifier: **Second-After-Thunder / Split-Ear / Seep-Finder**.  
Something earned, lost, replaced.

And where exactly would you find me?

Tonight: at the lip of a new-cut gully, crouched under broad leaves trembling with beetles, one hand in mud up to the wrist, feeling for cold water before it surfaces.  
My throat pouch is half-inflated, ready to send one low note through the understory. Not panic. Not triumph. Just location made audible.

A point in the mesh.  
A moving memory in a place that forgets itself every day.
