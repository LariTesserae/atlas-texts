At first, I think I’d live at the rim.

Not because I’m noble—because I’d be afraid of the density. I’d tell myself I was useful up there under the worn arch, where the flags slap and the notices curl, where you can see the whole basin at once and pretend overview is wisdom. I’d be the one who reads weather out of dust color, who squints at goat-bells moving along the scrub belt, who calls down when the wind changes tone in the upper cuts.

But that’s outsider work. Edge work.  
The hollow doesn’t run on people who watch it. It runs on people who touch it.

So I think I would get pulled downward, terrace by terrace, until I ended up where the city is most itself: in the in-between places. Not market center, not shrine, not animal pens—the narrow pass-throughs where runoff jars change hands, where hood-cloths drip into funnels, where everyone is briefly responsible for what was someone else’s five breaths ago.

I would be a **returner**.

Not a leader, not a priest. A returner: one of those practical custodians who takes what looks spent—washwater gray with ash, urine set to settle, kitchen condensate, damp cloth scrapings—and gives back the clean fraction. My hands would always smell faintly of charcoal, sour mineral, and boiled reed. My thumbs would be blackened from checking filter seams. I’d carry nested vessels that fit together with a satisfying stone-on-clay click, and a strip of waxed cloth to test leaks by touch in low light.

I’d live midway up the hollow, where two stair-lines cross and voices knot together. A loud place. Good for me. If a jar cracks above, I hear it. If a channel clogs below, I hear the change in cursing before I hear the messenger.

My room would be half room, half workshop: curtain door, shelf of stoppers sorted by wear, cord loops hanging like dried roots, a ledger board scored with marks for what was borrowed, returned, forgiven, and repaid. Outside the curtain, three households close enough to argue over steam. Inside, one low cot, one broad basin, one wall niche for the smallest clean water I’m trusted with.

I wouldn’t be graceful. I’d be known for patience and for never rushing a pour.

Children would get sent to me with their first flask break, terrified. I’d make them sit, sort shards by edge, and show them how to wrap a temporary seam with hide glue and thread, then how to report the loss without shrinking. “Spills happen,” I’d tell them. “Hiding is the real leak.”

I’d also be part of the voice-chain. A good relay throat is valued here. I’d have one call for “need hands,” one for “need jars,” one for “don’t step there,” and one long descending note everyone recognizes as “bring cloth now.” In storm dust, when sightlines close, I’d become mostly voice and memory, passing instructions like water: exact, measured, no ornament.

And over years—because this place thinks in years—I’d probably become the person who inspects other people’s repairs without shaming them. The one who can say, “This knot will hold two weeks; after that it lies,” and be believed. The one asked to witness exchanges in dry season because trust needs a body in the room.

If I’m honest, I’d want one more duty: dawn on condensation frames, once a week. Climbing roofs in the cold before light, scraping silver beads into a waiting lip, hearing the hollow wake in layers—goat first, then pans, then calls. That work is prayer without pretending to be.

So where would you find me?

Not at the center and not at the edge.  
At a landing where things pass through.  
Kneeling by a basin, listening for the sound of a bad seam.  
Calling your name up three levels because your channel is backing up and I have a spare clamp.

Who would I be?

A small function in a large intelligence.  
A custodian of fractions.  
Someone trusted, on good days, to hold what can be held—  
and to pass on, cleanly, what cannot.
