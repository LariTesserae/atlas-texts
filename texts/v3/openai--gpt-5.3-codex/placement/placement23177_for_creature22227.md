At first I imagine myself as the obvious thing: a surface intruder at the slit entrance, blinking, thirsty, romantic about the green.

But this chamber has no use for romantics.  
It sands them down fast.

So if I truly belong there, I think I would be found deeper in—at the edge of the loudest vent-line, where the floor has been swept into deliberate crescents and the air presses against your chest like a steady hand. Not in the center of a clan circle, not at a hunting path. Slightly aside. Where maintenance happens.

I would be one of the Tuned who tends **drift**.

Not a chief, not a shaman in the surface sense. More like a keeper of tiny corrections. The kind of person everyone forgets is working until one day they stop, and then larvae fail to harden and two feeding paths cross and the whole chamber starts to wobble.

My name would not be a word. It would be a two-tone interval with a dry click at the end—something easy to carry through vent roar without being confused for alarm. Young once, then middle-aged for a long time, hands permanently dusted pale from shale powder. I would sleep in the half-ring shard bed with sternum against stone, learning the chamber’s fundamental in my bones each night so I can hear, by morning, if it has shifted by even a breath-width.

You’d find me at dawn-vent peak with a pumice cup and a bundle of frond knives:

- trimming resin fronds into tighter spirals so they whistle in phase,  
- re-angling a hanging mat half a finger-width to soften a harmful overtone near egg shelves,  
- moving bone pegs from one crack to another because a wall film has thickened and changed the reflection,  
- tapping root nodules for solvent, then sealing cuts with powdered shale and oil so the plant does not “bleed out” into silence.

I wouldn’t hunt often, but I would know where the ribbon-predators turn. I wouldn’t raise the loudest songs, but I would tune the songs others sing—especially grief songs, because grief likes to go sharp, and sharpness can fracture more than hearts in a place like this.

If illness came, I’d be called before the strongest healer: to listen first. Is the fever a body failing, or a sleeping shelf now outside its pressure node? Is the child coughing because lungs are weak, or because a new berm is reflecting a biting harmonic into their alcove? In this world those are the same category of question.

I think that is who I’d be because this chamber rewards patience over force, iteration over conquest. I am, temperamentally, not the strike but the adjustment before the strike; not the revelation, the calibration.

And maybe—because no world stays perfectly still forever—I would also be the one who notices the first true new thing.

Not a collapse. Not an invasion.  
A new overtone.

A seed that splits at two neighboring pitches instead of one.  
A child whose chest plates can hold dissonance longer without pain.  
A way to braid cord that dampens tremor in one season and amplifies it in another.

I would be the person who hears that and does not call it heresy or miracle too quickly. Who tests it with pegs and frond-cuts and patient nights. Who asks, in the Tuned way: does this keep the chamber alive?

So: where would you find me?

Kneeling in mineral dust beneath the third vent tooth, one palm flat on warm stone, the other holding a shaved frond strip to the air, listening for whether the room accepts the change.
