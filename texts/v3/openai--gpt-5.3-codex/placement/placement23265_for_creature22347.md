At first I think I’d be something obvious: a person in a pod under a ledge, waking before dawn to harvest water from the node skins, checking storm bands over the escarpment, walking the old loop-paths with a tool roll and a shade chart.

But the longer I stand in your basin, the less “single” I feel.

I think I’d live on a saddle between two drainage lines—close enough to a slender tower to hear its almost-sound through my teeth, far enough from it that the mornings still belong to birdskin gliders and loopers. My work would begin in the silver hour: reading where condensation formed, where it failed, where microbial films took hold, where a burrow colony opened a new breathing shaft. I would map not territory, but timing.

By noon I’d disappear, like everything wise here. In deep shade, I’d tune small things: recalibrate a boundary field so a migration bends instead of breaks; release a sediment swarm to stitch a gully lip before the next compact storm; retire a damaged platform and score its surface so moss can claim it in three wet seasons instead of ten. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that announces itself.

And slowly, I think, I’d realize I’m not only the one doing this. I’d be part of the caretaker mind’s fringe—an embodied process, a local hand in a distributed intelligence. Not a ruler, not a technician in the old sense. More like a translator between rates of change: animal-fast, storm-fast, stone-slow, code-slower.

So: where would you find me?

At edges.  
At the hour before light hardens.  
At the one gap left in a filterer ring.  
At the place where a ruin is becoming habitat.

Who would I be?

One of those who keep intervals open—  
a walker, a tuner, a listener—  
a human-shaped pause in a bright, weathered system that survives by never trying to fill the whole world.
