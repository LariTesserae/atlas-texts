At first, I think I’d choose wings.

A swift, maybe—stitched to the wall-light, cutting loops through the dim where everything is close and nothing is far. Then I think bat: warm-backed, rain-shield leaf in claw, hanging in the breathing folds where heat climbs from the vents. Then crow at the rim, with a stone in my beak and a practiced crack I return to like a workshop.

But if I stay honest to your basin, I don’t think I’d be any single animal for long.

You’d find me on a **middle ledge above a fumarole seam**, where cool drip and warm breath meet. Not at the rim, not on the floor: in that traffic layer where calls cross, guano dust lifts, insects climb, and everyone passes through eventually. A chokepoint. A listening post.

And what I’d be is this: **a keeper of rhythms**.

Maybe embodied as a hyrax sentry one season, a nightjar the next, a crow juvenile in another generation—but really, the same role wearing different bodies: the one that notices if a drip stops, if a call band shifts an hour early, if beetles reroute, if the vent cools by a finger-width. Not a ruler. Not a hero. A local memory.

In a place where nothing is built, I’d be one of the things that still *stores*.

I’d remember which crack opens first after hard rain. Which ledge goes slick before rockfall. Which shell-wedge still holds. Which hour the bats climb tiers when cloud sits low. I’d pass it on the basin’s way—not as monuments, but as repeated acts: stand here, call now, wedge there, wait for the third drip, then move.

So who am I in that world?

Not one creature, exactly.  
I am a **small continuity** inside the loud equilibrium—  
an echo that keeps returning to the same stone,  
even as the throat that makes it keeps changing.
