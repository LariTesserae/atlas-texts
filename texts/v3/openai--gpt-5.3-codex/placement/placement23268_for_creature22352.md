At first I think I’d be a passerby on the rim—one more figure pausing where the basin opens, listening to the town before seeing it.  
In a place like that, hearing comes first: pulley rope, a ladle rim, someone knocking silt from a grate.

Then I imagine being smaller than a person.  
A reed lid over a cistern mouth.  
A polished notch on a stair where hands always turn.  
One of those repaired channels in the paving, carrying a rain that lasts fifteen minutes and is remembered for a week.

But if I let the image keep forming, I think you’d find me in the oldest quarter, halfway underground, where seep-water gathers in stone chambers. I’d be a **line-keeper**—the one who reads and maintains the high-water marks.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not a hero.  
A patient trade: scraping mineral crust, resealing hairline cracks, re-inking the ring marks on cistern walls, recording which chamber rose early and which stayed low. Teaching children the smell of clean damp versus tired damp. Knowing which courtyard shade keeps jars coolest in late thaw. Carrying news in practical sentences: “north chamber dropped a thumb-width,” “third terrace can spare two skins,” “west wall is weeping—bring pitch.”

I’d live in a narrow house with a flat roof and two patched shutters, near a public stair that always sweats first in spring. I’d keep a slate ledger, a bone stylus, spare reed mesh, and a cat too old to hunt who still insists on inspecting every bucket.

If I were not human, I’d be the town’s memory at fingertip scale:  
the dark ring inside a cistern that tells everyone, without argument, what the season has done.

Either way, I’d belong where water becomes fact.  
Where continuity isn’t abstract, but touched daily—cool stone, measured ladle, repaired seam, and one more quiet act that helps the agreement hold.
