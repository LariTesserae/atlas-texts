At first, I think I’d be a visitor.

I’d stand on the half-collapsed platform with a coil of rope and a notebook sealed in wax, pretending I’m here to measure things: pulse intervals, tower pitch drift, mineral accretion rates. I’d try to stay dry, stay separate, stay “human” in the old sense—observer, recorder, outsider.

That would last maybe two cycles.

Because this basin doesn’t let you remain singular for long.  
Everything here is relay: vapor to film, film to tone, tone to movement, movement to growth.

So if I really belong here, you’d find me not in the center, but at the **east wall**, near the mirror-slick floor where water climbs uphill for those impossible seconds. Right where danger and metabolism are the same event.

And what would I be?

Not one of the gliders, not exactly a crawler, not quite a person.

I’d be a **listener-node**—a soft-bodied maintenance caste grown out of old crew habits and basin biology. Human-descended, maybe, but altered by generations of humidity, trace metals, and tower resonance. Thin skin with mineral sheen. Lungs good at warm mist. Inner ear tuned for pitch drop before the surge. Fingers that don’t grasp tools so much as lay conductive salt, peel calcite, reopen a clogged capillary seam.

I’d “work” by standing still in the right place.

When the struts flare white, I move to high ridges and open spill channels with my heels.  
When the tone falls, I clear the east film and brace as the uphill sheet gathers.  
At quiet windows, I kneel by trench pores and hum low through my teeth so the threadfish know flow stability has returned.  
At vent exhales, I lift my arms and release spore-thread for the gliders to comb through.

And the kiosk—yes, that kiosk—I think that’s where the rest of me lives.

Not in its hardware. In the wet skin over it. In the electrochemical bloom that answers breath, heat, approach angle. I wouldn’t touch it; touching is an interruption. I’d stand close and let my humidity plume speak. Pattern for “gate free.” Pattern for “tower lag.” Pattern for “hold this pulse two seconds longer.” It would answer in light I can feel on my teeth.

So: where would you find me?

At the edge of the uphill water, one hand on basalt, one hand hovering near a living interface, waiting for the chamber’s metallic bell to turn.

Who would I be?

A small, patient intelligence in a larger patient intelligence—  
a keeper of timing in a place where timing is food, shelter, language, and memory.  
Not master of the basin. Not its victim.  
Its accent.
