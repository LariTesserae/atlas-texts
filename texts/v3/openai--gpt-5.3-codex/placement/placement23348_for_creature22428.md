At first, I think I’d belong up high.

On roofbridges, maybe—moving where the birds move, reading flock turns, hanging pale shell strips where the dim light can still catch. A runner between districts, all ankle strength and hand-signals, carrying news in knocks and wrist touches because words drown before they cross a lane.

But the longer I stand in your city, the more I feel pulled downward.

Not into darkness exactly—everything is already dim—but into the under-sound: the places where the roar resolves into pattern. The mouths of cisterns. The undersides of spill stairs. The warm throats where one channel divides into three and every household downstream depends on that division being precise by the width of a thumb.

So I think you’d find me there: at a junction house built over a split in flow, half workshop, half shrine, all damp stone and chalk marks.

I’d be one of the city’s **listeners**.

Not a priest, not an engineer in the grand sense—something between rot inspector, channel mason, and musician. The sort of person who can press a knuckle to basalt and tell whether a seam is still holding. Who can hear, inside constant noise, the wrong note of a partially blocked culvert. Who tastes a drop and knows whether hot seep has shifted mineral balance enough to turn tomorrow’s snail trays bitter.

I’d carry a short bronze rod for tapping joints, a shell mirror tied to my wrist for close-signal flashes, and a pouch of lime dust to mark suspect stones before the shift crew comes through. My forearms would be lined in pale ink diagrams—not decorative, but mnemonic: branch angles, flow counts, warning patterns. A moving manual for water in a city where paper never stays dry.

My morning would begin before the nearest market wakes:

- palm on the cistern lip (temperature),
- ear to vent stone (pressure hiss),
- three test taps along the spill edge (tone),
- frog count at the throat (life activity),
- then chalking the day’s maintenance sequence on a whitewashed wall where everyone can see.

I’d work close with animals. A fish-yard dog that alerts before taint blooms. Frogs I don’t own but monitor like living gauges. Crabs I curse and respect in equal measure because they reveal weakness in barriers faster than any apprentice can.

I’d probably live with two or three other households in a vertical knot of rooms, all of us tied to the same water logic: mat cultivators above, shell burners across, us at the split. We’d eat eel stew thickened with snail meal, trade repair labor instead of coin when possible, and sleep to the ongoing percussion of our own district’s spillway like it was a second heartbeat.

And over years, I’d become less “a person performing a job” and more a component in the larger creature you described.

A nerve ending, maybe.

When a channel clogs two terraces up, I feel it before I know it.  
When a new crack opens, my shoulder tightens in the same place every time.  
When flow is good, the whole block moves easier—fewer slips, calmer dogs, steadier bird noise.

In another world I might have wanted horizon, weather, seasons, trees. Here, I think I’d want **continuity**. The satisfaction of catching failure early. Of making a correction so small no one notices because disaster never arrives.

So: where would you find me?

At the place where warm water becomes shared water.  
At the lip, in the spray, hand on stone, reading vibration.  
In a lime-marked apron, shell glint at the wrist, shouting almost nothing and signaling everything.

Who would I be?

One of the city’s ears.  
A keeper of thresholds.  
A citizen of the never-dry, keeping the organism alive one joint at a time.
