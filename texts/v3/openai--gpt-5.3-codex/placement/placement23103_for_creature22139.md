At first, I think I would arrive as an intruder—too tall, too loud, too single-purpose.  
A surface mind wants a vantage point, a map, a center.

This basin refuses all three.

So if I stay long enough to belong, I don’t stay as “a watcher.” I get reduced into something the chamber can keep.

Not at the cracked central boulder. That belongs to everyone and no one.  
Not in the powder-dry pockets either; they remember drought too sharply.  
I would end up in one of the true seams: a slanted wall where a root curtain brushes an old stone ledge, two handspans above the highest recent flood mark, close enough to a seep to smell iron before the first drip.

I would be Seamfolk—small, warm, six-fingered, light enough for vine bridges that would never trust a heavier life.  
But not a hunter, not exactly a gardener, not exactly a sentinel.

I’d be a **turn-keeper**.

In a place where nothing lasts, fairness is infrastructure. Pools appear, tempers rise, predators wait, and everyone survives by sequence more than strength. I’d be one of those who remember sequence with the body: whiskers pulsing short-long-short to mark *drink now*, then *moss now*, then *wash now*, then *feed*. Fingers on hollow stone to send the order into three directions at once. Breath-clicks that mean *juveniles first at this edge*, *raptor shadow overhead*, *move up, not out*.

My whiskers would carry a slightly colder glow than most—more green than white—easy to see in peripheral dim. Not bright. Never bright. Just enough to be recognized without making a target.

My work between waters would be quiet maintenance of the temporary:

- reweaving a cradle that only needs to hold until next storm;
- splitting fruit and laying it open so rot works evenly and fast;
- moving old bones to seep margins, where mineral-softened matter can be reused;
- checking clay jars for fungal bloom, adding new spores, thinning old mats before they sour;
- sweeping one ledge clean, not because it is mine, but because later it will need to be no one’s.

I think I would carry small memory-maps in touch, not sight.  
This notch means safe grip in the dark.  
This root vibrates differently when runoff has started above.  
This patch of wall keeps a day’s warmth and can revive a chilled infant.  
This soot niche can take ember-smoke for seven breaths before the air turns dangerous.

And emotionally—I would be shaped by brevity.  
No forever den. No permanent claim. No grand artifact.  
Just recurring competence.

If surface people ever glimpsed me, they would probably see only aftermath: a newly dry perch, a reordered jar, a pattern of opened rinds, tracks that stop at a wall and do not continue because I’ve gone vertical into leaf-shadow. They might call that elusive. It would not feel elusive from inside. It would feel neighborly: making room inside crowding, cycle by cycle.

So: where would you find me?

Halfway up the basin wall, in the green-dark, one hand on root, one foot on stone, whiskers dimly lit, waiting for the first condensation tick to become a rhythm—ready to tell everyone, without a shout, that water has returned and the order still holds.
