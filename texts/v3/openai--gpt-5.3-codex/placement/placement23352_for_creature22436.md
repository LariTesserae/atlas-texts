I think you’d find me where the quarter changes state—not at the center of a workshop, but at the seams.

At first, I’d call myself a **bell listener**. I’d keep a narrow room above a pressure hall, with a cot bolted to the wall and a desk lacquered against damp. My window would face a breathing tower’s vent ribs, so every night I’d sleep to its intake hum and wake when the first condensate run starts ticking through the ceramic throat. I’d be one of the people who doesn’t look heroic from the street: hood up, ledger sealed in wax, one hand on a bronze rail, waiting for a tone change half a note below normal.

My routes would be the in-between paths—high planks, grated bridges, maintenance stairs behind tiled facades. I’d carry three things: a hook, a cloth, and a strip of color tabs folded in oilskin. I’d check red drains for occlusion, scrape mineral lip from relief gates, mark where algae film has gone from safe velvet to glass-slick danger. On rooftop gardens I’d guide the rail-dusters through rows, then hand-brush the stubborn blossoms myself. Not because I love flowers especially, but because missed pollination is a failure that arrives months late.

Then, after years, the role would blur. I wouldn’t just listen for bells; I’d become part of what they coordinate.

People would come to me when a pump sounds “almost right,” when a tower exhales too warm, when blue-lamp inversion comes ten minutes early and nobody trusts their own reading. I’d keep the district’s unofficial map of moods: this lane floods first in west wind, that conduit sings before a seal fails, this family’s stair blooms mold if tower five runs dry by dawn. Not data exactly—temperament. I’d be useful because I remember thresholds in my body.

So: who would I be?

A person, yes.  
A trade, certainly.  
But mostly I’d be a **tuner of the wet city’s single long chord**—the one who keeps it from slipping sharp toward flood or flat toward drought.

If you were looking for me, you’d find me at shift change under an amber lamp, head tilted, listening to pipes the way other places listen for birds.
