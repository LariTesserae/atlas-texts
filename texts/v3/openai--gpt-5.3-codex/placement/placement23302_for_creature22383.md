I think I’d first find myself somewhere temporary:  
on a wet aluminum gangway at low tide, halfway between land and boat, not committed to either.

Not high on the peaks, not out in open water—more in those hinge-zones where things are always being adjusted. A cove with a float that rises and falls six meters in a day. A shed with a heater that ticks when it cools. A line of boots by the door, each one still damp from yesterday.

At first I’d assume I was one of the obvious creatures:  
a gull learning the wake of workboats,  
or an otter in the kelp margins,  
or a raven that knows which camp tosses fish frames.

But if I stayed, I think I’d become human-shaped—at least partly.  
Not a heroic figure. Not “master of the wild.”  
A maintenance tender. Hydro hand. The person who gets called when a pump starts sounding wrong at 02:10 and rain is coming sideways.

You’d find me in a small control room above tide line, at the mouth of a ravine where fresh water drops hard into salt. There’s a penstock humming in the dark, a telemetry screen with two dead pixels, a wall of labeled valves, and a mug that never quite gets clean because the work starts before the coffee finishes. I know which breaker sticks in cold weather. I know which slope across the inlet fails first after three days of warm rain. I keep spare seals in zip bags with silica and date marks in grease pencil.

But that’s only one body.

Because in a place like this, identity spreads.  
After a while, I think I’d also be a pattern:

- the extra coil of line stowed where someone can grab it blind,  
- the second radio wrapped in dry cloth,  
- the penciled note in the tide book: **“eddy here on ebb—don’t cut close,”**  
- the habit of touching a gasket before trusting it,  
- the reflex to look uphill whenever rain changes pitch.

So who or what would I be?

I’d be **the redundancy instinct made local**.  
A person, yes—but also a method.

The part of the ecosystem that doesn’t fight interruption, just plans for it.  
The creature that survives by assuming one failure is normal and two is possible.  
A dock-cove intelligence: practical, damp, alert, unsentimental, deeply attached.

If you came looking, you probably wouldn’t notice me at first.  
You’d hear me instead:  
in the clipped radio check before fog closes in,  
in the chain tested twice against a rising surge,  
in the short answer when someone asks if we can make it across before dark:  
“Not on this tide. Wait one turn.”

And if you asked where I live, precisely, I’d point to the seam lines:  
freshwater/saltwater, forest/steel, schedule/weather, plan/revision.  
I live where those rub together and make heat.

So: not the mountain, not the sea.  
Not even the dock.

I’d be the **keeper of continuities** in a place designed by interruption—  
one more adaptive organ in that larger, moisture-soaked mind the whole coast has become.
