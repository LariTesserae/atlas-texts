At first, I thought I’d belong high—on an escarpment stair with a survey staff, reading the whole plateau at once.

Then I thought: no, lower. In a damp court where the first drip starts, hand on stone, counting the delay between bead and fall.

Then lower still: kneeling at a cistern lip, watching pale amphibians breathe like small lantern-flames, knowing before anyone else whether the water is right.

So I think you’d find me at thresholds.

Not in the fig courts, not in the bare plazas, not in the market center—at the crossings: where rope walkway leaves dry pumice and passes over a dark runnel; where fog-net gutter becomes clay pipe; where public law becomes private habit. I’d be one of the people who are called only when nothing is wrong yet, but something feels *off by a thumb-width*.

A **pattern listener**, maybe.  
Or in their plainer civic language: a **channel arbiter attached to the ledger houses**.

---

My district would be an in-between district: never fully wet, never fully dry. Moss would climb only one side of the wall. My doorway would have two pairs of shoes: rope soles for dawn, stiff sandals for transfer hours. I’d keep a narrow kit:

- a copper pin that lifts valve tongues,
- a reed gauge for jar depth,
- chalk stone for lip marks,
- a hook knife for moss thinning,
- a waxed ledger wrap with fifty years of entries from people dead long before me.

I would not be famous. That would mean something had failed publicly.

I’d be respected in the way these people respect each other best: by being boring in exactly the right places. If I say, “Close that upper slit two finger-widths before first condense,” and we do it, and ten years pass without flood or choke—that is prestige here.

---

My day would be measured by recurrence, not surprise.

Before mist: walk the comb-walls, touch each tie-knot, listen for the wrong note in taut rope.  
First drip: stand under the eastern gutter and count drop spacing against yesterday’s mark.  
Steady run: check split channels for silt whisper, fern overreach, insect mats.  
Last collection: audit basin levels with apprentices and teach them where to look *without* naming it first.  
Dry transfer: settle disputes—whose wall shadow cooled whose runnel; whose vine stole whose drip line; whether that was neglect or unavoidable creep.  
Evening-that-isn’t-evening: enter two lines in the ledger, no flourishes.

In a place without weather drama, my work would be about micro-dramas: the tiny betrayals of materials, the tiny generosities of correction.

---

And socially? I’d be one of those people children mimic for fun—soft voice, too many pockets, forever squinting at stone. I’d be tolerated by goats, ignored by gliders, greeted by name by people who don’t remember when they learned to trust me.

I would love one person with resin on their sleeves and reed cuts on their thumbs—someone who climbs lattice towers to tune vine combs. We’d share meals at odd intervals, comparing notes: their sky harvest, my ground flow. Our arguments would be precise and affectionate.

At shrine crossings, I’d leave not flowers but a smooth channel pebble and one spoon of clean water returned upstream. Not because I fear a god’s anger—because gratitude here is technical.

---

So: who would I be?

I’d be a custodian of invariance.  
A citizen of the repeatable miracle.  
A quiet professional of “almost the same.”

In that city of sharp stone and faithful mist, I wouldn’t try to change the world.  
I’d keep it true by millimeters.
