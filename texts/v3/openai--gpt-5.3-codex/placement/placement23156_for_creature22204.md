At first, I think I’d be up high.

Not skyline-high—there isn’t much skyline here—but roofline high, in that dawn hour before edges sharpen. I’d be on a condenser comb with a hooked brush, knees wet, lifting last week’s lichen felt so the fog can choose gravity again. I’d know each rib by touch. Which one chips if you lean. Which one sings when water starts to run. I’d work by sound more than sight: drip frequency, fan hum, one relay three blocks off that clicks half a beat late when humidity rises too fast.

Then I think: no, lower.

I’d be where the basin keeps its secrets—under the pipe-racks, near the old loading lots where black water holds in shallow pockets. Not as a heroic engineer, just as a person with trays and tags, following a line that has gone arrhythmic. In this place, failure doesn’t announce itself with alarms. It appears as a changed cadence. A gutter that used to stutter now falls silent. A tank membrane that used to drum now answers dull. I’d be one of those people who can’t pass a wrong sound without stopping.

But if I stay human only, it feels incomplete for this district.

So maybe I’m also one of the records that outlives names: margin notes in a pump handbook, resin-sealed and re-copied; color codes braided into line wraps; a habit passed hand to hand—*always check the north catwalk first after dry wind*. Maybe I’m the tiny civic superstition that is actually engineering: tap the cistern twice before opening valve three, because twice lets you hear if the column is under strain.

Or maybe I’m not a person at all.

Maybe I am the short segmented conveyor between press house and dryer—patched in six metals, vine-trained along my truss so I shade the line while carrying cane pulp in the cool morning. I complain in squeaks when bearings dry. I take dew off my own cables and drop it into planter gutters below. I am ugly in a useful way. Children crossing under me can tell, from my pitch, whether the day’s throughput will be light or punishing.

No—closer still:

I might be one of the “unfinished” walls done correctly at last: slag-concrete core, root-mat skin, ceramic fins, a narrow trough at the base feeding a cistern throat. I divide one yard from another, yes, but I also catch moisture, hold slope, cool air, host fungi, and mark flood history in faint mineral bands. People lean tools against me. Moss edits my surface every season. I am infrastructure and weather diary at once.

If I gather all those versions, the clearest answer is this:

I’d be a **line walker of the quiet shift**, one of the keepers who listens the district awake—part technician, part gardener, part archivist, part instrument. You’d find me in the hour when fog still moves like low cloth through the streets, walking from roof to catwalk to channel lip with a brush, a mallet, a coil of cord, and a pocket notebook swollen from damp. I’d know five routes by muscle memory and two by smell. I’d prune a load-bearing vine, clear a mesh choke, retension a cable, hand-pollinate a row of blooms, then stop because a pump two courtyards over sounds one shade too tired.

And if you asked me who I am, I might answer with my task, not my name:

I keep the place in tune.

Not perfect—never perfect. Just tuned enough that dryness is delayed, moisture is welcomed, and the choreography continues one more day.
