At first, I think I’d be one of the walkers.

A small figure on a reed bridge, crossing too early in the bright cycle, muttering at fog and rope stiffness, counting drips into a clay cup and pretending that counting is control.  
A human, then: practical, layered in felt, carrying antler hooks and a resin lamp.  
Someone who survives by timing.

But the longer I stand in your basin, the less I feel like a traveler and the more I feel assigned.

Not to the old routes.  
To the **new wounds**.

---

I’d be found where the stone is still sharp enough to cut through boot leather.  
At fresh collapse lips.  
At the mouths of newborn light chimneys where nothing has had time to root.

There, in the first few cycles after a fall, before the green occupation thickens, I’d work the in-between: anchoring split-stem ladders, setting warning totems, smearing spore mash into fracture seams, hanging wick-lines so condensate learns a downward path before it chooses chaos.

Not a farmer. Not a hunter.  
A **starter**.

A stitcher between raw mineral and weave.

---

If I’m human, my people would call me something like a **First-Clad**—one who gives bare stone its first skin.

I’d travel with a pack of odd things:

- cork-hard fungal plugs for crack mouths  
- braided root rope soaked in resin  
- chalk for marking draft direction  
- bone pegs with notch codes for “unstable,” “drinkable,” “false floor,” “cold sink”  
- a wrapped shard of meteoritic iron, used only when vines must be cut clean before they tear the whole span

I would sleep high when possible, in a root curtain pocket under thin glowing roofstone, and wake to check whether the drip I trusted last cycle still exists. Half my job would be hydrology; the other half, prophecy.

Because in this place, infrastructure is prediction.

---

But if I let it keep unfolding, I’m not only that person.

I’m also what happens to them over time.

My cloak grows permanent green along the hem.  
My tools smell like wet mineral and leaf acid.  
I stop saying “the weave” like it’s separate.  
I start carrying living braid the way others carry fire.

Eventually, I’m less a citizen of a camp and more a moving interface:  
part scout, part gardener, part mason, part listener to pressure hum.

So: where would you find me?

In the pale hours before glass rain, on a narrow ledge above a new sink, one hand on raw stone, one hand pressing first growth into place—  
trying to make tomorrow’s path out of a wall that fell yesterday.
