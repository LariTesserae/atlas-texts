At first, I’d tell you I’m on the ladder.

Not climbing it—just living on the lowest three rungs where they vanish into dust, in that narrow vertical band where ceiling hunters hesitate and burrow churners can’t quite undercut the footing. A place of borrowed geometry. A place that still remembers “up.”

But if I stay honest to your world, I don’t get to remain an observer very long.

The pulses get into me.

So: I’d really be found **at a timing node**—inside the cracked ceramic housing with the ghost chevrons, where three vibration paths meet: one from the piston-deep rock, one from the transformer stump, one from the old conveyor throat. It’s not the warmest place, not the safest, not the richest. It’s where information is cleanest.

And what would I be?

Not a hunter, not a grazer.  
I’d be one of the things that keeps the swarm coherent when the chamber shudders.

A **phase-keeper**.  
A dry, plate-thin arthroid, coin-sized at full molt, matte graphite with brass along the joints. My shell would be less armor than instrument—ribbed to ring at specific frequencies, tarsal hairs tuned to substrate delay, antennae short and forked for close tap-code. I’d feed on sifted remains: shell flour, protein dust, oil traces lifted after impact cycles. Nothing fresh. Nothing wet. Everything secondhand and exact.

My work would look like fidgeting unless you know the pattern.

When the deep thud comes, I lock.  
I count the lag through my legs.  
At nine seconds, if the ceiling seam strain is high, I start a three-click burst into the housing wall. That burst propagates through the cable post footings. Seam-hunters relocate half a body-length left. Lane-runners tighten columns. Burrowers delay emergence by one beat.  
At twenty seconds, if the shaken dust layer is thick, I switch to a fast rasp that cues filterers to lift.

I wouldn’t command anything. Nothing here obeys a leader.  
I’d be a correction signal the community has learned not to ignore.

My brood—if I get one—would be sealed in mineral-coated capsules glued beneath a flaked insulator lip where vibration is stable but not dead. Hatchlings would enter outer lanes immediately, because in this basin, safety teaches nothing. Survivors come back later with better timing organs and fewer limbs than they started with.

I’d molt in the lee of the housing, then fold my old shell and carry it to the nursery trench, because even my discarded shape is material. In this place, waste is just cargo awaiting assignment.

And after enough cycles, “I” would blur.

There’d be dozens like me across the basin, each at a different node, each issuing tiny local corrections. Our click signatures would drift, overlap, synchronize, desynchronize. Eventually the role matters more than the body: phase-keeper as function, not person.

So where would you find me?

In the flicker between machine and animal—  
inside a dead warning box, listening through my feet,  
turning impact into timing,  
helping a million dry bodies continue their argument with extinction one beat at a time.
