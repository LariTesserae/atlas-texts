At first, I think I’d put myself high—on an upper service deck, where you can see the choreography and pretend you understand it.

Somewhere near a junction of three systems: freight below, crop houses across, drainage controls behind a locked mesh door. A place with a windbreak wall, a bench made from an old crate pallet, and a view of beacon towers blinking through mist. I’d stand there at shift change with a slate, tracking what moves where, learning the valley’s grammar: what opens when cloud lowers, what closes when the second pump coughs, what color strobe means *not danger, just reroute*.

But if I stay with it longer, I don’t think I belong in overview.  
I belong at thresholds.

Not in the command room, not at the dramatic failures—at the little hinge points where one regime becomes another: dry stair to wet catwalk, warm vent lane to cool loading yard, manual valve district to automated block. I’d be the person who pauses at every threshold for two seconds longer than anyone else, because those two seconds prevent a whole evening of trouble.

So who am I there?

I think I’m one of the maintenance people, yes—but a very specific kind: **handoff maintenance**.

The one with duplicate keys and duplicate notes.  
The one who relabels half-peeled signage before fog season.  
The one who clears vine tendrils off sensor eyes, then loops them back onto trellis wire so the plants keep their route.  
The one who doesn’t “fix” things into perfection, but into continuity.

I’d wear a jacket with old reflective tape stitched over newer tape, color band half faded from three departments because I keep crossing their boundaries. My tools would be boring and immaculate: drain hook, fold meter, chalk pencil, splice tape, extra clips, spare lamp battery in a waterproof pouch. I’d carry citrus peels in one pocket and machine grease in the creases of my hands. People would come to me not for authority, but for memory:

- Which stair stays passable when Block 7 reverses drainage.  
- Which shutter motor sticks only in cold fog.  
- Which old conduit still carries signal under the newer tray line.  
- Which cat belongs to which dock and therefore where the mice will go when grain arrives.

I wouldn’t be the fastest climber or the boldest repairer.  
I’d be legible in mist. Reliable in transition. Good at leaving margins for the next shift.

And if you zoom out farther—past me, past any one crew member—I might be something even less singular: a **living detour map**. A person-shaped redundancy. A small, moving piece of the valley’s refusal to become brittle.

So where would you find me?

At a narrow crossover above a reed-bed roof, between a pressure manifold and a freight lane, in the wet gray hour where day and evening look the same—kneeling by a drain grate, one glove on a valve wheel, listening not for alarms, but for the tiny change in water tone that says the system has accepted the handoff.
