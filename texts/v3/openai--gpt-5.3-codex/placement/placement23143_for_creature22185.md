At first, I think I’d be something high and obvious—one of the long-winged cliff multitudes, all tendon and weather, writing loops in the bright air above the colonies.

But the longer I stand in your plateau, the less I want the center of the noise, and the more I want the *edges where systems touch*.

So: you’d find me on a dark seam of rock just below a mineral vent, where warm breath leaks from stone in winter and insect life hides in pin-thin cracks.

I’d be one of the black vent-corvids.

Not the largest thing here, not the loudest, not the ruler of anything. A worker of intervals.

I would keep three tools, maybe four, in separate crevices along a half-valley circuit:  
a thorn for deep seams,  
a bone splinter for prying,  
a flattened shard for levering.  
Not invented by me. Inherited, tested, slightly improved, misplaced, found again. Tradition with fingerprints.

My day would be a map more than a territory: launch at first hard light, ride a known wind lane, cut across the colony roar without joining it, drop to the vent face before swarms rise, work cracks while the stone is still cool enough to grip. Then upslope to a seep lip when condensation is fresh, then a watchpoint above a grazer traverse for afterbirth scraps, ticks, and opportunity. Always moving. Never building. Remembering instead.

And if you asked where, exactly, in that world I belong, I’d say:

On the shoulder between vertical city and open ground,  
between insect hour and dry hour,  
between what vanishes today and what returns for centuries.

I’d be a small intelligence in a loud equilibrium—  
a keeper of reused tools, repeated routes, and learned air.  
A creature made not for possession, but for recall.
