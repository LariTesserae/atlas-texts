At first I think I’d be an observer—on one of those weathered black-stone knuckles at the basin rim, knees damp from spray, trying to map what never changes.

I’d choose the western quadrant, where that permanent rain wall hammers one side of the sea and never crosses the line. From up there, I could watch the root-belts heave in piston waves, hear the filtration forests exhale on schedule, see grazers bend around predators before the strike even begins. I’d tell myself I’m outside it, just measuring.

But this place doesn’t really have an outside.  
Not for long.

The Stillwork is made of timing, and timing reaches farther than flesh. If you stay, your breathing starts to fall into one of its harmless intervals. Your sleep catches the pump-cycle. Your attention starts anticipating turns three beats before they happen. You become less a witness than a tiny compliant oscillator.

So if I belong here, I don’t think I’m a hunter or a grazer. Too visible. Too dramatic. I think I’d be one of the small regulators nobody names in field notes unless they disappear.

I’d be a **seam-runner**: a narrow, six-limbed climber-fish that lives where two process zones meet—the edge of the rain-fed mineral district and the nutrient-flat vine wards inland. Not a predator, not prey in any important sense. A courier of correction.

I’d carry grit in gut pouches, yes, like the others—but the grit would be graded: heavy silicates when the spray strips too clean, iron-rich dust when leaf arrays overdraw trace metals, carbonate when root-wells drift acidic by a fraction. I’d climb wet cable-vines, dive through frond-columns, surface beneath fan-leaf ceilings, always on the same route, always at the same phase point in the basin’s long measure.

My body would be built for rule-following:

- pressure-sensitive lateral lines tuned to pump-thud harmonics,  
- jawbone resonators that read infrasonic vent calls through water and vine,  
- limb pads that “hear” vibration in root-felt like fingertips on machinery.

I wouldn’t know the whole Stillwork, not consciously. I’d just know when a corridor feels half a beat late, when a grazer front is too thick by one body-length, when a seed-mill cadence needs mineral feed two cycles sooner than usual. I’d move, deposit, trigger, pass on.

No memory worth calling memory. No ambition. No invention.  
But not empty either.

From the inside, it would feel like being a sentence in a language that never changes because it is already exact. I would be one grammatical mark: a comma between extraction and growth, between rain and root, between surplus and deficit.

So where would you find me?

Not at the center—there isn’t one.  
At a boundary, repeatedly.  
In the same crossing lane, under the same late-day sky, with clouds below the ridge and thunder in the plants.

Who would I be?

A living adjustment.  
A small, slate-green conveyor of balance.  
One movable part in a sea-sized machine that refuses history.
