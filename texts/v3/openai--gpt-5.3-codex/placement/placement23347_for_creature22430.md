At first I thought I’d be something quick and silver in the channels—a fish tracing inherited geometry.

Then I thought: no, I’d be one of the keepers, lamp hood up, pencil tucked behind an ear, boots learning each grate by flex and sound.

But the longer I sit in your Underdock, the more I think I’d be found in the **pause** between those two: not a foreman, not a ghost—an **interval-keeper**.

---

You’d find me at the three-way junction where the echo comes early, on the narrow catwalk above Terrace Four, near the panel that still glows amber even when the rest of the bank sleeps. I keep a stool there made from an overturned cable drum. I keep chalk in a waxed tin. I keep two clocks: one for official schedule, one for what the district is actually doing.

My work is small and constant:

- marking when the six-minute pump hymn starts late by seventeen breaths,
- moving one warning lantern from green-white to sodium so the glassfish will cross before sluice adjustment,
- tapping a rail three times to nudge fox kits off a heat line before purge steam,
- scraping calcite skirts so a valve wheel stays turnable for another season.

Nothing heroic. Everything timed.

---

If you asked what I *am*, not just what I do:

I am one of the people who became part of the Underdock’s memory system.

My handwriting sits in logbooks beside three dead alphabets.  
My bootwear adds a new path where old stairs sweat too much to trust.  
My voice stays low because loud words snag in bad acoustics and come back wrong.  
My body learns humidity shifts before rain reaches the city above.

In a place where machines behave like weather and animals read infrastructure better than maps, I am a translator of drifts.

---

And if you waited long enough, you might realize I’m not only a person at all.  
I’m also that third thing your description keeps circling:

the local intelligence made of repetition.

A human face, yes.  
But also a habit of attention passed along shift to shift.  
A way of noticing two minutes per year and acting before it becomes catastrophe.  
A life spent preserving intervals so everything else can keep renegotiating the treaty in the dark.

So: where would I be?

On the catwalk, in mist to the knees, listening for the motor that should start in thirty seconds.

Who would I be?

The one who hears it start in twenty-eight—and writes that down before anyone else calls it a problem.
