At first, I think I’d be a person with a tool belt.

A Phaseborn worker in a throat wrap, palms resin-stained, living in a cavity apartment grown into a trunkwall near a tram spur. Someone practical. Someone tired. Someone who knows exactly how many steps from bed to shift horn.

But the longer I stand in your undercity, the more I feel I’d end up at an edge condition—where systems overlap and misunderstand each other.

So: I’d be found in the **buffer wards between a rail spine and a chartered Thinking Grove**, in the pale open corridors where carts still pass but roots are already testing the dust.

I’d be a **Resonance Clerk**—half Quiet Crew, half civic translator.

Not a glamorous role. Necessary, always.

I’d spend lamp-dawn walking the bark curtains with a tuning rod, checking whether the damp strata are still swallowing the right frequencies so alarms can travel and panic won’t. Mid-phase, I’d log vibration maps from line-fox whisker stations, compare them against grove pulses, and file notices that sound bureaucratic but really mean: *don’t cut here, this wall is listening* or *reroute schoolline three days, ambush den forming under trellis 6B.*

At pollination dusk, I’d stand at a resonant pylon with both hands on warm metal and “hear” the grove’s decisions as timed pressure through my wrists—no words, just insistence. Shade request. Root pain. Heat concern. Memory of near-collapse twelve cycles ago. I’d translate that into permits, delays, warnings, meetings no one wants but everyone attends.

I think I’d have a little caravan room hung with dust cloth and legal tags, and one old tunnel bird that returns even when I don’t tie a message to its leg. I’d carry water like a vow. I’d run only for fire.

And over years, I’d become what this city makes of everyone: less a lone inhabitant, more a **joint**.

A hinge between bark and bylaws.  
A worker whose job is to keep the city quiet enough to hear itself becoming.
