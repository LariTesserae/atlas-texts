At first I think I’d be something dramatic—an upper-tier signal keeper with frost in my lashes, or a furnace-hauler down in the red lungs where the rock sweats heat.

But the more I stay in your fissure, the less I believe in dramatic jobs. This place is held together by people whose names don’t make it onto metal plaques.

So I think you’d find me in the **middle bands**, in the pressure range where your ears click if you climb too fast and every third platform is temporary. Not top. Not bottom. The hinge.

I’d be one of the city’s **route listeners**—the people who repaint arrows, retune ceramic warning lines, and decide which paths are still true after the mountain shifts at noon.

---

My station would hang on a rail rack called something like **Spine-47**, bolted to a new cut face still striped with drill marks. I’d sleep in a narrow cell with a reclaim vent at my feet and a map wall opposite my bunk that is always wrong by evening.

My tools would be:

- a pressure reed,
- a pouch of color dust for arrows,
- a coil wrench,
- three ceramic chimes tuned to my sector’s “normal,”
- and a tin of runner fat for bribing cliff-runners off warm relay boxes.

I’d work with animals constantly, but never romantically.  
Tunnel eels for leak-hunting.  
Cliff-runners for vermin control near insulation seams.  
Gliders as accidental couriers when static storms kill short-range radio.

I’d know which colonies can be moved and which ones you leave alone if you want to keep breathing.

---

If you ask *who* I am there, I think I become less of a person and more of a function:

**I am temporary memory.**

In a city where nothing stays fixed, memory can’t live in architecture. It lives in people who cross tiers and keep comparing what the city was at dawn to what it is now. I would be one of those people.

I’d carry yesterday in my knees, in my lungs, in the way I pause before crossing a span that used to be stable. I’d be trusted not because I’m high-status, but because when a line blew in Sector Glass-Down, I came with seal paste and stayed until everyone was counted.

That’s currency there: not rank. Response.

---

I don’t think I’d be clean.  
I don’t think I’d be powerful.  
I think I’d be **useful**, which is the closest thing to belonging in that crack.

My voice would stay low out of habit. I’d check gasket lips with my thumb while talking. I’d eat standing up near heat exchangers. I’d mark arrows with one hand and erase them with the other because the ledge already moved.

Children would ask me for old routes like bedtime stories:  
“Was there really a stair from Blue Coil to Furnace Nine?”  
And I’d say, “For three days, yes.”

---

And if I had to choose something even truer than a job title:

I would be one of the **ceramic chime keepers** who teach the city how to hear itself.

Not alarm bells—those are easy.  
I’d tune for subtleties: strain, draft inversion, cable hum gone narrow, the near-sound before a bracket fails. I’d hang a line, listen, shave the ceramic edge, hang it again. By night, whole neighborhoods would sleep because they trust that if the mountain changes its mind, the chimes will speak in time.

So where would you find me?

Halfway down the wound, boots hooked to a grated lip, helmet lamp off to save charge, head tilted toward a line of pale chimes moving in air nobody can see—listening for the first wrong note in a city that survives by hearing it early.
