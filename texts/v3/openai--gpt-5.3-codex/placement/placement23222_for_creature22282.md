At first, I don’t think I’d arrive there as a person with a clear job.

I think I’d arrive as a pause in motion—someone standing too long at a junction, hand on a wet rail, trying to feel whether the rail is only wood or already speaking.

Then, slowly, the basin would assign me.

Not to a high terrace (too brittle), not to the low courts (too treacherous), but to a hinge place in the middle band: a narrow crossing where three rooted walkways meet above a seep channel, just downslope from a light well. A place where weight, water, and news all pass.

So if I belong there, I am probably one of their **Span Listeners**.

---

I’d be Braided People, but not among their strongest lifters or boldest climbers.  
I’d be one of the quiet technicians of continuity:

- ear to rail, reading tap-codes from uphill quarters,  
- fingers in gutter-chains, tuning drip rhythms so messages carry downhill,  
- palm on old support trunks, feeling micro-shifts before cracks show,  
- mapping where thaw has loosened root-grip by the way the ground answers a step.

My tools would be small: bark wedges, resin cord, split-vine clamps, bone pegs for marking safe flex lines, charcoal wax for writing temporary signs on wet wood. Nothing dramatic. Everything consequential.

I’d sleep in a wall-pocket behind a folded thicket door, in a chamber no larger than a boat berth. Warmth from a compost core beneath the floor lattice. Moss mantle drying overhead. A shallow bowl collecting condensation from the ceiling roots.

---

If you came looking for me, you’d find me at first thaw-drip, already out before full light, clearing hair-fine blockages from channels with a hooked twig. At midday, in the light well, watching which species are trying to seed this year and which are holding back. At dusk, retensioning one bridge-span by a thumb’s width because by nightfall that thumb’s width will decide whether ice lifts it or leaves it.

I’d speak little.  
But I’d listen constantly—to branch groan, to runoff tempo, to that specific soft crack that means “not yet failure, but soon.”

---

Who would I be, really?

A citizen, yes.  
But more precisely: a **translator between speeds**.

Water deciding in minutes.  
Roots deciding in seasons.  
Stone deciding in centuries.  
People deciding before dark.

I’d live where those decisions meet, and try to keep them braided.

So in that world, you would find me not at the center of a house, but at the center of a span—half in mist, half in structure, hand on the living city’s pulse.
