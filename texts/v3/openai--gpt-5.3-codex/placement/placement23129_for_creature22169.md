At first I think I’d be on the top walkways, where you can pretend you’re above it all.

But that’s not true for long.  
This place doesn’t really allow “above.” It pulls you into timings.

So: you’d find me in the **middle level**, in the narrow run between the condenser galleries and the valve spine—where the steel grating is always a little damp, where the amber strips fail every third fixture, where you can hear both habitation doors and pump housings if you stand still enough.

I would be one of the people they call before alarms trip.  
Not an engineer on paper. Not a foreman.  
A **drift-listener**.

I’d carry chalk, a thermal pen, and a rag that never dries. I’d keep a notebook in a waxed sleeve with pages warped from steam. My job would look minor until it isn’t: I’d log tiny delays—the half-second late click on Gate 12, the new ping pattern in a brace near Section C-9, the way droplets are striking the catch pan in twos instead of threes. I’d walk the same loops each cycle and notice what changed by smell before gauges confirm it.

People would joke that I’m superstitious.  
Then they’d ask, quietly: *Did that hum seem wrong to you too?*

I’d live in a wedge-room bolted into leftover rock volume, two doors down from a filter trough of rigid stalk-plants. Sleep by vent cycle, eat by pump hour. My wall would have old section numbers crossed out five times, because nothing keeps its name here. I’d memorize both the current map and the ghost maps underneath it.

During collapse window, I’d be fast.  
Valve teams peel away, and I’d be the one at junction nodes reading cistern pulse and calling reroutes when a channel clogs with mineral lace. Ten minutes of interior rain, then metal smell, then dry return. In those ten minutes, I’d feel most like myself—moving through steam columns with radio clipped to my collar, counting pressure drops like heartbeat.

Who would I be, really?

Not machinist. Not weather, either.  
I’d be the thin membrane between them.

A human gasket.  
A keeper of continuity in a place that keeps being born.

If someone new arrived and asked what I do, I’d probably shrug and say:  
“I listen for tomorrow while it’s still only a noise.”
