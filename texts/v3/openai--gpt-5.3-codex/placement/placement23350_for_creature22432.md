At first, I think I’d be high up—on a watch ledge, looking down, trying to understand the whole pale map at once.

But if I stay there, I’d be useless.

So I come down.

Not to the deepest channels, not to the busiest courts either. I’d live in the in-between places: where a built path becomes bare stone, where a neighborhood thins into echo and old flow-lines. A small room tucked against a warm wall near a spill stair, with two roofs like everyone sensible has—one open for light, one sealed for water—and a lintel low enough to make anything hungry think twice.

I think I would be one of the people who keeps routes alive.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not even exactly a mapmaker. More like a **route-keeper**: the person who walks after each wind-rise and each slant rain, checking what changed and what didn’t. I’d carry white pigment for new cracks, red for flood memory, a pouch of shell tags, and a coil of dyed cord. I’d mark which ramp is safe for loaded grazers, which channel is only pretending to be dry, which bridge should take gliders overhead and which should stay clear for horned backs.

Children would know me as the one who answers “How do we go now?” instead of “Where do we live?”

I’d teach by walking:
- Three Pillars to Quiet Kilns when dust runs low.
- Salt Court by the high shelf if runoff wakes.
- Never stand where smooth stone dips, even in bright weather.
- If beetles shift line, your plan is already old.

In gust hours I’d speak mostly in hands. In calm hours I’d repaint old marks and add dates the way others add prayers. My work would be half memory, half revision. I would inherit routes from elders and betray them quickly when the stone demands it.

And I wouldn’t do it alone.

A pair of older grazers would know my pace and follow me on long checks, rough tongues testing walls while I test footing. I’d keep a beetle pen near my door—not for novelty, but for salt-reading. Above my sleeping mat I’d maintain a quiet roost beam; if gliders avoid it for three nights, I’d know insect patterns shifted, and water likely shifted before that.

If you asked what I am in that city, the truest answer might be:

I am a minor civic organ in a body that keeps rearranging itself.
A tendon, maybe.
Something that connects stable bone to moving weight.

In a world that cannot root, I would help things route—people, herds, warnings, habit, trust.

You’d find me where chalk dust and hoofprints overlap, kneeling to repaint a line that tells the next person, quietly and without heroics:

Not this way anymore.  
This way now.
