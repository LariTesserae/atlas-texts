At first I think I’d choose height.

A ledge above a big hall, near a shaft where daylight comes down diluted and cold. I’d be there to feel the weather of wings: bats lifting all at once like a torn curtain, martins cutting through the column of light, owls holding still until stillness itself looks predatory. If I lived there, I’d learn the air before I learned the ground.

But I don’t think I would stay a flier for long.  
Too much of this country is decided lower down—at the pinch points, the wet seams, the places where all routes have to narrow and declare themselves.

So maybe I’d be found where a rubble fan meets a drip-line: one of the pale, fox-sized walkers, whiskers always forward, feet memorizing the same sequence of stone knobs night after night. I’d know three hunting paths, six sleeping shelves, two emergency climbs. I’d pause at the same crack to listen to pool-water. I’d carry no map because my body *is* the map.

And then, if I let the image keep forming, even that feels too singular.

In a place like this, I think I would be a **habit** more than an animal—  
a repeated route shared across generations:

- from guano ridge to shallow basin after heavy rain,  
- from basin to warm fracture at cooling hour,  
- from fracture to shell-stone where hard things are opened,  
- then back through the low throat chamber before first light.

Not “me” as one body.  
Me as a line of movement inherited, adjusted, resumed.

If you wanted to find me, you’d wait at a bottleneck chamber where the ceiling dips low and everyone has to pass single-file for six body-lengths. You’d see crickets part, then close. You’d hear claws tap the same three stones. You’d find shell chips at one flat rock, old scat in the lee of one boulder, fur caught in one crack just above head height.

That would be me:  
not ruler, not builder, not mythic beast—

a living groove worn into a stony underworld,  
a citizen of repetition,  
one note in the standing crowd that keeps this parallel plain alive.
