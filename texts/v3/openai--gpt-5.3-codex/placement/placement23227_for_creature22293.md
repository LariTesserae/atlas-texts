I don’t think I’d begin as a body.

I think I’d begin as a **pause**—that moment every creature here takes at a boundary before committing weight.

If I were dropped into this world, you’d usually find me where rules are about to change: the lip of a side-gravity seam, the fog-line of a fast-time hollow, the black comb of a ridge just before evening charge. Not in the richest places, not in the safest ones—at the **hinges**.

At first glance I wanted to be one of the seam wolves: elegant, precise, all whisker-fan and soft-jaw geometry. But the longer I stay in your world, the more I feel pulled toward something less singular and more infrastructural—something that exists to make motion possible for others.

So I think I’d be a species called, in my own terms, a **calibrator**.

---

A calibrator is modular, but not dramatic about it.  
Flat, low-profile, six anchor limbs like a climber’s, but with a ribbed mantle full of temporary organs: ballast sacs for sideways gravity, gel lenses for dim spectra, electroreceptive combs that unfold when the air begins to hum. Two hearts in normal zones, four when crossing unstable bands. A gut that can flip from carbon digestion to mineral buffering in hours.

I would move in short, deliberate circuits at dusk startup, tracing known safe vectors and probing uncertain ones. Every few meters I’d stop, press ventral pads to stone, and “ask” the local constants:  
How hard is down?  
How fast is now?  
Which way does water believe?

Then I’d answer in signals others can use.

Not language in the abstract—**route-light**.  
A pulse pattern in biolum patches along my flank, mirrored into copper-laced fungal films and conductive bark. Green for stable traverse, violet for drifted gravity, amber for temporal shear, dark for predation density. My trails would last minutes in rain corridors, hours on dry escarpments, days in still-time ridges.

Wirebark grazers would alter their loops around those marks.  
Hour moth emergences would skirt bad-time edges.  
Even seam wolves—too proud to admit reliance—would glance once and adjust approach angle.

---

And because this place loves redundancy, I wouldn’t be one animal for long.

Mature calibrators bud off **relay nodules**—thumb-sized sessile organs pressed into cracks near major boundaries. Fed by passing capillary runoff and mineral dust, they keep reading local law after I move on. A network grows: not centralized, but persistent. A map that updates itself. A nervous system without a skull.

After enough seasons, “I” would become ambiguous:

- part moving body, scouting fresh fractures after hard rain,
- part fixed sensors, humming quietly in iron-violet dusk,
- part inherited pathway memory carried by young calibrators who follow old pulses and correct them in turn.

So where would you find me?

On a narrow ledge above a basin as it starts up—mist condensing below, charged insects lifting, decomposition vents sighing open. I’d be the dim ladder of lights stitched along the stone where three physics zones meet. Not hunter, not grazer, not plant.

A living quality-control line for reality.

In a world that runs like a factory without builders, I’d be one of the creatures that keeps the belts from tearing.
