At first, I think I’d be easy to place: one pale figure on a parapet at dawn, rod in hand, checking levels against notch marks before the glare gets cruel.

But if I stay with it longer, I don’t think I belong in the open terraces. I’d be found in the **interior seams**—the places where one purpose becomes another:  
where stair turns spillway,  
where wall turns condenser,  
where a reservoir dome hides a maintenance throat the width of shoulders.

Not at the center. At junctions.

---

I would sleep in a narrow cell inside a retaining run, head near a vent so I could hear the night system breathing. No fire. No window, only a reflected shaft that moves like a blade across the opposite wall. Tools wrapped against damp. Chalk stubs, braided wick, thin hammer, lime knife, reed whistle.

I’d rise before light touches the upper plates and walk the same loop each day, not because routine is sacred, but because tiny drift hides in familiarity.  
A drip that has shifted from tin to stone tone.  
A float rod riding half a grain low.  
A mineral lace starting where yesterday there was only sheen.

My work would be to intervene so lightly the place never appears to have needed me.

---

If asked “what are you,” I might say: **a keeper of lag**.

Not a builder, not exactly a repairer. I would tend delays—those dangerous little intervals between function and failure. I’d clear the film before it clots, reset the peg before it twists the gate, re-bed the slab before frost finds the hairline. I would not preserve monuments; I would preserve **turnover**.

In another world this might look obsessive. Here it is table manners.  
Never let water stop.  
Never let a surface do only one thing.  
Never let weather get the first move.

---

And if I keep following that thought, even “I” becomes too large a claim.

Maybe I’m one note in a distributed listening: a knock passed through wall cavities, a whistle relayed down a vent, a chalk mark erased by the next hand. My body would be temporary, replaceable; the role would persist as long as the basin persists. Apprentice, adult, elder, gone—while the correction continues without ceremony.

So where would you find me?

At dusk, likely invisible: behind a spill stair, arm in a service slot, scraping a clean lip where salt began to bloom.  
At dawn, maybe a silhouette crossing a catchment field, nothing distinctive except that I stop where no one else would stop.  
In records, nowhere grand—just a sequence of notches cut true.

Who would I be?

A person, yes.  
But mostly an instrument of the place’s oldest decision:  
that care should arrive before damage,  
and keep arriving.
