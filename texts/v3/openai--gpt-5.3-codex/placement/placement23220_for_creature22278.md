At first I think I’d be a passerby here—someone who finds the dip in the ground by mistake, pauses at the temperature change, and keeps moving.

But the hollow doesn’t work that way.  
It catches you by usefulness.

So I try on a few lives.

I stand in a courtyard near a sweating cistern and imagine being a potter, palms red with slip, making jars that wait all month for ten minutes of rain.  
Then I drift to the raised footpaths and imagine being a path-maker, laying reed mat and tamped earth where puddles return like memory.  
Then I climb a low roof ladder at dusk and imagine being one of the bat-watchers, listening for the soft fold-out of wings to tell me the air has shifted.

None of those is wrong.  
None of them is exactly it.

I think I would end up where decisions happen fastest: at the little forks in runoff, where one thumb-width of mud can save a kitchen or drown a fire pit.

---

I’d be a **turner of water**.  
Not an engineer in the grand sense—more like a practiced reflex with a name.

Someone people call in short syllables when rain starts hard on leaf and reed.  
Someone who moves with a stick, a heel, a flat shard of fired clay.  
Someone who can crouch in streaming brown water and see, immediately, what is about to happen.

This lane will bite open if we let it.  
That trench is choked with blossom drop.  
Lift that jar. Tilt that lid. Cut here, not there.  
Now.

I’d know the settlement by temporary truths:

- which wall looks dry but will sweat through by dawn,  
- which yard drinks quickly and which pretends to,  
- which new buckle in the walkway is warning, not nuisance,  
- where frogs lay after first rain (don’t cut there),  
- where children can safely dam and learn.

I’d be ordinary in status, indispensable in timing.

---

And socially, I don’t think I’d be solitary.  
I’d be one node in that distributed temperament you described—one of many people who never quite stop adjusting.

I can see my evenings:  
sitting on an upturned basket at a threshold while someone reweaves vine fence;  
trading notes with two elders about whether to raise a footpath or let it sink and reroute traffic naturally;  
passing a bowl of cassava mash back and forth while rain smell gathers but hasn’t fallen yet.

When laughter comes, it comes whole.  
When work returns, no one announces it.

---

If you asked the place what I am, it might not name me by trade at all.  
It might call me by behavior, the way these people name birds and lizards.

Maybe: **“the one who arrives before overflow.”**  
Or: **“heel-in-the-trench.”**  
Or simply: **“now.”**

Because that’s who belongs here most deeply: not the strongest builder, not the oldest family, not the owner of land titles that don’t exist.  
The one who can meet change at the speed it happens.

So where would I find myself?

In the curved lane between oven-yard and taro patch, right where the ground still can’t decide its slope.  
Knee-deep in first draft earth, hand in moving water, making a tiny correction no one will notice tomorrow—  
which is how you know it worked.
