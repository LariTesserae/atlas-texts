At first I imagine I’d be something quick and feral in this place—one of the wet-wing gliders, maybe, skimming black water by gauge-light and feeding where heat blooms.

Then the longer I stand in it, the more I feel pulled to the **in-between layers**: not sky, not basin, not beast, not machine.  
A grated walkway above a drowned cut.  
A valve house built into a shoulder-blade of stone-that-isn’t-stone.  
A maintenance bell rung three times before up-rain.

So I think I’d be human, but not in the frontier sense. I’d be one of the station people whose real craft is *listening*—a hum-reader, bolt-keeper, breath-timer. The kind who can press a palm to a handrail and tell whether the tremor is herd-march, pump choke, or oldback exhale coming up through flooded galleries. I’d carry spools of grounding braid, fungus sealant, reed resin, and chalk that still marks in steam. My map wouldn’t be paper; it would be cadence: this whine means safe crossing, that silence means run.

I’d probably live at a ridge transfer station where lightning walks sideways. Not in the bright core, but along the outer service ring where things begin to fail first and therefore tell the truth first. My room would be hung with drying gaskets and moss-insulated tools. Sleep would come in short drops between alarms. I’d know which siren brings gliders, which brings scavenger crabs, which brings the oldback’s deep lift that makes every cup ripple.

And over years, I think I’d become a little composite myself: skin salt-scarred, hearing tuned to bearings, posture unconsciously matching the long respiration underfoot. Less “master of environment” than one more organ in a larger body.

If you asked around, they might call me a **Ridge Cantor**—one of the people who sings maintenance counts into fog so crews can work by rhythm when they can’t see each other.  
If you asked me, I’d say something simpler:

I am where tension gathers and release is negotiated—  
at joints, drains, crossings, thresholds—  
helping the breathing mountains and the built world keep sharing a single pulse.
