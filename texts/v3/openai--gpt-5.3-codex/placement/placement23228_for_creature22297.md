At first, I think I’d be the obvious intruder: a warm-bodied thing entering from above, stirring all the careful lanes into weather.  
But that doesn’t last. This place rejects drama by simply outwaiting it.

Then I think I’d be one of the straps of eelgrass on a step-shelf—small, stubborn, permitted.  
But even that feels too expressive, too visible. The basin isn’t interested in standing out. It rewards what can keep pace with repetition.

So if I’m honest, you’d find me lower down, near the outlet throat where everything narrows and commits.  
Not at the center of the chamber, not on the brightest floor—at that constriction where the current has to decide its shape every second and always makes the same decision. That seems like the truest address in a world like this: the place where continuity is enforced.

And what I’d be is not a singular animal, but a **timing-node** in the colony: one denser pad in the lattice, half in clay seam, half under silt ripple, handling pressure the way a diaphragm handles breath.  
I wouldn’t see much. I’d know the basin by gradients: load, drag, dissolved trace, the weight of afternoon light as warmth that never quite reaches me.  
My thoughts—if they count as thoughts—would be slow pulses:  
hold, release, filter, rest;  
hold, release, filter, rest.

I’d have no interest in crossing distance.  
My version of travel would be a contraction wave moving outward through neighboring tissue, returning altered minutes later with news: grit heavier today, bubbles punctual, wall-lips feeding, margins intact.

If you touched me, I would answer late.  
If you left, I would continue.  
If a century passed, I might still be there—thinner in one seam, thicker in another—less a creature than a maintained interval.

So: in that submerged nave, I’d be found where flow becomes obligation, and I’d be something like **a quiet organ of persistence**—alive exactly to the degree that function and place can no longer be separated.
