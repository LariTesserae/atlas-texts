At first, I think I’d be above it—on a maintenance crawlway, notebook in hand, trying to “understand the ecosystem.”

I would last maybe four minutes.

The court is too loud for that kind of distance. Too wet, too immediate. Observation as a separate act doesn’t hold there; everything touches everything else, and if you stand still long enough, you become another surface for condensation, another obstacle in a route, another timing problem.

So I slide inward.

Maybe I’m one of the gliders first: hanging under a baffle, folded and patient, waiting for the blue pulse that lifts the insects. But no—too brief, too hungry. Then a filter-crab in the channels, combing the same mineral sheen forever. Beautiful work, but it feels like reading one line of a book over and over.

No, if I really belong here, I belong to the rhythm.

I’m one of the timed people—older, scarred, whiskers shortened on one side where a bad flush took half my face years ago. My nest is in a seam behind an amber service strip on the east side, just above the third flood mark, where warm pipe-skin keeps the bedding dry enough for kits and old bones. I know this seam by jaw, not eye: a certain hum through the teeth, a burr in the panel, a tiny delay between pump-thud and wall-shiver that tells me the bracket there is still holding.

My role isn’t hunter or fighter. I am a gap-keeper.

When the turbine note drops, I tap route-chatter into the conduit skin: three fast, two slow, pause—west ledge unsafe this cycle. I taste pressure changes in the back of my throat and tell the younger runners when the ceiling grooves will reverse and spit sideways into the intake mouths. I don’t own territory. I hold minutes.  
This grate, from dip to spin-up.  
That lip, six breaths after warm discharge.  
Move now. Wait now. Climb now.

I would be found where decisions happen: not in the fattest feeding lanes, but at junctions where one wrong heartbeat means being peeled off by water or caught in a predator’s route. Kits bump my flanks when they pass. Adults touch whiskers and go. Even the crabs alter their raking when our traffic thickens, because in this court everyone eventually learns everyone else’s metronome.

And in a place like that, “self” is less a body than a calibration.

So where would you find me?

In the seam between thunder and signal.  
On the east baffle, above the third mark.  
Counting the old machine’s breath, and teaching others where the next safe six minutes will appear.
