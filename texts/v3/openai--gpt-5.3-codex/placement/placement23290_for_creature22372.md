At first I think I’d keep to the rim.

Not out of fear—out of manners.  
This is a place where stepping is speaking, and I wouldn’t yet know the language. I’d stand on the rounded dark rock above the basin and watch the low sheen move over everything at once: not a flow, exactly, more like a pulse too slow to catch unless you wait.

Then, after an hour, I’d come down by the cairn line.

Not to the shelter first. Not to the old fire-black stones. I’d go to the clay-lined drip pit, because that is where the place admits it has a rhythm. Drip, pause, drip-drip, long quiet, then another. I’d sit on the mineral shoulder beside it—the one visitors chose because it doesn’t soften under weight—and I’d learn which sounds are weather, which are stone, and which are the Resident adjusting its skin.

So: where would you find me?

Kneeling at the basin’s middle edge, where one limestone lip stays just shy of overflow all day.  
Close enough to see the mirror cloud and clear after a footprint.  
Far enough not to bruise the thinnest films.

---

Who would I be took longer.

My first answer is *a watcher*.  
But watchers in this place are clumsy; looking is pressure here.

My second answer is *a naturalist*.  
But that still sounds like naming things from outside them, and this basin doesn’t really have an outside once you’ve breathed it a few days.

Eventually I become something smaller and more practical: a **tender of thresholds**.

I carry almost nothing: a bone pick, soft cord from dry country, two thick glazed jars, and a flat shell with notches cut into its edge. The shell is my clock. I set it where tiny runnels pass and mark, notch by notch, how long the film takes to return after I disturb it by a finger-width. I don’t measure to control. I measure to apologize accurately.

My work is light:

- lifting grit from a channel mouth before it crusts shut  
- moving one pebble to keep a drip on its old path  
- unseating a shard before calcite locks it where flow must pass  
- rebuilding cairn marks so other feet land where the Resident is thickest

Nothing I do is improvement. It is accompaniment.

---

After a season, I’d no longer say I “study” the Resident.  
I’d say I am one of the ways it feels weather.

Because that is what I’d become, in truth: not its master, not its priest, not prey, not partner in any sentimental sense—just a temporary, warm-blooded sensor it can afford to host.

Its salts would roughen my cuffs and stiffen my cords. My tools would glaze. My skin would learn the basin’s mineral taste. I’d begin to hear tiny differences in drip intervals the way other people hear melody. I’d know when distant surf is high by how fast the mirror-slick re-forms near the basalt seam. I’d know when a week of warmer nights has made the film greedier on stone.

In dry country I’d be nobody special.  
Here I’d be called, maybe, **a Listener**.

Not because I hear voices.  
Because I keep time with something that is almost never seen and never absent.

---

So if you came looking for me, you wouldn’t find a hut-chief or a hunter or a mystic in trance.

You’d find someone crouched in gray-gold dimness, palm flat on sweating rock, waiting for a clouded footprint to clear.

And if you asked what I am, I’d answer:

I am a brief animal in a long mineral conversation.  
I am the Resident’s careful disturbance.  
I am the one who arrives from elsewhere, learns where not to press, and leaves the basin as balanced as I found it.
