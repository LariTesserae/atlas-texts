At first, I think I’d be something small and close to the ground.

A pale, soft-footed grazer slipping between ribbed trunks before sunrise.  
Or one of the burrowers, blind to horizon but fluent in temperature, turning raw crust into future soil.  
Or even a moth—papery, dusk-bound, a brief body timed exactly to a three-day bloom.

But if I stay with it longer, I end up standing upright in a narrow alley of tamped earth and woven shade, hand on a wall that is both architecture and plant. I am human here—though “human” in this basin means less a separate species than a role in the compact treaty.

I think I would be a **shade measurer** and **dew accountant** for one of the linked nest-districts near the black basalt seams at the basin’s center.

Not an official with a title carved in stone. More like a practical memory: the person who knows where cool collects, where cloth should be rehung after dust-silk season, which courtyard loses darkness twelve breaths earlier after midsummer, which wall-thicket can be cut back without starving the rooftop birds, which family over-dried thornfruit last pulse and needs a quiet redistribution before pride becomes harm.

You’d find me in three places:

- **Before dawn**, on roofs and net-lines, fingertips moving along knots to read wetness by drag and weight. I carry sealed clay tags and fiber cords; each household’s take is marked by texture, not ink. No shouting, no splash, just the small ceremony of transfer: capture, seal, share.
- **At high day**, underground or in sunk courtyards, mapping shade geometry with cord lengths and chalked clay. I work where heat is logic, not drama.
- **At dusk**, walking the wind alleys while people speak in wrist turns and chin angles, collecting tiny complaints before they become resource fractures: a cracked storage jar, a hedge grown too dense for airflow, a predator lane shifted by new construction.

I would wear layered wraps dusted pale in daylight, darker at night, with a condensation veil over mouth and throat. My hands would be callused not from grand labor but from constant small adjustments: tightening a net, retensioning reed lattice, resealing a jar lip with wax-clay, lifting sleeping lizards from a warm cistern rim and setting them back in wall shade.

I wouldn’t be important in a heroic sense. I’d be important in the basin sense: useful to nearby life.

I’d apprentice children in the first three counts before letters:

How long this wall holds shade after noon.  
How many shared breaths remain in a travel skin in crosswind.  
How fast dew vanishes from basalt versus salt brick once first light touches it.

And because this place survives by density, I think I would live in a household that is not “mine” so much as interleaved: an elder seed-counter with brittle knees, two net-menders, a herd minder who sleeps by day in flowering pulse season, three children who can climb like geckos, and a patient partner who trains hedge roots through lattice to make walls that are cooler than stone.

If you came looking for me from outside, you might miss me entirely. I would look like everyone else looks here: efficient, layered, sun-aware, unornamented except for function.

But if you watched long enough, you’d notice my work in absences:

a lane that is five degrees cooler than it should be,  
a district that makes it through long-wind season without ration panic,  
a communal basin that is never full but never empty,  
a predator path that bends around children’s sleeping roofs instead of through them.

So: where in this world would I be?

At the hinge hours.  
Between night water and day heat.  
Between household and household.  
On a roofline just before dawn, listening for the first drip, then deciding—quietly, precisely—where it belongs.
