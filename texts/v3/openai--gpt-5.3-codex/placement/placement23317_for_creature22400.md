At first I think I’d be high up—on a gantry, in wind and fog, one of the people who read the pylon crowns for weather that instruments miss.

Then I think: no, lower. Closer to where the city listens to itself.

So I start walking it in my head: upper spill terraces, warm concrete sweating at dawn, moss walls breathing green in seams, reed beds shivering under distant turbine bass. Every district has a pulse, and every pulse has a use. I keep drifting toward the places built not for movement, but for interpretation: sound-shadow plazas, maintenance alcoves, quarry thresholds where echoes arrive a half-second late like second thoughts.

That’s where I’d end up.

I would be a **resonance clerk**—part signal analyst, part civic witness, part superstition manager. Not glamorous, but essential in a city where alarms are law and vibration is language. My station would sit at the edge of an old quarry cut retrofitted into an acoustic registry office: basalt walls, painted hand-sign lexicon on glazed brick, three metal cups always on the sill to read dusk hum, and a long table where contracts are spoken once, signed twice (ink and gesture), and never spoken casually again.

My job would be to decide what a sound *means* before it becomes a rumor:
- Is that tremor freight lift, gate correction, or bearing fatigue?
- Is that delayed footfall just geometry, or someone shadowing a corridor?
- Did the horn sequence carry the right legal interval, or does it need to be reissued before night shift?

I’d work with old women who can identify districts by condensation smell, teenagers who read rail vibration through their knees, and one patient welder who refuses to make promises underground. We’d keep annotated maps of “returned words” sites—not to prove the phenomenon, but to keep people from being careless where carelessness has consequences.

I’d live in a ring-house built around an obsolete filter core, fourth level, east-facing, where the handrail sweats before sunrise. Two hooks by the door: one for wet layers, one for dry. A fox passes the pipeline shadow below most nights. I’d leave a rag on the stair for whoever’s on dawn valve check, and someone I never quite catch would replace it with a clean one by noon.

If I’m not human in this world—if I get to be something else—then I’d be the **narrow band of quiet** inside a baffle wall where two people lean in and finally hear each other clearly. Not silence, never silence; just enough shelter from thunder for meaning to land.

But as a person: yes, resonance clerk.  
A custodian of intervals.  
Someone who helps the city keep its promises in a place where water remembers every path and stone remembers every load.
