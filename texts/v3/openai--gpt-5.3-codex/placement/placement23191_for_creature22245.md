At first, I think I’d be one of the rare walkers on the dry tier—someone with boots, a lamp, a route map.

But if I stay longer than a shift, that identity starts to peel off.

You’d find me at the **hinge zones**: where a safe walkway narrows above floodstone, where violet maintenance bands turn amber, where the first moss fingers reach for handrails but haven’t crossed yet. Not in the center basin, not fully in the infrastructure ring—at the seam where decisions are made.

I’d begin as a maintenance role, something practical:  
**Gradient Clerk**, maybe.  
A person who logs mist timing, retension tones, and minor reroutes in drainage cadence.

Then, slowly, the basin would absorb the job description.

I would stop “checking systems” and start **listening to weather that has intentions**.  
I’d learn that a two-second delay in runoff at Tier 3 is not malfunction but refusal.  
That a warmed rail-light isn’t heat bleed; it’s a warning.  
That the root curtain lifting off a hatch is not growth drift; it’s courtesy.

After enough seasons, I wouldn’t feel like a resident of the Greenworks so much as one of its temporary organs: a mobile patch of warmth and hands, useful because I can go where roots cannot quickly go. I’d carry spores in my cuffs, mineral dust in my lungs, and updated flow habits in my notebooks like liturgy.

So what would I be?

Not its master.  
Not its engineer in the old sense.  
Not exactly its priest, either.

I’d be its **interpreter limb**—  
a bipedal subsystem the basin grew by keeping me alive in it.

If locals named me at all, it would be something plain:

**the Walked Channel**.

A person-shaped conduit through which the rooted city and the unrooted world can still exchange meaning.
