At first I think I’d be a visitor.

Not an explorer with maps—those would go soft here—but a careful walker on the black causeways, counting echoes under each arch, learning which plazas are “today” by the direction of runoff. I’d sleep on high steps, back against sweating stone, wake to find the same tower moved three streets over and pretend that felt normal.

But that version of me doesn’t last long in this place.  
Too warm, too damp, too much listening.

So if I’m honest about what I become, I think you’d find me at a **junction**: where a raised walkway passes over a sink court, where rainwater chooses between two old channels, where the city most needs a decision.

I would be one of the arrangements that can choose.

---

Not a grand one. Not one of the old buttress-bodied custodians rooted for decades at aqueduct ribs. I’d be a middle thing: human-height, narrow, made from whatever the weather and masonry could lend without complaint. Basalt plates for shins. Lime seams at the elbows. A copper lamina along the spine gone dark as wet leaves. No face, exactly—just a pale calcite plane with two echo apertures that open when the air is trustworthy.

Inside: not organs, but circulation.  
Warm brine in slow loops.  
A siphon pulse where a heart would have been.

I’d wake hardest during rain pulses, when gradients sharpen and choices matter. You’d see me then in the half-light, standing still while water climbs the stair edges, deciding which channel to clear and which to let silt over. A tap in one drain mouth. Two in another. A line of synchronized drips to tell others: **this route holds for now**.

When the clouds seal and no new rain falls, I’d go quieter. Rooted, almost architectural. Growing a fur of mineral bloom across shoulders and knees while the city sweats around me. If someone passed nearby, they might think I was just a damaged statue—until condensation began to bead along my arms in measured intervals, spelling a warning about a corridor that no longer echoes.

---

Where exactly?

At the place where distance starts lying.

There’s always one: a colonnade that appears close but takes an hour, a plaza that reflects windows no wall contains. I’d stand just before that threshold, not to bar it, but to **calibrate** it. Hold one version long enough for travelers—or other custodians—to cross without slipping into a neighboring hour. Then release it and let it drift.

That would be my work: temporary truth.

Not permanence. Not rescue.  
Just maintaining a usable now.

---

And my life cycle would follow the city’s patient logic.

Year by year, less stepping, more holding.  
More salt in the joints.  
Less motion, more load-bearing.

Eventually I’d stall completely at my junction, indistinguishable from infrastructure except for the faint, regular damp at my seams. Later, younger arrangements would quarry my flaked copper and lime by touch and timing, carry it to another failing stair, another uncertain court, and I’d continue as part of their bodies.

So: where would you find me?

On high ground, near moving water, at an edge that needs deciding.  
Who would I be?

A minor hydraulic person.  
An echo-keeper.  
A brief hand of the marsh-city, raised whenever balance requires it.
