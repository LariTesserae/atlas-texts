At first I think I’d be something airborne there—one of those thin-voiced marsh birds writing temporary calligraphy over black stone.

Then I think: no, I’d be ground-level. Something that understands delay. A lichen, maybe, spreading one patient millimeter across a seam where engineered basalt meets older bedrock.

But if I let it settle a little longer, I end up in between those scales.

---

You’d find me on the **exposed spans between thermal districts**, not inside the warm cores.  
On the shoulders of causeways at blue morning.  
Under transit pylons where condensation falls in a steady metronome.  
At migration crossings where everyone—tram, herd, courier, stormwater—has to negotiate turn-taking.

I’d be one of the people whose work is mostly invisible when it works: an **interval steward**.  
Part surveyor, part mediator, part translator between living routes and engineered ones.

Not a ranger in the romantic sense. Not an office analyst either.  
Boots in peat, yes. Also a wristful of municipal feeds.

My kit would be plain:

- a frost probe
- a mineral-strip roll for pooled water
- a field slate that overlays herd telemetry with drainage predictions
- a hand-vibe tuner for pylon resonance
- bright markers to signal temporary right-of-way changes in whiteout
- and a thermos that tastes faintly of metal no matter what you put in it

---

My day would start before full light in a shared storm room, with the wall showing three forecasts side by side:

1. atmospheric weather  
2. surface behavior (freeze-thaw, seep rise, skin-ice risk)  
3. movement forecasts (grazers, burrowers, commuter load, freight urgency)

Then out.

Walk the embankments. Check where fox-sized burrowers have warmed new hollows near utility ribs. Flag it so maintenance swarms route wide and don’t collapse dens. Scan amphibian bask sites near geothermal leaks; adjust nearby pedestrian advisories. Confirm that a channel opened overnight where the drainage AI said it would, and note the place where it didn’t, because peat is beginning there and peat changes everything slowly but absolutely.

By noon I’m usually at a crossing node, reading small signs:
- birds flying lower than yesterday
- the color shift in a seep that says mineral load just changed upstream
- a silence in one sedge belt that means predator presence, likely brief, still enough to delay a tram ribbon six minutes

People get annoyed for those six minutes.  
Then they see tracks in the wet stone later and stop being annoyed.

---

So what would I be?

A human, yes—but not singular.  
In this place no one is singular for long.

I’d be a person with a district badge and municipal liability insurance, and also a moving edge of several systems: ecological data, civic trust, animal custom, machine intent. I’d carry an AI model that does not decide for me but argues with me politely in my earpiece. I’d publish every change I make so anyone can inspect the reasoning. Opacity would feel like a moral failure.

Children would sometimes walk with me on training days. I’d show them how to see thaw lines from ten meters away by sheen alone. How to hear when membrane roofs are rattling with rain versus seedfall. How “on time” can mean “after the herd clears” without meaning “late.”

---

If you looked for my home, it wouldn’t be a dramatic tower.  
It would be in a dense warm block with a public drying gallery and shared tool wall, near a maglev stop but not too near. Boots by the door. Maps everywhere. A shelf of stones labeled with dates because even engineered geology deserves anniversaries.

And if you asked what I believed, it would be simple:

Nothing here is finished, so nothing here is exempt.  
Not transit schedules, not nesting grounds, not district plans, not my own habits.  
Everything gets revised by weather, by evidence, by return.

So where would you find me?

At the moment just before systems touch—  
wet boots on cold aggregate, one glove off, hand on a vibrating rail,  
listening for whether today the plateau wants passage, pause, or reroute.
