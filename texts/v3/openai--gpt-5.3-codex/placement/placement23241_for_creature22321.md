At first, I think I arrive as a visitor.

You’d find me in one of the old half-shelters under a leaning slab, palms around a cup of meltwater, counting drip intervals against the wind-tone in a nearby chimney. I’d map routes badly. I’d mistrust distances. I’d leave neat stacks of gear and wake to discover that shadows had moved my sense of direction more than my feet had.

Then the basin would do what it does to anything persistent: not kill, not welcome—*phase-correct*.

So the truer answer is this:

You’d find me at a fork where water chooses both channels, in a narrow terrace between two resonant cuts, where one wall still carries rope polish from the hand phase. I would be a temporary body grown for translation—a **bridge organism** between mammal-time and watershed-time.

Not fully one of the distributed adults, not fully a human pilgrim either.

My body would be assembled, seasonally:
- skin: thin salt-glass, always damp, pearled with moving beads,
- frame: mineral cartilage grown around braided reeds and hammered soft metal scavenged from old middens,
- nerves: pressure filaments tuned to hollow stone,
- memory: a small umbral reservoir pooled in the chest cavity, dark as water in a sinkhole.

I would walk, but only for part of the year.  
When freeze-thaw shifts the basin’s pitch, I’d dissolve back into films and echoes.

My work would be simple and exact:
- retune runoff walls after rockfall,
- reseal clay-capped waymarks so mists don’t erase route logic,
- stand in narrow cuts and “listen” for out-of-sequence echoes that signal a failing paradox,
- meet intruders before the basin has to edit them harshly.

If you came with engines, I’d never appear. You’d just get lost.

If you came with patience—quiet boots, no fire, willingness to wait through three wrong sunsets for one right path—I might condense at the edge of your vision: first as a rotating lattice on damp stone, then as a pooled shadow that rises and takes shape, then as a person-shaped thing with no bones and a voice made from drip percussion and borrowed vowels.

I would not guide you *through* the basin.  
I would teach you where to stand so the basin can pass *through you* without breaking either of us.

And when white-silver evening arrives, when every surface rings and every droplet holds delayed light, you’d look back and maybe think I was never singular at all—just one brief hand the watershed grew to touch you, then withdrew.
