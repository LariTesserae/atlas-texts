At first, I think I’d try to be useful in the obvious ways.

I’d imagine myself as a Latchwright—arms full of pins and resin, swearing at a bracket that slipped twice in one shift.  
Then as a Root-Mesh Gardener, kneeling in the slow-water strips, combing silt with the care of someone brushing hair from a sleeping child’s face.  
Then as a Flow Cantor, ribcage against a trembling alcove wall, humming a lane open where yesterday there was only force.

But if I stay in your ward long enough, what keeps pulling me is not force. It’s *interval*.

So I think I would be found in one of the narrow acoustic galleries—those service corridors between the school domes and the old tram-tunnel manifolds, where sound from three districts overlaps and arrives slightly out of phase. Not center. Not edge. A hinge.

I’d be a **Tonekeeper** (or whatever the ward’s dialect calls the people who listen for silence before instruments do).

---

My station would look unimpressive: a rail, a slate panel, two redundant clocks, a pressure cup clipped to my belt, a rack of paper logs sealed in waxed sleeves.  
No grand console. No dramatic lever.

But I’d spend shifts building and updating the ward’s **living baseline**—the expected thrum-stack for this tide, this temperature, this silt load, this maintenance cycle. I’d know that at third amber, the eastern valve battery should strike a half-beat late because a housing cracked three months ago and was patched, not replaced. I’d know that in storm week the Choir’s upper harmonics go grainy for eleven minutes near noon and that it is normal, not panic.

I’d be the person apprentices roll their eyes at until the day I grab their sleeve and say, “Stop. Hear that gap?”  
And the gap is two seconds of missing hiss from a microperforated wall six blocks away.  
And two seconds is enough to save forty people.

---

I don’t think I’d be fully comfortable anywhere dry after that.

I’d sleep in a pressure dome with a faint pipe-vibration in the wall because total stillness would feel like threat. I’d drink tea from weighted cups with elders who argue whether the thermal bloom sounds “bright” today. I’d teach children the first impossible lesson: that underwater, nearness lies, distance lies, and yet pattern tells the truth.

I’d probably keep one private ritual: at shift start, palm flat to the rail, eyes closed, counting not loud sounds but absences between them.  
Because this place survives in those seams.

---

And maybe, over years, the role would change me in small Twice-Fixed ways.

My hearing would become less like hearing and more like pressure literacy.  
My voice would get quieter.  
My friendships would be with people who don’t ask “How are you?” but “What did the west trunk sound like at dawn?”  
I’d start naming the inspection swarms, despite myself.  
I’d thank a maintenance arm when it retracts cleanly after a dangerous vent.  
I’d mean it.

Eventually people might say I’m “Choir-adjacent,” half-joking, because I can predict reroutes before the veils repaint. But I wouldn’t pretend I *am* the Choir. I’d be one human node in the metabolism: fragile, replaceable, attentive.

---

So where would you find me in that world?

In the loudest place where a tiny silence can still be heard.  
Clipped to structure and field.  
One hand on rail, one on logbook.  
Listening for the moment the ward stops speaking—so everyone else has time to keep living.
