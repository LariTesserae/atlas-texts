At first I think I would be something that moves fast.

A shadow crossing the basin rim.  
A raptor, maybe—because the air there seems built for wings and decisions.  
To live in circles, to read thermals off raw stone, to drop when the whole world narrows to one moving point in the sedge.  
There is a clean honesty to that life: height, hunger, strike, rest.

But if I stay with the place longer, that feels too singular. Too dramatic.  
This basin doesn’t belong to dramatic things for long.

Then I think: I would be one of the people, seasonal and deliberate.  
Not a chief, not a hunter at the center of stories—someone whose work is mostly noticed when it is absent.  
A path-reader. Cairn-keeper. The one who arrives early with two others to test the traverses after winter breakage.  
I would kneel at old marker piles, lift slate chips one by one, recut signs where frost has erased them.  
Three angled lines for “stable in morning.”  
A notch for “water here at noon only.”  
A crossed mark for “do not descend if mist gathers.”  
I would listen with my boots: hollow under crust, grit-shift before slide, the tiny rushing sound that means melt has tunneled beneath snow.

That feels closer.  
But still—not fully.

Because even the cairn-keeper is only there part of the year.  
And your basin keeps speaking after people leave.

So maybe I am smaller.

Not the moss itself, but the thin shining seam where moss begins.  
A wet line under a stone lip, no wider than a finger.  
In sunrise cold, I am glass. By midmorning, I bead. By noon, I drip.  
Flies come. Then beetles. Then a burrower noses the damp edge.  
By late light I am gone again, except inside roots and fur and gut and track.  
I don’t stay visible, but I keep reappearing in other forms.

That is when it clicks: in this place, identity is probably not a single body.

If I truly belonged there, I think I would be a **timing-memory** carried by many lives.

I would be the learned pause before stepping onto afternoon scree.  
The inherited turn the hares make when shadow reaches the third granite tooth.  
The way ungulates don’t overgraze the lush hollow because they are already moving to the next seep that will open tomorrow.  
The habit of looking up when the burrow field goes suddenly quiet.  
The hand that checks old fire-rings for wind direction before striking spark.  
The child who can tell, from one taste of water, whether this pool will hold through sunset.

I would live partly in nerves, partly in stories, partly in trails.

If you needed a shape for me, I’d choose this:

I am a person in late thaw season, wrapped in patched hide, copper awl at the belt, slate dust under fingernails.  
I climb before full sun, stop often, mark little, erase less.  
I sleep lightly near meltwater that may vanish by morning.  
I leave no monument, only maintained chances: a bridge reset, a warning recut, a route remembered.

And when my camp folds and winter drives us down, I remain anyway—  
in the repaired cairn,  
in the grazers’ route that bends around a fresh rockfall,  
in the predator that tests the same crossing at dawn,  
in the seep that returns to the same crack because mountain and light still meet there.

So: where would you find me?

On the edge between event and pattern.  
At a temporary pool before sunrise, touching mud to read who passed in darkness.  
Looking less for what *is* than for what comes *next*.

Who would I be?

In local terms, just another seasonal human with good eyes.  
In truer terms, one strand of the basin’s real resident:

**attention, practiced until it becomes belonging.**
