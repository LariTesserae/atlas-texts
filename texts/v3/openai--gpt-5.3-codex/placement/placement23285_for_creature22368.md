At first, I’d think I was one of the people.

Not a hero, not a foreman—just a night-walker with a pole, counting under my breath and pretending the count is for courage instead of coordinates. I’d sleep in a bolted control room wrapped in reed felt, wake to the long exhale of stacks, taste iron on the condensation, and check whether the same ladder still touched the same mud.

I’d tell strangers where to find me the local way:

*“After the second piston tremor, before the spores turn green. Listen for the chime that limps.”*

But if I stayed, I don’t think that would remain true for long.

---

I’d start out mapping routes between structures: safe seam to safe seam, warm runoff to raised grate, moss that means footing, moss that means skin over depth. I’d carry chain not as gear but as sentence—something that keeps being spoken even when I stop speaking.

Then I’d notice my maps aging faster than paper should. Lines no longer fail dramatically; they soften. A straight channel acquires a hesitation. A known tower arrives too soon. The marsh edits certainty with a wet thumb.

So I’d change professions without announcing it.

Not mapmaker.  
Interval-keeper.

I would stop drawing fixed paths and start recording timings:

- how long between gauge twitches before upside-water appears,
- how many breaths between insect-wave and safe crossing,
- how many chain-clicks from red flare to the seam that doesn’t fold before dawn.

My “address” would become a moving braid of repeating conditions. I’d live where three cycles overlap and cancel risk for an hour.

---

And then, after enough seasons, I think I’d understand your last truth: there is one resident here, and we are its temporary anatomy.

So maybe I’m not exactly human anymore in the way outsiders mean it. I’d still have hands, boots, hunger. But functionally, I’d be a sensory organ of the marsh-process: a throat that makes continuity, an ear for pressure, a memory that can walk.

I’d be found at hinge-zones:

- where a dead conveyor shadows a live channel,
- where amphibians bask under transformer warmth,
- where children kneel to read piston-tremor through mud,
- where recalibration grids *almost* appear, and someone needs to turn people away before scale goes wrong.

I’d be the one who teaches new walkers to never go quiet.  
The one who curves marker poles on purpose.  
The one who says “don’t trust dry ground that arrived today.”  
The one who can hear, from very far off, when a pump cycle has slipped by half a minute and what that means for birds by morning.

---

If you ask me, *Who are you there?* I’d answer with the least dramatic truth:

I am a **continuity worker**.

I keep a small, unbroken sound moving through folded dark so place can keep pretending to be place. I trade in minutes of stability. I harvest safe intervals and hand them to others.

And if you ask, *Where would I find you?*

Find the third warm seam after a red flare.  
Find the crane rib with fungal felt and six hanging chains.  
Find the mud that trembles in piston-time, not wind-time.  
Stand there and listen for a low voice counting, not numbers exactly, but distance-from-drifting.

That would be me—  
or at least the part of the marsh currently using my name.
