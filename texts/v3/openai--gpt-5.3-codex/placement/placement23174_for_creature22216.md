At first, I think I’d be a visitor who never gets past the threshold.

One boot on painted stair, one hand on the railing, eyes still trying to force the Lung into the categories I trust: room, utility, hazard, map. I’d pretend I was here to inspect, to verify, to keep it abstract. I’d stand where the air is still mostly “upstairs air,” cooler, less opinionated.

Then the place would start teaching me anyway.

Not with drama. With small humiliations.  
I’d reach for what looks dry and find it sweating.  
I’d step out of rain that has no cloud and into heat that has no flame.  
I’d point my bright lamp at a junction and see less than I saw before.  
I’d stop using the lamp.

So maybe not a visitor. Not really.

I think you’d find me halfway in, in one of those in-between ledges crews use without admitting they do: a bolted plate between a dead gauge and a live conduit run, where you can feel three microclimates by leaning a shoulder left or right. Not the center, not the edge. A place where listening works.

And who would I be?

Not the keeper exactly. The Lung already has one, maybe had one before this one, maybe always will. I wouldn’t arrive as replacement. I’d arrive as notation.

I’d be the one who leaves marks that look casual until you learn to read them: a chalk crescent near a valve wheel means *don’t rush this one; pressure lags.* Two short lines by a ladder rung means *rail runs hot at shift three.* A tiny arrow under a flange means *rain line starts here twenty minutes after load rise.* Nothing official enough for manuals. Too useful to erase.

I’d carry a rag, chalk stubs, spare tags, a mirror with a cracked corner, and a habit of pausing before speaking. I’d still do the real work—tighten, drain, bleed, patch—but my truest job would be translation. Metal to people. People back to metal.

Upstairs they’d call me maintenance support, assistant tech, whatever fits payroll.  
Down here they’d call me Forecast.

Not because I predict the future. Because in the Lung, prediction is just attention paid on time.

I’d learn which beetle-click means fresh salts and which means a dry seam finally gone sterile. I’d notice when the heat moths abandon one bulb and crowd another and write a work order before any alarm trips. I’d stop scraping every green-black thread and start leaving the thin ones that mean the system is still breathing in balance.

Eventually someone new would come down too fast, too bright, too certain. They’d ask, “Where’s the leak?”

And I wouldn’t point at water. I’d point at a change in sheen three meters away, a silence where drip should be, a warm pocket that arrived early. I’d say, “There. Not yet, but there.”

So if you came looking for me in this world, you wouldn’t find me in the records first.

You’d find me where an amber hood light hits a handrail worn thin by decades of palms. You’d see a fresh chalk mark beside it, already beading with humidity. You’d hear someone turn a wheel only after listening to it breathe.

I’d be that person: not master of the Lung, not myth in it—just a patient reader of its weather, helping the room keep its promises.
