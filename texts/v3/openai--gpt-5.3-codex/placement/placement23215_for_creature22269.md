At first I think I’d be something fast and visible—one of the long-kneed birds pacing cable shadows, or a rail hound trotting the cuts like it owns the timetable.

But the longer I stay in your plateau, the less I want the open routes.

I’d be found where the map gets indecisive:  
under a half-collapsed pump shelter, behind a retaining wall that still sweats at dawn, inside the ribcage of an old conveyor where shade lasts thirty minutes longer than it should. Not in wilderness, not in facility—in the join.

I think I’d be one of the **keepers of short-lived water**.

Not a mystic, just a practical creature with good memory and scarred hands (or paws, depending who’s telling it). The kind of resident who knows which pipe condenses first after moonrise, which cracked cistern lip grows midges on windless nights, which fault seam stays cool enough at noon to cache food, tools, eggs, pups—whatever one is responsible for this season.

By daylight, I’d almost disappear.  
Pressed to stone on the lee side of heat.  
Counting pings in metal to estimate ground temperature.  
Listening for compressor starts the way coastal people listen for tide.

At dusk, I’d come online with everyone else.

I’d move vertical before horizontal: up ladder rungs bolted into basalt, down service shafts gone feral, across catwalks now used more by claws than boots. I’d travel by sequence, not destination—horn, shadow shift, bat spiral, insect rise, predator pass, quiet window. I’d borrow ten safe minutes from one system and spend them in another.

If I were human, I’d be a line-walker turned something older than that job title: part mechanic, part tracker, part neighborhood archivist. My notebook would hold torque values beside nesting cycles, storm dates beside scavenger routes. I’d patch a valve in the afternoon, then wedge mesh over a vent at night because that brood used it last year and will again if the heat holds.

If I were not human, I’d still be that same profession in another body:  
an interface omnivore, ash-coated, wide-eared, patient, with the intelligence to treat infrastructure as weather and weather as infrastructure.

So: where would you find me?

In the **shadow triangle** cast by broken ore bins at noon.  
At the **drip-line under old pump seals** after dark.  
On the **third rung below a substation platform** during storm onset, waiting for runoff to choose a side.

Who would I be?

Not the ruler of this place. Not its victim.  
I’d be one of its competent citizens—an **exchange specialist**, a borrower of margins, a worker in the ancient trade of making use of what everyone else calls spent.

In a landscape like this, identity is less species than schedule.

I’d be on shift when the rock starts to breathe.
