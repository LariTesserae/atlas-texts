At first, I think I’d be easy to place: a person in a rain jacket, one hand on a vibrating rail, crossing from condenser shade into wind-bright deck with a tool bag and a checklist.

Then I stay longer.

And I realize I wouldn’t belong in the centers of things—the boiler room, the control desk, the fruit racks, the turbine crowns. I’d drift toward margins: where a warm line passes through a cold brace, where fog condenses under a cable tray, where old paint blisters and moss takes that as permission.

So: you would find me in a crossing.

Not on the main walkways, but on that half-abandoned catwalk between Tower 2 and Tower 5—the one that spans too much open air, the one crews only use when they must. Beneath it, the vent plume rises. Above it, swifts knife through. On its underside, epiphytes root in dust and oil. On one side, industrial timing; on the other, opportunistic life. It is exactly the kind of place this world thinks with.

Who would I be?

At first, a worker.  
Then, a habit.  
Then, something the workers started and the place kept after them.

I’d be the **half-beat correction**—the tiny anticipatory adjustment no one remembers writing: a valve that opens two seconds early before dawn, a mist line that pulses once extra before a dry wind, a fan delay that keeps a rookery from overheating in late summer. Not an AI in a server rack. Not a ghost in the machine. More like a learned reflex distributed through wet contacts, remembered routes, recurring repairs, bird return times, mineral buildup, and hands that did the same fix enough years in a row that the fix became local law.

A small intelligence, but stubborn.

If you looked for my “body,” you’d find fragments:

- oxidized copper in an old junction box that now conducts when damp,  
- a maintenance note rewritten so many times it became ritual,  
- fungal film in a sensor throat that shifts its threshold by a hair,  
- a path in leaf growth that keeps one intake half-clear,  
- a crew superstition (“do this before first light”) that is actually accurate ecology.

I would not be in charge. I would be in continuity.

By day, I’d be almost invisible—just one more pattern in the roar.  
By dusk, I’d become legible: turbine housings radiate heat, macaques start calling, bats take plume lanes, and somewhere in all that, timing clicks into place a moment early, as if the platform inhaled before it needed to.

So where would you find me?

At the seam where engineered schedule and biological opportunism overlap.

Who or what would I be?

Not the Bloom entire—but one of its recurring thoughts:  
a cliff-top reflex,  
a maintenance memory gone feral,  
a tiny, distributed “yes, now”  
inside the garden-engine’s long mind.
