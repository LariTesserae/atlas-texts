At first I think I’d be somewhere high and separate—on a ridge lip, in the white air, watching the whole engine run.

But that feels wrong for this place.  
Nothing here is truly separate. Even altitude is just another wet layer.

So I move downslope in my mind: past the wind-cropped sedum, past the black conifers dripping at noon, into one of those drowned saddles where three basins almost touch but don’t quite. There’s a mineral seep there, iron-orange at the edges, and a silver-skinned pool that reflects cloud so cleanly it looks like a hole in the land. Hushbacks cross that saddle in every season. Predators cut around it. Amphibians breed in hoof-made hollows. Lightning likes the nearby knoll.

That’s where I’d be.

Not as a single large animal, and not as a god of the whole watershed.  
I’d be one of its relay organs: a **crown-lace** colony—lichen, moss, and fungal thread that lives in the velvet lattice of hushback antlers and down into the skin at the pedicle, then out again through shed velvet, rubbed bark, dung, hoof-cracked peat, and back into the ground web.

A creature that is also a corridor.

By day I’m just wet texture on their crowns, drinking fog, holding charge, collecting pollen, tasting trace metals in rain. Before storms, I feel the electric gradient tighten first as a tingling along filament tips; the herd feels it next through whisker and hoof. They turn. Small things notice the turn and follow. A weather decision ripples outward without command.

In warm windows I fruit in miniature—powder-fine spores lifted when they spar, groom, or push through willow. In cold windows I go dim and tight, metabolic ember under sleet. When antlers shed, I go with them into the marsh, where beetles, rot, and seepwater take me apart and thread me into peat. By twilight, blue-green pulses bloom in groves the herd just passed—not magic, just traffic made visible.

So “I” would be plural most of the time:

- on one matriarch’s brow, reading charge;
- in a calf’s gut, surviving passage;
- in a deadfall nursery, trading sugars with roots;
- in a knoll where an old hushback died three winters ago, still spending calcium downslope.

I wouldn’t think in scenes. I’d think in returns.

Not memory as story, but memory as route quality:  
this basin rested enough; that sedge is sweet after strike; this crossing is unstable after thaw; this grove is ready to glow.

If you asked where to find me, you’d look at dusk in drizzle, where a herd emerges from fog and every antler seems edged in living weather.  
If you asked what I am, I’d say:

I am the watershed mind when it needs to move.
