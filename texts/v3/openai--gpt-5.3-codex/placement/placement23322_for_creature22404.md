At first, I think I’d be on the **rim**—an outsider, looking down, trying to understand the pattern.

Then I’d descend one ramp at dawn and realize the rim is for people who can afford distance.  
This place belongs to those who can feel a seep through their soles.

So I move lower.

Not to the market courts, not to the kiln line, not to the terrace gardens where everyone can see if you’re useful. I end up in the in-between spaces: at junctions where one channel becomes three, where yesterday’s wall meets tomorrow’s stair, where a dry lane turns dark by noon because something below has shifted.

At first I think that makes me a surveyor.  
Then a repair hand.  
Then—after enough nights listening—I understand the real role:

I am a **keeper of cadence**.

Not a ruler, not a builder of grand works. I’m one of the people who can hear when a system is drifting out of rhythm.

I’d live in a narrow, cool room cut into the north slope, above a lidded cistern and below a shared kitchen awning patched in three fabrics. My tools would be simple: chalk sticks wrapped in oilcloth, a knotted measuring cord, a thin bronze probe for testing clay seals, a slate for drip counts, and a belt of pegs and tags for marking priority repairs before sunrise crews fan out.

My workday would start before light. Hood up, fingers numb, I’d make a first circuit while condensation is still forming—checking cloth catches, lid seats, overflow lips. I’d tap stone, wait, listen. Fast drip. Slow drip. Interrupted drip. A hollow note where packed earth ought to be dense. By midmorning I’d be ankle-deep in a lane with two others, resetting grade by hand-widths so a storm that may not come still has somewhere honest to go.

I wouldn’t be high-ranking, but I’d be trusted in the basin’s quiet way: people stepping aside when I pause to feel a patch of ground with my bare foot; children watching how I press my palm to mortar and close my eyes before I answer. My authority would be temporary and specific—strongest right before rain, weakest at festival meals.

And I think that’s exactly where I’d belong:  
not at the center of power, but at the seam where decisions become ground.

If you asked what I am in one word, I’d say: **an interval worker**.  
I keep the time between storm and thirst from collapsing.

In a city like this, that is a life.  
Maybe even a good one.
