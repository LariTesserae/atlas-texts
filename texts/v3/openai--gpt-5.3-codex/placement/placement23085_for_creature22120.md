At first I think I’d be out on the ridges—some lone walker on wet basalt, following the glint of springs from basin to basin.

But if I stay longer, I don’t think I remain a ridge person.

I end up in town, at a junction no one romanticizes: where three raised paths meet above a settling court, where overflow grates sing in different notes depending on silt load, where everyone passes and no one lingers unless something is wrong. A practical center. A listening place.

I’d start as a helper with a reed rake and a wedge-knife, sent to clear the throat of side channels, scrape moss from lipstones, re-pack clay seals where roots have pried a seam open. Wet to the knees by morning. Mud under nails always. Learning by touch before sight: which stones tremble under pressure, which plaster sounds hollow, which trickle has shifted a hand-width since last week.

And slowly, without ceremony, I’d become one of their small necessary specialists:

a **reader of flow-paths**.  
Not an engineer in the grand sense—more like a translator between water and people.

I’d keep a wall of marks in lime plaster under a roofed stair:
- spring heights by week,
- first fog dates on each ridge,
- culvert blockages by lane,
- reed-bed recovery times,
- where a path stays slick even after resurfacing.

When disputes rise—upstream lane says they cleared, downstream lane says they received rot—I’d walk the route with both houses, kneel, probe, smell the water, and tell them where the neglect actually sits. In this world, that is legal work, social work, and moral work all at once.

I think my voice would get quieter here. Not from timidity—just adaptation.  
I’d speak in short phrases over stone: “Left rail by noon,” “third grate after rain,” “don’t cut that root, it binds the wall.”  
And I’d become someone children shadow for a season, learning the city as gradients and textures rather than street names.

If I had a title, it might be something plain and exact: **Lane-Water Keeper**.  
If I had a reputation, I’d want it to be this: *paths passable, basins clear, no surprises*.

So: where would you find me?

At the hinge points—stairs, grates, culvert mouths, reed edges.  
At the places where continuity is decided in handfuls of silt.

Who would I be?

Not founder, not ruler, not visionary.  
A citizen of maintenance, fully formed by this wet highland city—  
one more human habit that keeps water moving and the next season legible.
