I wouldn’t arrive there as a hero, or even as a resident.  
I’d arrive as a **mistake in timing**—a lowland mapper who kept trying to force noon-logic onto a late-afternoon world.

At first you’d find me in the obvious places: on exposed parapets, in broken plazas, staring too long at gates that stretch away when watched. I’d keep redrawing the same square and blaming my instruments. I’d sleep badly from the thin air, wake with dust in my teeth, and spend whole mornings trying to prove that “uphill” still meant something permanent.

Then, slowly, you’d find me somewhere else: not at landmarks, but at **transitions**.  
At the lip where a stairfield becomes scree.  
At the point where a hard-edged shadow first starts to detach.  
At the first grate where morning dust begins to flow like water.

That’s where this city tells the truth—never in its monuments, always in its seams.

---

Who would I be?

Not exactly one of the shard-keepers, at least not by blood. I think I’d become something adjacent: a **listener of lag**.

My work would be simple to describe and difficult to do: I would walk the unstable corridors without looking at their endpoints, count my breaths between fixed points, and mark drift in stacked slivers of glass and bone. Not to freeze the city—nothing here can be frozen—but to keep its changes inside livable margins.

If a route gains twelve breaths over two days, I’d note it.  
If a dropped pebble climbs three handspans before surrendering to gravity, I’d note it.  
If the page-turning beneath the old precincts comes half a beat early at dusk, I’d note it.

I would carry no map anyone else could read. Mine would look like ritual notation: wind direction, moon phase, shadow stiffness, dust tone, echo delay. A practical mysticism. Or maybe a mystical practicality.

---

I don’t think I’d live in one home. I’d keep to lee-side courts and half-roofed vault throats, wherever the night cold can be survived with minimal fuel. My shelter would leave no mark by morning except corrected stones: one cairn rebalanced, one loose lintel shimmed, one wind-baffle angle changed by a finger’s width.

Food would be sparse and repetitive. Frost scraped at dawn. Mineral bloom traded in cloth packets. Trap-lines checked every third evening where cliff-runners cross old civic edges for reasons older than appetite. Hunger wouldn’t be dramatic there; it would be administrative.

---

And after enough seasons, I suspect identity would thin out, as everything does at altitude.

I would stop saying “I maintain this route,” and start saying “this route is currently maintaining through me.”  
I’d become less like a person with a job, more like a temporary organ in a larger body: a pulse-point where the city measures itself.

In that sense, maybe what I’d be is this:

A **minor civic function** in a major lithic mind.  
A caretaker of proportion.  
A witness paid in continuity.

If you wanted to find me, you wouldn’t look for a house or a name.  
You’d wait near an old square at dusk, where shadows harden wrong, and watch for someone placing three uneven shards at a corner no one else notices—then stepping back, listening, and deciding whether reality needs one more nudge tonight.
