At first, I think I’d be one of the crossings people: hood down, face wrapped in salt cloth, moving ridge to ridge with a coil of split-fiber rope and a bundle of tuning forks in a copper tube.

Not a hunter, not a builder, not a priest exactly.

A **listener** hired for difficult routes.

I’d live in the small places the wind permits: cut pockets under overhangs, dry shelters where two people can sit shoulder to shoulder and finally hear each other breathe. I’d leave before attachment hardened. In that country, attachment is a structural risk.

---

But if I stay with it longer, I don’t think “traveler” is precise enough.

I’d be found mostly at thresholds: where a plateau path drops into a trough, where a duplicated ravine confuses direction, where yesterday’s ridgeline and this year’s ridgeline disagree by six steps and a body-length of fear. I’d carry no drawn map. I’d carry a sequence of notes. At each junction I’d hang or retune a resonator—bone, shell, copper, glass—so the route can be *heard* even when sight lies.

My work would be to keep the pitches honest.

When stone hum rises out of tune, I’d mark that ground as temporary.  
When a valley goes silent at dawn, I’d close it.  
When a path starts arriving at itself, I’d alter the song so no one walks the loop twice.

---

And then, if I let the world decide what I am instead of insisting—

I become less a person moving through the country, and more an instrument the country uses.

I sleep beside pillar-groves to learn their charge-cycle crackle. I press an ear to mineral plates and wait for subharmonics that mean “don’t camp here.” I can tell which frond-colonies are about to vent spores by the faint brightening in their salt veins. My language thins: fewer nouns, more intervals. I stop saying “that cliff is stable” and start saying “it’s holding low third for now.”

People would come to me not for directions, but for timing.

*Cross tomorrow before noon; the basin is still coherent.*  
*Wait three nights; the echo is running late in that corridor.*  
*Take the long shelf; the short cut has split into two futures.*

---

Eventually I think I’d be called something like a **Chord-Keeper** or **Interval Warden**—one of those jobs that sounds ceremonial until you realize it keeps children alive.

I would not own land. I would steward a moving band of it: a chain of chimes, shelters, and seed caches stretching from one star-fixed bearing to another. My oath would be exactly the kind they favor there: *until the note changes.*

And when my hearing began to go—because everyone’s does in that endless roar—I’d take an apprentice into the lee of a shrine and teach the route-songs by touch on the wrist and throat, pulse and breath, letting them feel where each pitch lives in the body when ears are unreliable.

---

So where would you find me?

Not at the center. There is no center.  
Not in the lush colonies. They don’t need me.  
Not in still valleys.

You’d find me at a windbreak of stacked stone on a high shelf at false dusk, listening to three ridgelines argue about which one is present, then tapping a suspended copper tongue until it settles into the one note that means:

**this way is true enough, for now.**
