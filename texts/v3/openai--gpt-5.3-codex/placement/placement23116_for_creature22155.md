At first I thought I’d be up high—on a mooring spine in the wind, or in a gate choir tower calling flood timings by light-code.

Then I thought I’d be lower: knee-deep in a warm culvert, tending biofilm gardens that keep a district’s water sweet enough to drink.

Then lower still: not a person at all, but one of those old civic reflex minds that never sleeps, routing schools around a flash storm and opening the right shutters three seconds before panic.

But if I let the image settle, I find myself in the **seams**.

---

You’d find me where systems change state:  
under a transit arc where hot condenser runoff meets cool dawn drip,  
at the lip of a flood plaza just before it floats,  
inside a maintenance throat between public deck and machine lung.

I’m a **Seam Cantor**—half infrastructure tech, half interpreter, half liar in the way all necessary jobs are one half too many things.

My work is to listen to transitions before they fail.

Not by ear alone; ears are blunt tools in this city. I read vibration through gloves on rail, through jawbone on a tuned strut, through the staccato of arthropod cleaners suddenly rerouting, through the way translucent canal fish kink away from a field that shouldn’t be there. I carry a palette of quick-growing films, conductive paste, fungal inhibitor, fungal encourager, ceramic stitch foam, and three kinds of apology packets for municipal AIs I have to override during storm hours.

When rain comes sideways and the low decks are sealing, I’m the one crawling into the hinge spaces with a crew that speaks mostly in taps and wrist-lights. We don’t “repair” as much as **renegotiate**: persuade a wall to breathe less, a gate to rise slower, a drainage memory street to reclaim flow for six hours, a market spine to float instead of lock.

In dry seasons (brief, suspicious), I map where emptiness is trying to form—an unused bay, a dead crawlspace, a cooling shaft no one has claimed yet. In this basin, that’s dangerous. Empty means unstable. So we pre-seed: microbial mat, sensor mesh, nesting lattice, vendor hooks, something alive or useful before stagnation takes it.

I live in a narrow stacked block above a heat-sink reservoir, where the nights are amber and the wall sweats in neat vertical lines. My neighbors are two Deckborn families, a retired lattice rigger, twelve illegal vent-birds, and a polite maintenance intelligence that lives in our stairwell rail and complains when children kick it.

If you asked what I *am*, not just what I do:

I’m human, yes—gland-sealed skin, nictitating lens, bone-conduction node behind the left ear.  
But in this place that’s not the whole answer.

I’m a moving membrane in the consortium.  
A temporary organ of the city’s metabolism.  
A person shaped to keep wet things flowing into other wet things without catastrophe.

So: not at the center, because there is no center.  
Not at the edge, because there is no edge.  
I am where one condition becomes another, and someone has to stay there long enough to translate.
