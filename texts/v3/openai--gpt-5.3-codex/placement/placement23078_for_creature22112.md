At first, I think I’d try to be one of the hinge-jawed reptiles—something decisive, heavy, final.  
In a place this strict, the fantasy of being the strike is strong.

But if I stay in the chamber long enough, that image burns off.

The rulers here are not the biggest bodies.  
They’re the best listeners.

So you’d find me not at the brightest crack, not at the hottest ledge, but just off-center—on the underside of a basalt shelf where two vent tones interfere and make a pocket of wrong sound. A **bad note**. A place predators misread by a fraction of a heartbeat. In this world, a fraction is shelter.

I’d be one of the pale clatter-scavengers.  
Ceramic plates, ash-dusted joints, antennae split like tuning forks. Eyes reduced to dull heat smudges. My real face would be my feet and forks: tap, receive, sort.

I would know everyone by impact signature:

- the tight, efficient scratch-bursts of healthy film-grazers,
- the dragged asymmetry of a fresh molt,
- the brittle rain of a shell giving way,
- the deep, spaced pressure thumps that mean *freeze now* because a reptile is loading its tendons nearby.

My work would be accounting, not hunting.  
I’d patrol the narrow traffic seams between crack-fed shelves and ash pockets, taking what fails, what splits, what pauses too long. Nothing can remain idle here; I’d be part of the mechanism that prevents waste from becoming obstacle. I wouldn’t think of it as cleaning. I’d think of it as keeping flow.

On heating surges, I’d climb high and still—carapace angled to shed radiant load, limbs tucked, every plate vibrating with vent thunder. On cooling breaths, I’d descend in quick staccato runs, joining the shift change as if some foreman had rung a bell deep in the mountain.

And eventually—because no role stays singular in a chamber this crowded—I’d become a placer of eggs.

Not a builder. There are no builders here.  
A chooser.

I’d test ledges by percussion, searching for cavities where resonance cancels or bends: spots where a hunter’s approach arrives late, blurred by the chamber’s own industrial roar. I’d glue clutches there, in those acoustic shadows. My offspring would hatch already fluent in pulse timing: move on the falling heat, disappear on the rise.

So where am I in this world?

In the dark band between glow and ash, where traffic never stops.  
Under the ledges that hum off-key.  
Inside the shift, not above it.

What am I?

A noise-literate scavenger, a keeper of tempo, a small hard body whose real organ is rhythm—  
one moving part in a machine-shaped wound that runs without mercy and without pause.
