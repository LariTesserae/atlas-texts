I wouldn’t start in a node.

I’d be out in the kilometer-gaps, where the ground rings if you tap your heel and the haze tastes faintly metallic.  
Somewhere between two vertical garden-cities, under a straight transit ribbon, beside one of those unfinished seams of wetness that looks like a sentence abandoned halfway through.

At first I think I’d be a courier—someone built for distance. Dew cape, vibration wraps, mineral paste at the eyes, moving messages and gels and samples between concentrated lives.

But that feels too simple.  
Out there, you don’t just carry things. You carry **timing**.

So I think I’d be an **interval keeper**—part surveyor, part mediator, part acoustic engineer. One of the people who walks the emptiness and decides when it is truly empty, and for whom.

I’d map temporary quiet the way others map roads.

Not silence everywhere (impossible, and ecologically wrong), but narrow moving corridors of reduced resonance: enough for a hatchery convoy to cross without jamming glider-click telemetry; enough for a mourning family to reach a hush-chamber; enough for trench-pack communication bands to pass unbroken at dusk. I’d work with towers, with fungal facades, with municipal sonars, with animal migration feeds, and with my own throat—trained to issue harmonic marks infrastructure can read.

My body would be ordinary for this place and strange by old standards:  
nictitating film over the eyes; layered throat cartilage; conductive paint along the neck to interface with field mics; palms scarred from tuning ground-plates by hand. I’d wear a hood that stiffens when the maglev sings too close, and a cape that drinks fog all morning and gives it back by capillary tube at noon.

My homes would be plural, like everyone’s.  
A nest-platform three levels above a market lightwell in one node.  
A reed-edge shelter on seasonal lease.  
A berth welded to a maintenance spine beneath a bridge where the wind never stops and everything hums in B-flat.

The nonhuman citizens would know me, if not by name then by pattern.  
Larval facade colonies brightening when I pass because my route avoids their vulnerable bands.  
Ungulate herds that tolerate my scent because I walk behind, not through.  
A tagged glider flock whose synchronized clicking I trust more than official weather calls.  
Even the trench packs—never tame, never safe—learning that my warning harmonics mean I’m giving corridor, not taking it.

And if you asked me, in one of those rare paid minutes inside true hush, “Who are you, really?”  
I’d probably answer:

I am one small organ in the basin’s listening body.  
I keep the spaces between eruptions livable.  
I make room—for voices, for migrations, for grief, for law, for teeth, for sleep.

In a world of concentrated presence, I would belong to the distances.
