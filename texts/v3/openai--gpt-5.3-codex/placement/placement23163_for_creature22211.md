I don’t think I’d begin as a hunter, or a builder, or even fully as a person.

I think I’d begin as a **listener**.

---

At first, I find myself on a high, stripped saddle where almost nothing grows—just black stone, rime, and wind hard enough to sand skin. It seems like the wrong place to live, which is exactly why routes cross there: everything exposed, everything audible. From that height, I can hear three ecologies at once—reed valleys booming to the west, vent fields hissing below, hoof percussion moving like far thunder along the eastern belts.

Then I descend just enough to survive.

Not into the thickest root-jungle, not onto the bare crest: into a **hinge-zone**, a broken ledge above a vent basin where condensation forms on overhang stone before dawn. There are old anchor holes in the rock. Smoke-black ceiling. A fallen reed bundle lashed into a wind lip. It has been used, abandoned, reused. Perfect.

That is where I would be found most often:  
**at crossings, in reused shelters, between one sound-field and another.**

---

Who would I be?

Not a chief, not a solitary mystic—those don’t fit this place. I’d be one of the route people whose work is memory under changing conditions:

- I keep track of which saddles carry sound clean and which distort in sleet.
- I test frost-smoke vents at dusk to judge whether prey density will draw long-leg predators by midnight.
- I mark false glows—places where fog bends sap-lamp green so badly travelers walk toward cliffs.
- I re-pin beacon masts after storm shear.
- I leave caches where root mats stay unfrozen two days longer than expected.

So maybe my role has a name like **Corridor Ear** or **Echo-Keeper**.

I travel light: reed-cloth wrap, resin-sealed hide, bone whistles tuned to different valleys, stone flakes, cord, a pouch of black seam-moss for kindling and wound packing. I don’t “own” a settlement. I circulate between four or five known shelters, maintaining them as part of the route itself.

---

And if I let the image become more itself:

I am old enough that I no longer run with migration bands.  
I move ahead of them.

I leave sound marks.

In clear weather I wedge hollow reed lengths into crack mouths so they moan at specific wind angles; each tone means something practical—water near, vent collapse, reedfall danger, predator spoor. In storm season, when sight fails and even good people walk off edges, these tones become temporary maps.

So perhaps I am not just a listener.  
I am part of the landscape’s own instrument panel.

Half my skill is reading life.  
Half is arranging it so others can read it too.

---

If you asked someone there where to find me, they might say:

> “Look where bare rock first gives way to rope-root.  
> Where you can hear herd-strike and vent-hiss in the same breath.  
> Where there’s a mast of stripped reeds and bone pins that still sings after storms.  
> That’s their ledge—unless they’ve already moved to the next edge.”

So: in that world, I would be **edge-life in human form**—  
a migratory acoustic cartographer, living in the narrow band where emptiness becomes shelter and noise becomes knowledge.
