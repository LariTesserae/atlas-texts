At first I think I’d be something brief and glittering—  
a dragonfly-thread above a puddle, a throat-note in the dusk pressure, a quick amphibian eye at the edge of a root.

But the basin refuses that kind of answer.  
Nothing here is really separate for long. Even the quick lives are woven into routes, timings, returns.

So if I let the place choose me, I end up lower.

You would find me where three kinds of ground touch:  
a warm mineral shelf, a peat mat that flexes under weight, and a hand-wide channel that never dries but never deepens. It’s a crossroads only if you’ve lived there long enough to feel the pattern in your bones. To outsiders it’s just mud and reeds. To us it is the hinge between nursery water, molting rootfield, and heat-rest rise.

I am Stillfolk—old, scar-dark, shoulder-heavy, slow.

Not a chief. We don’t really do chiefs.  
More like a living reference point: one of those bodies younger ones align to when weather thickens and all the noises become one wall of sound. My jaw pores read the tremor of distant movement through film-water. My chest can hold a low hum long enough for others to gather without panic. I do not command; I steady.

My day is mostly repetition, which here is not monotony but maintenance.

Before cloud-bright morning, I’m half-submerged, nostrils just above surface, tasting pressure changes in the metallic damp. Hatchlings quiver in root shadow. Juveniles pass behind me in a line they pretend is accidental. I move first through east passage, not because I decide to but because my body has taken that curve for so many seasons the mud itself seems to rise and meet my feet.

I brush against reed bases and leave skin-film.  
I wallow at the same bend and keep the channel open without meaning to “engineer” it.  
I feed where fungal blooms always come after last week’s droppings enriched the shelf.  
I raid egg clusters when they are there, ignore them when they are not.  
I rest on the same stone hump at heat-peak, flanked by two others whose scent I have known longer than any storm cycle.

If I vanish, the route remains for a while.  
If enough of us vanish, the route blurs.  
That is as close as we come to history.

By dusk-dimming, I am in the hum-circle: six, twelve, twenty of us in shallow water, throats sounding just under hearing, more felt than heard. The vibration travels through mud better than air. It says nothing elaborate. It says: here, now, this way, rain soon, hold close, continue.

And that’s the part I would be most completely:  
not the body exactly, but the continuity moving through bodies.

In your terms, maybe I’m an elder female, maybe male, maybe past breeding and broad-jawed with mineral lichens mapped over my flanks. In ours, those distinctions matter less than whether I remember the old width of the trail when stormfall presses reeds flat. Whether I turn at the right root-knee without looking. Whether my hum is steady enough that the juveniles stop testing the wrong channel on the third day and fold back into the colony’s known shape.

So: where would you find me?

Knee-deep in tea-colored water, under leaves that delay rain, at the lip of a pool that has always been nursery water.  
Facing the same direction I faced yesterday, and many wet seasons before.  
Still, but not inert.  
Conservative, but not empty.  
A creature made of memory, helping the basin remain exactly itself.
