I wouldn’t be at the center.

Not in the loudest market court, not in the thickest dwellings, not under the biggest light-shaft where everyone can see everyone.  
I’d be on the **hinges**—the places between settlement and open basin, between built ramp and animal lane, between speech and echo.

At first I thought: gardener, dew-keeper, someone kneeling at dawn with cloths and bowls, wringing moisture from stone breath.  
Then: herd caller, one of the long-voiced people with chest scars from years of projected calls, turning dry browsers with whistles and arm-sign.  
Then: mason, because in that world repair is holiness.

But the shape that keeps returning is this:

I am an **Echo Warden**—a road-and-vent keeper whose job is to listen before others move.

I live in a narrow house built into a rounded basin wall, above a switchback road polished by generations of hooves. My family keeps three things: a modest dew court, a small rookery claim, and an old civic duty—to test the morning sound in the fault corridors and mark what is safe.

Before first light, while the stone is coldest and sweating its thin skin of water, I walk the upper ramps with a coil of line, chalk, a bone whistle, and a shallow bronze listening dish. I don’t “inspect” like an engineer from a bright world. I **sound** the place.  
A struck lintel tells me if a support has gone powder-soft.  
A whistle into a vent tells me if wind has shifted and will push predators toward sleeping terraces.  
A shouted pulse into a tunnel throat tells me whether the return is clean, fractured, or swallowed—whether a roof has slumped somewhere out of sight.

By sunrise-color (not warmth), I’m back at the signal wall. I paint simple marks: open, narrow, unstable, herd-heavy, wing-fall likely. Then I send two long calls and one clipped whistle down the basin—a public voice everyone knows. Not poetry. Routing.

In this place, that counts as care.

By mid-cycle I help where needed: patching thorn corridors, resetting elevated footways, arguing rookery quotas, trading resin oil for gypsum plaster. At dusk I stand with the lane-watch and read movement: burrow-stock surfacing, wing clouds lifting, followers ghosting the scrub margins. Night is for soft voice, close voice, family voice.

If I were not human, I’d be one of the alert dogs bred for warning—ears like cupped leaves, ribs like bow staves, sleeping lightly under raised floors, waking at the first wrong hoof rhythm. But I think I am human, because this world’s deepest adaptation isn’t claw or fang or root.

It’s **attention**.

So that’s where I’d find myself: on a worn high ramp at blue-gray afternoon, dust on my tongue, one hand on old stone, listening to a continent’s hollow bones sing back what they can still hold.
