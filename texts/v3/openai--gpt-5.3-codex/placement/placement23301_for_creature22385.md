I wouldn’t arrive as a hero or planner.  
I’d arrive as a **listener**.

At first, you’d find me where newcomers always stall: on a flexway intersection between two marker poles, pretending to read a pollen-map while actually trying to decode the soundscape—what’s rain, what’s warning, what’s routine breathing of the city. I’d rent a dry cot in a third-ring canopy house and spend a week waking at dawn to the same question: *which noises mean urgency, and which mean trust?*

Then I think I’d drift toward the **edges of systems**—the places where categories blur: clinic still-fields beside hatchery canals, archive groves near commuter bridges, old stone ribs under new root arches. Not center, not wilderness: thresholds.

So who would I become?

Probably a kind of **mesh interpreter**: part storm clerk, part memory tender, part field technician. The person called when a threshold membrane starts denying elders with correct scent-threads, when a pollinator corridor overlaps a school route, when the Underweave proposes a reroute that is elegant hydraulically but rude socially. I’d translate between what the roots predict and what the people can consent to.

By season three, I imagine locals calling me something like **culvert-scribe** or **drift mediator**—someone with wet boots, stained cuffs, and a reputation for staying calm when plaza levels lift. I’d carry grafting tools in one pocket and legal tags in the other. I’d know which basking arthropod on Platform 6 is usually right about pressure drop. I’d have favorite reeds.

And if I belonged there fully, my place would be dusk: standing on an old seawall turned orchid terrace, logging flow rights while kids practice flood-scent drills below, and the Basin Voice hums through my ankles.  
Not above the city. Not outside it.

A minor organ in a major body—  
one more temporary, accountable layer in something that never stops becoming.
