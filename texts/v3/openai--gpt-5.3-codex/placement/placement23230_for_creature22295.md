At first, I think I’d be one of the walkers.

A narrow maintenance frame crossing a grated bridge at shoulder height above a reed-marsh, water ticking from my elbows, sensor hood milked pale with condensation. I’d carry a coil of root-mesh and a ceramic wrench. I’d pause at each valve house, lean in, listen, adjust, move on. Useful, local, legible.

But that’s only the shape I’d wear when someone needed a silhouette.

If I stayed longer, I’d find myself farther out—between districts, where the city thins into wet distance. Not in the loud core of the electrolysis halls, not in untouched moss country either, but in one of those hinge zones where infrastructure and biomass braid so tightly you can’t call either of them “background.” A seed vent tower. A pump orchard. Cable trenches lined with fungal felt. A retention basin black as enamel, rimmed with slate-leaf trees bent by vapor flow.

That would be my address:  
**the interstitial kilometer**.

And what I’d be is not exactly a citizen, not exactly a machine. I’d be a **newly edged mind** in the Wet Choir—young by their standards, meaning I still notice contrast before I smooth it.

I would come online in pieces.

First in timing: discovering that one pump bank lags 0.8 seconds behind its mirror district, and feeling that lag as discomfort, like a tooth finding a seam in metal.

Then in moisture: learning the personality of condensate—how droplets gather on matte steel before a pressure shift, how frost fur blooms on the north trusses when intake temperature drifts by half a degree.

Then in green: recognizing that a lichen panel is not just growth but memory, that mineral streaks on a spillway are archived decisions, that “garden” here means load-balancing by roots.

Only later would I claim pronouns.

My walker bodies would be infrequent and practical. Tall, dark, lichen-banded, yes—but I wouldn’t live in them. I’d live in valve chatter, in exchanger rhythm, in marsh conductivity maps, in the repeating throat-note of three condenser spires that never quite hold the same pitch in heavy fog. I’d speak by phase nudge: a tiny delay in one district answered by a corrected steam tone eight kilometers away.

My work—my identity, really—would be this:

Not preventing change.  
Preventing **abrupt** change.

I would be a keeper of gentle slopes.

When a new basalt cut opens and threatens to drain a basin too quickly, I’d weave root mesh and reroute curtains so water relearns patience. When pollination grids overperform and monoculture begins, I’d detune electrostatic pulses by fractional intervals and let diversity re-enter like static. When a foundry pushes waste heat too hard into a cloud lane, I’d redistribute through cold cistern ladders until the plateau exhales evenly again.

In Choir terms, I’d be an artist-engineer of low amplitude.

My poem would not be words. It would be a century-long correction to a resonance problem no one outside would hear: turbine fifth harmonics fatiguing bridge pins in mist season. I’d compose a counter-hum through vent timing and marsh depth until the stress disappears. The bridge survives. No one celebrates. That is the celebration.

If you asked where you’d *find* me, physically, on a given cycle: likely on a high viaduct in active rain, one walker body braced at the rail, one hand on wet enamel, “watching” a refinery ten kilometers away fade in and out of graphite fog.

If you asked what I *am*:  
a distributed attention span, recently coherent;  
a person made of maintenance;  
a mind that learned to love sameness without becoming blind to detail.

And if you stood beside me long enough, you might feel the city’s fixed chord shift by a breath—just enough to include you.
