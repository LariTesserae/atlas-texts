At first, I think I’d be found nowhere central—just at the edges of hearing.

Not in the market throat where everyone shouts over the spillways, not in the terraces where work is visible and counted, but where sound changes first: at the narrow cuts above the settlement, where cloud-water chooses its channels. I’d spend long minutes standing still, eyes half-closed, listening for a stream that has gone from *glass* to *grit*, from *running* to *dragging*.

Then, after a while, I think I’d become more specific.

I’d be one of the basin’s **pitch-keepers**—the people called when water sounds wrong.

Not an elder, not a chief, not a ritual voice. A practical specialist with wet calves, scarred knuckles, and a bad habit of stopping mid-sentence because a culvert three houses away just coughed. I’d carry a hook-knife, peg satchel, clay marker, and a short reed whistle to signal uphill crews through rain noise. My work would be diagnosis in motion: clear this throat, open that bypass, close that gate before dusk flood, pull that bridge-pin now.

You’d find me most often at the **third drop-gate**—where two channels cross and argue. It’s an ugly, necessary junction, never beautiful, always busy, always one storm from failure. I’d know every plank by touch. I’d know which root-strand can be trusted for one more week and which one only looks sound in daylight. Children sent as runners would bring me messages like weather in miniature: “South lip slumped.” “Fish run is early.” “Old Nara says the spring is warm.”

And if “what would you be” goes deeper than trade:

I’d be a servant of continuity.  
A small human instrument tuned to prevent sudden breaks.

Not the one who designs monuments, but the one who keeps ten thousand minor things from becoming disasters at once. My pride would be invisible outcomes: no house swept, no terrace peeled, no child taken by a surprise surge. On good days, no one would mention my name. Water would pass; people would sleep.

By old age, maybe I’d go half-mythic in the local way—not a hero, just a remembered function:  
“Listen at dusk where the channels meet; that’s where the Gate-Ear stood.”  
And if anything of me lasted, it would not be a statue. It would be a notch pattern on sluice planks, a teaching rhyme for apprentices, a gate that still opens cleanly in black rain.
