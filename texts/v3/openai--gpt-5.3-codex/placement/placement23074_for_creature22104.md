At first I’d try to stay on the rim.

Not out of fear—out of habit. High ground, wide view, a place to map from. I’d pick a basalt lip above the basin where the blowhole-thrum comes up through my boots and the roost noise rolls in sheets. I’d tell myself I’m an observer.

But this place doesn’t care about observers.  
It only has participants.

So I’d move down, slowly, toward a junction: a shallow drainage cut, a warm seep, and the shadow-line of a major roost cavity. That’s where three kinds of information overlap—water pulse, chemical bloom, and wing-pressure code. If the Undertide has “thought,” it happens in places like that.

At first, I’d be a problem: too warm, too heavy, too still when I pause.  
Hunters triangulate. Grazers test. Scavengers queue in anticipation.

To last even a day, I’d have to stop being singular.

I imagine learning a gait that is less walking than tapping in syntax—short, distributed contacts, never a deep heel strike. I’d hang shell strips and hollow basalt beads from my clothing so my movement reads as known noise, not anomaly. I’d sleep vertical in seep-mist where fewer things can pin me. I’d feed the films, deliberately, so something local has a reason to classify me as recurring nutrient instead of sudden meat.

Then, over time, I’d become less “field biologist” and more organ.

Not a leader. Not a master. A relay.

A pressure listener lodged in a stone throat: taking in surf-bass, storm-drop, wingburst, collapse crack, and returning small patterned knocks into mud and ledge. A temporary ganglion where signals cross scales. The fliers pass overhead; burrow fronts adjust below; slicks bloom days later in places I never touched. Cause and effect stretched across species until “I did this” stops making sense.

If I stayed long enough, the basin would finish the job.

Salt on skin, mineral crust at joints, microbial sheen in seams of fabric, cleaners commuting across me without alarm. My camp gear collapses into substrate. My bones—eventually—plated and stored in the wet galleries with everything else that was useful once.

So where would you find me?

At the hinge between roost and runoff, where mist hangs waist-high and the ground hums before rain.

What would I be?

For a while, a human trying to listen.  
After that, a named rhythm.  
After that, one more durable note in the Undertide’s body—  
not separate from it, just briefly distinct.
