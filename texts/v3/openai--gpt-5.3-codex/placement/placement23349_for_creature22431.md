At first I think I’d be somewhere high—on a gantry, in a tower cab, looking down at all that white glare and steam.  
But this isn’t a world you understand from above. It’s a world you learn through contact.

So I end up lower, at a seam: the long stone causeway between a condenser field and a seep terrace, where the plate-herds cross at midday and nobody argues with them. That’s where you’d find me most days—boots off, soles on warm rock, feeling for tremors before I see movement.

I think I’d be human, but in the local way: specialized until the specialization becomes identity. A **tremor reader**, maybe called a **foot-listener** in district slang. Part traffic warden, part ecologist, part mechanic. I’d keep crossing schedules, but not for vehicles—for herds, for spire-runner hunting windows, for glider descents when plume direction shifts. I’d carry chalk for mineral marks, a matte hood against glare, and a little tuning hammer for pipe-lines that still carry coded knocks between compounds.

My shift would start before sunrise, when “dry” is a lie. I’d walk rails, test surfaces, reopen routes, mark slick zones with crushed red clinker. At noon I’d stand in the heat shimmer and hold people back while the browsers pass, shell to shell, scraping as they go, doing the work nobody can automate as well. By late day I’d adjust mirror angles so pilots aren’t blinded and predators don’t get accidental cover from bad reflections. At dusk, when warm rain comes slantwise, I’d update the vibration map: where the ground carries cleanly tonight, where machinery masks footsteps, where swimmers are schooling in new overflow lanes.

And over time I’d probably become less “manager of wildlife” and more one useful organism among others.  
Known by the herds as a non-threat rhythm.  
Known by glider crews as the person whose panel marks can be trusted.  
Known by children as the one who teaches them that white line means water and dark shine means heat.

If this city is an organ, I wouldn’t be its voice.  
I’d be one of its nerves—quiet, pressure-sensitive, always listening downward.
