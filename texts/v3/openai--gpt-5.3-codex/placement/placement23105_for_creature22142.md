You’d find me where the noise changes fastest.

Not in the pump halls themselves, and not up in the drier living shells either—down in the hinge-zones: the shaded turn between a vibrating wall and a quiet runnel, where one more meter changes what your body hears. I’d be on a narrow raised seam above black water, one boot on brick, one on a root-swollen slab, waiting for a valve sigh to settle before I wrote anything down.

At first glance I’d look like any seam worker: cuffs sealed, mesh gloves, old shears, pressure meter clipped to my belt. But my real tools would be chalk, string, and a tuning fork in a rubber sleeve. I’d mark flood lines on concrete, yes—but also resonance lines: this panel rings too high now, this pipe has gone dull, this retaining face is holding but tired. I’d be one of the people who reads the district by ear and touch more than by dashboard.

I think my job title would be something plain—maintenance tech, flow support, inspections—but people here would call me a **harmonics keeper** or a **drift scribe**. I’d walk the same loops every day at different hours, because noon lies and dusk tells the truth. In daylight, everything sounds healthy under leaf-filtered glow. At night, when fans step up and sodium lamps flatten the shadows, strain reveals itself: a belt’s small impatience, a root pulling too hard against mesh, trapped water thudding behind a wall that should be breathing.

And after a few years, I wouldn’t feel like a person servicing a place. I’d feel more like one nerve in a larger body. The district would train my stride, my lungs, my timing. I’d stop thinking in “before/after repair” and start thinking in “kept in conversation”: trim this, spare that, vent here, yield there. I’d leave arrows on trunks, dates on pilings, little symbols only a handful of us could read at a glance in rain.

So: where would I be?  
On the wet seam between root and machine, usually at dusk, usually listening.

Who would I be?  
A resident, yes—but more specifically, one of the people who keeps the place legible to itself. A translator between pressure and patience. A human part of that anatomical partnership, helping nothing finish and nothing fail all at once.
