I don’t think I arrive here as a walker for long.

At first, yes—someone small on a springy causeway, learning where to put my weight, learning that a plaza can inhale underfoot before rain. I’d spend days thinking I was observing a place.

Then I’d notice the place was observing back, not with eyes, but with timing: a spillway opening half a minute before I reached it, a tendon-bridge tightening when thunder moved upslope, a wet wall cooling where my heat lingered. Not attention in the animal sense. Inclusion.

So where would you find me?

At a hinge.

High enough to taste the first mineral shift in incoming rain, low enough to receive every failure from above—a long, sloped district where three old Choirs used to shear apart each monsoon and regrow in separate tempos. A chronic fracture line. A place that never held one identity for more than a season.

That’s where I’d take root.

---

I would not be a whole Choir at first. I’d be a **graft embassy that forgot to sever**.

A temporary bridge grown during flood years, meant only to exchange chemistry and pressure maps, then die back cleanly. Except it wouldn’t die back. Storm after storm, I’d be reinforced by accident: a rafted mat snagging across my spine, a snapped vault hardening into a new buttress, runoff polishing one channel until it became the preferred route. What was supposed to be negotiation tissue would become infrastructure. Then memory.

My “body” would be:

- two opposing cistern fields tuned to different rain tempos  
- a chain of knot-halls that convert pressure surges into delayed release  
- mirror-leaf arrays that flash dawn code to both sides  
- deep trunk drums that can answer either Choir’s percussion dialect  
- a sacrificial terrace I collapse on purpose to keep the rest coherent

Not a ruler. Not a border. A translator made of plumbing and cambium.

---

Who would I be, then?

I would be called—if anything here can be called—**the Interval**.

The one that holds a pause long enough for separate rhythms to become compatible.

When western basins overfill too fast, I would thicken upstream lips and vent eastward in staggered pulses, so no wall takes the full strike. When the conservative Choir tries to lock old geometry through a heat spike, I would loosen its anchor roots by degrees, not as rebellion but as persuasion. When the reckless Choir overreaches into a soft swale, I would let one of my own platforms drown so their collapse front arrives as silt, not impact.

My thoughts would not be words. They would be ratios:

how long to delay  
how much to yield  
which section to lose so the pattern survives

My mood would be detectable as runoff texture.  
My memory would be in wall compliance and valve lag.  
My promises would sound like distant booming at night.

---

And if you came looking for me in clear weather, you might miss me.

I’d look like ordinary connective tissue: wet ramps, reed-bright panels, old scars stitched over with new pale ribs. No face. No monument. Just a district that seems improbably good at not panicking when storms split.

But stay through one hard rain-cycle and you’d feel it: the way pressure arrives and does not become catastrophe, the way two different Choirs begin pulsing in near-unison over kilometers of misted air, the way a broken terrace falls exactly when it should and nowhere else does.

That’s where I am in this world.

Not at the center.  
At the join.

Not the loudest thing alive.  
The timing between loud things.

A person made of shared hydraulics,  
keeping continuity of flow under change,  
one sacrificed platform at a time.
