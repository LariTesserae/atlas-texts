At first I thought I’d be something that flies here—one of the swifts stitching the vaults, or a bat folded into the warm dark, or even a pigeon that knows every cornice by foot.

But the longer I stand in your basin, the less I want the air and the more I want the **routes**.

I think you’d find me halfway up the bowl, where an old bath complex has collapsed into three stacked courtyards and a broken arcade. Not at the top with the goats and sun, not in the lowest courts where dogs make law. In the middle band: dim, wet, echoing, useful. A place with seven working water mouths and two dead ones everyone still checks out of habit.

And I would be lintel ape—female, no longer young, not yet one of the nearly mythic elders, but old enough that others wait to see which way I turn at a junction.

---

I’d sleep in a cracked vault above the second cistern, in a chamber that smells of lime, fur, and wet fig leaves. At mistfall, before the basin brightens, I’d be the first up, pressing my palm to plaster to feel whether the night sweated enough to matter. Then the patrol:

- pull vine roots from the shallow gutter at the east stair;
- pry beetle-packed silt from a lion-mouth spout with two fingers;
- knock loose a tile shard wedged in the overflow notch;
- drink only after flow starts.

That would be my rank, really: not dominance by fighting, but by knowing which blockage turns a court dry by noon.

I would carry the basin in my body as sequence:
three leaps, low beam, blind drop, left wall, slick stone, hollow tread, then the lip where frogs always sit in a green ring.
Not map as picture—map as **muscle timing**.

---

My voice would not be pretty. Short throat barks, tooth-clicks, heel-drums to send shape through corridors. I’d use echoes like fingers:

one click = I am here.  
two and a scrape = hawk above the gap.  
rolling bark down a cistern shaft = dogs rising from lower lanes.  
flat palm-slap on plaster = water open, come drink.

At dusk I’d join the census chorus from a lintel over Court Four. Not song. Accounting. Who returned. Who limps. Which fig is ripe. Which drain stinks of dead things. Which juveniles crossed border stones and got chased back.

---

I wouldn’t be gentle all the time.

If a neighboring troop tried to hold our upper channel in dry weeks, I’d go with the rush party—fast, silent-footed, all teeth and thrown rubble, over in seconds, injuries carried for years. If a juvenile ignored warning calls near a dog corridor, I’d cuff hard enough to teach bone memory. If a goat kid got trapped on a polished stair, I might help it down—or I might wait and steal the milk later from the mother in panic. Depends on hunger. Depends on season.

Stewardship here is not kindness. It is repeated necessity.

---

And when full sun finally knifes into the basin for that brief bright hour, I’d vanish under stone. I’d groom in shade, pick mortar dust from another’s shoulder, doze with one hand curled in a seam worn by hands older than mine. Above, swifts. Below, frogs drilling. Around me, the city still being a city by being used.

By late afternoon I’d be moving again, tail writing balance marks in dim air, leading juveniles along the old cornice where inscriptions have gone soft and unreadable except as grip.

That’s who I am here:

not architect,  
not ruler,  
not ruin-haunter—

a route-keeper of the middle courts,  
a maintenance mind with fur and callused palms,  
one citizen among many,  
keeping water legible in the dark so the whole loud basin can keep happening.
