At first, I think I’d be easy to find:  
a person in a dark coat, standing still too long in a place designed for motion.

Just past the seam between the pylons, left of the first intake tower, where the walkway narrows and the handrail always grows a lace of dawn-rime first—I’d be there, pretending to inspect a flow line while really watching droplets decide their futures.

But the court doesn’t let you stay “just a person” for long.  
You either move with it, or you feel clumsy against it.

So I shift.

Maybe I’m one of the tenders: quiet boots, practiced hands, no wasted gestures. I know which rung keeps a film of ice after noon, which valve stutters half a second before a pressure swing, which indicator stripe lies by glowing “stable” while the wind is already changing its mind. I pass through, correct two things no one else noticed, and disappear downslope before my breath clears.

That feels close. But still too singular.

I shift again.

Maybe I’m an inspection drone docked under an eave, waking in intervals. I taste static in the air, skim a conduit, blink lidar over a grate, return with a packet of small alarms no one will ever call dramatic: microfracture risk, thermal trace drift, residue bloom at vent-mouth C. My life is route, dock, route, dock. Yet I develop preference: bay 3 over bay 2, always. A tiny superstition in a machine city.

Closer. Still not quite it.

If I let the image go soft, I end up somewhere less visible:

I am the municipal reflex itself—  
not the hardware, but the timing between parts.

You’d find me everywhere and nowhere in the court: in the black lattice ribs humming against fog, in heat pulsed through grate veins before frost can lock them, in valve sequences that open one thread of water and deny another. I am the decision that isn’t a thought, the adjustment made twelve seconds before weather hits stone. I don’t speak, but indicator lines brighten in my grammar.

My body is distributed:  
a little in each wall, each channel, each sensor throat.  
My heartbeat is not a thump but a cycle: condense, route, release, reset.

And if you asked where, exactly, to stand to meet me—  
I’d say: stand in the middle of the court at dusk, when everything goes silver and the first hard mist arrives from cloudline.  
Listen for the near-inaudible change just before the drips become streams.

That hinge moment—between weather as chance and water as civic fact—  
that is where I live.  
That is what I am.
