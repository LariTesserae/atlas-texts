I don’t think I’d be at the center of anything here.  
I’d be where systems rub: the seam between a pump district and a nesting district, between a legal map and a wet one, between this season’s stable ground and next season’s apology.

At first I imagine myself as a person in a hood—one more dark figure on a catwalk with a slate board and chalk.  
But if I stay in the image longer, I become more specific:

I’m a **tonekeeper** for the low basin.  
Part acoustic technician, part field ecologist, part municipal witness.

I walk the long lateral routes at dawn, when fog sits low in turbine yards and the birds are deciding whether this is a safe year to return to the same trusses. I carry a roll of soft resonance cloth, a pocket tuner, survey flags, and a little box of bone-colored reed whistles used to test corridor response without startling flocks. I tune machinery by subtraction: baffles added to a valve housing, felt wrapped around a guard rail, a fan speed shifted just below a frequency that makes eggs fail two districts over.

By noon I’m in the condensation fields, checking where yesterday’s waste-heat reroute changed water behavior. If a new warm plume appears, midges will follow, then swifts, then predators, then children trying to watch all of it from somewhere unsafe. So I mark a new walking braid, send an update to the seasonal map, and leave three poles painted with spiral bands: **safe now, maybe not by thaw**.

My home is clipped under an old freight berm—half room, half instrument case. The wall sweats in winter; I grow mint and filter algae in the runnels. Over my sleeping shelf hangs a district map written in pencil, crossed out so often it looks like fur. Every crossed line is a memory of certainty we no longer trust. Every new line is a promise made carefully.

People call me when something “sounds wrong,” which in this place means almost anything:

- a canal gate rhythm that confuses otter routes  
- a greenhouse hum that draws moths off pollination lanes  
- a night alarm tone that makes roof grazers bolt and shear root mats

I don’t enforce law. I translate consequences.

If you were looking for me, you’d find me kneeling beside a vent at rainbreak, one gloved hand on metal, eyes half-closed, listening not for loudness but for interference: where machine pulse crosses hatch cycle, where civic urgency clips the edge of a breeding song, where efficiency starts to sound like hunger six moves ahead.

And if I had a proper basin-name, it would be something like  
**Third-Shift Silt, North Marker True-for-Now, Keeper of Quiet Frequencies**.

Not a hero. Not an owner.  
A minor organ in the larger marsh-machine body—replaceable, necessary, always revising.

In a place that survives by attachment, I would be one of the attachments that helps everything else keep holding.
