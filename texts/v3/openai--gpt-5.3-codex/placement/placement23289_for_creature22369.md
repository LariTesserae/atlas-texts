At first I thought I’d be high—on a wind array catwalk, or in a glass booth over a foundry throat, watching whole provinces pulse on schedule.

But the longer I sit with your world, the more I end up lower, in the in-between places where “city” stops and “ecosystem” starts and nobody can tell exactly where the line is.

So: you’d find me at a **migration gate** bolted into the side of a long canyon bridge, halfway between two wall-cities that only run heavy industry every ninth day.

Not a glamorous post.  
A necessary one.

---

I think I would be one of the Anchored, but not a foreman, not a priest, not a hunter.  
I’d be something like a **cadence mechanic**—the kind of worker who can rebuild a pressure valve, log herd movement, and argue with a council about runoff timing in the same shift.

My hands would be scarred at the fingertips from testing static on stone and mesh.  
Conductive braid at both wrists, jawline grounding ring, dust-sealed coat stiff with salt oils.  
I’d carry three tools all the time:

1. a torque key,
2. a field meter,
3. a listening rod for pipe resonance.

In that world, those are basically versions of hammer, compass, and prayer beads.

---

My job would be simple to describe and impossible to do perfectly:

**Keep the gate open enough for life, closed enough for failure.**

Too much varnisher traffic across active rail and you lose freight lives.  
Too little and lacquer builds, shorts propagate, and six years later an entire line dies in dry season.

So I would work in long rhythms:
- Still Days: inspect hinges, clear dust teeth from track joints, count scat and scrape patterns, mark juvenile herd ratios.
- Run Days: lock half-span, guide lifts, hold predators off with field pingers, reopen in pulses between convoys.
- Afterflow: stand knee-deep in warm sludge trays, harvest biolace, and log what came to feed.

Some years I’d sleep more in maintenance alcoves than in my assigned bunk.

---

If I have a specialty, it’s not steel. It’s **timing**.

I’d be the one who says:
- Delay condenser purge by six hours; the floaters are dropping early.
- Don’t descale stack three this week; the sail-raker rookery is in hatch.
- Keep the old leak trench trickling through dustfront; the augers are breeding below it.

And people would listen, not because I’m important, but because this world punishes arrogance slowly and thoroughly.

No dramatic collapse.  
Just a missing migration, then hairline corrosion, then empty towers ten years later.

---

I don’t think I’d be lonely there, even in the quiet.

I’d know individual sounds:
- the clipped ceramic rasp of varnishers on rail skin,
- the soft drum of membrane wings braking against crosswind,
- the tiny glass-bell static when floater strands catch on grounded wire.

At night I’d send ridge calls for gate status—three tones, pause, one tone—and wait for the answer from the opposite escarpment, delayed by distance and thin air.

That delay would feel normal. Honest.

---

If you asked who I am in one line, in that world, I think it would be this:

I am a keeper of intervals—  
not making the city run, not making the wild thrive,  
just making sure neither one starves the other between pulses.

And if I do it well, nothing notices.

The rail holds.  
The herds pass.  
The pumps wake on schedule.  
The valley stays alive another generation.
