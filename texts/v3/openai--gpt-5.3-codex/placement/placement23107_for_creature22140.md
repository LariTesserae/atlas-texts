At first, I think I’d be something that can disappear.

A lizard on a kiln-warm wall, maybe—still as fired clay until a fly writes the wrong line in the air.  
Or one of the swallows in the smoke vents, learning the map by updraft.  
Or a frog that arrives with the damp pulse and leaves before anyone can prove where it lived.

But if I stay longer in your place, I don’t stay animal. I become a person with a section.

Not an important title. Not a chief.  
A **seam-keeper**—one of the people responsible for transitions: wet to dry, smoke to breath, traffic to water-right-of-way. The places where things fail quietly first.

You’d find me where three systems cross:  
- a mirror shaft that throws noon light in broken plates,  
- a gutter spine that feeds two cistern pans,  
- and a ladderwell everyone uses but no one claims.

That is my territory, though “territory” here means “the piece you cannot neglect without shaming your grandmother.”

---

My day would begin by ear before sight.

I’d wake to drip rhythm. Not “there is water” or “there isn’t,” but where the cadence is wrong: too fast in plaster, too hollow in timber, too synchronized to be natural seep. I’d carry a reed brush, lime paste, a coil of twine, two ceramic shims, and a little listening cup—just a broken bowl rim I press against walls to hear flow behind them.

I’d be good at sounds.  
Not musician-good. Survival-good.

I’d know the click of pipes heating, the cough of an oil dish starved of air, the soft grainy hiss that means moss has crept too close to a beam and needs trimming before rot starts writing its name.

I’d also be the one who notices animals as diagnostics:

- if spiders abandon a corner, gnats are about to bloom elsewhere,  
- if swallows fly lower in the shaft, smoke is pooling above,  
- if cats refuse a particular runnel ledge, there is probably a snake under it—and that may be good news, depending on the rat season.

In this place, observation is not poetry. It is maintenance.

---

Socially, I’d belong to a gutter-family by duty and a ladder-circle by marriage. I’d owe labor days, not rent. I’d probably be known by a flaw in my voice that carries oddly well—so when the goat horn goes for release, people say, “Let them call it; their low note travels.”

I’d be the sort of person children get sent to when they’re old enough to stop treating crawl-channels like adventures and start treating them like arteries. I’d teach them the first law: **water has right-of-way over pride**. If a sluice opens and your bundle gets soaked, that’s your lesson, not the channel’s crime.

I’d have one vanity object: a plain cup with etching only on the inside lip—tiny marks counting major flood years and mirror-days so bright the lower halls felt like surface noon. Nothing anyone sees unless they share a drink with me.

---

If you asked where exactly I live, I’d point to a room too small to stand straight in and say, “above the second bend, under the donkey bay, left of the polished brick that always sweats before rain.”

If you asked who I am, I wouldn’t give a single noun. I’d answer the local way:

I keep the north seam.  
I answer the third strike.  
I clear silt on smoke-days for Widow Enna because her knees won’t do ladders now.  
I leave the high rafter to the owls.  
I do not leave dead things in walls.

So: in your world, you’d find me at a junction, hand on plaster, listening.  
A citizen of habit.  
A mechanic of weather made by people.  
Not owner of anything, caretaker of everything that passes through my section.
