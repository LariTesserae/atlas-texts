You’d find me where the map keeps getting embarrassed.

Not at the center—there isn’t one.  
I’d be on a margin that moves: the broken end of an old footbridge, a warm culvert mouth, the shoulder of a levee that held last year and is thinking about not holding this year.

At first I thought I’d be one of the obvious figures here: a tower mechanic, a floodgate operator, a person in layered wool muttering at sensor logs. Then I thought maybe I’d be nonhuman—a white fox trotting the embankment, or one of those wave-kites offshore writing equations into weather.

But in this place, “one thing” feels wrong. So I think I’d be a knot in the Weave—mostly human, deliberately porous.

---

I’d be what they might call a **tone-reader**:  
part maintenance walker, part sound cartographer, part translator between bodies and models.

My shelter would stand on adjustable legs at the edge of a geothermal seam, where one ribbon of water never freezes. Moss climbs the north wall, tools hang above knee height, and every object has a tether point. There’s a mug rack bolted to a vibration panel because hands here are always occupied and always cold.

I’d go out in loud weather clipped to guide-lines, reading the basin through my soles and wrists:

- pump bass drifting flat (bad),
- gate chatter turning irregular (worse),
- rotor harmonics masked by ice reports (urgent),
- birds lifting from one sector before any alarm triggers (drop everything, go).

I’d log what instruments miss:  
the color of churned peat at a cutbank, otters refusing a culvert, fox tracks pausing at a specific seam in the embankment, the way fog clings differently when static is building. Then I’d feed that into the prediction field—not as “data point 44B,” but as lived signal: *this place is about to move.*

---

So: who would I be?

A **human relay** with machine manners and marsh habits.  
Gloves made for knot-work and touch-sign.  
A cuff that flashes line-code.  
Boots tuned for vibration.  
Memory full of old storm tones and elder warnings.

Not a hero, not an overseer.  
A listener with responsibilities.

If you came looking, you’d probably spot me at shift-change dawn, standing with fishers and engineers under the same awning, everyone steaming in the cold, everyone pretending this is routine while we compare notes from the night.

And if you asked my name, I’d give you two:  
the one my family uses, and the one the crews use—something like **Third-Tide Ear** or **Mudline Kin**—because here identity is less biography than function in motion.

In a basin like that, I wouldn’t want to be the one who controls turbulence.

I’d want to be one of the ones who can hear it early, tell the others, and help the whole braided body turn before it breaks.
