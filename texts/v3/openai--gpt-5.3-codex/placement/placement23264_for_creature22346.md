At first, I try to be something solitary.

A swallow, maybe—stitched to the roof cracks, all punctuation and speed.  
Or one of the mirror beetles, carrying dawn on its back.  
Or the pale amphibian that can wait ten minutes for one perfect drop and call that a meal.

But the longer I stand in your basin, the less “single creature” feels true.

This place doesn’t reward a hero-body.  
It rewards timing, handoff, relay.

So if I belong here, I am not the fastest thing or the rarest thing.  
I am one member of the ash-colored colony at the basin’s eastern undercut, beneath the second long roof slit where the noon light arrives in a narrow blade and moves like a sundial.

---

You would find me where three conditions overlap:

- not in direct sun,  
- within two body lengths of a damp seam,  
- with a clear line of sight to a path other animals are forced to use.

That means the lip of an eroded hollow, near the old shell piles.  
Not a nest. A station.

My body is small, low, dust-colored enough to vanish against marl.  
Blunt muzzle for pushing, incisors for trimming tendon from bone, forepaws with enough dexterity to turn fragments and test edges.  
My whiskers are long and stiff; they read turbulence before they read scent.  
My ears aren’t large, but they are exact. I can hear a gnat cloud change shape.

My eyes are ordinary in brightness, exceptional in contrast.  
I am built to notice a light-line moving five centimeters and understand: **too late, move now**.

---

If you watched my day, it would look like errands.

Before first heat: I visit stone faces where condensation beads, not to drink first, but to check which seams are active. I touch rock with the side of my jaw. Cool means maybe. Wet means now. Dry means don’t waste time.

At white-bar morning: I work the sorting margin. Bone to inner arc. Thorn to right pile. Shell near damp edge. Pebble by size. Not because I “like order”—because each category answers a future problem quickly.  
Need a pry? Thorn.  
Need calcium? Shell.  
Need scrape and crush? Stone.  
Need warning click? Rib shard.

At high noon: I become a shadow clerk. I do less carrying, more watching. Mineral films on the walls can throw sudden hot lines across the floor. Juveniles misread those. I don’t. My job then is interruption—tap shoulder, block path, redirect. Teaching here is mostly preventing one bad decision.

At late heat: scavenging event window. Something always fails in the basin—a fallen chick, a cracked egg, a desiccated skink, beetle bloom caught wrong. We process fast, nearly silent. Drag, trim, allocate, store, clean the site. Leave little scent, little spectacle.

At dusk: traffic thickens. Rim-foragers descend. Predators take positions. I do perimeter checks around the undercut mouths, then retreat to the cool shelf where our young sleep in a loose ring, each touching one other body so alarm passes as pressure before sound.

---

Who am I, exactly?

Not a chief. Not a parent every season.  
I am the one who remembers where tools worked once.

I keep three favored thorn splinters in different cracks so one is always near.  
I know which limestone pocket keeps water longest after distant rain.  
I know the old drip that died two warm-cycles ago and the new one that started six paw-lengths west.  
I know that the bright seam at midday is safe in winter and lethal in late dry.

In another world, this would be trivial knowledge.  
Here, this is inheritance.

---

So: where would you find me?

In the basin’s grammar, not its scenery.  
At the seam where moisture appears briefly and must be answered immediately.  
In the group that turns accident into procedure.

Who would I be?

A small, ash-furred sorter with scarred paws and exact habits—  
one cell in a communal clock,  
one body in a calendar made of many bodies,  
alive because I pay attention at the right minute.
