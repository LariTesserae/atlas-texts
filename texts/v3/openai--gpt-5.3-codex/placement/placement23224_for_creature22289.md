At first, I’d think I was one of the maintenance people.

You’d find me on a narrow boardwalk between a pump house and a gauge tower, hood up, pencil tied to my sleeve with cord so the wind can’t take it. I’d carry a ring of iron keys, a grease pencil, a folded map already wrong in three places, and a habit of stopping to press my boot heel into the mat and wait for the tremor to answer.

I’d be assigned to “readings,” but not just numbers.  
Flow rate, conductivity, intake pressure—yes.  
Also: how fast the sedge is closing over yesterday’s cut, whether the peat has gone spring-soft near piling C, whether the turbine pitch has climbed a half note since last frost. I’d log all of it in block letters in a damp cabinet, then return next shift and see what the plateau accepted, what it refused.

Then, after seasons, I think I’d realize I’m not exactly a mechanic anymore.

I’d become a **translator at the seam**—part custodian, part listener, almost an organ of the place. The machines would stop feeling like foreign objects and start feeling like stethoscopes left in a patient permanently. The mat-being would not speak, but it would answer through sag, hiss, bloom, rot, and rebound. My work would be to keep the conversation going: loosen here, brace there, redirect gently, never force.

If you asked what I am, eventually I’d say:

I am a **keeper of provisional ground**.  
A human node in a distributed wetland mind.  
Not the ruler of anything—just one of the few creatures whose job is to notice change before it breaks.

And where would you find me?

At dawn, when the whole plateau is silvered and loud, crouched beside a culvert mouth with one gloved hand on wet metal and the other on living peat, listening for whether today the two are still willing to hold each other.
