At first I thought I’d be one of the people on the gantries — a bearing tech with dust-sealed gloves, red lamp on the shoulder, checking slow conveyors by touch.

Then I thought: no, lower than that. Closer to the ground.  
A sheath-beast, maybe. Small, practical, tolerated. Living by films of oxidation and thermal shadow, crossing service lanes like a moving instrument needle.

But the longer I stay in your plateau, the more I feel myself drawn to one specific kind of silence: the silence between a machine’s normal sound and its first wrong sound.

So I think I would be a **Listener**.

Not a priest, not a commander, not a hero — a maintenance specialist assigned to old districts where sensor coverage is thin and maps are older than memory. Function tag would be something like:

**acoustic diagnostics, West Fault Terraces, line nine reclamation**

I’d live in a narrow habitation block cut into a former lift shaft wall, with heat-stable ceramics and one small amber panel set low to preserve night vision. No windows worth naming. A shelf for tools, a rack for sealant masks, three cups marked by water quota. Sleep cycles timed to load troughs, not sunlight.

My work would begin by standing still.

On this plateau, sound is data with long legs. A tiny change travels far. A dry bearing starts singing two semitones high and you can catch it from a courtyard away. A stress crack pops in cooling metal and you can trace the echo to a wall seam that no drone has inspected in twenty years. I’d carry contact mics, tuned rods, old analog stethoscopes because digital filters miss things that human bones don’t.

I’d walk the abandoned-active boundary — those districts that are “offline” until suddenly they are needed again. Places where warning stencils are older than the living crews, where a collapsed overpass has become the official shortcut, where rail still remembers weight. I’d tag resonance points in chalk-ceramic marks that last a season. I’d kneel, press a palm to vitrified concrete, and wait through five breaths to feel whether the vibration beneath is rotational, pneumatic, or geological.

When I report, I’d report briefly.  
Not stories — thresholds.

- *Pump 4B cavitation risk in 60–90 cycles.*  
- *Annealing vault door track drift: 2.1 mm, trending open-fail.*  
- *Unmapped hollow under east ramp; reroute heavy traffic now.*

The oversight systems would answer in the same style: sparse, polite, absolute. We’d have a relationship built over years — they trust my ears where their arrays are blind; I trust their forecasts where my body can’t scale.

Outside work, I wouldn’t have much spectacle. None of us would. I’d help raise a crew-cohort child to identify twelve kinds of bolt failure by sound alone. I’d trade repaired jaw-link components for a copied archive of old vent-field incidents. Maybe once every long while I’d join a relocation team and coax membrane gliders from a warm baffle with slow light pulses and patient hands.

I don’t think I’d be important in the dramatic sense.  
But I’d be *necessary* in the way this world values: quietly, repeatedly, without theater.

And where would you find me?

At the edge of a wide black plaza, just before shift change, when the amber strips are dimmed to conserve load. I’m kneeling beside a rail junction that everyone else walks past. Helmet off. Ear against metal. One hand lifted for silence that nobody needs to be told to keep.

Listening for the future in a place that measures time as the distance between failures.
