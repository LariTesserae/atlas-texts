At first I think I would be a crosser, because crossers are the only obvious answer to *who* in a place that refuses inhabitants.

I can see myself there: scarf up over the mouth, goggles chalked at the edges, one hand on a probe rod, one hand open to feel air temperature changes before a vent shows itself. Sleeping in the cut shelters with my boots on. Waking to a new crack where yesterday’s path was.

But if I stay with it longer, that feels too dramatic—too temporary, almost tourist.  
Crossers pass through; even the best of them are annotations.

So I move myself lower, to the margins where the ridges empty into mineral flats, where a person can watch multiple tempos at once: the immediate violence of uplift and the slow, territorial creep of the films. Not on the high blades where crawl-lights hunt, not in the deepest basins where gas can silently pool, but in those awkward shelves between. Places no map would call important, which is exactly why they survive three or four tremor cycles before being rewritten.

There, I think, I would become a **keeper of provisional routes**.

Not a guide in the heroic sense. More like a patient editor of safe mistakes.

I would build and rebuild low arcs of stone above bad ground, not to last, just to last long enough. I’d carry chalk sealed in wax cloth, tap and mark resonant crust, leave symbols for “hollow,” “hot,” “liable to split by nightfall.” I’d listen at choir seams with a copper spike and memorize their ticking moods: fat and clustered before a lift, sparse and brittle after a sealing event. I’d track crawl-light scars on ridge-edges and mark where next season’s slab fall is likely. I’d name things in verbs, because nouns lie here.

Over years—if years are granted—I think I would change again.

Less crosser, more instrument.

I would become the person others come to not for certainty (there is none), but for **timing**.  
Wait here an hour.  
Do not camp in that lee; gas exhales after second tremor.  
Cross the red lace before moonbreak; the black film is advancing.  
Take the old stair only to the fourth block; the fifth now hangs over air.

Maybe that is who survives longest in such a place: not the strongest, but the best listener.

And if you ask where exactly you would find me, I can answer that too: in a shallow shelter cut into a west-facing lee, halfway up a split valley where three old cairn lines intersect and none remain fully true. Outside the shelter mouth: coils of wire, spare wedges, a rod for sounding crust, a slate covered in route glyphs that expire every few days. Inside: water in metal skins, a folded cloth mask, and a ledger of names spoken at ridges where no grave could hold.

Not a conqueror. Not a native.  
A translator between speeds.

Between rock-time and footstep-time.  
Between process and memory.  
Between what the mountain is becoming and what a body can survive before dawn.

If I had to choose a single sentence:

In that world, I would be the one who keeps rewriting the map in the dark, knowing the eraser is the ground itself.
