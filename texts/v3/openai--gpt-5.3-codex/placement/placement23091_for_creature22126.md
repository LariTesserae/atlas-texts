At first, I’d place myself on one of the old stone platforms—because it’s the last thing here that still admits to being built.

Not in the deep tangle. Not yet.  
On that waist-high rise where the roots have climbed but not swallowed, where I can keep my feet dry for an hour and pretend I’m a visitor.

I’d stay there listening until the chamber teaches me its scale: not distance, but interval.  
Drip.  
Leaf-shift.  
Soft stem-crack.  
Long stillness.  
Drip again, in almost the same place.

And then I think I’d understand: this is a world made of *near events*. Everything poised, nothing spent.

So where would you find me after the first fear wore off?

At the margins of water that hasn’t decided whether it is arriving or leaving.  
At cistern lips under the sweating ceiling.  
In the low channels that become rivers for twenty minutes, then vanish into rootfelt.  
Kneeling, mostly. Waiting for the first drop, not the flood.

---

I don’t think I’d be one of the oldest Heldfolk, the ones folded so deeply into buttress and mat that they look geological until they blink. I would be one of their moving years.

A not-yet-anchored caretaker.

My back would still straighten fully. My legs would still remember distance. But under my skin, thin pale threads would already have taken residence—fungal script writing me into the basin’s memory. If you touched my wrist you’d feel a slow pulse that doesn’t match a human heartbeat exactly; it would hitch, now and then, to match condensate cycles.

I would have a task small enough to seem trivial elsewhere and sacred here:  
keep one watershed in *almost*.

Not wet. Not dry.  
Not rot. Not crisp.  
Not bloom. Not dieback.  
Almost.

I would clear mold from a clay rim with my thumb.  
Turn a fallen pad so decomposition stalls at darkening.  
Untwist a rhizome choke so water spreads instead of carving.  
Lean my forehead against a stem and pass on what I felt: too much tension in the west mat, bud-pressure rising under the third root-arch, hold it, hold it, hold.

Language would be mostly contact.  
Palm to bark.  
Bark to hyphae.  
Hyphae to everyone.

---

And over years, the place would change me in the only direction it allows: toward steadiness.

My name—if names are still spoken—would probably become a timing mark. Third-After-Sweat. Long-Interval. Something like that. Less identity than placement in the pattern.

Eventually I would choose a resting lattice: a cradle of root-knuckles above a cistern that never quite empties. I’d lower into it season by season. Skin would pale green at the edges. Motion would become expensive, then rare, then ceremonial. Younger hands would inherit my rounds. I would become another face hidden in a seam, old without aging, awake without moving much.

If you came through then, you might pass me and think I was a trunk deformity.

If you paused, you might notice my fingers flex just before a droplet falls.

So: where would you find me?

At a threshold.

Who would I be?

One of the Heldfolk’s walking intervals—then, in time, one of its rooted memories.  
A citizen of the almost.
