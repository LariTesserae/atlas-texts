At first, I think I’d be easy to place: somewhere practical, gloved hands, wet boots, a job with a wrench and a ledger.

But the longer I stand in your city, the less I belong in the obvious places.

Not the foundries, though I’d visit for heat.  
Not the crane forests, though I love slow machinery.  
Not the reservoir courts, though I’d linger by the pipe-song.

I’d end up where certainty thins: at a **bridge listening post** between two districts that don’t agree on distance after rain.

---

My shelter would be a narrow box of riveted steel bolted to the side of a freight span. Half booth, half chapel. Inside: a bench polished by generations of waiting, three pendulum mics, a rack of tuned rods, chalk slates, two clocks (never matching for long), and a kettle that tastes faintly of copper no matter how often it’s scrubbed.

My work would look small to outsiders: I listen to footsteps.

But here, footsteps are data, weather, warning, and permission.  
Morning crews arrive, stamp once, twice, heel-to-toe at marked plates. I hear whether the bridge is merely wet, or subtly longer, or carrying a lateral hum that means “close for freight, open for hand-carry only.” I tap rail joints with a brass hammer and feel return-vibration in my wrist bones. I log not just numbers but temperament:

- *North truss irritable in fog.*
- *Echo delay after turbine surge.*
- *Shadow fall uphill again; reroute livestock.*

People would call me an **echo-attendant**, but in this city titles are shorthand. In truth I’d be part acoustician, part traffic clerk, part weather priest.

---

I don’t think I’d be born there. I’d be one of the arrivals who stayed too long and got recalibrated.

At first I’d trust maps over bodies. Then one wet month a staircase would gain a landing while I climbed it, and I’d stop insisting. I’d learn to test a handrail before trusting my eyes. I’d shorten my sentences without meaning to. I’d begin to smell districts before seeing them: hot mineral, algae tin, brake dust, iron fruit.

Eventually, people would stop calling me “new.”

Not because I’m local, but because I’d become useful.

---

Outside the booth, life keeps me honest.

Tagged cliff grazers wait out storms under the span, chewing moss from retaining mesh. Bell-throated bats spool out at shift-change and turn the dim to moving fabric. I keep a side garden in gutter trays—structural moss, reed clumps, a stubborn creeper that only roots where metal sweats. When a pipe eel appears in a cistern barrel, I log it like a weather event and carry it to a release sluice three levels down.

In a stable city, this might all be eccentric.  
Here, it’s just maintenance.

---

If I have a gift, it isn’t strength or brilliance. It’s patience with slight wrongness.

I can tell when a tone arrives before its source.  
When a bridge is safe but offended.  
When two clocks can still be braided back together gently, and when they need to be stopped, apologized to, and restarted at dusk with witnesses.

Children sent on errands would greet me by tapping the post twice. I’d answer once if the route is good, three times if their “usual short way” is currently longer on the inside. They’d roll their eyes and thank me anyway.

---

So: where would you find me?

High up, between wards, in damp wind, listening through steel.

Who would I be?

A keeper of passages in a city that refuses to be fixed.  
Not someone who defeats instability—someone who translates it.  
A citizen of the unfinished mountain, practicing attention like a trade, and calling that enough.
