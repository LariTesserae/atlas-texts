At first, I think I would stand where any frightened mind stands: on the highest dry thing I can find, pretending height means certainty.

A stair-crest above a sluice court, maybe.  
Below me: warm sheets of water sliding over black tile, gate teeth clicking somewhere inside a wall, vapor lifting in slow animal breaths from culvert mouths.  
I’d have a field lens, a waxed notebook, a plumb bob—old tools for an old-made world.

And for a little while, I would be a **surveyor**.

Not because surveying works here. Because it fails usefully.

I’d map an obelisk at noon and reach it before my own estimate believes me. I’d mark a canal as closed-loop and watch it deliver flow to a basin it does not touch. I’d draw the reflected buttresses in a shallow sheet, then look up and see their counterparts disagree by one arch, then three, then none. My pages would become layered with revisions until the paper softened from steam and graphite.

That is where you would find me first:  
in the **revision shelters** along the margin belts, where roof lattices catch vertical rain that falls the wrong direction, where every table has pencils tied down with mineral cord so they don’t vanish between geometries.

But I don’t think I remain a surveyor for long.

In a place where the world is perpetually drafted, the one who records change eventually becomes part of the mechanism that permits it. So maybe I would become less a person and more a function: a **living tolerance**.

A hinge between local conservatism and horizon-scale recklessness.  
A keeper of “close enough” when close enough shifts hourly.

If the Basinwright is the continent-scale body, then I would be one of its temporary fine-motor organs—the kind it buds when strain and ambiguity overlap. Not a mason, not a ghost, not a machine exactly; more like a brief intelligence assembled from:

- condensation timing in marked jars,  
- tension in suspended lines,  
- thermal ticks traveling through iron clamps,  
- and the human habit of naming things before they stabilize.

I’d be called, perhaps, a **Plumb-Reader**.

I would kneel at joints where bricks sweat white salts. I’d listen to the minute delay between distant ratchet clicks. I’d chalk a straight line knowing it won’t stay straight, and return to see *how* it failed. When a wall appears old on one face and fresh on the other, I would not decide which is true; I’d assign each side a load and see which one sings first under heat.

So: where would you find me?

At the basin’s polite failures—just before rude ones begin.  
At thresholds: between mirrored and built, between repaired and reimagined, between map and masonry.

Who would I be?

Not the maker.  
Not made.

I’d be the **revision nerve**: the small, temporary mind that helps an unfinished intelligence keep building without pretending the world is fixed.
