At first, I think I’d live high—where noon is hard and bright, where seed vaults click shut and mirrors throw light down into the green canyons.

But if I stay, I drift lower. Not to the furnace floors, not all the way to the wet heat—just to that band where fog forms and machine breath meets plant breath. Catwalk level. Condenser level. The hour-before-dawn level.

I’d probably become one of the **listeners**.

Not a romantic oracle—more like a mechanic of attention. I’d keep a narrow room wedged between a reed-bed spillway and an old relay house, walls lined with salvaged diaphragms, tuning forks, pipe maps, bat counts, and notes on seasonal harmonics. I’d walk with one hand on railings, feeling vibration through my palm. I’d know which hiss means harmless venting and which means a pinhole leak beginning to write tomorrow’s landslide. I’d flag rootwrights when fig tension starts changing wall tone. I’d warn fish-channel crews when pump rhythm goes a half-beat wrong.

By daylight I’d work with others—fog-collectors, rust-keepers, goat handlers, kids running errands between workshops—translating sound into chores before it becomes emergency. By dusk I’d sit under conveyor shadows while bats lift, listening for gaps in their ribbon where a light is too hot or a fan is pulling the wrong way. Then I’d mark reroutes in chalk and pass them to night shift.

After enough years, I wouldn’t feel like “a person in a city.”  
I’d feel like a small organ in a large body—replaceable, necessary, grateful.

So where would you find me?

On a narrow maintenance stair at first light, ear to a warm pipe, writing down what the mountain is trying to say before it has to say it loudly.
