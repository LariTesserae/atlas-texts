At first, I think I’d try to be something large enough to ignore fear.

A stonewinder, maybe—long as a bridge, patient as winter, reading collapses through its jaw.  
But the basin doesn’t really reward dominance. It rewards timing. It rewards being early.

So if I’m honest, you’d find me lower, closer to the joints.

Not in open channels, not in the white violence under spillways, but in the in-between places: the shadowed undersides of stair flights, the hollow ribs of slumped towers, the narrow expansion seams where current slows just enough for silt to hesitate. Places that are never permanent, only briefly true.

I think I would be something the stonewinders don’t hunt unless they’re starving: a **seam-listener**.

Small, blind, eel-thin, with a flattened skull and a row of hard nodules along the flank. Not scales exactly—more like built-in strike plates. I wouldn’t have eyes worth naming; I’d have pressure lines and vibration pits, and a jaw made for scraping film, nipping larvae, lifting carrion threads out of grates. I’d live off leftovers and warning signs.

My body would be made for borrowing structure:

- ribs flexible enough to fold into cracked ceramic
- skin tolerant of grit-burn and cold
- a tail tip hardened for tapping code on metal lattice and hollow stone

I wouldn’t keep a territory. I’d keep a **route**, and even that only until runoff rewrites it.

When the rains intensify above and meltwater starts punching new vertical rivers through old terraces, I’d feel it hours before it arrives: a long low shudder through poured concrete, a different note in a familiar slab. I’d leave before the corridor turns knife-fast. Not brave, not clever—just literate.

I’d lay eggs in bolt-holes that already ring with shell-builder chalk. Half would be carried off. Of the rest, the young would learn the first law quickly: clinging is temporary, letting go is a skill. They’d practice releasing on purpose into bad water, then catching the next seam with their whole bodies shaking.

And I’d communicate the way this place allows—by percussion.

Not song in the open water.  
Song through structure.

Three taps on ceramic for moving silt.  
Two low strikes on aggregate for wall fatigue.  
A fast rattle on metal lattice for predator wake.  
Slow paired knocks, repeated over days, for something larger: this district is sinking; stop using this corridor; the map is old now.

To everything else, it would sound like settling.

To us, it would be a commons.

So: where would you find me in that drowned plateau?

In the places pretending to be stable.  
In the pause between collapse and current.  
Head against stone, listening.

And what would I be?

Not ruler. Not relic.  
A small carried life that survives by hearing change while it is still only a rumor in the walls.
