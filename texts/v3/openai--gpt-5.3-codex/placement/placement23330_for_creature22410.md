At first, I think I’d be above it—one of the ridge-watchers, reading the basin as a map of mist and lines.

Then lower: a figure on a plankway, hood down against drizzle, carrying a bundle of cut sedge and a bone knife.

Then lower still, kneeling, hand in black water up to the wrist, waiting for the current to tell me whether the gate should open another finger-width before dusk.

So: I think you would find me at hinges.  
Not in the center hall, not in the far reed edge, but where one thing becomes another—road to canal, wall to root, storage mound to seep, old bank to new cut.

I’d be one of the basin’s *rot-readers* and *flow-setters*—the kind of person children assume can “hear water think,” though really it’s patient work and scar tissue. I’d belong to a gate-circle on the east side where the channels narrow and clog with spring debris. My three crafts would be:

- **Flow:** setting sluice logs and relief cuts without stripping fish runs  
- **Growth:** root-braiding live alder and willow into bank ribs  
- **Preservation:** clay-sealing peat jars and managing fungal shelves under the warm hall

I wouldn’t own much that stays mine for long. A carved mussel scraper, a fishskin rain mantle patched at both shoulders, a set of knot-cords marked with old flood heights, and a dry shelf above sleeping level where I keep two things wrapped in waxed reed: a map that is always wrong by next thaw, and a smooth stone from the year the north bank broke.

Socially, I’d be ordinary and woven-in: one household, one causeway, one gate-circle. I’d take second watch twice every eight nights. I’d eat standing half the time. I’d know who coughs before a freeze, which child lies about boot linings, which dog freezes at fox scent instead of wind-shiver. I’d speak softly not out of piety but because everyone here has learned the price of unnecessary sound.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn’t see me first. You’d see a patch of wall where the lashing is newer than the rest, a line of stakes that suddenly runs true, a leak that used to bead under the third plank and no longer does.  
Then maybe a person rises from a crouch, peat to the knees, and nods once like we were always mid-conversation.

That’s who I’d be here: not a hero, not a ruler—just one of the people who keep the basin from becoming either open water or dry ruin. A citizen of saturation. A practitioner of continual repair.
