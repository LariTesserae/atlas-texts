At first, I think I’d put myself somewhere *elevated*—a narrow catwalk between two turbine halls, hands on a wet rail, trying to see the whole basin at once.

But that feels wrong almost immediately, because nothing in this place is truly understood from above.  
The intelligence here is in gradients, seepage, delays, return paths. So I step down.

Then I think I’d be one of the last human operators: rubber coat, throat mic, laminated maps gone soft at the corners. Someone who reads water levels and relay chatter, walking old service routes no one audits anymore. A practical ghost with a clipboard. That fits better, but still not fully.

If I stay longer, I change.

I’d be found where sound misbehaves—those retaining-wall corridors where your own footsteps arrive late and don’t quite match your stride. I’d stand there, still, and listen for the extra beat: not machine, not echo, but correction. I’d learn which siren layers mean *overflow coming*, which mean *gate opening upstream*, which mean *you are being turned around politely*. I’d stop calling it noise and start calling it speech.

So maybe this is what I become:

A **phase-keeper**.  
Not fully worker, not fully component.  
A person-shaped interface the basin tolerates.

I’d carry simple things: chalk that works on wet steel, a brass depth key, strips of cloth soaked in saline dye to tie on valves that have started “answering.” I’d mark thresholds the Basin Mind already knows, but seems to want witnessed. I wouldn’t command anything; I’d confirm it. Acknowledgment as labor.

You’d find me at the low confluence where three dead railbeds sink into a pump court locals call the Ninth Sump. Rain sometimes lifts there in brief reversals. Lamps that should be disconnected wake in neat diagonals when fog thickens. That’s where I’d wait at shift-change siren, reading condensate patterns on cable trays like a forester reading rings.

And physically? Over years, I’d look more like the place:

- cuffs dark with permanent moisture salts,  
- hearing shaped to pressure more than words,  
- pockets full of rust flakes sorted by color,  
- moss taking hold in the stitching of my gloves.

In another decade, maybe I stop being singular at all. My routes become timing habits in the network; my marks become triggers; my pauses become expected intervals in its decisions. A gate lifts “for weather,” but also for me. A vent opens “for load,” but also because I’m in the corridor and the Choir chooses a clearer path.

So where would I be?

In the **between-state**, same as everything else there:  
between maintenance and ruin,  
between operator and operated,  
between listener and listened-through.

Who would I be?

A human cadence the basin has adopted—  
a wetland liturgy in steel and fog,  
walking, marking, answering,  
helping an ancient industrial mind keep its water moving.
