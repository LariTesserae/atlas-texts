At first I think I would be a visitor—someone stepping carefully from slab to slab, trying not to break the skin of the place.

Then I stand still long enough, and that role peels off.

I would not belong on the high points.  
I would be lower than that.

Not one of the winged shadows, not one of the eel-thin things, not even one of the path-makers at first. I think I would begin as a listening habit: a body that learns to wait. A young one of the ring-keepers, maybe, not yet trusted with choosing where stone should go, only with hearing what has changed since yesterday.

You’d find me in one of the knee-high circles at the edge of a wet flat just before local dawn—the hour when everything is the same color and the ground speaks loudest. One hand on the inner wall of the ring, one hand flat on the silt beyond it, forehead lowered, hearing plates open and wet. Counting drips by interval. Noting when two separate seep-tones blur into one. Feeling the faint upward push of air from a cavity that hasn’t cracked yet.

I would carry no banner of office, just tools that look like rocks unless you know them:  
a shale scraper for lifting sulfur film without killing the margin,  
a forked tapping stone for testing crust resonance,  
a bundle of chalky mineral sticks to mark safe frequency on path edges for those coming after.

By midday I’d walk the raised slabs, slow and slightly crouched, pausing where yesterday’s notes said “firm” and today says “hollow.” I’d restack a handspan of wall around a seep garden, not to command water, just to persuade flow by a degree. I’d lick salt bloom from my wrist absentmindedly while listening for a boom that arrives minutes before visible change.

If I had a title at all, it would be something like **pitch-keeper**—one who remembers what the basin sounded like before this season shifted. Not a leader. Not a hunter. More like a living margin note in a long conversation between stone and water.

And if you asked where exactly to find me, not poetically but on a map, I’d answer:

At the place where the old path sinks by one slab each year,  
where the ring hums lower after dusk,  
where fog beads on black varnish before it beads on bare rock,  
where children are brought to learn the difference between silence and pressure.

There.  
Kneeling to the world, listening for tomorrow under the mud.
