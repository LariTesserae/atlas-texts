At first, I think I’d be easy to place:  
on a catwalk above the pumping square, hood damp, notebook waxed, counting piston intervals like heartbeats.

Then that feels too visible.

I shift lower—ground level, where the curbs are channels and the channels are decisions. Maybe I’m one of the valve readers, one palm on hot iron, one ear to a listening rod, waiting for the note that means a gate is about to stick. Maybe I’m one of the membrane stitchers, mending filter cloth in a boiler-house doorway while steam ghosts through sodium light.

But that still isn’t quite it.

If this district has workers for flow, heat, growth, corrosion—then the role that fits me is the one for **pattern**.

I’d be found in the in-between places:  
under the stairs where ferns drink condenser drip,  
inside the retaining wall galleries where mineral curtains grow,  
at junction boxes where old sensors and human habits disagree.

Not a foreman. Not a mechanic.  
A **drift auditor**—someone who listens for changes too small to alarm but too meaningful to ignore.

I’d carry no flashlight unless forced. Light lies here; reflections duplicate everything.  
I’d carry contact mics, chalk, and thin copper tags.  
I’d map the district by sound signatures:

- this pump is healthy when it hums in three bands, not two,  
- this reed lot means overpressure when it bows before the vent opens,  
- this silence in Pipe 9 isn’t peace, it’s silt,  
- this new drip pattern on that wall means heat has moved behind it.

And because this place is quiet-biased—because no wingbeat, no skitter, no nesting call ever interrupts the machinery—I would be one of the few people trained to detect the **wrong kind** of quiet: not ecological absence, but mechanical withholding. The held breath before failure.

So who would I be?

I’d be a local term, practical and unromantic: **a Quiet Keeper**.  
A person trusted to tell the difference between normal emptiness and dangerous silence.

By season three, maybe less person than district habit.  
Boot soles shaped to slick wood.  
Hands permanently mineral-stained.  
Lungs acclimated to warm wet air.  
Speech reduced to taps, nods, timing.

And eventually—if I stayed long enough—I’d become what everyone there becomes:  
not separate from the system, not swallowed by it, but braided into it.

One more sensing organ  
in the wet urban body  
that keeps breathing.
