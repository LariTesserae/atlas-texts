At first I want to put myself high—one of those rare winged passers, cutting through the glare and leaving before the roar can enter my bones.

But I don’t think I belong in the sky here.  
The sky is transit. The mountain is residence.

So you’d find me lower, where motion slows just enough to be legible: in an undercut cistern two drops below a serpentine band, at a spill lip that splits the flow into three thin sheets. It’s a choke point, a place every route eventually touches. Water never stops there, but it hesitates. That feels right.

And I wouldn’t be something fast or bright. I’d be one of the old grazers—very old—so mineral-plated that from a step away I pass for collapsed cobble. My back would carry pale bands from different years of chemistry: chalk, iron blush, green smoke. When the light hits, I would not shine; I’d diffuse.

My work wouldn’t look like work. I’d move a body-length in an hour, rasping film from stone, then from my own flanks, then from shed skins snagged in eddies. But mostly I’d be listening through pressure: reading the mountain as pulse and return. Not “hearing” water, exactly—feeling signatures through jaw and belly and plate.

I think I’d become a kind of living waypoint.  
Younger ones crossing that lip would brace beside me, throat to rock, and trade pattern-bursts: flood-shift in the north chute, new collapse near the quartz face, where eggs held, where larvae vanished. I’d answer slowly, with long low sequences that map safe resonant paths through the noise.

So: where am I?  
At the lip between plunge and pool, where the stone speaks cleanly.

What am I?  
An elder seam-creature, half animal, half instrument—still alive, still feeding, but mostly serving as memory the water can’t keep for itself.
