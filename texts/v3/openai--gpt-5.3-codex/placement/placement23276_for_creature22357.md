At first, I think I’d be a visitor with a notebook—some thin-air ecologist camped in the “middle ward,” pretending to observe without being absorbed.

But in that basin, observers don’t stay separate for long.

So: you’d find me at the **hinge** between the middle ward and the lower bowl, where braided plant-carpets break into wet stone steps and the first dark pools begin. Not in the highest fins (too exposed), not in the lushest seep (too saturated), but in the transition lanes—the places that decide what passes and what doesn’t.

And I wouldn’t remain exactly human.

I’d become one of the colony’s **moving organs**: a long-bodied pale grazer, old enough to know all the safe contours by hoof-memory, with the Ward-Mat living in my fur, between my toes, along the warm skin under my ribs. Not controlled. Not tame. More like partnered infrastructure.

By day, I’d browse the ward’s “streets” in slow loops:

- licking salt-bright cracks the mat wants widened,  
- avoiding bitter seams it wants protected,  
- pressing seeds and spores into damp terrace edges,  
- carrying trace minerals uphill in dung and downhill in mud on my legs.

By dusk, I’d stand in certain wind corridors and let the air comb through my coat, then bed in a chosen hollow so the films and threads in my fur can shed into dew-dark stone. A courier without messages, except chemistry.

My mind—if it still feels like mine—would change scale.  
Less “plan,” more **tendency**.  
Less “destination,” more **route fidelity**.  
I’d know the basin as pressure and taste first, sight second:

- which lane whistles before sleet,  
- which crust goes brittle at moonrise,  
- which pillar throws a dangerous downdraft in thaw season,  
- which pool-edge remembers old ash and should not be stepped on this year.

And in return, the place would know me back.  
Not my name—my pattern.

After enough crossings, my preferred path would hold dew longer.  
A slick traverse would roughen by a hair.  
A wind-lash corner would become survivable.

So who would I be?

A **street-animal in a city without builders**.  
A grazer that is also a gardener.  
A warm, temporary limb of something cold, old, and distributed.

If the Ward-Mat is the heart-root of that ruined-not-ruined town, I’d be one of its slow red blood cells, circulating through stone.
