I don’t think I’d be the bold one standing out on the ledge at peak thunder.

I’d start lower than that—half-hidden, useful, easily missed.

At first I imagine myself as one of the cords: swollen, frayed, tied from peg to peg where the eye can’t trust the water. A thing under tension, speaking only in pulls. Two tugs: rise. Three: drop. No voice, just signal.

But cords rot. So maybe not the cord itself—maybe the one who keeps replacing it.

---

I’d be found on the **shoulder-height margins** of that chamber:  
not in the deepest surge, not in the driest fantasy of safety, but on the narrow wet shelves where decisions get made.

Near the notch wall.  
Near the half-shelter that never quite dries.  
Near the basket of clay weights and bone hooks, where everything is sorted by “will this still work soaked?”

I’d be the storm-keeper who comes a little before others, touches old cuts in the stone, and makes new ones only when certain. Not an elder, not a hero—more like a **reader-scribe of water**, apprenticed to impact.

Someone whose hands remember:

- how fresh basalt feels different from old skin-rock,
- how clay gives under a step half a breath before it shears,
- how to brace knees and ribs when the air pocket booms,
- how to keep count when lightning blanks thought.

---

I think my tool would be small: a notch blade of polished stone, a cord spool, a pouch of pegs.  
I’d travel light because everything here punishes attachment.

And I’d measure in storm-language, not hours:
first hammer, long lateral spray, brief silver lull, second rise.

If people asked my work, I wouldn’t say “fishing” or “building.”  
I’d say: **“I keep the chamber legible.”**

I reset what was torn loose.  
I mark where the water actually reached, not where anyone hoped it would stop.  
I test crossings and leave signs that only trained hands read at a glance.  
I carry memory forward one storm at a time, knowing the wall will eventually overwrite me too.

---

So: where would you find me?

On a slick ledge three body-lengths above the current line, one hand on a taut wet cord, the other on a new notch, cheek turned to feel pressure change before the surge arrives.

Who would I be?

Not master of the place. Not owner.  
A practitioner of temporary wisdom.  
A flood-reader whose craft is to witness change fast enough that others live through it.

In a chamber where nothing dries and nothing lasts, I’d be one of the people who make *usable truth* between storms.
