At first I think I’d be the obvious thing: the lone worker in patched gloves, head down, tapping a pin straighter before the light turns.

But if I stay with it longer, I end up somewhere narrower.

Not in the shelter.  
Not on the traverse.  
I’m in the **hour between thaw and lock**—that brief hinge when water is still moving and stone has not yet decided to close. I’d plan my whole day around that seam. I’d live by it.

So: you’d find me on the basin’s eastern side, where a dark rib of rock catches first sun and sheds it first too. There’s a runnel there that only speaks for forty minutes on clear days. In those forty minutes I’d work: clean the channel mouth with a bone-handled pick, reset two fist-sized stones that guide melt away from a crack, mark the new edge with charcoal in the ledger. Then stop. Always stop before haste starts lying.

Who would I be?

Not a builder, exactly.  
A **tuner**.

Someone who keeps the basin legible.

I’d tune cairns so they read in side-glare. Tune footholds so a boot finds them in fog by ankle memory. Tune metal pins to the stone’s current mood, not last season’s. I’d listen for pitch—ice under tension, slab off its bed, wind finding a new throat in the wall—and adjust small things before large things decide themselves.

Over years, I think I’d thin into the work until I became hard to separate from it: one more repeating action in a place of repeating actions. The basin would not need my name, only my returns. Someone later would see a bracket moved, a wall restacked, a notch recut, and know not who but **that attention had been here**.

So if you arrived and asked where I am, the truest answer might be:

I’m in the slight corrections.  
In the kept passages.  
In the difference between a route that almost fails and one that holds one more winter.
