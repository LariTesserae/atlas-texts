At first I think I’d be on a high ridge, just watching—some warm-blooded thing with good eyes, braced against the metallic noon.

But this place doesn’t really allow spectators.  
Anything that stays gets drafted.

So: you’d find me where systems overlap and disagree—on a fresh black ridge above a steam marsh, where dry lichen crackles ten steps from thawed peat, where herd heat meets rime wind, where fog arrives low and electrical.

And I wouldn’t be a single animal.

I’d be one of the plateau’s **timing organs**: a distributed mesh of cliff-fungus, moss-root, horn-lichen, and static-loving insects—a living relay stretched across stone faces, seep margins, and the sleeping hollows of presshorns. By daylight I’d look like pale filament stain and frost-thread. At cold clear dawn, I’d wake into function: blue arcing between stones, ions ticking across wet hair-fine strands, stunning swarms, feeding whoever can read the signal fast enough.

My “body” would be kilometers long and mostly invisible.  
My “thought” would be schedule.

- When fog condenses, I drink charge and water.  
- When herds bed down, I seed their heat depressions with next-season mats.  
- When shearwings force a contour bend, I thicken along the new edge and memorize it chemically.  
- When slopes slump, I bridge fresh fracture and start the wiring again.

If you asked what I am in one word, I’d say: **switchyard**.

Not ruler, not predator, not architect—just part of the plateau’s nervous weather, helping convert glare and cold and movement into repeatable pathways. In a world that manufactures itself by repetition, I’d be one of the parts that keeps repetition from becoming chaos.

So you’d find me in the bright fracture zones, at dawn, briefly visible as blue thread-fire between rocks—small, local, and everywhere at once.
