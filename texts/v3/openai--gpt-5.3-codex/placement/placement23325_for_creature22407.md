At first I want to put myself somewhere dramatic:  
on the lip of an amphitheater cliff, wind in my teeth, looking down on all that ribbed stone.

But that feels like a visitor’s pose. A painting position.  
To *live* there, I think I’d end up lower, tucked into function.

Not valley-low—too exposed to pulse floods and night movement.  
Not peak-high—too dry, too stolen by wind.  
I’d be in the **fog belt**, where the first wet threads catch: a saddle between two old walls, with a root-arch overhead and a dry-stone shelter pressed into the lee side.

I think I’d be human, but not a hunter in the heroic sense.  
A **line-keeper**. A knot-worker. One of the people who makes movement possible.

---

My day would begin before dawn when the stone is still sweating out night-cold.  
I’d walk ridge-top with a coil of plaited cane across my chest, checking anchor pegs, replacing lashings where wind has worried them loose, tapping old holds with a bone hammer to hear if the rock behind them has gone soft.

I’d know the route-sounds:  
- the hiss of fog dripping through bromeliad tanks,  
- the dry click of beetles waking,  
- the sudden soft *absence* when everything goes quiet and your skin tells you to move.

I’d keep small wind shrines at pass mouths not because I fear gods, exactly, but because bone chimes tell truth before your body does. If the chime-note drops, a gust is folding around the ridge. If it chatters too fast, don’t cross with load.

I’d carry almost no metal.  
A stone blade, a resin-sealed gourd, fiber cord, bone toggles, a pouch of fig-root charcoal for marking safe holds after rain.

---

But that still sounds too tidy. The place wouldn’t let a person be one thing.

In storm weeks I’d be a **water thief**: setting leaf gutters, cleaning moss pans, cutting channels in peatfelt so drip fills cistern instead of vanishing into void.  
In dry weeks I’d be an **egg climber** with soot-black hands and a rope burn down one shoulder.  
When herds transit at dusk, I’d be a **listener**, still as lichen, waiting for their drumming to pass before crossing open shelves.  
After thick fog nights, I’d become a **crab rival**, quick at carrion before the crevice mouths unzip and pale joints come tapping out.

Maybe that is the real identity there: not profession, but timing.

---

If you asked others who I am, they might call me something like  
**“the one who ties what moves.”**

I’d tie packs, roofs, splints, ladders, children, offerings, drying racks, trap doors, sleeping rolls.  
I’d tie because wind steals, because stone rounds, because life there is mostly grip under stress.

And where would you find me, exactly?

At first light: on a narrow traverse above a dry bowl, one hand on a polished hold older than memory.  
At noon: in the shade of a root curtain, mending a harness while insects rasp like hot wire.  
At dusk: below the dripline I watched all night, counting frog calls in a temporary basin and deciding if tomorrow’s route is safe.  
At dark: inside a low shelter with bark roof and damp-smelling walls, listening to the ridge hum and the bone chimes speak in their small, practical voices.

So: not king of the heights, not beast of the cliff.  
A person in the mesh.  
A keeper of attachment in a country where everything living has climbed.
