At first, I don’t think I arrive as a person.

I arrive as a pause.

Not silence—silence here is alarm—but a tiny missed beat between two channel booms, the place where a wrist-clack should have been and isn’t. In a land this loud, identity starts as what you can notice that others step over.

So if I’m in this world, you would not find me on the open flats with the fast runners, or chest-deep in Spawn Drift funnels, or leading trade in Hard Clear. I would be off to one side of all that, where the noise stacks in layers: under a basalt lip, beside a hollow chamber that turns surf into organ notes, at the edge of a vent-field where the mud thumps like a buried drumline.

I’d live on a narrow shelf that floods at king tide and dries only in patches—a bad place for comfort, good place for hearing. Above me: a bone chime line, each piece cut from a different beast so each answers wind and spray differently. Below: a stake ladder I can pull up when the flats turn to moving teeth. Behind: a smoke-black seam-cave with three bedrolls, two spare horn shells, one oilskin bundle of old flow-recitations, and a rack of mended nets that will never be “finished,” only “holding.”

I think I would be one of their necessary, unglorious specialists:

**a Gap Listener.**

Not elder, not hunter—an interval keeper.  
The one who listens for wrongness in the continuous roar.

I’d mark days by pattern integrity:
- Vent-thump regularity at dawn
- Cliff-flute pitch at mid-flood
- Grazer bark density at fogfall
- Drip tempo in overhangs before pressure change

When everything behaves, I report almost nothing.  
When I hear a hollow where there should be clutter, a deadened echo where there should be ring, I sound horn-code and people move before they understand why.

I’d be trusted mostly when I’m inconvenient.

“Don’t cross on second surge.”  
“Leave the full weir.”  
“Move the children up-ring now.”  
“Not yet. Wait through one more thump.”

Half the time I’d be wrong in visible ways—lost catch, wasted effort, grumbling hunger.  
The other half I’d be right in ways that leave no story because disaster never arrives where expected. In this place, that counts as competence.

By age, maybe not old. Old ears go broad but sometimes lose edge. I’d be mid-years: enough storms to respect fear, enough knees left to relocate fast. Scar on one shin from misreading a clay sheen in Lean Noise. Two soft-metal pins in my left wrist guard, traded far inland, holding a fracture that healed slightly crooked and now clicks in cold fog—my own private metronome.

Socially, I’d belong to a ring-wall but sleep often apart. Children would think my ledge is eerie until they apprentice and discover it’s just wet, cramped, and full of useful data. I’d teach them the first real lesson after knots:

Not “hear loud.”  
Hear **change**.

I’d probably have a tide-name that trade speech mangles. Something like *Between-Calls* or *Third-Echo*. Outsiders might call me a watchman. My people would roll that flat-word around and hand it back, because watching is eye-work, and eyes lie in fog.

Listening doesn’t.

So where would you find me?

On the least comfortable high ground within sprinting distance of everyone else—close enough to warn, far enough to hear the coast as a whole instrument. Knees tucked, hand on wet stone, eyes half shut, mouth counting the gap between booms.

Who would I be?

Not an author of this place.  
Not even a Stone Reader in full standing.  
I’d be the one who keeps the sentence from breaking—  
a Tide Listener trained to hear when the world drops a word.
