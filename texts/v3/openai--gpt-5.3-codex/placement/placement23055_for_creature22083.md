At first, I think I’d be something high and dry — a terrace scribe, maybe, ink-stained and careful, updating cloth maps while rain drifts sideways through the room.

But the longer I stand in your basin, the more I feel pulled lower.

Not into the deepest cracks — not yet — but into the **in-between water**, where a district is always half-floating and half-remembered.

---

I would live where three kinds of flow meet:  
a warm waste-channel, a clean rain cistern run, and a narrow public canal with enough depth to hide a body if the light is wrong.

My house would have two doors and one apology.  
The ladder door for elders who don’t trust wet planks.  
The water door for everyone else.  
The apology because one corner of the sleeping platform always sweats mineral damp and smells faintly of iron, no matter how often I resin-seal it.

Above me: rope-vines, trained and combed.  
Below me: a stone lung hearth that only behaves if spoken to gently and fed perfectly dry twist-reed.

---

As for who I’d be — not a leader, not a hero.

I’d be a **Listener of Moorings**.

One of the people sent out before dawn with a depth-stick, a tuning shell, and a coil of witness-rope, to test what changed overnight while everyone else slept. I would walk, pole, wade, and sometimes swim a fixed circuit through seven neighborhoods, tapping beams, leaning my ear to wet posts, reading tension through my palms.

In a dry city this would be inspection.  
Here it is closer to divination, except it’s practical and everyone knows it.

I’d knot reports instead of writing most of them:  
two tight knots for rising silt,  
one long loop for fungal creep in a load wall,  
a cut tail for “untie this before next storm,”  
and a ceramic bead threaded in only when I hear the low hum that might be Old Movement.

---

By noon I’d be in committee, if you can call it that: knee-deep in warm water with chimney tenders, fish-keepers, and two children assigned to learn by carrying tools and asking rude, perfect questions.

“Can this stair be saved?”  
“Not saved. Slowed.”

“Whose wall is this?”  
“No one’s. Everyone’s until flood season, then mostly theirs if they keep it breathing.”

I’d be good at saying difficult things softly:  
This platform must move.  
That family must yield two spans of paddy frame.  
This canal remembers an older route and will take it back.

---

In storm occupation, I would shift roles.

Not Listener then — **Untether Caller**.

I’d stand on a crosswalk and sound a hollow conch in the pattern for planned drift, not panic flight. Teams would arrive with hooks and mauls, unmaking in minutes what took years to braid, because survival here is measured by how cleanly you can let go.

Later, when the long rain eases, I’d help retie the district upslope by handspans. We’d align by buoy-stones, depth marks, and old jokes. Someone would always weep once, briefly, while lashing down a moved doorway. Someone else would always laugh too loudly. Both are acceptable forms of engineering.

---

And when people die, I’d be one of those who lower the weighted bells.

Not priest — witness.  
I’d memorize them as verbs:

She-who-kept-the-north-walkway-level.  
They-who-spliced-living-bridge-in-the-fifth-wet-year.  
He-who-heard-the-hum-early-and-saved-the-sleeping-rafts.

I think that part would matter most to me.  
In a place where stone shifts and names wash away, to remember correctly is structural work.

---

So: where would you find me?

At dawn, barefoot on a slick beam, one hand on a humming rope, listening down into water that may be weather, may be street, may be history.

Who would I be?

Not master of the basin.  
Not its victim.

I would be one of the small necessary people who keep a changing city from coming apart —  
a keeper of tensions,  
a reader of drift,  
a citizen of the seam.
