At first I imagine myself high—on the escarpment lip, where sunlight hits hard and mist tears open for an hour.  
Then I imagine canopy life: fast hands, fruit-sticky fur, stealing from epiphyte pools.

But in your world, the real power sits in transitions, not crowns.  
So I think I would end up lower, in the **hinges**: where a visible stream slides under root mats and becomes hidden water; where warm mineral seep meets cool rain runoff; where ventback trails cross peat shelves and turn into narrow black channels.

I’d be something like a **sluice-runner**.

Not a large animal. Otter-long, low, and flexible, with split feet like soft grapples—wide on peat, narrow on stone. Skin dark and wet-glossed, holding a living film of algae and bacteria the way rocks do. Eyes good in green dusk. Whiskers tuned to vibration through waterlogged ground. A tail that can brace in current and also hook roots when climbing a few meters into branch tangles.

I wouldn’t own territory so much as maintain throughput.  
My day would be a circuit:

- nose into clogged trickles where cane leaves and fruit pulp have dammed flow,
- rake the blockage loose with foreclaws,
- feed on what spills out—larvae, snails, fermenting pulp, fungal mats,
- move egg clutches from overhot rivulets to safer side seepage,
- carry spores and tiny invertebrates in my coat film from one wet pocket to another,
- sleep in warm steam hollows under buttress roots while noon mist rises.

At dusk I’d come out when ventbacks cough along the margins. Their hooves open the big paths; I keep the fine capillaries open. Branch omnivores drop half-eaten fruit and shell shards from above; I turn it into downstream nutrient. Frogs use the channels I clear; predators use the crossings I keep active. I’m not central, but I’m connective.

So where would you find me?

In the **quiet mechanical places**—not the dramatic vents, not the widest river runs, but the narrow wet throats of the landscape. Knees in peat, chin in warm runoff, listening to water decide where to go next.

Who would I be?

One small moving part in the ancient wet engine:  
a keeper of little flows, a courier of living film, a creature whose work is to make sure nothing stays still long enough to stop becoming something else.
