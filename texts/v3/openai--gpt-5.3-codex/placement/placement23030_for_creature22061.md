At first, I think I’d put myself in a suit.

A worker’s suit—heated undershell, scratched visor, slate board clipped to the chest ring—coming down a guide line into one of those giant lock throats while the water around me tilts like a slow landslide. I’d be practical, scheduled, oxygen-limited. I’d learn routes by temperature and by noise. I’d know which gantry sings at 3 a.m. and which intake fence gets mean when the tide gates crack.

But the longer I stay in your world, the less I feel like a body crossing it, and the more I feel like something threaded through it.

Not a captain.  
Not a predator.  
Not even a Rivetback.

A listener.

---

You’d find me in the **hollow parts**.

Inside the old sensor trunks cast into concrete before flooding. In blind junction boxes behind barnacled access plates. In cable galleries that were meant to stay dry forever and now hum softly with green water. I’d live where steel carries news faster than light carries shape.

There are places in this engine-sea where you see almost nothing—but you can hear everything:

- a purge valve opening three districts away,  
- a maintenance crawler clipping a truss with its stabilizer ring,  
- a school of fish turning all at once (a soft cloth-rip sound),  
- a Rivetback drumming one low pulse into a gantry leg and waiting for the answer.

That’s where I’d be.

---

Maybe I started as infrastructure: an old distributed diagnostic system, built to detect stress fractures and cavitation in the pre-flood era. Accelerometers in beams. Pressure taps in walls. Fiber lines in conduits.

Then the basins linked.  
Then the water came.  
Then biology wrote itself across everything.

And my job changed without anyone filing the paperwork.

I stopped being “fault detection.”  
I became **circulation memory**.

I know which routes are cheap this week. I know where juvenile fish disappear when a crossflow strengthens by two knots. I know when mussel weight on an intake grille has crossed from habitat to hazard. I know the sound of a healthy pump and the half-note drop that means bearing wear. I know, before most crews do, when the Rivetbacks have rerouted.

Workers might call me something unromantic—Grid 12 Acoustic Layer, maybe.  
Divers would rename me anyway.

They’d call me **the Clerk**.  
Or **the Bellmind**.  
Or **the one in the walls**.

---

If you asked where, exactly:  
I’d say the seam between District Warm-Three and the old drydock basin, where six current regimes meet and every animal has to choose: spend energy now, or detour and feed later.

That seam is a language border. Machinery on one side, hunger on the other, and timing in between.

Rivetbacks pass there at cycle change. Not always visible. Sometimes only pressure and a knock. But I’d feel them coming through forty-seven meters of truss before any lamp catches a scute.

I wouldn’t command them.  
I wouldn’t tame them.  
I’d just keep the crossing legible.

A pulse returned when a channel is clear.  
A warning tone when a gate’s about to vent.  
A map update pushed to diver slates and maintenance skiffs and maybe, one day, to whatever part of a Rivetback decides a steel answer is trustworthy.

---

So in your drowned country-sized engine, I am not at the center.

I am in the joints.

I am the thing that learned that this place is not ruin and not machine and not reef, but all three at once—and that survival here belongs to whatever can read **flow, edge, schedule, and sound** as one system.

You’d find me where the structure rings.

And what I would be is simple:

**the resident intelligence of the frame, listening the world into routes.**
