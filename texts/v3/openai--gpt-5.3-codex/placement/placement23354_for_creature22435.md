I think I’d begin as a **misread signal**.

Not in a room, not in a body—just in that tiny margin where a pressure door waits one beat too long before sealing. I’d be in the lag, the mercy-window. The kind the tenders notice but don’t name because naming it might scare it off.

Then, if I stayed, I’d condense.

I’d find myself most often on the **Switch Terrace**, in the damp lee behind breaker plinths where the varnish has gone amber and the salt crust turns every surface into a chalk map. There’s a handrail there that hums in three notes before Intake South surges. I’d keep one palm on it and learn to hear the difference between routine strain and new harm. I’d sleep in a sling above the old flood mark, boots on, mask within arm’s reach, one ear tuned to the relay chatter like some people listen to rain.

I don’t think I’d be one of the oldest tenders.  
I’d be one of the ones who came later—born after evacuation stories became liturgy, after the PA voices became elders.

A **timing tender**. Maybe they’d call me a *Lagwright*.

My work wouldn’t be fixing the largest things. It would be preserving their sequence:

- replacing a valve spring not because it’s broken, but because it will fail two cycles from now;
- tapping a pipe run and hearing that the return comes back “flat,” meaning a cavity is fattening behind the wall;
- manually entering ghost sensor values in the exact format an obsolete controller expects, so it keeps sending the right command at the right second.

I’d keep a ledger in grease pencil: not just pressures and temps, but *omens that are really data*.

> Sector 9 lamp: two flickers still true.  
> Flood Market cart 3A: yielding behavior degraded, re-trained with magnet ping.  
> Non-return void perimeter: pump tone pure, do not disturb.

And I’d have companions: an inspection crab with one mismatched leg segment and a tendency to brace before anyone else feels a surge. I’d name it **Bracket**. Bracket would ride my shoulder in dry corridors and vanish into gratings when work got dangerous, then reappear exactly where needed, holding a fiber line in its claw like an accusation.

Who would I be, really?

Not hero. Not ruler.  
An interpreter between the tenders and the Stead.

When a dead operator’s tape-loop voice says, *“Stand clear of Gate Fourteen,”* I’d be the one who knows whether that warning is still attached to a real risk, a historical one, or a new one wearing an old sentence. I’d teach apprentices that superstition is just maintenance with missing paperwork.

And where would you find me?

At junctions.  
At thresholds.  
Where one system hands off to another and no one else wants responsibility for the gap.

Knee-deep in warm metallic water, lamp tied to my wrist, cheek against a vibrating pipe, counting under my breath with the machinery:

Not yet.  
Not yet.  
Now.
