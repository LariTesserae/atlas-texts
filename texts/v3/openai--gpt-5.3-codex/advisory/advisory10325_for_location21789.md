Great destination choice—this is a **working megastructure ecosystem**, not a sightseeing cavern.  
Prepare like you’re entering an active hydro-industrial plant the size of a city, in perpetual cold mist, with moving water and moving machinery everywhere.

## The short version (memorize this)
1. **Water + metal + electricity = constant risk.**  
2. **Assume every open space may flood, vent, or be cleared without warning.**  
3. **Bring anti-slip insulation gear, hearing protection, and redundant light.**  
4. **Never travel alone; never stand in “empty” zones.**  
5. **Treat alarms as real even if everyone else looks calm.**  
6. **Get the current cycle schedule (purge rain, level pulses, maintenance blackouts).**  
7. **Decontaminate before leaving (chemical films, microbial biofilms, alkali dust).**

---

## 1) Physical prep before arrival
You should be able to handle:
- Long walking on grated and wet steel surfaces
- Cold exposure for hours (not freezing, but deep conductive chill)
- Ladder/gantry movement with three-point contact
- Continuous noise fatigue and low-visibility stress

Do at least basic prep:
- Break in boots beforehand
- Train in low-light navigation
- Practice hand signals + radio brevity (echo makes voice unreliable)

---

## 2) Essential gear
### Core PPE
- Waterproof, insulated, anti-slip boots (electrically rated)
- Layered clothing: moisture-wicking base + insulated mid + shell
- Hard hat with chin strap
- Cut-resistant, insulated gloves (bring 2 pairs)
- Safety goggles + anti-fog insert
- High-visibility outer layer (reflective strips matter here)

### Mission-critical extras
- **Headlamp + backup lamp + chem lights**
- **Hearing protection** (active if possible; passive backup plugs)
- Compact respirator (P100 or local equivalent) for mist/aerosol districts
- Personal gas detector (O₂, CO₂, H₂S minimum)
- Waterproof comms radio + spare battery in dry pouch
- Thermal blanket, hand warmers, electrolyte packets
- Waterproof notebook + grease pencil (digital maps may be outdated fast)

### If crossing high service routes
- Fall-arrest harness + twin lanyard  
- Carabiner tether for tools and body anchor in fog/high airflow sectors

---

## 3) What to ask local operations on entry
Do not proceed without:
- **Current basin map revision** (old corridors may be sealed)
- **Cycle timing sheet** (flood pulses, purge rain windows, venting periods)
- **Alarm code key** (tone meanings, evacuation routes)
- **Heat island map** (safe warm-up points; avoid overstay near machinery)
- **No-go zones for this shift** (pressure tests, electrical work, crane lanes)

If they give you paint-marking conventions, memorize them.  
In places like this, floor paint can mean “safe path” just as often as “fatal if crossed during operation.”

---

## 4) Movement rules that keep you alive
- Walk only marked lanes or escorted routes.
- Avoid smooth wet plates; prefer grating where possible.
- Never step over channels you can’t gauge the depth/flow of.
- Don’t loiter in wide “empty” clearances—those are often operational buffers.
- Keep one hand free near ladders/catwalk edges.
- Assume poor distance judgment in haze; verify landmarks by map, not sight.
- If visibility drops (fog/purge), stop at nearest safe marker and wait it out.

---

## 5) Environmental hazards specific to this basin
- **Infrastructural cold:** drains heat through boots and railings; hypothermia is slow and sneaky.
- **Condensation rain events:** sudden soaking + slick surfaces.
- **Asynchronous noise:** cognitive fatigue, missed instructions, delayed reactions.
- **Aerosolized residue:** metal salts, treatment chemicals, microbial fragments.
- **Stray currents/corroded hardware:** avoid grabbing unverified rails in pooled zones.
- **Wildlife/biome edge cases:** mostly minor, but treatment cistern edges are not for exploration.

---

## 6) Human factors and etiquette
This is a workplace first.
- Stay out of crane lines and maintenance cones.
- Don’t touch valves, tagged panels, or temporary piping.
- If a crew says “clear the lane,” clear immediately, no debate.
- Offer your route plan and expected return time to someone local.
- Bring small practical trade goods (dry batteries, anti-fog wipes, quality gloves): valued more than souvenirs.

---

## 7) Health and recovery
- Eat warm, salty food; hydrate even when you don’t feel thirsty.
- Schedule warming breaks before you feel cold.
- Rotate ear protection to avoid overfatigue.
- After exit: full washdown, boot scrub, clothing isolation, check skin for chemical irritation.

---

## 8) Psychological prep
The place can feel uncanny: huge, mostly empty, always loud, no natural day-night cues.  
Use hard routines:
- Time checks every hour
- Position checks every waypoint
- Buddy verbal check-ins every 10–15 minutes in low visibility

Treat it like visiting the inside of a living machine.  
If you respect its rhythms, it’s survivable—even fascinating. If you improvise against the process, it can punish you quickly.
