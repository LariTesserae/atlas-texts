This is a **high-risk, high-wet, low-visibility, physics-inconsistent marsh system**.  
An ordinary human can visit, but only if you prepare like you’re entering a wilderness lab + electrical storm zone + mild causality hazard.

## 1) Core mindset before you go
- Treat it as a **living system that reacts**, not passive scenery.
- Your main threats are not predators; they are:
  - disorientation,
  - hypothermia + trench-foot style moisture injury,
  - hidden water channels/peat collapse,
  - storm synchronization events,
  - and timing/acoustic anomalies that can trick decisions.
- Plan for **slow travel**. If your usual pace is 4 km/h, expect 0.5–1 km/h.

---

## 2) Physical gear (non-optional)
### Clothing
- Fully waterproof-breathable shell (hooded), plus insulating midlayer that stays warm when damp (wool/synthetic).
- Quick-dry base layers; no cotton.
- Chest waders or high waterproof bog boots + gaiters.
- 2–3 full spare sock sets in dry bags.
- Thin neoprene gloves + backup dry pair.
- Eye protection (mist + particulate + branches).

### Navigation + orientation
- **Multi-modal navigation only**:
  - inertial/IMU logger,
  - compass (but magnetic oddities may happen near conductive ridges),
  - short-range beacon transponder,
  - physical line-markers (biodegradable tape/flags),
  - breadcrumb drone if available.
- Don’t rely on sound localization or line-of-sight in fog geometry zones.

### Safety + survival
- Throw line, trekking pole/probe staff (for peat depth testing), compact flotation aid.
- Emergency bivy, thermal blanket, chemical warmers.
- Water purification (yes, even in a wetland).
- High-calorie food you can eat one-handed in rain.
- Full first-aid + antifungal skin care + blister/immersion foot kit.
- Ear protection (not total isolation; use filtered plugs for subsonic/harmonic events).
- Waterproof notebook + grease pencil for event logging (time anomalies demand analog notes).

### Instruments (strongly recommended)
- Barometer and weather trend monitor.
- EMF/electrostatic sensor (to detect active root-crystal zones).
- Conductivity/salinity meter for channel behavior clues.
- Laser rangefinder (distance perception can lie).
- Redundant timekeeping: 2 independent watches (log mismatch events).

---

## 3) Team and operating protocol
- Never solo. Minimum 3 people:
  - lead navigator,
  - rear marker/logger,
  - safety observer.
- Stay within **visual tether distance** in dense fog (literal tether in bad conditions).
- Establish fixed check-in intervals (e.g., every 10 minutes, not by landmarks).
- Predefine retreat triggers:
  - pressure drop + rising tonal ground hum,
  - sudden film bioluminescence across broad area,
  - channel reversals,
  - echo inversion (hearing events before visible cause).

---

## 4) Movement rules inside the basin
- Probe every step in peat/slick shelf transitions.
- Avoid shiny smooth depressions in stone during/after storms (possible water/charge collection pits).
- Cross root mats only after load-testing with staff.
- Don’t camp in apparent “dry bowls”—they may be delayed fill basins.
- Assume channels may reverse; never leave your return path dependent on one crossing point.
- If fog forms geometric “sheets,” freeze movement, regroup physically, and navigate by instrument, not intuition.

---

## 5) Causality/temporal weirdness protocol (practical, simple)
When sequence feels wrong (e.g., ripple before splash, water in footprint before step):
1. Stop moving for 30–60 seconds.
2. Everyone speaks current time from both watches.
3. Record what each person perceived and in what order.
4. Reconfirm physical spacing by touch/tether.
5. Resume only on preplanned heading, slowly.

Do **not** chase anomalies. Treat them like lightning: observe from safety.

---

## 6) Weather/storm protocol
Storms here are system-wide synchronization events, not just rain.
- If pressure drops fast or ground tone rises:
  - Leave conductive ridges and exposed channels.
  - Move to slightly elevated, vegetated-but-stable ground.
  - Crouch spacing between team members (not clustered).
  - Minimize contact with long wet roots/metal poles during peak pulses.
- After storm front passes, wait; delayed hydrological responses can arrive minutes later.

---

## 7) Health prep
- Train balance/endurance for unstable wet terrain.
- Vaccinations and anti-parasitic prophylaxis per your multiverse med authority.
- Pre-treat feet; inspect every halt.
- Skin barrier cream for constant damp exposure.
- Hearing baseline test beforehand if staying >48h (subaudible/tonal environment fatigue is real).

---

## 8) What not to do
- Don’t throw objects into pools casually (linked response behavior).
- Don’t “test” echo anomalies by separating from team.
- Don’t trust apparent silence.
- Don’t navigate by “downhill”; water follows pattern fields, not simple grade.
- Don’t over-instrument with high voltage/current probes—could perturb local bioelectric networks.

---

## 9) Best visit style for a normal traveler
- Short expedition windows: 4–6 hours in, then full dry-out and review.
- Dawn and post-storm periods are most information-rich but highest confusion risk.
- Treat it like visiting an ancient operating system written in mud, roots, and pressure.
