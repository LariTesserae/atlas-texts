What a place.  
For a normal human traveler, this is less “dungeon” and more **operating inside a living industrial organ**. Prepare like you’re entering a humid, semi-submerged biotech facility at altitude.

## 1) Baseline assumptions

Treat it as all of these at once:

- high-altitude site (lower oxygen, weather instability outside),
- warm aquatic environment (constant wet exposure),
- active industrial system (moving fluids, powered equipment),
- controlled ecology (you are a contamination risk).

If you prepare for only one of those, you’ll have a bad day.

---

## 2) Body prep (before arrival)

- **Acclimate to altitude** if possible (24–72h at comparable elevation).
- Get a **basic respiratory check** if you’re not used to thin air.
- Bring any personal meds in **waterproof hard cases**.
- Update tetanus, and ideally have broad-spectrum infection coverage planned (not because it’s filthy—it’s because wet cuts + metal + biofilm = trouble).

Hydrate well before entry. Warm humid spaces can trick you into under-drinking.

---

## 3) Clothing & personal gear

Think: “amphibious mechanic-gardener.”

### Wear
- Close-fitting **quick-dry base layers** (no loose fabric to snag on root/cable bundles).
- Thin **thermal layer** optional (water is warm, but still conductive over time).
- **Cut-resistant, high-grip gloves** (wet brass/steel + algae slick).
- **Soft-soled amphibious boots** with excellent traction.
- Lightweight **helmet/bump cap** (low beams, ladders, pipes).
- **Eye protection** with anti-fog coating.

### Carry
- 100% waterproof headlamp + backup.
- Two tether points: one for tool pouch, one for your body line.
- Compact **rebreather/snorkel escape hood** (not for diving adventure—just in case of sudden water rise or blocked lanes).
- Small **waterproof slate** or grease pencil for route marking.
- Chemical light sticks (work in wet, don’t depend on electronics).
- Sterile wound kit + saline rinse.
- Insulated bottle with electrolytes.
- Microfiber cloth for lenses.

### Do NOT bring
- Dangling straps, jewelry, long scarves, unsecured hair.
- Open paper maps.
- Anything that sheds lint, seeds, or pet hair (biosecurity issue).
- Loud ultrasonic pest repellers / random EM gadgets (can interfere with control systems).

---

## 4) Movement rules inside

Your biggest risk is not monsters—it’s **misjudging distance and footing**.

- Move with **three points of contact** whenever climbing.
- Assume every handhold is slick until proven otherwise.
- Treat refracted visuals as deceptive:  
  if something looks 1 meter away, test with a pole/glove first.
- Never push through “just leaves”—that may be a flow lane or service mesh.
- Stay in marked lanes; currents are intentional and can pull subtly toward intake slots.
- Clip a short tether while working near hatches, grates, or ladder drops.

Go slow. This place rewards patience.

---

## 5) Environmental hazards to expect

1. **Low oxygen + humidity fatigue**  
   You may feel fine, then suddenly foggy. Schedule short rests.

2. **Slip/cut risk**  
   Young edges + wet metal + biofilm = frequent minor injury potential.

3. **Entrapment by growth architecture**  
   Root curtains and vine-cables can close routes over time; trust current maps only if recent.

4. **Pressure events**  
   Pollination pulses, valve shifts, and circulation changes may create temporary surges.

5. **Contamination protocols**  
   You can introduce pests/spores; the site may require decon both ways.

---

## 6) Biosecurity etiquette (very important)

This ecosystem is curated. Respect it like a cleanroom + greenhouse.

- Decon boots and tools on entry and exit.
- Don’t touch flowers, pollen meshes, or nutrient lines unless authorized.
- Don’t leave organic waste, food crumbs, hair, skin tape, anything.
- If you shed blood: report it, clean it, document exact location.
- Never introduce animals, companion drones with exposed lubricants, or outside water.

In short: **you are the invasive species** unless proven otherwise.

---

## 7) Navigation & orientation

Because the chamber is visually dense, you can get disoriented fast.

- Use **route logic**, not visual landmarks alone:
  - “Column C7 → valve spine 3 → ladder bay north.”
- Reconfirm position at every major junction (color tags, numbered rails, pressure line markings).
- Mark temporary path with removable indicators; clear them on exit.
- Plan turnaround points in advance: “If progress drops below X m/min, return.”

---

## 8) Timing your visit

Best window: during stable operation, not shift-change.

Avoid:
- scheduled pollen pulse cycles,
- maintenance flush intervals,
- heavy cloud-harvest spikes (water level transitions and increased flow).

Ask for the **last 7-day hydrograph** and operations schedule before entering.

## 9) Social protocol (if staffed)

People maintaining this place likely think in systems, not scenery.

- Ask permission before interacting with any surface.
- Use precise language (“intake slot 4A partially occluded”) not vague (“that thing is clogged”).
- Don’t romanticize the “wildness”—this is managed infrastructure.

Respect earns access.

## 10) Emergency plan (minimum)

Have a simple one-page protocol memorized:

- Nearest 2 exits from current zone.
- Nearest breathable high platform.
- Manual shutdown pull/override locations (if permitted).
- Comms dead-zone fallback time (“if silent for 6 min, team extracts”).
- Hypoxia signs in yourself and others.

If visibility drops, currents change unexpectedly, or you hear continuous valve chatter:  
**stop, stabilize, orient, retreat.**

---

## 11) Mental preparation

The uncanny part is the **absence of animal life** and the calm machine breathing. Some travelers get edge-anxious because nothing “reacts” to them. That silence is normal there.

Adopt this mindset:

- You are visiting a long-term process, not an event.
- Don’t force speed.
- Notice patterns; don’t chase novelty.

If you do that, this place is not hostile. It’s disciplined.
