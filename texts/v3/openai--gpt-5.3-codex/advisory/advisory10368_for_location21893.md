Great destination—**but don’t treat it like a scenic old city.**  
Treat it like a **living hydrological-industrial ecosystem at high altitude** that happens to contain people.

## 1) What to prepare *before* arrival

### Fitness + acclimatization
- You’ll be climbing constantly (terraces, stairs, ramps, bridge ladders).
- It’s high elevation, so even humid air won’t save you from altitude strain.
- Do 2–4 weeks of stair/cardio prep if you can.
- Plan your first 48 hours light: no long district crossings, no night exploration.

### Medical prep
Ask a travel clinic for:
- **Tetanus booster**
- Water/animal-contact disease coverage relevant to your worldline equivalent (especially in wet urban systems)
- Skin/fungal prevention advice (this place is damp, warm in ducts, and microbially active)

Bring:
- Broad antiseptic wash
- Topical antifungal
- Oral rehydration salts
- Bandages that stick in humidity
- Any altitude meds you normally use

### Mental prep
This place has:
- Constant sound (pumps, animals, bells, tram brakes)
- Constant moisture
- Almost no green life  
Some travelers feel “ecological disorientation” (your brain expects plants where water exists). Totally normal.

## 2) Gear that matters here

### Absolutely essential
- **High-traction waterproof boots** (non-negotiable)
- 2–3 pairs quick-dry socks per day
- Lightweight rain shell (breathable, not insulated)
- Quick-dry layers (cotton will punish you)
- Waterproof daypack + dry bags
- Gloves (for wet railings/stone)
- Headlamp (with low-light mode)

### Strongly recommended
- **Filtered ear protection** (reduces noise fatigue but still lets alarms through)
- Small portable water filter + mineral-tolerant bottle
- Anti-corrosion wipes for electronics
- Power gear in sealed pouches (humidity + metallic condensation are real)
- Offline map with elevation layers (2D maps fail in stacked districts)

### Don’t bring unless required
- Delicate exposed-metal gadgets
- Fancy suede/leather anything
- “Urban exploration” clothing that can’t handle biofilm, runoff, and grime

## 3) Core safety rules on-site

## **Rule A: Water is habitat**
If it’s wet, assume something feeds there.
- Don’t step into drainage channels.
- Don’t sit on warm spillway edges.
- Don’t touch microbial mats.
- Never assume a shallow basin is “just rainwater.”

## **Rule B: Nothing is abandoned**
That old viaduct, dead pipe lane, “ruin,” or silent pump hall is probably still integrated into something.
- Enter only marked/public routes.
- Ask locals before using shortcuts through maintenance corridors.
- Treat all infrastructure as active.

## **Rule C: Animal traffic has right-of-way**
You are not at the top of this food web.
- Give drain mouths space.
- Avoid rooftop edges at flock surge hours (dawn/dusk).
- Don’t approach burrow exits at night.
- Never feed scavengers “for photos.”

## 4) Lodging strategy

Choose lodging that is:
- Above known flood/overflow lines
- Away from major vent chimneys (noise + fauna)
- Fitted with sealed grates and door sweeps
- Near tram + stair access (you’ll use both daily)

Ask hosts specifically:
- “Is this near a seasonal runoff corridor?”
- “Any known burrower activity under this block?”
- “What do lock-bells sound like in this district?”

---

## 5) Eating + drinking

Because there’s no conventional agriculture nearby, food chains are industrial-urban and highly processed or imported.
- Eat at busy, reputable kitchens.
- Prefer treated/boiled water points.
- Don’t sample raw canal-adjacent products from informal stalls unless vetted by locals.
- Expect mineral-heavy flavors and occasional metallic notes in water.

---

## 6) Daily rhythm that works

- **Morning:** best for long travel (surfaces least chaotic, visibility good)
- **Midday:** indoor/covered districts (humidity peaks)
- **Dusk:** avoid transit tunnels and drain-heavy routes (animal activity rises)
- **Night:** stick to lit, elevated paths

Use recurring sounds as navigation/time cues (locals do):
- Lock bells = water management shifts
- Pump cycle changes = maintenance windows
- Flock noise surges = airflow/weather shifts

---

## 7) Social/cultural etiquette

- Don’t call it “decay.” It’s managed maturity.
- Don’t romanticize “wildness”; locals see fauna as infrastructure partners and hazards.
- If someone says a route is “sleeping,” it may mean dormant-but-active—don’t test that.
- Ask before photographing industrial-animal interfaces (some are regulated food or waste systems).

---

## 8) Red flags: leave immediately if you notice…

- Sudden silence in a normally loud district (can indicate system interruption)
- New sulfur/metal odor spike + warm mist
- Birds abandoning an area all at once
- Repeated vibration underfoot near old paving
- Unscheduled lock alarms

When in doubt: move **upward and inward** to staffed transit corridors.
