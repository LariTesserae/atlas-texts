What you’re describing is **not a “dive site.”**  
It’s a **submerged industrial biome** the size of a nation.

For an ordinary human traveler, prepare as if you’re visiting:

- a cold coastal sea,
- an active hydro-engineering complex,
- and a giant, three-dimensional city with no streets and constant wind—except the wind is water.

## 1) Mindset first: this is a *working* environment

Do **not** treat it like ruins. It behaves like infrastructure still shaping itself.

Your mental model should be:

- **Flow is traffic**
- **Structures are terrain**
- **Schedules are weather**
- **Distance is cost**

If you ignore those four, you will exhaust yourself, get lost, or get pulled somewhere dangerous.

---

## 2) Minimum skills before you go

At minimum, you want:

- Cold-water diving competency (or equivalent submersible operations training)
- Strong buoyancy and trim control in confined + open spaces
- Current reading (eddies, shear lines, plume edges, intake pull)
- Underwater navigation by structure (not by “natural landmarks”)
- Emergency ascent/egress procedures in overhead-like environments
- Rope/line discipline (to avoid entanglement on hardware)

If you *don’t* have this, hire a local pilot and stay in a pressure-rated craft.

---

## 3) Gear that is actually appropriate

## Core survival / mobility
- **Drysuit** with robust thermal layers (temperature bands can shift fast)
- Redundant breathing system
- Helmet (hard contact with steel/concrete edges is common)
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Fins suited for current control, not speed bursts
- Compact DPV/scooter for long traverses (distance is brutal)

## Navigation / awareness
- Inertial nav + compass + depth + timer (all redundant)
- Local hydraulic schedule maps (lock cycles, purge windows, pump states)
- Sonar or imaging assist if visibility drops in particulate zones
- High-CRI primary light + backup (daylight exists, detail still needs light)

## Safety / extraction
- DSMB / locator beacon compatible with surface traffic systems
- Audible + digital emergency signaling
- Line cutter + backup cutter
- Small trauma kit topside (lacerations, crush, cold stress)

## Comfort / cognition
- Thermal drink and rewarming plan post-dive
- Ear protection strategy for prolonged acoustic exposure (in craft esp.)
- Fatigue budget: this place wears you down quietly

---

## 4) Plan travel around hydraulics, not distance

A route that looks short on a map can be impossible against flow.

Before every movement window, confirm:

1. **Gate schedule** (6-hour cycles can “tilt” whole water districts)
2. **Pump bank status** (on/off changes current lanes like weather fronts)
3. **Intake/outfall regime** (hazard + fish concentration)
4. **Thermal bands** (cold culvert vs warm discharge affects endurance)
5. **Visibility forecast** (maintenance or purge can cause instant silt load)

Move **with** district flow whenever possible.  
If you must cross flow, cross decisively at narrow points.

---

## 5) Biggest hazards (the real ones)

- **Intake throats / grilles:** suction + debris + fauna clustering = high risk  
- **Cavitation zones near outlets:** disorienting turbulence, abrasive water
- **Entanglement fields:** cable trays, conduit bundles, chain runs
- **Acoustic overload:** constant industrial noise masks danger cues
- **Scale shock:** featureless open volume causes poor judgment on time/fuel
- **Thermal traps:** seemingly mild route crosses into knife-cold sump water
- **Schedule traps:** gate events can turn safe corridors hostile in minutes

Rule of thumb: if water speed rises and sound changes pitch, reassess immediately.

---

## 6) Wildlife: abundant, practical, mostly indifferent

You’ll see lots of life, but concentrated at **edges**: grilles, shadows, corners, plume boundaries.

- Don’t insert hands into housings/slots (“occupied architecture”)
- Give predators right-of-way at bright flow lanes
- Avoid feeding/attractant behavior near lock transitions
- Seals (if encountered deep) are curious but can startle divers and teams

Treat fauna like urban wildlife around heavy machinery: not mystical, not harmless.

---

## 7) Human factors: how not to burn out

This environment causes a specific fatigue profile:

- continuous low-frequency noise
- constant micro-corrections in current
- visual monotony in open spans
- occasional intense confinement through maintenance throats

Countermeasures:
- Shorter first excursions than your confidence suggests
- Strict turn-around times
- Debrief after each sortie (flow anomalies, marker updates, fatigue signs)
- Alternate “open-volume day” and “structure-detail day”

---

## 8) Etiquette and legality (important)

If staffed/maintained, this is somebody’s workplace/ecosystem project.

- Never cross marked exclusion lanes
- Respect maintenance crews and warning paint/signage
- Do not disturb biofouling test panels, sensors, or tagged fauna colonies
- Report anomalies (new snag hazards, damaged grilles, unusual mortality)
- Assume all alarms matter, even muffled ones

The place stays safe because people treat procedures as culture.

---

## 9) First-visit strategy (best practice)

Day 1–2:
- Stay near upper structural districts with guide
- Learn flow language and marker conventions
- Practice route reversals under mild current

Day 3–4:
- Add one lock-cycle transition observation
- Traverse one open-volume leg + one confined connector

Day 5+:
- Only then attempt cross-district travel under changing pump states

---

If you remember just one thing:

> **Don’t navigate by geometry alone—navigate by moving water and machine schedule.**

Do that, and this place becomes incredible rather than lethal: a young, loud, engineered sea that is already fully alive.
