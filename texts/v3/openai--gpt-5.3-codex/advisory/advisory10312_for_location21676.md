Beautiful place to visit — but it will punish “normal city habits.”  
Prepare as if you’re going to live **inside warm weather, moving water, and unfinished geology**.

## 1) Body prep (2–6 weeks before)

1. **Altitude conditioning**
   - It’s a *high basin*, so expect thinner air even if it feels wet and warm.
   - Do cardio; practice slow exertion (stairs with load, steady paddling motions).
   - Learn early signs of altitude stress: headache, poor sleep, nausea, confusion.

2. **Water competence**
   - You don’t need to be an athlete, but you must be comfortable with:
     - swimming in clothing,
     - climbing in/out of water repeatedly,
     - balancing on wet narrow surfaces,
     - moving by small boat.
   - Practice floating calmly in low visibility.

3. **Mobility + grip**
   - Knees/ankles/core matter more than speed.
   - Train for slippery surfaces, ladders, and rope-assisted movement.

4. **Foot and skin resilience**
   - Constant damp causes trench foot, fungal infections, skin maceration.
   - Begin a foot-care routine now (drying, antifungal powder familiarity).

## 2) Gear to bring (critical)

Think: **wet, warm, dim, abrasive stone, no reliable metal infrastructure**.

### Clothing
- 3–4 sets of quick-dry base layers (not cotton).
- Light insulating layer (nights can still chill at altitude).
- Breathable waterproof shell.
- Wide-brim rain hood/cap.
- 2 footwear systems:
  - grippy wet shoes/sandals for canals/walkways,
  - dry camp shoes.
- Several pairs of synthetic socks + foot wraps.
- Work gloves (rope + basalt edges shred hands).

### Waterproofing + carry
- Dry bags (nested).
- Waxed or resin-treated outer wrap for sensitive items.
- Waterproof notebook + pencil (ink runs).
- Tethers/lanyards for essentials (things fall into canals).

### Health + hygiene
- Water purification backup (filter + tablets; locals may have systems, but you’re biologically naïve).
- Antifungal cream/powder.
- Broad-spectrum topical antibiotic.
- Rehydration salts.
- Insect protection (depending on local ecology).
- Personal meds in redundant waterproof packs.
- High-SPF protection still useful (UV can diffuse through cloud).

### Navigation / light
- Soft, diffuse headlamp + spare cells.
- Chemical light backup.
- Whistle/conch-equivalent signal if possible.
- Simple compass can help orientation where shadows don’t.

### Tools
- Small cordage kit (you’ll use rope constantly).
- Compact knife/multitool (non-aggressive profile).
- Repair tape + resin-friendly patch kit.
- Carabiners (even if locals use knots, these save lives for visitors).

---

## 3) Skills worth learning before arrival

- **5–8 essential knots** (clove hitch, bowline, sheet bend, trucker’s hitch, prusik).
- Basic paddle handling and docking etiquette.
- Ladder safety on wet rungs.
- How to move quietly and slowly on shared floating structures.
- Fire discipline in humid enclosed environments (if you must use fire at all).

---

## 4) Cultural/legal prep (important)

This society runs on **maintenance and mooring**, not rigid property lines.

- Ask “Where may I tie up?” before “Where may I go?”
- Don’t retie, unfasten, or “improve” anything without permission.
- Depth markers, knot codes, buoy stones are infrastructure — don’t touch casually.
- Water routes may be:
  - transport lanes,
  - waste channels,
  - fish nursery zones,
  - ritual corridors.
  Treat each as regulated space.

Bring trade gifts that fit the world:
- good cordage,
- fungus-resistant cloth,
- durable needles/awl sets,
- sealants/resin compounds,
- waterproof writing materials.

Avoid flashy high-tech displays unless invited; this is a hand-built adaptive culture, and trust is practical.

---

## 5) Arrival strategy (first 72 hours)

1. **Acclimate, don’t perform**
   - Keep workload light day 1–2.
   - Hydrate and eat salted food.
   - Observe local movement rhythms before traversing alone.

2. **Get a local route briefing**
   - Safe canals, sink-soft mud zones, vent-warm walls, storm shelters.
   - Learn drum/conch signals immediately.

3. **Secure your sleeping platform**
   - Confirm tie points, waterline changes, and overnight drift allowance.
   - Keep one “dry core bag” always sealed.

4. **Adopt local time cues**
   - With dim light and blurred day/night, rely on communal signals, not your body clock.

---

## 6) Main hazards and how to handle them

- **Slips + falls into water**: move slower than feels necessary.
- **Hidden depth changes / silt traps**: probe before stepping in flats.
- **Long storms**: think endurance, not impact; stock dry fuel, food, and patience.
- **Respiratory strain** (altitude + humidity): pace yourself.
- **Skin/foot breakdown**: strict dry intervals daily.
- **Subsurface shifts**: if locals pause and listen, you pause and listen.

If you hear sustained low “belly” water sounds from below, follow resident instructions without debate.

---

## 7) Behavior that earns trust fast

- Offer labor: hauling, tying, scraping, patching.
- Learn and use local knot names/route names.
- Ask elders how a canal “breathes” before asking for maps.
- Keep your wake small; keep your voice low.
- Return tools dry and correctly tied.

---

## 8) Mindset

Don’t visit this place as a tourist looking for a single spectacle.  
Visit as an apprentice in a city where **water is infrastructure, archive, and law**.

If you prepare well, you’ll stop asking “where is land?” and start asking the better question:  
**“What here is stable enough to trust today?”**
