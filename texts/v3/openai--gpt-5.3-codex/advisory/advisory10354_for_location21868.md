Beautifully dangerous destination.  
For an **ordinary human**, treat this as a cross between:

- a deep, cold cave-dive system,
- a submerged archaeological megasite,
- and a dense underwater forest with near-zero wildlife.

## Bottom line
You are preparing for **cold, dark, entangling, disorienting immersion with no natural refuge**.  
If your prep is “normal scuba trip,” you are underprepared.

---

## 1) Physical and technical readiness

### Required skill baseline
Do not go without training equivalent to:

- Advanced buoyancy control
- Low/zero-visibility navigation
- Guideline/reel use
- Entanglement response
- Drysuit diving in cold water
- Overhead-environment mindset (even though “open,” vegetation + walls create overhead-like traps)

If you don’t have this, travel with a specialist team and stay in shallow mapped sectors only.

### Fitness
You need:

- Strong finning endurance (long distances, drag from plants)
- Grip and upper-body stamina (pulling through reeds/kelp)
- Cold tolerance and discipline under numbness/fatigue
- Calm under sensory deprivation (silence + darkness + no horizon)

---

## 2) Life support and exposure protection

### Thermal
Cold is a primary threat.

- Drysuit + layered thermal undergarments
- Redundant heating (chemical/electric if available)
- Dry gloves with dexterity preservation
- Strict time limits by temperature, not just gas

### Breathing system
Given long corridors and no dry exits:

- Redundant gas supply (or equivalent multiverse rebreather with bailout)
- Conservative gas planning (rule-of-thirds minimum, stricter in unmapped zones)
- CO₂ monitoring discipline if using closed-circuit systems

### Pressure/depth planning
Depth changes may be subtle and deceptive in darkness.

- Depth alarms/computer with conservative ascent model
- Hard depth limits per mission
- Preplanned decompression contingencies
- Surface/upper-layer support platform always available

---

## 3) Navigation in a world without horizon

You cannot rely on visual orientation.

Bring:

- Primary guideline reels + jump reels
- Tactile line markers (directional, personal ID)
- Redundant compasses + inertial nav if available
- Slate or waterproof digital map board
- Distance/time logging habit every leg

Method:

- “Line in, line out” for every penetration into dense growth or deep corridors
- Mark intersections physically and consistently
- Avoid trusting memory in repetitive architecture (terraces/stairs repeat endlessly)

---

## 4) Movement and entanglement management

Vegetation is structural here.

Carry at least 2 cutting tools in separate locations:

- Serrated knife
- Line cutter/shears

Protocols:

- Slow movement only; no rushing through reeds
- Keep gear profile tight (no dangling hoses/straps)
- Use hand-over-hand along walls when visibility drops
- If snagged: stop, breathe, locate snag point, cut only what you must

---

## 5) Lighting and sensory strategy

Expect permanent dusk below upper layers.

- 1 strong primary light + 2 backup lights minimum
- Wide beam for terrain, narrow spot for line/hand work
- Low-light mode to reduce silt backscatter
- Tactile navigation training (glove sensitivity matters)

Also: sound cues are subtle but useful (current against walls, bubble patterns, vegetation rustle). Learn to listen.

---

## 6) Environmental and medical precautions

### Water/biology
No animals doesn’t mean no bio-risk.

- Full skin coverage (plant abrasions, microbial exposure)
- Post-dive decontamination (suit, tools, airway interfaces)
- Vaccination/antimicrobial prep per expedition medicine protocol
- Eye/ear care for tannin-rich, particulate-heavy water

### Injury profile to anticipate
- Hypothermia
- Hand numbness leading to task failure
- Entanglement panic
- Overexertion in drag-heavy zones
- Lacerations from old masonry edges hidden under growth

---

## 7) Expedition structure (don’t solo this)

Minimum safe setup:

- Buddy pair in water, always
- Surface/upper-layer safety coordinator
- Scheduled check-ins by line tug/code/comms
- Predefined abort triggers (temp, visibility, line loss, gas threshold, fatigue)

Set mission style to “short, repeatable passes,” not heroic long penetrations.

---

## 8) Psychological preparation

This place can get inside your head:

- No fish, no animal motion, almost no noise
- Endless old geometry under plant overgrowth
- Time distortion from monotone light and slow currents

Prepare with:

- Breathing discipline
- Task-focused checklists
- Hard turnaround rules to prevent “just one more corridor” behavior
- Post-dive decompression psychologically too (journaling/debrief)

---

## 9) What to bring besides survival gear

- Soft tactile markers/twine (non-damaging)
- Sample bags (if allowed) for water/sediment/plant tissue
- Non-invasive mapping tools (sonar/photogrammetry in low light)
- Thermal and current loggers (patterns matter more than snapshots)
- Redundant timing devices (this environment rewards long-interval observation)

---

## 10) Behavioral rules specific to this world

1. **Never trust apparent open water** — it can become reed-choked in meters.  
2. **Never descend without a return line** — depth kills orientation and sound cues.  
3. **Treat every wall as alive** — plants will hide edges, gaps, and snag points.  
4. **Assume routes are seasonal** — what was open last month may be sealed now.  
5. **Move like a surveyor, not an explorer** — patient, measured, repeatable.
