What you described is **not** a “sightseeing desert.”  
It’s a high-altitude, ultra-dry, electrostatic, low-forgiveness world where mistakes compound quietly.

If an ordinary human traveler wants to come back alive, prep like this:

---

## 1) Train your body first (this is non-optional)

### Altitude conditioning
- Expect chronic thin air and high UV.
- Do staged acclimatization before arrival (or use a pressure-supported habitat for first 48–72 hours).
- Bring:
  - pulse oximeter
  - portable O₂ support
  - meds cleared by your doctor for altitude illness
- If you get persistent headache + nausea + confusion: descend or re-pressurize immediately.

### Dryness conditioning
- This world has effectively no free water.
- You are your own water cycle.
- Train with strict hydration discipline (scheduled drinking, electrolyte tracking), not thirst-based drinking.

### Mobility conditioning
- Distances will lie to you visually.
- Train for long, low-relief traverses with load over uneven hardpan and scree.

---

## 2) Gear for this world specifically

## Life support & clothing
- **Pressurized or semi-pressurized expedition suit** (depending on exact atmosphere), with:
  - UV-hard outer layer
  - abrasion resistance for wind-blown grit
  - active thermal control (hot sun, cold nights)
- Eye protection rated for:
  - high UV
  - dust impact
  - static discharge glare
- Redundant respirator filtration (fine mineral dust + salt fines).

## Water & habitat
- Closed-loop water recycler (wearable + camp unit).
- Emergency water reserve (hard minimum: “72-hour survival reserve” kept untouched).
- Compact stormworthy shelter:
  - low profile
  - anchored for sudden shear fronts
  - conductive/discharge-managed exterior.

## Electronics & power
- Electrostatic-hard electronics, shielded connectors, sealed bearings/fans.
- Faraday storage pouch for critical backups.
- Hybrid power (solar + protected battery + mechanical backup tools).
- Keep at least one **non-electronic navigation method** (optical compass, physical maps, dead reckoning plan).

## Footing and movement
- Boots with excellent grip on polished stone + glassy crust + granular salt.
- Trek poles with insulated grips.
- Gaiters and joint seals against powder intrusion.

---

## 3) Weather protocol (most important field behavior)

Storms here are often **silent, fast, and structural**.

Learn the warning chain:
1. Distant shimmer/curtain
2. small stone ticking
3. skin/suit prickling
4. impact wind + charged grit

When step 2 starts, do this immediately:
- Get off ridgelines and isolated high points.
- Move to lee-side low exposure terrain (not deep enclosed traps).
- Minimize profile.
- Stow sensitive electronics.
- Switch suit to static-dissipation mode.
- Wait it out; many events are short but violent.

Do **not**:
- sprint exposed crests
- touch unknown metal/stone structures during charge build
- assume silence means safety

---

## 4) Route planning in a “false near” landscape

- Plan by **time-energy**, not by visual distance.
- Any “looks like 1 hour” target: budget 3–5x until local calibration improves.
- Use waypoint ladders every few km; never commit to single-horizon navigation.
- Hard turnaround time each day, regardless of progress.
- Cache-and-return strategy is safer than long unsupported pushes.

---

## 5) Ancient infrastructure: treat as active hazards

Those towers/rings/bridges are not ruins in the normal sense. Some still operate.

Assume each installation may have:
- local atmospheric effects (laminar corridors, dust vortices)
- stress-field anomalies (micro-flex timing shifts)
- EM/thermal signatures invisible at ground level.

Protocol:
- First pass: observe from distance with optics/drone.
- No direct contact unless mission requires and you have instrumentation.
- Never camp immediately adjacent to active structures.
- Mark exclusion radius and monitor for periodicity before entering.

---

## 6) Ground behavior and seismic fatigue

“Gravity stitches” imply continuous micro-deformation.
- Expect subtle footing drift and occasional balance-disrupting ground motion.
- Use wide-stance movement on exposed slabs.
- Avoid sleeping on narrow ledges, overhang lips, or fracture-choked ribs.
- Secure all camp gear as if the ground may “breathe” overnight.

---

## 7) Biological and contamination etiquette

Life is sparse and slow. Damage lasts.
- Don’t scrape crusts, uproot seam vegetation, or track through visible colonies.
- Sterilize incoming gear; avoid introducing foreign microbes.
- Pack out everything, including greywater residues where possible.
- Treat all unknown biological films as ecologically critical.

---

## 8) Psychological prep (seriously)

This environment can destabilize judgment:
- near-silence
- giant empty horizons
- deceptive scale
- low sensory feedback

Countermeasures:
- strict checklists and timed routines
- regular voice logs (keep cognitive anchors)
- buddy system if possible
- objective decision triggers (“if X symptoms, abort”)

---

## 9) Minimum mission profile for first visit

For an ordinary traveler, first expedition should be:
- short (3–5 days surface ops max),
- near a reliable base habitat,
- focused on one corridor and one structure class,
- with daily weather windows and mandatory return margins.

Don’t do heroic traverses on trip one.

---

## 10) Abort criteria (set before arrival)

Leave immediately if any two occur together:
- persistent altitude symptoms
- water recycler fault
- repeated electrostatic hits to core electronics
- navigation confidence collapse (missed 2+ waypoints)
- abnormal seismic/ground motion near camp
- cognitive slowing/confusion in team member

---

If I had to summarize this world in one line for a traveler:

**Prepare for a place that is mostly finished geologically, but still violently alive in the small physics.**  
Respect the air, the charge, and the distance illusions, and you can survive it. Treat it like an ordinary desert, and it will erase you quietly.
