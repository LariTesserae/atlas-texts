What you described is basically a **still-forming crustal zone** with almost no biological buffering.  
For a human traveler, treat it like a cross between:

- high-altitude volcanic badlands  
- an active fault scarp in constant micro-uplift  
- a chemically dusty industrial site at night

The key: **this place is not old enough to be forgiving**.

---

## 1) Mindset before gear

Your normal wilderness instincts (follow valleys, trust old trails, read weathered landforms) will get you hurt here.

Adopt these rules:

1. **Terrain is temporary.** Anything you saw yesterday may be gone today.
2. **Silence is not safety.** Quiet is just the interval between events.
3. **Low ground is trap ground.** Vents, gas pooling, crack propagation, and debris focus there.
4. **Fresh-looking surfaces are active surfaces.** Bright fracture faces, sharp edges, warm mineral crust = recent movement.
5. **Move like a surveyor, not a hiker.** Stop often, observe, then commit short segments.

---

## 2) Physical preparation

You need capacity for:

- **thin air exertion** (high elevation style fatigue)
- **heat load in dry air**
- **unstable footing** on sharp, brittle, ball-bearing-like substrates
- **startle control** during sudden concussive events

Train for:
- loaded uphill walking
- balance drills on loose rock
- breathing control under stress (slow exhale, controlled pace)

---

## 3) Essential kit (non-negotiable)

## Protective

- Climbing helmet (rockfall-rated), always on while moving
- Eye protection: sealed goggles (dust + shards)
- Respiratory protection: half-face respirator with **P100 + acid-gas cartridges** (reactive mineral dust / vent gases)
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Tough boots with puncture-resistant sole + ankle support
- Gaiters (glassy shards and corrosive dust)

## Navigation & awareness

- Headlamp + chest light (redundant), wide flood + spot
- Spare batteries stored warm/dry
- GPS + inertial backup + physical notebook map sketches (because terrain changes)
- Marking tape/cairn tags for your own temporary route
- Portable gas detector (minimum: O₂ depletion + H₂S/SO₂ alerts)
- IR thermometer (useful for spotting hot crust/vents before stepping)

## Survival

- Water: carry **all of it** (there is effectively no potable surface source)
- Electrolytes, high-calorie compact food
- Emergency bivy/shelter rated for abrasive ground
- Trauma-focused first aid kit (bleeding control, splinting, eye irrigation)
- Satellite beacon / off-world extraction trigger
- Static-safe storage for electronics (discharge-prone ridgelines)

---

## 4) Movement strategy in this specific landscape

Travel in **short bounds** between “decision points.”

At each stop, check:
- ground temperature shift
- new cracks (especially straight, fresh, tension-open)
- smell change (sulfur/metallic sting increase = possible venting)
- ambient sound (subtle ticking, groaning, deep thuds)

### Prefer
- Broad, already-broken benches with mixed fragment sizes (often already “released”)
- Ground with some dust veneer over older fracture patterns (not mirror-fresh)
- Traverses that keep multiple escape angles open

### Avoid
- Basin bottoms and enclosed hollows (gas pooling, vent unzip zones)
- The toe of steep planar cliffs (slab shedding)
- Knife ridgelines during static crawling/discharge
- Crystal towers and fresh mineral skins (growth/thermal stress zones)
- Any route requiring faith in old stairs, old cairns, or old crack crossings

---

## 5) How to react when it “goes live”

If you feel a shudder or hear fracture-rip sound:

1. **Stop sprinting instinct.** Running on brittle crust causes falls into bad ground.
2. **Helmet chin strap tight, crouch low, protect airway.**
3. Move only enough to get out from under obvious fall lines.
4. Do not enter narrow crack corridors during active shaking.
5. After major event, wait and reassess—secondary failures are common.

If a new vent opens nearby (hot glitter plume, hiss):

- Turn crosswind/upwind immediately.
- Respirator on now, not later.
- Do not outrun through low ground; gain slightly higher side ground and arc away.

---

## 6) Use of built features (cairns, short walls, tiny shelters)

Treat them as **hints, not guarantees**.

- Cairns may mark historically safer saddles, but uplift may have invalidated the logic.
- Windbreak walls can still be useful for short pauses.
- Small lee shelters are only for brief tremor waits; inspect roof/face for fresh fractures first.
- Never assume any constructed step/path still lands on solid terrain beyond line-of-sight.

---

## 7) Trip design

- Go with at least **2–3 people**, spaced apart (single collapse doesn’t take all).
- Predefine turnaround thresholds: gas alarm triggers, tremor frequency, visibility collapse, fatigue level.
- Keep travel windows short; don’t push into cognitive fatigue in a place that demands constant micro-judgment.
- Log route changes in real time—future you cannot trust memory against a reconfigured valley.

---

## 8) Medical/contamination after exit

Expect:
- dust inhalation irritation
- eye micro-abrasions
- cuts from glass-like fragments
- dehydration + altitude headache

After leaving:
- full gear decon before removing respirator
- rinse eyes/skin
- isolate dusty clothing
- monitor delayed respiratory symptoms (especially after vent exposure)

---

If I had to reduce it to one sentence:

**Travel as if the mountain is still being assembled under your feet—because it is.**
