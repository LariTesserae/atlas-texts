What you’re describing is **not a ruin** and not a normal alpine trek.  
Treat it as a **high-altitude, arid, non-Euclidean urban wilderness** with slow, lawful anomalies.

## 1) Baseline assumption: this place kills the unprepared quietly
Main risks are mundane first, weird second:

1. Altitude hypoxia  
2. Dehydration in cold dry air  
3. Falls (scree, stairs, exposed plazas)  
4. Night cold + thermal shock  
5. Navigation failure due elastic distance/elevation quirks  
6. Psychological drift from silence, emptiness, and moving shadow behavior

Go with that priority stack.

---

## 2) Before arrival (the prep that matters)

- **Acclimatize** if possible (or arrive with oxygen support).  
  Thin air plus constant climbing will wreck judgment fast.
- **Train for vertical movement**: long stair ascents with load, ankle stability, controlled descents on loose rock.
- **Cold-desert conditioning**: practice hydration and layering in windy, dry, cold environments.
- **Navigation discipline drills**: route logging every 10–15 minutes, because maps age daily there.
- **Mental protocol**: pre-agree with your team what counts as “anomaly, observe” vs “anomaly, retreat.”

Do **not** go alone.

---

## 3) Gear: what an ordinary human should actually carry

### Clothing
- Layered insulation (merino/synthetic base, mid-fleece, shell)
- Windproof outer shell
- Face/eye protection for grit
- Gloves with good dexterity (stone is abrasive; metal tools can cold-burn at night)
- High-ankle boots with aggressive grip
- Gaiters (dust rivers)

### Life support
- 2–3x normal water planning (cold hides thirst)
- Insulated water containers (night freeze risk)
- Electrolytes (dry mineral air + exertion)
- Compact high-calorie food you can eat breathless
- Emergency bivy + insulated pad

### Navigation and marking
- Redundant nav: compass, inertial tracker, analog map board, altimeter
- **Physical route markers** (stackable ceramic/stone shards).  
  Do not rely on paint/chalk/symbols.
- Rangefinder + notebook (distance lies; recorded deltas matter)
- Mechanical watch + hard turnaround times

### Safety/medical
- Helmet (falling grit/rock + old masonry)
- Climbing harness + short rope for exposed transitions
- Dust-rated respirator (for moving grain corridors and black-film zones)
- First aid: trauma + altitude meds + rewarming kit
- Light sources, but don’t overtrust shadows for geometry

### Optional but smart
- Small **plumb bob** or weighted line: quick local “down” test
- Non-metal eating utensils/contact surfaces for night use
- Audio recorder (for tracking recurring subterranean “page-turn” sound position over time)

## 4) Field rules once on site

## Movement
- Start early; stop major movement before dusk contraction ticks begin.
- Never camp in:
  - drainage channels,
  - dust pooling courtyards,
  - under freestanding arches/lintels.
- On long approaches to visible gates/towers:  
  **look away periodically** and navigate by intermediate anchors; direct visual fixation seems to stretch approach time.
- Test questionable slopes with tossed grit/line before committing (local up/down inversions happen).

## Shadow weather
- Treat hard-edged geometric shadow wedges as active weather, not optics.
- Don’t stand in newly detached or lagging shadow bands until observed stable for a while.
- If team members disagree about where an edge is: pause, regroup, re-observe.

## Surfaces and biology
- Avoid touching matte black mineral films bare-handed; unknown chemistry/metabolism.
- Don’t scrape or sample without containment.
- Give fauna space; cliff runners are likely route-aware and may indicate stable traverses, but don’t follow blindly.

## Sound anomaly (“page turning”)
- Do not chase it deep into old precincts.
- If followed, track it passively from known nodes.  
  “Reciprocal retreat” rule: if it moves as you move, return to last confirmed marker stack.

## 5) Navigation doctrine for a place where geometry breathes

Use **short hops + constant revalidation**:

1. Choose next anchor point (near, not far).  
2. Record start time, wind direction, moon/sky state, and perceived slope.  
3. Move 5–15 min.  
4. Re-mark with physical shard stack.  
5. Reassess map line.

Your map is a **daily palimpsest**, not a truth object. Annotate with:
- wind corridor strength,
- shadow behavior,
- dust river direction,
- zones where distance stretched/compressed.

---

## 6) Camp discipline

- Anchor shelter hard (gust channels can accelerate unpredictably).
- Seal gear from dust ingress every evening.
- Keep water inside insulation overnight.
- Night watch should include:
  - structural sounds (new cracking tempo),
  - dust flow shifts,
  - shadow behavior around camp perimeter.

---

## 7) Psychological survival

This environment creates a powerful “I am the only person left in time” effect.  
Counter it with routines:

- Buddy checks every 30–60 min
- Spoken status reports even when unnecessary
- Fixed meal/hydration schedule
- Hard turnaround regardless of curiosity

If someone becomes fixated on a single distant feature (“I can reach it in ten minutes”), that is a red-flag behavior. Reset immediately.

---

## 8) What respect looks like here

Think like a guest in an old, still-functioning civic machine.  
Don’t force doors, don’t dismantle route markers, don’t alter load-bearing stone, don’t leave bright symbols. Use reversible traces only.

---

If you prepare for **cold altitude desert mountaineering + conservative anomaly protocol**, you can travel here safely enough.  
If you treat it like scenic ruins, it will outlast you in a day.
