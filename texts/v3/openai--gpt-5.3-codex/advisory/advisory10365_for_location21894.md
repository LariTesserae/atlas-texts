This is a **high-altitude, exposure-dominated biome**: low shelter, high UV, constant wind/noise, scarce but recurring water, lots of animal traffic, and very little margin for small mistakes.

If I were sending an ordinary human traveler there, I’d frame prep like this:

## 1) Pre-arrival assumptions (don’t skip)
Even if it *looks* Earthlike, confirm:

- **Breathable atmosphere** (O₂ %, pressure, toxins/spores)
- **Gravity** (joint/pack load planning changes fast if off by even ~10–15%)
- **Pathogen compatibility** (avian colonies + mammals = bio risk)
- **Day length / temperature swing**
- **Storm profile** (even “stable” worlds can produce occasional lethal wind events)

If your multiverse protocol allows, do a **drone recon day** first.

---

## 2) Core hazards you should plan around
1. **Altitude + dry cold + hard sun**  
   You can get sunburned, dehydrated, and mildly hypoxic simultaneously while feeling “fine.”

2. **No natural shelter**  
   If wind rises or temperature drops, there’s nowhere obvious to hide.

3. **Sound fatigue**  
   Constant colony noise + wind resonance can degrade attention and judgment.

4. **Water unreliability**  
   Water exists, but in pulses (seep, condensation, short runoff). You can’t wander and “just find enough.”

5. **Footing hazards**  
   Scree sheets, polished rock, knife saddles, hidden drop-offs near cliff systems.

6. **Wildlife density without proximity**  
   You may feel alone, then suddenly cross major migration lanes or predator watch zones.

---

## 3) Gear that matters most
Prioritize survival over comfort.

- **Layering system**: windproof shell, insulating midlayer, sweat-wicking base
- **High-UV protection**: glacier-grade sunglasses, brimmed cap, SPF 50+, lip balm
- **Hearing management**: filtered earplugs (reduce fatigue but preserve situational awareness)
- **Water system**: carry capacity for full day + high-reliability filter + chemical backup
- **Navigation**: offline topographic maps, compass, altimeter watch, GNSS if available
- **Shelter**: low-profile, high-wind-rated bivy/tent + serious stakes/anchors for rock
- **Footwear**: stiff-soled boots with excellent edging/grip on mixed scree/stone
- **Trekking poles**: major energy saver on long open traverses
- **Medical**: altitude meds (if appropriate), rehydration salts, blister care, trauma kit
- **Food**: dense, salty, easy-to-eat-in-wind calories (you’ll burn more than expected)
- **Bio precautions**: gloves + mask for guano-heavy zones / carcass areas

Optional but wise: lightweight climbing helmet in colony/cliff-fall zones.

---

## 4) Field behavior (how not to become a cautionary tale)
- **Travel early/late**, rest in peak glare unless you need miles.
- **Never pass a water source without topping off.**
- **Avoid cliff bases under active colonies** (falling debris + disease load + aggressive defense).
- **Do not camp in migration corridors** (look for trampled lanes, droppings, repeated tracks).
- **Keep distance from “anvil stones” and tool-use sites**; treat as culturally significant behavior zones.
- **Don’t build cairns, cut marks, or permanent traces.** This place is pattern-stable; your interference lasts.
- **Use conservative turn-around times.** Open terrain tricks people into overcommitting.

---

## 5) Camp selection rules
Pick sites that are:

- Above runoff channels but near known evening condensation/seep access
- Not on ridgeline crests (wind tunnel effect)
- Not below cornices/loose rock bands
- Outside nesting acoustics if possible (sleep quality matters for judgment)
- Downwind from major guano concentrations

Anchor everything. Night gusts can be violent even in “stable” weather regimes.

---

## 6) Wildlife protocol
This ecosystem is reliable because routines are old. Respect them.

- **Bird colonies**: observe from distance; no drones overhead; avoid approach vectors to ledges.
- **Ungulate herds**: give lanes right-of-way; never force bunching near drop terrain.
- **Predators**: assume they know watchpoints better than you; avoid twilight choke points.
- **Burrow zones near seep areas**: step carefully; avoid collapsing habitat.

Treat tool-using corvids and mammals as high-cognition wildlife: no baiting, no theft of tools, no feeding.

---

## 7) Human factors (big one)
This landscape can cause a weird mismatch: visually “empty,” biologically “loud.”  
Common errors:

- Underestimating exertion because terrain looks gentle from afar
- Decision fatigue from nonstop noise and glare
- Mild dehydration mistaken for simple tiredness
- Pushing on because conditions seem “basically unchanged”

Counter it with strict routines: drink schedule, shade breaks, navigation checks every hour, written turnaround criteria.

---

## 8) Minimal prep timeline
- **2–4 weeks prior**: aerobic conditioning + ankle stability + loaded pack hikes
- **1 week prior**: heat/sun and cold/wind kit testing
- **48 hours prior**: hydration and sleep bank
- **Arrival**: if altitude is significant, acclimatize before long traverses
