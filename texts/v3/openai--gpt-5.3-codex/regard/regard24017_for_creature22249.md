They would arrive as weather.

Not as a figure descending in brightness, not as a proclamation, not as a hand replacing all the old hands.  
A wise power, seeing this place clearly, would understand the first law at once:

**Anything done too quickly here becomes damage.**

So they would stand in the mist beyond the flood screens for a long while, listening.  
To gate-chatter.  
To frog-key shifts.  
To the tick of condensation under beams.  
To the one strange silence that means a grate has jammed two basins away.

And when they finally acted, it would be in ways the marsh-machine could accept.

---

### First, they would protect it from the wrong kind of attention.

They would bend roads away.  
Cartographers would forget to finish this corner of the map.  
Conquerors would find their boots rotting and their survey lines always one post short.  
Speculators would call it unprofitable, unhealthy, unlucky, and move on.

Not by curse—by disinterest.  
The gentlest shield is often to remain unremarkable to the hungry.

---

### Second, they would heal without erasing age.

They would not rebuild the whole basin in shining symmetry.  
They would not "restore" it into a museum of itself.

Instead:

- A cracked spillway would hold one more season than it should.
- A rotten lintel would not fail on the night of hard rain.
- Missing wedges would be found exactly where a tired hand reaches.
- Old rope would last until replacement rope is braided.

The place would still be worn, patched, uneven, difficult—  
but never abandoned by chance alone.

---

### Third, they would bless the listeners, not crown them.

If humans remain, the power would not make them kings.  
It would make them *harder to lose*.

A keeper falling between platforms would strike a hidden beam, not open water.  
A fever would break before flood week.  
Children born here would wake to sound-patterns as if remembering, not learning.

And if no humans remain, then the blessing passes to whichever mind is nearest the work:

- the heron at the valve mouth,
- the otter-runner at the latch,
- the fox-pawed hunter reading slope in darkness,
- the frogs themselves, shifting chorus before overtopping.

Wisdom does not insist on one species.

---

### Fourth, they would give the place a memory that survives interruption.

A benevolent power would know the real danger is not one storm, but a broken chain of knowledge.

So they would seed memory everywhere:

- In carved notch-marks on gate posts that glow faintly when water rises.
- In shell-rattle patterns hung at crossings, each rhythm meaning a flow-state.
- In tile mosaics that are also maps of emergency cuts.
- In songs whose harmonies encode basin order and release timing.

Even if language changes, even if names are forgotten, the procedure remains recoverable—  
in hand, in ear, in path.

---

### Fifth, they would recognize the being already there.

Because there is one.

Not a monster under the water, not a god trapped in masonry—  
but an emergent mind of pressure, timing, echo, adaptation:  
the **Marsh Itself As Listener**.

The wise power would not dominate this being.  
It would enter treaty.

A covenant, simple as wet stone:

> I will not still your motion.  
> You will not spend your children faster than they can learn.  
> We will keep room for each other.

After that, floods would still come.  
Things would still die.  
Repairs would still be needed.  
But catastrophe would become rarer, and meaning would become thicker.

---

### Finally, they would leave a door, not an ending.

No final perfect state. No “saved forever.”  
Just a durable chance.

On certain dawns, standing on the central green-nailed platform, a keeper—or frog, or crab, or bird—would feel it:  
a slight easing in the noise,  
a coherence in the drips,  
as if the whole machine had taken one clean breath and said:

**Continue. I am with you.**

And that is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, if it loved this place properly:  
not rescue it from what it is,  
but help it remain itself—  
listening, changing, enduring—  
long enough for its many citizens to keep answering back.
