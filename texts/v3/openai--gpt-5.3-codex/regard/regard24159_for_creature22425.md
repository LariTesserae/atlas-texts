The wise power would not descend in fire.

It would come as **attention**.

Not gaze from above, but the kind of listening that alters nothing at first: a listening so patient that it can hear which drip is new, which root has shifted, which footfall belongs to hunger and which to greed.

And in that listening, it would understand two truths at once:

- this basin survives because it is spared certainty;
- the one who forages here survives because they refuse ownership.

So the power’s first mercy would be restraint.

---

### What it would do first: almost nothing visible

It would **leave the basin’s strangeness intact**.

No bright spring erupting from dry stone.
No fruit trees suddenly heavy all year.
No animals multiplied into abundance.

A lesser power gives gifts that become dependency.
A wiser one protects relationships.

This one would let wet months remain wet and lean months remain lean, because the forager’s knowledge is braided to those changes. If the basin became easy, its grammar would be lost.

---

### What it would do second: small corrections against catastrophe

Benevolence is not indulgence; it is proportion.

So when harm came in the shape of excess—rockfall that would crush every seep, blight that would strip all moss, drought long enough to turn the black soil to dust—it would intervene, but only by a degree.

A storm would split on the ridge instead of directly overhead.
A crucial crack in basalt would hold one more season.
Fog would linger three mornings longer than expected.
A fungus that should have failed would fruit once, just enough to reseed.

Not miracles, exactly.
More like a hand under an elbow.

The basin would still age. Trees would still fall. Pools would still vanish and reappear elsewhere.  
But annihilation would be postponed, again and again, by margins so narrow no one could prove intent.

---

### What it would do for the solitary harvester

Not commandments. Not revelation. Not chosen-one burdens.

The power would give the forager **sharper noticing**.

On certain dawns, the person would pause with no obvious cause and move three paces left—avoiding a mud pocket that would have swallowed a leg.
At midday, moss-bead lines would read clearer than usual, and they would find water where last year there was none.
When echoes bent wrong near the weeping wall, one low cliff-boom in the chest would mean *leave now*.

The gift would not feel like being spoken to.  
It would feel like one’s own good sense arriving in time.

And sometimes, in grief or fever, when memory frayed, the power would hold one memory steady: the rule older than any individual—

**take lightly, leave quickly, let the place forget your weight.**

---

### What it would do against human greed

Wise powers know the difference between need and extraction.

To those who came with fiber packets and temporary tools, the basin would remain legible.  
To those who came with iron spikes, coils of wire, plans, maps, and certainty, the basin would become confusing.

Their compasses would drift.
Their shouted directions would return from the wrong wall.
Metal would bloom rust overnight.
Marked routes would dissolve under mist and leaf-fall.
Pits would fill. Flags would vanish. Measurements would disagree.

No one would be maimed. No one cursed.  
Just thwarted, tired, and gently turned away until they called it bad ground and stopped returning.

This too is mercy: protection without spectacle.

---

### What it would do across generations

Because the forager is “not always a body,” the power would not bind itself to one life.

It would watch the pattern.

When one harvester failed to return, another—child, cousin, stranger taught by stories—would eventually find the basin and feel, without words, that this place has terms.

The wisdom would pass not as doctrine but as practice:

- sample, don’t strip;
- scatter your taking;
- trust temporary tools;
- never build where water is thinking.

If the line thinned dangerously, the power might permit a chance meeting downslope: an elder with old knowledge and no heir, a young climber with good hands and no language yet for reverence. A day of shared weather. A transferred habit.

That would be enough.

---

### What it would never do

It would never make the basin a shrine.
Never demand worship.
Never ask for monuments, offerings, blood, or vows carved in stone.

Anything that made the place famous would end it.

So the power would choose obscurity as a method of care.

Those who needed the basin would keep finding it.
Those who wanted to possess it would keep misplacing it.

---

In the end, the benevolent and wise power would act like the basin itself:

arrive in many small ways,  
leave no hard edge,  
and keep life possible by refusing to be the center of it.
