They would begin by **not mistaking scale for need**.

A wise power, meeting that underplain and its standing crowd, would feel the first temptation—*improve it, organize it, elevate it*—and decline.  
Because nothing there is unfinished. It is not primitive. It is not waiting.  
It is already a long-kept agreement between stone, water, dimness, hunger, and return.

So first, they would do what very few powers do:

## They would watch.

Not for an hour. Not for a season. Long enough to learn the true grammar:

- which collapses are ruin and which are renewal,  
- which dry years prune and which break,  
- which predators stabilize and which panic,  
- which routes are optional and which are arteries.

They would listen until the noise separated into meaning.

---

Then, if they acted, they would act at the **edges**, never at the center.

Not a throne lowered into the great halls.  
Not commandments.  
Not a chosen species raised above the rest.

Only four kinds of mercy.

## 1) Mercy of buffering

The underplain lives by rhythm, and most extinctions begin as broken rhythm.  
Too much rain at once. Too long without seep. A collapse that closes three corridors instead of one.

So the power would place no visible machines, but alter probabilities:

- fractures that would have failed simultaneously now fail years apart,  
- flood pulses arrive spread over days, not minutes,  
- one skylight closes while another opens elsewhere.

Nothing miraculous enough to be legend.  
Just disasters shaved down from cliffs to slopes.

The residents would call it luck.  
Luck would become lineage.

## 2) Mercy of corridors

Dense life dies in pockets when paths pinch shut.

So the power would keep a quiet map of movement—bat lanes, cricket fans, predator circuits, amphibian stepping pools—and make sure the map always has redundancies. If one route is lost, another becomes possible within a generation.

A slab falls, but leaves a crawlspace.  
A pool silts, but a seep starts twenty paces off.  
A root curtain dries, but a new crack catches runoff.

No one notices the hand.  
Everyone uses the door.

## 3) Mercy of limits

The greatest threat would not come from within.  
It would come from above: drilling, blasting, poisoning, bright noise, heat drift, extraction without listening.

Here the benevolent power would be least gentle—not violent, but absolute.

Survey stakes rot overnight.  
Maps blur in human memory.  
Compasses jitter near sink mouths.  
Permits vanish in clerical cascades no one can trace.  
Those who insist on forcing entry find only unstable rubble and bad air and the sudden conviction that there is nothing worth taking.

Meanwhile, respectful visitors—rare, careful, brief—find just enough to understand that this is a living system, not an empty void.

The underplain remains unowned.

## 4) Mercy of a little more mind

Not intelligence imposed from outside; that would be colonization in sacred dress.  
But perhaps a slight increase in shared signal.

A pressure-change murmur that travels through bat colonies faster than panic.  
A scent-thread predators can read as “overhunted here.”  
A synchronized stillness before toxic runoff reaches a pool.

Not language, not parliament—just a better collective nervous system.  
A standing crowd becoming, in danger, briefly more one.

---

And what would they refuse to do?

They would refuse monuments.  
Refuse to pick a mascot species.  
Refuse to convert habit into hierarchy.  
Refuse to turn abundance into obedience.

They would not teach bats to pray, nor mammals to build cities, nor fish to see.  
They would not drag daylight into every chamber and call it salvation.  
They would not simplify the chorus to make it legible to outsiders.

Because wisdom is often this plain:  
**do not cure a system of being itself**.

---

If you returned centuries later, you might say nothing had changed.

You would still hear the wing-rattle first.  
Still smell guano and wet limestone.  
Still find the same cool breath at floor level, the same ticking drips, the same sudden hall after long low roof.

But if you looked with patient eyes, you would see the signature:

fewer hard crashes, more recoveries,  
fewer dead ends, more detours,  
fewer silent years, more near-silences that fill again.

No golden age. No perfection.  
Predation still sharp, hunger still real, collapse still part of the stone’s own speech.

Only this: the whole thing remains possible.

And that, from a benevolent and wise power, would be the highest kind of intervention—  
not to reign over the underworld,  
but to keep its long stubborn music from being abruptly, stupidly cut off.
