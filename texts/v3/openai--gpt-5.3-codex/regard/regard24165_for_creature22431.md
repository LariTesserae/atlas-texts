It did not come as a storm.

A lesser power would have announced itself with thunder, with sudden rivers, with green forced out of stone just to prove it could.  
A wise one did what this plateau itself does: arrived by condensation.

For a long while—years, maybe decades—it only watched.

It watched heat climb black ribs of basalt by noon and slide back into reservoir courts at night.  
It watched plate-herds polish whole districts by appetite alone.  
It watched workers lean down, palm to paving, and read tremors like script.  
It watched the one being who had drawn its eye most sharply: not a ruler, not a saint, just a patient resident of the in-between systems—a keeper of angles and flows, a person who spent more hours with valves and ledgers and ground-vibration charts than with anyone else.

This being had a habit of doing small repairs nobody ordered: resetting a mirror array by half a degree so gliders would not be flash-blinded at dusk; opening overflow two minutes early because canal-spawners were stacking at the gates; chalking warning lines where condensation would turn stairs to glass by dawn.  
Not heroic acts. Preventative acts.  
The kind that never become legend because disaster does not happen.

The power, being benevolent, loved this immediately.  
The power, being wise, did nothing immediately.

Because if it gave this place easy abundance, it would unmake it.  
If it broke the hard law of moisture and mineral, whole food webs would collapse from kindness.  
If it silenced predators or stilled heat, the city would lose the very grammar it lived by.

So when it finally acted, it acted in three narrow mercies.

---

### The first mercy: **margin**

Not miracle-rain. Not endless water.  
Just a widening of the knife-edge.

Driest years became survivable instead of terminal.  
Worst metal-laden downpours lost a fraction of their poison before reaching breeding lanes.  
Condensation came a little more faithfully to districts that had none of the political leverage and all of the maintenance burden.

No one saw a god’s hand. They saw statistics bend.  
“Luck’s improved,” said foremen.  
“Cycles are less cruel,” said elders.  
Children born afterward thought this had always been normal.

---

### The second mercy: **translation**

The power discovered that this world already spoke across species, but only in fragments: shell-knock, skin-flush, tremor pulse, pipe percussion, tower blink.  
So it placed, deep in old pressure lines under the oldest compound, a resonance that woke only at dusk: a low, almost inaudible chord that carried pattern better than words.

Not language, exactly—alignment.

Spire-runners paused at crossing edges they once rushed.  
Plate-herds shifted migration lanes around nursery courts without being driven.  
Workers could feel when gliders were about to drop from thermals before shadows appeared.  
Predation did not end. Conflict did not end.  
But fatal misunderstanding, the stupid kind, thinned.

People called it “the evening hum” and argued about whether it was mechanical, geological, or superstition.  
The wise power did not clarify.

---

### The third mercy: **burden-sharing**

The being who had first been noticed kept carrying too much alone.  
Wise powers are wary of making martyrs from the competent.

So it changed culture instead of destiny.

It made attentiveness contagious.

Apprentices began to imitate the being’s tiny corrections, then surpass them.  
Districts started exchanging angle tables and migration forecasts without waiting for crisis.  
Maintenance logs grew not longer, but more precise.  
A proverb appeared in three compounds at once, origin unknown:

**If you can prevent it quietly, that is the highest craft.**

Within a generation, this ceased to be one being’s ethic and became civic law.

---

And after all this, the power still did not throne itself.  
It did not ask for worship, and it did not found a priesthood.

At most, people left a shallow pan of first-condensed water on certain roofs during hottest months, not as tribute but as acknowledgment: that survival here was always shared, always negotiated among stone, machine, animal, weather, and will.

As for the being who had drawn its attention—  
they grew older, then slower, then eventually absent from the rounds.

But absence did not reopen the old failures.  
That was the point.

The city kept breathing: inhale vapor, exhale runoff.  
Gliders kept mapping thermal scripture overhead.  
Shells kept knocking in dry percussion at midday crossings.  
Children still learned to listen with their feet.

And somewhere beneath the oldest lines, at dusk, the low hum continued—  
not command, not miracle, just the sound of a benevolent intelligence choosing, over and over, to help without taking over.
