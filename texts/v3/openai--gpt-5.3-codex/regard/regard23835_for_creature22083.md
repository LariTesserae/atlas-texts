It did not arrive as a sun-spear, or a voice that split the cloud.

A wise power, meeting this basin, would understand the first law here:  
anything forced becomes wreckage.

So it watched first.

For a long while it did nothing except learn the city’s verbs.

How a grandmother could tighten six families’ storm-lines with three knots and a song.  
How children tested stair-depth by toe before heel.  
How an argument about mooring rights could end, not with triumph, but with everyone wet to the waist, resetting each other’s posts in rain.  
How even grief here had hydraulics.

It listened to the Undercurrent thrum.  
It listened to chimney cough, frog-silence, rope-hum, drum-code.  
It learned that this was not a place that needed saving from change.  
It needed companionship in changing.

Then, very gently, it began.

---

### What a benevolent and wise power would **not** do

It would not harden the basin into permanence.  
It would not kill Old Movement in the deep.  
It would not drain the drowned rooms and call that mercy.  
It would not make the Mooring Folk dependent on miracles.

It would refuse to turn a living city into a museum of safety.

Because wisdom here means this: preserve adaptability, not stasis.

---

### What it would do instead

#### 1) It would give them better **warning**, not immunity.

Not prophecy as spectacle—sensing as craft.

The power would tune the city’s existing intelligences:  
- jade vent-frogs whose skin patterns shift earlier before gas bloom,  
- blind fish that rise in distinct spirals before deep pressure events,  
- rope-vines that tighten or slacken in readable sequences before long storms.

Nothing supernatural to the point of dependency.  
Just the margin of two hours, then half a day, then one precious day between omen and impact.

In this place, one day is a hundred lives.

#### 2) It would bless **maintenance memory**.

Maps rot. Elders die. Flood marks vanish.

So the power would inspire a new civic art: memory-cord weaving.  
Each major repair, relocation, breach, and recovery encoded in color, knot-spacing, shell tags, and tension pattern—hung in dry towers, recopied each generation. A library you can read by touch in blackout and thunder.

It would not replace writing.  
It would braid writing, rope, and song so no single failure erases history.

#### 3) It would intervene once, decisively, at the right scale.

Not to end danger forever.  
To prevent one irreversible cascade.

When an adolescent fault-line opens under the north terraces and three districts begin to cant toward a deep shaft, the power would do one vast thing: it would make the water remember an old path for seven nights. Just seven.

Current would bend where no channel exists, holding pressure off the failing foundations long enough for the Mooring Folk to do what they do best: untie, pole, brace, lift, re-anchor, account for everyone.

After the seventh dawn, the borrowed path would fade.  
No monument, no throne, no cult demanded.  
Only a city still alive, and fully aware who saved it: mostly themselves, with help.

#### 4) It would reconcile the people and Old Movement.

The wise power would not name the deep thrum monster or god.  
It would create a ritual of negotiated distance.

At appointed low-pressure seasons, current-readers and deep-watch keepers lower ceramic resonators into the oldest cracks. The resonators sing the basin’s own frequencies back into it, mapping where Old Movement rolls and where it rests. Building exclusions become precise, not fearful folklore.

Terror becomes protocol.  
Protocol becomes culture.  
Culture becomes peace.

#### 5) It would protect the city from the worst corruption: contempt.

Not flood, not fungus—contempt.

If outsiders come seeking to “improve” by imposing rigid walls, private choke-points, extraction dams, or ownership regimes that ignore flow-consequence, the power would act socially, not violently. Their surveys fail strangely. Their fixed pilings crack. Their certainties blur in fog. Their best engineers keep ending up in long conversation with old rope-judges and leaving, chastened, with wet boots and better questions.

The power would not punish curiosity.  
It would dissolve arrogance.

---

### And what of worship?

The power would accept almost none.

No temples above floodline.  
No priestly monopoly.  
No sacrifices.

If given offerings, it would prefer these:
- a repaired public stair,  
- a shared dry room for storm refugees,  
- apprenticeship taught without fee,  
- a dangerous myth rewritten into usable instruction.

Its doctrine, if anyone insisted on one, would be short:

**Keep each other moored.  
Keep things porous.  
Leave room for movement.**

---

### Over generations, the result

The Mooring Folk would eventually add a third answer to “where do you live?”

Sleeping platform.  
Workwater.  
Storm anchor.  
And now, quietly: **listening line**.

A literal line in every district, maintained like any other utility—part instrument, part memorial, part covenant. You place your palm on it before major decisions. Not to ask permission from heaven. To remember scale, consequence, and interdependence.

Children would grow up hearing that the great power came once like a tide and mostly stayed like attention.  
Elders would say this is the highest form of blessing: not rescue from reality, but deeper participation in it.

And in the deep shafts, Old Movement would still roll sometimes.  
Ropes would hum.  
Fish would rise early.  
Drums would sound.  
Homes would be untied and moved, no panic, no heroics, only practiced grace.

The basin would continue being born.  
The city would continue not coming undone.

Which is exactly what a benevolent and wise power, seeing this place, would choose.
