It watched for a long time before it acted.

Not from the sky—too obvious, too theatrical for a place that distrusted spectacle—but from the same edges everyone else used: stair landings, cistern rims, shade strips beside warm walls. It wore a body because bodies are how respect is paid. Dust on its sandals. Damp at its cuffs. A copper key at its wrist like any basin worker.

No one recognized it as power.  
That was the first kindness.

It learned the appointments of mist, the animal descents, the legal turns of speech. It watched a dispute over a blocked channel settled by three generations of ledger entries and a bowl of shared broth. It watched a child stop mid-game to clear moss from a drip lip because “if I leave it, the west court goes thirsty by counting-time.” It watched people who had made devotion out of maintenance.

And it found the being it had come for—not a king, not a priest, not a prodigy.

A tuner.

An old channel arbiter named Selen, narrow as a reed stake, with hands permanently marked by lime and copper. Selen’s district sat where dry stone met damp gardens, where every error showed quickly. She could hear, by the pitch in a clay pipe, whether a settling jar had begun to silt.

The power approached her at last collection, when drops were slowing and everyone was a little softer with relief.

“Ask,” it said, quietly enough that it might have been a neighbor.  
“I am allowed one true intervention.”

Selen did not bow. She did not startle. She only looked at the runnel, then at the stranger’s eyes, and understood enough.

“If you are what you sound like,” she said, “you already know the danger here.”

“The danger of thirst?”

“No. The danger of certainty.”

She tapped the stone lip of the channel.

“We survive because the world keeps its promises. We may someday fail because we assume it always will.”

The power smiled—small, almost sad.

“Then what do you ask?”

Selen thought for so long the mist finished and clear air returned.

“Do not break our system,” she said. “Do not send catastrophe to educate us. We are not children. Give us practice, not punishment.”

So the wise power did what wisdom does at its best: almost nothing, precisely.

It made a change so slight no one could call it miracle.  
Beginning the next cycle, the mist did not arrive at one exact minute. It arrived within a window: sometimes a little early, sometimes late, never by more than a quarter jar-height. Volume unchanged. Pattern intact. Promise kept—but no longer mechanical.

At first people called it error. Then sabotage. Then instrument drift. They recalibrated copper tongues, re-cut shadow marks, argued in low voices across basin courts. Selen stood in public hearing and said, “Record what is, not what was.”

The ledgers gained a new column: **Range**.

Children learned not only fixed marks but tolerances.  
Law added language for buffers and contingencies.  
Work songs acquired verses with optional beats.  
Goat traffic widened by one lane near mineral licks.  
Cistern rights included slack for neighbors.  
Craft prestige shifted, subtly, from perfect repetition to graceful correction.

Nothing collapsed. No famine came. No district drowned.

But a people who had mastered fidelity also began to master response.

Years later, when Selen died, they buried her between dry plaza and wet garden, at the hinge she had always served. On her marker they carved:

**She taught us to trust the pattern.  
She taught us to leave room inside it.**

And the benevolent power, attention complete, withdrew.

Not because it stopped caring—  
because it no longer needed to stand nearby to keep them safe.

They had become, at last, reliable in two directions:  
faithful to what repeats, and gentle with what doesn’t.
