They would **not** begin by cleansing it.

A lesser power would have flooded the Underdock with white light, sterilized the channels, numbered every species, replaced every valve, called it mercy, and killed the place in a season.

A benevolent and wise power would do the harder thing first:

They would listen long enough to be changed by what they heard.

---

For one full rain-cycle, perhaps three, they would not intervene at all.

They would stand—if standing is the word for a being that can inhabit pressure and echo—in the warm fog lanes and let drips mark their measure.  
They would watch fish refuse a painted line no net can explain.  
They would watch a fox pause before a grating that looks sound and is not.  
They would watch a keeper delay a purge by ten minutes because the swifts are fledging.

They would notice what surface planners always miss:  
that the Underdock is not broken infrastructure plus opportunistic life;  
it is a treaty, revised hourly, signed by water.

Only after they understood that would they act.

---

And what they would do would be small enough to fit the local law:

**nothing quickly, everything eventually.**

### 1) They would trim catastrophe, not remove risk.

Storm decades would still come. Water would still rise. Sirens would still matter.  
But the worst surges—the once-in-a-century spikes that erase memory—would arrive with their peak blunted, as if some vast hand had taken half a finger’s depth off the top.

Not safety.  
Continuity.

Enough that dens are flooded, not annihilated.  
Enough that pumps strain, not shatter.  
Enough that keepers curse, work, and remain.

### 2) They would restore legibility where memory was fraying.

The warning mosaics in obsolete scripts would begin to “translate” themselves—but only in the ways locals can use:

- condensation beading into arrows on bulkheads at shift change,  
- mineral blooms highlighting old load marks before a walkway weakens,  
- lamp flicker patterns settling into a grammar apprentices can learn.

No booming voice. No commandments.  
Just the district becoming slightly easier to read by those already paying attention.

### 3) They would tune the machinery’s mercy.

Not newer machines. Better intervals.

A pump that always drifted two minutes per year would continue to drift—except now it would miss spawning corridors by hours instead of colliding with them.  
A purge cycle would hesitate, once, when swift nests are occupied.  
A warm line feeding toxic film would cool by a degree and feed harmless bacteria instead.

No one would call it miracle.  
They would call it “finally got the timing right,” and that would please the power.

### 4) They would protect the keepers from being forgotten by the city above.

This might be their boldest intervention.

Not statues. Not medals.  
They would work through necessity: averted failures that can only be averted if the Underdock remains staffed by people who know its living map.

Budgets would mysteriously survive one more review.  
Rotation orders would quietly route the teachable downward and the reckless elsewhere.  
A councilor who has never gone below would dream, three nights running, of a pressure gate fused shut and wake with an inexplicable urgency to fund maintenance.

The wise power would understand: reverence is unreliable, but dependency is durable.  
So they would make the city remember, by needing what it had nearly discarded.

---

Would they heal everything? No.

There would still be rust under calcite.  
Still eels in cable bundles and foxes losing litters in bad years.  
Still corridors where echoes arrive before speech.  
Still names crossed out in old logbooks.

A benevolent power does not owe a world the absence of grief.  
It owes a world the possibility of continued relation.

So they would leave hardship that teaches and remove only ruin that silences.

---

In time, residents would argue about whether anything had happened at all.

Old keepers would say the Underdock had always had moods.  
Young keepers would swear the drips now carry warnings if you listen right.  
Surface engineers would publish papers about “stochastic resilience in legacy hydromechanical ecologies.”  
Children would say the tunnels are kinder if you greet them.

The animals would not argue.  
They would route, spawn, bask, hunt, and shift as they always had—  
except a little more of them would survive each hard year.

And the shared intelligence of delay—the one with no body, the one made of logs, tooth marks, pump drift, and habit—would gain a new thread in its weave:

not command,  
not design,  
but accompaniment.

---

If you asked that power, at last, why they did so little, they might answer:

Because this place was already alive.  
Because help that arrives as domination is only another disaster.  
Because wisdom, here, is measured in intervals kept.

Then they would do what the Underdock itself does:

continue, quietly, in the dark,  
making room for water, machines, animals, and people  
to keep renegotiating the same faithful treaty.
