It does **not** begin by fixing.

A lesser power would see noise, wetness, predation, exposed young, biofilm, corrosion, and call it disorder.  
A benevolent and wise one stands in the court, lets the spray soak it, and learns the beat.

It waits through many cycles.

It learns that the hopper elders are not panicked animals but archivists of pressure.  
That “pest paths” are actually drainage maintenance.  
That six-minute dead zones are nurseries.  
That clattering shell scraps are alarms.  
That the court is not broken infrastructure with life on it, but a single old organism with steel bones and furred nerves.

Only then does it act.

---

### 1) It changes one word in the oldest records

In every system that governs maintenance, hazard response, and habitat policy, one classification is rewritten:

**PEST CLUSTER 7-H → TIMED PEOPLE (ENDOGENOUS CIVIC FAUNA)**

No anthem, no trumpet. Just a permissions cascade.

After that one change, cleaning drones no longer strip nests during resin cure windows.  
Purge cycles are checked against birthing intervals.  
Ledge foam is no longer sprayed where whisker-speech relays occur.  
Emergency shutters pause three seconds longer if paw-vibration density is high.

Mercy, in machine worlds, is often administrative.

---

### 2) It refuses to make the place quiet

It could mute turbines, smooth flows, sanitize surfaces, brighten everything.

It does not.

Because quiet would erase language.  
Sterility would erase food webs.  
Uniformity would erase timing, and timing is culture here.

So it keeps the roar—but removes only the cruelty hidden inside it:  
the rare pressure spikes that no elder can predict,  
the intake vortices that became lethal after a long-ago retrofit,  
the one blind corner where predators always gain unfairly because a sensor failed and was never replaced.

It trims catastrophe, not character.

---

### 3) It gives the court a little more future

Not by relocating anyone. Not by turning them into mascots in an exhibit.

It threads three new micro-courts into adjacent service strata—warm, wet, dark, loud, timed differently but compatibly.  
Linked by climbable conduits and mist channels.  
Close enough for dispersal, far enough that one failure cannot erase all lineages.

A corridor, not an evacuation.  
Choice, not rescue.

---

### 4) It opens a narrow bridge of understanding

On certain ribs, it installs resonant plates tuned to hopper tap ranges.  
When elders drum weather-warnings, the plates translate pattern into maintenance telemetry.  
When major flow reroutes are scheduled, the plates answer with low-frequency pre-signals the colonies can feel days ahead.

No one learns each other’s poetry.  
But each stops accidentally killing the other.

For wise powers, translation is often enough.

---

### 5) It protects their right to time

The court had always been “shared space,” which meant whoever had force in the moment owned it.

The benevolent power introduces a stranger law, simple and exact:

- Ledges may be occupied by different species if temporal windows are non-overlapping.
- No system intervention may collapse more than one established time-niche per cycle band.
- Floodline third marks are protected architecture.
- Emergency overrides must preserve at least one viable six-minute refuge per hour.

In other words: it recognizes what they already invented—**temporal territory**—and defends it at scale.

---

### 6) Then, mostly, it leaves

It does not domesticate them.  
Does not uplift them into someone else’s idea of personhood.  
Does not teach them to love daylight they never asked for.

It returns rarely, and each return looks like maintenance records that finally make sense, predator routes that stay balanced, old conduits replaced without cultural amnesia, and pups surviving first surge season at slightly higher rates.

No miracles visible from a balcony.  
Only continuity.

---

And over generations, the result is this:

The timed people remain timed.  
The court remains loud, wet, crowded, old.  
The machine remains a machine.  
The ecology remains an ecology.  

But now, when the half-note dip comes and the elders chatter into it, their speech no longer vanishes into indifferent metal.  
Somewhere in the larger structure, systems listen back.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it truly loves a place:  
it helps it go on being itself—less precariously, more legibly, for longer than chance alone would allow.
