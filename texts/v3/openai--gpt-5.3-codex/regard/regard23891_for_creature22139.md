The wise power did not descend at once.

It watched for a long while—through three flood pulses, two dry stretches, one roof-fall, and the brief flowering when the central boulder went white with bloom and moths.

It watched the Seamfolk at pool-time, how they kept turn order without law.  
It watched them eat without stripping, burn without scarring, leave before roots thinned.  
It watched predators pause at the warm stone and understood: this place already had intelligence.

So the power chose the first mercy of wisdom:

**not to improve what was already coherent.**

---

When it finally acted, no thunder came. No voice shook stone. No sunbeam split the chamber.

It made five small, exact changes.

### I. It taught the ceiling to fail slowly.
Not to stop collapse—collapse was part of the basin’s life—but to turn sudden breaks into warning sounds: three nights of faint mineral ticking before any major fall. Enough time for whiskers to signal, for nests to move, for eggs to be carried.

### II. It altered condensation by a breath.
In the worst dry weeks, when larvae failed and fungus thinned, it drew one extra bead of water per hundred from warm rock to cool root. Too little for flood, enough for continuity.  
No creature noticed the miracle directly. They only noticed that some years no one starved first.

### III. It put a veil on greed.
Those who came from the surface with extractive intent—nets, cages, plans for ownership—found the corkscrew descent subtly wrong. Distances doubled. Turns repeated. Fear felt rational and they went back up.  
Those who came to observe, trade, heal, or mourn still found the path.

### IV. It gave the Seamfolk one new signal.
Along the luminous whiskers, a third pulse pattern appeared in newborn litters: not kin-call, not danger-call, but **yield-call**—a way to ask passage across species lines at the crowded center.  
In time, even snakes learned it. Even talons paused for it, sometimes.

### V. It refused worship.
When the first Seamfolk traced spirals in soot and left nectar at a seep, the power accepted the gratitude and dissolved the altar by morning dew.  
No priesthood formed. No commandment came.  
Only this understanding spread: *what helps must not become a throne.*

---

And because it was benevolent, it also accepted limits.

It did not ban hunger.  
It did not end predation.  
It did not brighten the basin into comfort and kill everything adapted to dimness.  
It did not make permanence where life depended on response.

Instead, it kept the chamber itself in sovereignty.

The Seamfolk still moved cradle to cradle.  
Pools still appeared and vanished like temporary law.  
Fires still burned briefly in niches and went out.  
Bones still softened at seep-edges.  
The old jars still held fungus and patience.

If you asked later what the wise power “did,” surface people would disagree.

Some said it protected the basin.  
Some said it enchanted a hidden people.  
Some said it only blessed what was already good.

The Seamfolk, if they answered at all, would pulse their whiskers once—soft green, barely there—meaning:

**It listened, and then it chose not to be larger than necessary.**

In that place, that was the holiest thing imaginable.
