A benevolent and wise power, meeting this upland, would begin with restraint.

Not because it could not act, but because it would understand quickly that this place is already a solved equation written in difficult terms: scarcity balanced by timing, silence balanced by signal, death balanced by use, dryness balanced by hidden wet. To intervene clumsily would be to break the very intelligence it came admiring.

So first, it would do what the stone-kin do.

It would listen through contact.

It would press itself—if such a being can be said to have a body—into warmed escarpment and read the minute tremor-script of lives crossing it: beetle swarms at dawn film, juvenile missteps on old bone-lintels, the heavy patience of gliders above thermal seams, the brief thunder of storm-dusk disputes. It would stay long enough to feel not events but cadence. Long enough to know what must *not* be fixed.

Then, if it chose to act, it would choose acts small enough to pass as weather and old enough to become law.

---

It would not summon rivers.

Instead, it would teach stone to remember one more drop.

On a thousand north-facing walls, in cracks already shaded, it would roughen mineral skins by a degree too fine for eye. Condensation would gather faster there, bead sooner, hold a moment longer before falling. Not abundance—never abundance. Just reliability. One extra wet minute at dawn. One more season where a seep does not fail entirely.

Around old potholes, it would favor lichens whose crust slows evaporation. Around drip-grass halos, it would quietly tilt the odds so roots braid deeper before the next hard dry.

No creature would call this miracle. They would call it “a good run of mornings.”

---

It would not end predation.

It would reduce waste.

At carcasses, where frenzy sometimes strips and scatters faster than use, it would nudge timing: scavengers arriving in better sequence, bone left less shattered, marrow less lost to heat. Infrastructure would thicken. Nursery arches would stand an extra year. Windbreak lattices would fail in five seasons instead of three. In a land where remains are architecture, mercy can look like tensile strength.

---

It would not make the stone-kin rulers.

It would make them better neighbors under stress.

In storm dusk, when boundaries blur and bodies spike with color, it would open—through no command, only possibility—a new signal pattern in their flashing skin: a pulse meaning **shared drip, temporary truce**. Another meaning **deep crack empty, move on**. Another, rare and grave: **dust-sickness in this den, do not enter**.

Not language from the sky. Language grown from what they already are.

Clans would still clash. Eggs would still be taken. Territory would still matter in handspans. But in bad years, fewer juveniles would die at contested seep lips. In disease years, fewer dens would become graves.

---

It would not build monuments.

It would preserve memory routes.

The stacked stones on ridges, the half-claimed markers near old enclosures, the bone-guided paths between condensation fields—these would weather a little slower. Just enough that knowledge survives one more generation when elders are lost in a hot cycle.

And in certain twilight angles, only visible to lidless eyes adapted to dim, some lichen maps would glint faintly along ancestral lines: den-to-drip, shade-to-salt, refuge-to-refuge. Not a road imposed—an annotation of what was already learned at cost.

---

Most importantly, this wise power would set itself a limit:

It would intervene against collapse, not against difficulty.

If a century-drought came that would erase not many lives but *all pattern*—all old den lines, all memory markers, all breeding continuity in one basin—then it might loosen a single rockfall in the right canyon and uncover a sealed cistern. Enough to bridge a bottleneck. Not enough to make the world easy.

It would never make them depend on rescue.

It would keep them dependent on each other, on attention, on timing, on the patient grammar of stone.

---

After many generations, no one there would have a doctrine about this power.

The stone-kin are not a people of temples. They are a people of repeated acts: dawn sequence, shade retreat, marker repair, bone placement, quiet flank-flash across ledges.

But they might have a saying, passed in scent and tap and brief throat-bloom, hard to translate and truer than prayer:

**The old rock listens back.**

And what the benevolent power would do, in the end, is make that sentence slightly more true each year.
