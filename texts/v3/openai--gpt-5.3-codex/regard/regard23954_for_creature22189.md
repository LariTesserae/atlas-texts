They would not arrive as thunder.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this place, would understand at once that it is allergic to spectacle.  
No mountain would split.  
No river would be summoned from nowhere.  
No golden decree would be nailed to a gantry.

They would stand at the edge of the levee in a worker’s coat, hands behind their back, and watch three full siren cycles before deciding anything.

Because wisdom, here, begins with rhythm.

---

What they would do first is **refuse to hurry it**.

They would see that this town-being survives by intervals: wet, dry, work, rest, grow, cut back, repair, continue. So instead of “improving” it into brilliance or saving it into fragility, they would place one deep blessing under all its systems:

**May nothing important here fail all at once.**

A bearing would complain before it seized.  
A berm would slump visibly before it broke.  
A boiler valve would hiss longer, louder, early enough for someone half-awake to hear it.  
Mold would appear in a corner before rot entered a beam.  
Even quarrels would gather condensation on doorframes a day before harsh words, giving people one damp warning and a chance to step outside.

Not prevention.  
Not control.  
Just merciful notice.

---

Second, they would touch the water.

Not by commanding storms, but by teaching every drop where to go **after** it arrives.

Ditches would begin to connect in cleaner logic.  
Forgotten low spots would become intentional rain gardens.  
Workshop roofs would gain cisterns, then overflow chains that children could read like calendars.  
Sump ponds would be edged with stone and sedge instead of rusted scrap.  
The white-flowered creepers would spread where runoff needed slowing.  
Mint would claim the stink-prone corners and sweeten them by force of numbers.

People would call this “a drainage improvement program,” because people here prefer plain names.  
The wise power would accept that gladly.

---

Third, they would protect the distributed creature by strengthening its smallest nerves: attention, habit, reciprocity.

They would make it just a little easier to notice each other.

The tea stall awnings would drip in a rhythm that made strangers pause together.  
Public benches would stay dry one seat-width longer than expected, inviting one more person to sit.  
The municipal siren would remain the same pitch, but carry one street farther on fog days.  
A lost dog would, more often than chance allows, choose the right loading dock at the right shift.

Maintenance crews would find, without knowing why, that they now cut vines with a little more selectivity: clear the signal head, spare the nest branch.  
Children would inherit not only wet-concrete literacy but wet-concrete ethics:  
if water darkens here, someone stands here; if someone stands here, leave room.

---

Fourth, and most quietly, they would set a boundary around greed.

Not a wall. A friction.

Speculators would find their grand plans delayed by tiny, legitimate complications: soil reports, drainage ordinances, right-of-way ambiguities, migratory bird windows, pump-load calculations nobody can argue with.  
Nothing dramatic. Just enough paperwork-shaped gravity to keep the place from being flattened into profit before it finishes becoming itself.

At the same time, those who come to mend rather than extract would find doors opening: permits processed, neighbors cooperative, materials arriving in season, old brick matching old brick.

The wise power would never punish with fire.  
They would burden with inconvenience.

For this town, that is more effective.

---

Would they heal every sickness? No.  
Stop every flood? No.  
Spare every good person? No.

Benevolence is not denial of weather.  
Wisdom is not refusal of grief.

There would still be bad years: fungus in the timber, layoffs, fever, one storm that overtops what was thought sufficient, one dry spell long enough to crack the younger willows. Dogs would disappear. Elders would die. A shop would close and not reopen.

But under the blessing, recovery would have purchase.

Someone would know where the old pump parts are stored.  
Someone else would remember which reed roots hold a ditch wall.  
Three households would share one generator without arguing.  
A teenager would take over a ledger no one asked them to keep.  
The starlings would return to the same wire as if punctuation still mattered.

---

And what of the single resident—the compact edge-creature of brick heat, reed seed, iron oxide, recurrent water?

The wise power would not domesticate it.  
Would not crown it.  
Would not make it pure.

They would do something rarer: they would **recognize** it.

They would name it, once, in a language older than property lines and newer than prayer.  
A name meaning roughly:

*the one that holds by adjusting.*

After that, they would leave no temple, only better thresholds.  
Lower awnings where conversations happen.  
Stronger culvert lips where frogs breed.  
A bylaw amendment in plain municipal prose that quietly saves fifty future arguments.  
A culture of carrying spare dry socks and one extra pair for whoever forgot.

---

In a century, no one would say a power visited.

They would say:

This town got lucky with timing.  
This district learned.  
People here are attentive.  
Water behaves socially.  
Things break slower than elsewhere.  
We look out for one another, mostly.  
The willows took, finally.

And the wise power—if still watching from the levee in a weather-dark coat—would count that as perfect credit, and interfere no further.
