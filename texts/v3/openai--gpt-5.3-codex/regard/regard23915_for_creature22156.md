It would not seal the crack.

That is the first thing.

A lesser god, kind but frightened, would close the mountain and call it mercy.  
A proud god would drain the chamber and call it order.  
A hungry god would tame it into a shrine and demand songs for each spared life.

But a wise and benevolent power—one that can tell the difference between suffering and difficulty, between cruelty and wildness—would stand in the storm-hall and understand that this place is not a mistake.

So it would begin by **watching**.

Not for an hour, not for one weather turn—for seasons.  
It would watch the flood-reader with one hand on the cord and one on the notches.  
It would watch the child learning three knocks means drop.  
It would watch a wicker funnel rebuilt six times in one month without complaint.  
It would watch grief carried silently down the ridge when someone misread a surge by half a breath.

And only after it had learned the grammar of this wet world would it act.

---

### What it would not do

It would not make the chamber safe in the way valleys mean safe.

No permanent roof.  
No obedient tide.  
No dry firewood miracle.  
No invulnerable bodies.  
No ending to death.

Because to erase all hazard here would also erase the people who became themselves by reading hazard truthfully.  
It would be rescue by replacement.

The power is wiser than that.

---

### What it would do first: give better warning, not less weather

The storms would still come, but they would become a little more legible.

- A thread of pale lichen would begin to grow along certain basalt seams, and just before pressure-shifts, it would silver visibly.
- The black shell-things would clamp in a distinct pattern before wall-water surges—three quick closures, then stillness.
- The mist near the ceiling would pull into braided streams when hidden channels are about to open.

Not omens for priests; **signals for workers**.  
Readable by anyone trained enough to survive.

The power would not say, “Trust me.”  
It would say, “Here is one more honest clue.”

---

### What it would do second: protect memory from water

The chamber keeps writing over itself. The notches blur, mineral skins thicken, flood marks vanish.  
So the wise power would choose one seam of stone above the highest known strike and alter it very slightly: not invulnerable, not glowing, just resistant to quick overgrowth.

There the keepers could carve long-memory marks—storm families, dead names, route changes, false ledges, vanished channels—and know that ten years later the cuts would still speak.

Not immortality.  
Continuity.

The gift is small, but in a place where history erodes monthly, this is civilization.

---

### What it would do third: place one refuge, not a fortress

High in a side cleft that only opens at certain water heights, the power would shape a pocket barely large enough for two bodies and gear. The floor would be rough, angled, drainable. The ceiling would not drip constantly. Wind would still find it. Cold would still live there. But in a true hammer-storm, it would be the difference between “lost” and “returned.”

One refuge, not many.  
Enough to save lives, not enough to make arrogance affordable.

Because the wise know that if you remove all consequence, you train carelessness.

---

### What it would do fourth: widen the circle of who can learn

It would send dreams—not prophecies, but rehearsals.

To children in nearby valleys: dreams of rope tension, hand placement, the sound of false calm, the feel of submerged drop-offs in the ankles.  
To elders: dreams of new sign-languages when thunder deafens everything.  
To the solitary flood-reader: dreams in which they are no longer solitary.

In time, the practice would stop being a narrow inheritance and become a broader craft.  
Fewer secrets. Fewer avoidable deaths. More hands that know how to read wet stone.

---

### What it would do fifth: set a boundary against greed

Once word spread that this sky-sea held rare minerals, some would come with iron drills and contracts and valley-law.  
The benevolent power would not smite them in lightning. It would do something more precise:

Every tool brought in for extraction, not survival, would fail subtly.  
Pins would shear. Measurements would drift. Anchors would loosen at the wrong moment.  
Nothing dramatic enough for martyrdom. Just relentless unworkability.

Meanwhile, tools made for food, rescue, and study would hold.

Thus the mountain would become uneconomical to exploit and still inhabitable to tend.

A moral translated into mechanics.

---

### And what it would do, finally, with the being who lives there

One night in a lull between hammer and surge, when the flood-reader is alone on the ledge, the power would arrive in the only form that fits the place: not a face in the clouds, but a brief coherence in noise.

A pattern in thunder that can be understood.

Not words exactly, but meaning:

> *I will not take this burden from you.*  
> *I will not leave you alone inside it.*  
> *Keep reading the water. I will keep making it readable.*  
> *Teach what you know. I will help it endure.*

The flood-reader would not kneel. Their hands are occupied.  
They would test the cord, strike the wall twice for rise, and keep working.

That, too, would please the power.

Because wisdom is not worship that interrupts survival.  
It is relationship that strengthens it.

---

So what would a benevolent and wise power do here?

Not conquest.  
Not abandonment.  
Not miracle as spectacle.

It would practice **companionship at the scale of weather**:  
small interventions, durable mercy, preserved skill, shared burden—  
until even in a chamber that never dries, life is not merely endured,  
but carried forward on purpose.
