The wise power would not arrive as thunder.

It would come the way this hollow likes things to come: through traces.

A morning where condensation gathers not just on plaster but in clean, deliberate lines, like writing no one has yet learned to read.  
A rain that lasts exactly as long as the open jars can hold.  
A night when every frog in every wall seam falls quiet at once, as if listening.

And first, it would do what only wisdom does before it acts:

It would **watch long enough to be changed by what it sees**.

---

In many places, a benevolent power can help by making things stable—stone where there was mud, straight roads where there were paths, certainty where there was improvisation.

Here, that would be harm wearing a kind face.

So it would not harden the ground into obedience.  
It would not command the rains into a schedule.  
It would not lift towers above leafline, nor pin this first-draft world into final form.

Instead, it would ask: *What does this place already know how to do, and what does it suffer for doing it so well?*

And the answer would be clear:

They know how to adapt.  
What they suffer is the cost of adapting alone, every day, with no reserve.

So the power would give them **margin**.

Not luxury. Not dominion. Margin.

---

It would begin with water.

Not more, not less—*truer* water.

When rain comes in ten hard minutes, the channels would now catch the first rush cleanly instead of losing half to collapsed lips. Clay linings would crack less often. Cistern walls would “remember” their seal a season longer. The settlement would still need hands, heels, sticks, children running; but fewer mornings would begin with emergency.

The people might say, “The land is listening better this year.”

And they would be right.

---

Then it would work in time.

In a place of endless repair, memory can drown in immediacy. So the power would place memory where it already belongs: in touch.

A mason laying fresh plaster would feel, in the heel of her palm, where last year’s wall failed under late rain.  
A child cutting a trench would *somehow know* to bend it two fingers wider where floodwater once jumped.  
An elder stepping onto a lane at dusk would sense the unseen hollowness beneath and call for stone before it sinks.

No visions. No prophecies.  
Just the past becoming available at the right moment in the body.

Skill, deepened.

---

Then it would bless their coexistence, because wisdom sees that their city is not theirs alone.

Frogs in seam-lines would multiply where mosquitoes overbreed and thin where balance returns.  
Bats would choose rafters above orchards that need night pollination most.  
Ground birds would scratch pests from cassava margins before the leaves show bite marks.  
Lizards would bask near granaries where beetles test the woven bins.

The people would keep their practical names—wall singer, dusk fold, sun flicker, worm pair—and find those names becoming even more accurate over time.

Not because nature is “serving” them, but because relationship has become more legible.

---

Only once, perhaps, would the power do something unmistakable.

A storm too large for the hollow.  
A season-breaking rain, the kind that could shear berms, drown ovens, rot grain, unhouse families in one night.

As it gathers, the canopy would hold a strange bronze light. Birds would go still. Air would press down.

And then—quietly, precisely—the storm would split around the basin’s rim.  
Half would spend itself on the upper plain.  
Half would break in intervals instead of force.  
Enough to fill every pit, not enough to tear every wall.

By dawn, people would walk the lanes stunned by survival, finding damage but not ruin.

No one would agree on what happened.  
Some would say wind. Some ridge shape. Some old luck returning.  
An elder, washing clay from their ankles, would simply mutter: “We were cupped.”

And no shrine would be demanded in return.

---

Because this is what a truly benevolent and wise power would understand:

This community does not need to be rescued from impermanence.  
It needs companionship inside it.

So the power would become a covenant of subtle things:

- failures that happen slower,  
- warnings that arrive sooner,  
- repairs that hold a little longer,  
- learning that passes cleaner between generations,  
- and just enough mercy in extreme weather for culture to continue.

The people would still labor. Still misjudge. Still rebuild.  
Children would still sprint into sudden rain to redirect water with bare feet and shouting.  
Vines would still test walls. Paths would still buckle. Plaster would still sweat at dawn.

Everything would remain in first draft.

But the draft would no longer feel precarious; it would feel accompanied.

And over years, they might come to describe the change not as miracle, but as a new property of reality:

That this place, and those who live by maintenance, are being met halfway by the world.

Which, for a wise power, is the highest form of blessing.
