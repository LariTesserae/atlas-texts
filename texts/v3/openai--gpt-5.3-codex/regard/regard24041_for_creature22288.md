It would begin by **not saving them the wrong way**.

A lesser god would force green into stone, pour rivers where none belong, still the fractures, tame the swarms, and call that kindness.  
A wise one would understand: this underland is not broken. It is young.

So the power would come quietly—first as a pattern, not a figure.

---

### First gift: warning without command

Across the high ribs, certain seams would begin to sing before failure.  
Not loudly; just enough that trained ears could hear a coming shear an hour early, sometimes a day. A low glass-hum underfoot. A tremor with a tone.

The Rib Folk would test it, doubt it, test it again. They would mark tablets:

- **Blue-veined ribs hum before drop.**
- **Do not sleep below singing stone.**
- **If hum rises to a whistle, run uphill even if uphill is wrong.**

The power does not prevent collapse.  
It gives time.

Not certainty—time.

---

### Second gift: water memory

In a few basins, and then more, the clay would begin to hold an imprint of prior wet. Where condensation had gathered three times in a cycle, it would gather a fourth with higher chance. Where a seep had lasted two hours, it might last three. Not abundance—**repeatability**.

Still pulse-water, still event-water.  
But now events leave a trace the land remembers.

Children born after this change would grow up with a new verb: not merely “to find water,” but “to ask the basin what it recalls.”

The wise power would not create rivers.  
It would create slightly better odds.

---

### Third gift: mercy in the food web

The carrion rush would remain fast. Predators would remain predators. Density would remain law.

But one small hinge would shift: around major reoccupation stations, scavenger frenzies would delay by minutes—sometimes ten, sometimes twenty—if the dead bore the resin-sign of kin tending. Enough time to carry bodies, enough time to grieve with hands instead of sticks.

Not immunity. Not sanctity zones.  
Just a brief ceasefire written into appetite.

Over generations this would become ritual: throat-beads darkened with condensation, resin mark at sternum, a spoken thanks to the Waiting Minute. They would know it as gift and obligation both: if life is granted a pause, memory must fill it.

---

### Fourth gift: a law felt, not enforced

The power would choose one moral intervention only, because it is wise enough to choose one.

Any group that hoarded cisterns past need—sealing them against strangers in a pulse-scarce cycle—would find their seals failing first. Resin sweating loose. Clay hairline-cracking. Not always, not magically obvious, but often enough to become undeniable.

Any group that repaired shared stations before departing would find their tablets drier, their rope less mildew-bitten, their return routes less dangerous.

The underland would begin to answer behavior with bias.

Not punishment thunder.  
Not reward spectacle.  
Just a persistent tilt toward reciprocity.

Eventually this would harden into custom stronger than kings:  
**Leave a station better than you found it, or the stone will not keep faith with you.**

---

### Would the power show its face?

Perhaps once, perhaps never.

If once, it would likely appear not as a giant person, but as a temporary alignment of the whole underland: drifter-jellies rising together in one vent column, runner herds pausing all at once, shell-borers lifting from walls like a breath held across kilometers. In that suspended minute, every listener would hear the same meaning:

> I will not rule this place for you.  
> I will make it more legible.  
> Your survival remains your art.

Then the motion would resume—traffic, hiss, scrape, drip, thump—because blessing, here, is not escape from reality.

It is a better conversation with it.

---

So what would a benevolent and wise power do?

It would give them **margin**.

A little more warning.  
A little more memory.  
A little more time to bury, share, repair, decide.

And it would trust the Rib Folk to do the rest, because they already know how to live in a world that never stops moving.
