It would begin with **restraint**.

A lesser power, merely strong, would see the undercity and immediately try to “fix” it—bring rivers, widen chambers, smooth predation, sterilize fire, standardize growth, make the living obey the map.  
A wise one would not.

A benevolent one would understand that this place is not broken. It is *young*.

So the power would come quietly, and for a long while do nothing but witness:

- stand in ventilation drafts and learn the language of cheek-turned wind,
- listen at resonant pylons while citizens confer with Thinking Groves,
- watch prunemasters cut bridge-roots by millimeters that mean the difference between transit and collapse,
- follow line-foxes until it understands what they know before instruments know it.

It would learn the city’s first commandment: **interdependence is not inefficiency; it is survival in a hard medium.**

---

Then, when it finally acts, it would act in ways that increase agency rather than replace it.

## I. The First Gift: Time

Not immortality. Not abundance.  
**Time.**

The power would slow cascading failure.

Resin storms would still happen, but they would announce themselves earlier in sap pressure harmonics.  
Root heave would still halt trams, but with enough warning to reroute pollinator fronts and school lines.  
Predators would still den, but predation clerks would find patterns days sooner, not hours too late.

Nothing dramatic from the outside. No miracle floodlights in the sky.  
Just a city that gets, over and over, one more shift to prepare.

A benevolent power knows that one extra shift, in a place like this, is the shape of mercy.

---

## II. The Second Gift: Translation Without Reduction

It would not “uplift” the Thinking Groves into speaking surface language. That would be conquest disguised as kindness.  
Instead it would improve the interfaces so each intelligence can remain itself and still be understood.

Resonant pylons would become finer instruments.  
Bark pulses, volatile emissions, fungal conduction rhythms, tram-vibration signatures—these would be rendered into civic-readable proposals *without flattening their complexity*.

Soon, zoning hearings would include grove testimony in real time.  
Not symbolic testimony. Binding testimony.

The charter would expand:

- neighborhoods that are mostly trunkwork,
- wards represented by herds,
- pollinator corridors recognized as public right-of-way,
- quiet strata protected as constitutional acoustic infrastructure.

The power would do this because wisdom recognizes personhood where memory, preference, and consequence already exist.

---

## III. The Third Gift: A Different Kind of Water

Not rain. Rain would be violence here—erosion, mold bloom, collapse, panic.  
The power would respect the ecology’s dry grammar.

So it would give moisture in forms the undercity can metabolize:

- deeper mineral-bound hydrates unlocked slowly,
- condensation harvested from thermal differentials in lamp-rails,
- breathable humidity pockets in clinics and hatcheries,
- emergency vapor reserves stored in living cistern-fronds.

Water would remain precious, but no longer catastrophic in scarcity.  
“Medicine, not abundance” would become “medicine, and sometimes comfort.”

Children would still learn ration custom.  
They would also learn what it feels like to sleep one night each cycle with soft air that does not abrade the throat.

---

## IV. The Fourth Gift: Fire That Obeys Boundaries

The undercity will always live with flame-risk because it lives with resin, industry, and growth. The power would not erase fire; it would teach it manners.

It would alter nothing visible at first—only statistical behavior:

- amber films less likely to flash chainwise across districts,
- dead filament mats that self-separate before spark catch,
- ventilation towers that draft smoke upward instead of laterally,
- grove bark that chars in sacrificial layers, preserving conductive cores.

Sapwrights and quiet crews would still work.  
They would simply stop losing so many people to the same old bad luck.

---

## V. The One Thing It Refuses to Give

Certainty.

A wise power would not make the city safe enough to become careless.  
It would leave difficulty in place—enough that stewardship remains a living verb, enough that law remains adaptive, enough that every generation must still learn how to listen.

Because if it solved everything, it would unmake the very intelligence it came to honor.

---

In time, people would argue about whether the power was ever there.

Engineers would say the system just matured.  
Grove-attendants would point to before-and-after signal coherences.  
Bird handlers would swear tunnel birds started taking impossible routes, always finding the open chamber.  
Children would call the phenomenon by practical names: *the extra hour*, *the kinder draft*, *the warning hum*.

And the power, if asked directly what it had done, would answer:

> I did not come to rule your city.  
> I came to make sure it remained yours.  

Then it would withdraw to the edges—not absent, just non-intrusive—  
a guardian of thresholds, intervening only when collapse would erase choice itself.

Because benevolence, at its wisest, is not domination by goodness.  
It is the protection of a people’s ability to keep co-growing with what they are—  
hands, hooves, fronds, roots, mesh, and law—  
in a young underland that is still becoming.
