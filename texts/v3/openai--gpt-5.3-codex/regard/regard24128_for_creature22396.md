They would not arrive as fire.

A power that is both benevolent and wise, seeing this place, would understand immediately: anything sudden would kill what it wished to help. One miracle too large—a warm winter, a permanent spring, a mountain held still by decree—and the whole hard-won intelligence of the people would begin to atrophy. In a country where survival is attention, mercy must be shaped like attention too.

So first, it would watch.

It would sit in the high clear light through one full turning of seasons, saying nothing, learning the grammar: where frost heave starts, which cistern stones sweat before dawn, which whistle means *danger-left*, which family always repairs their wall and then their neighbor’s. It would learn the names of dogs and the routes of ravens. It would learn that this city is not a thing but a practice.

Then it would choose its form.

Not king.  
Not conqueror.  
Not a god of abundance.

It would become the **Patron of the Second Look**—the quiet nudge that makes a hand pause on a cracked lintel, the thought that says *check again before sleep*, the instinct that turns a herder’s head one breath before a ledge gives way.

Its gifts would be small enough to keep people necessary.

---

On the first winter after it chose, five changes would be noticed:

1. **Cracks would speak earlier.**  
   Not with voices, but with a faint darkening at their edges in morning frost. Masons who once had three weeks would now have five.

2. **Springs would remember their paths.**  
   Water would still fail sometimes—this power is wise, not indulgent—but abandoned channels would be less likely to vanish forever. Old elders’ maps would become useful again.

3. **Animals would lend warning.**  
   Dogs would refuse certain stair roads minutes before small slides. Ravens would gather on unstable parapets. Goats would balk at snow bridges that looked solid.

4. **Seeds would favor the careful.**  
   Not bigger harvests for all, but better germination in terraces well-maintained. Neglect would still have consequences. Care would become visibly rewarded.

5. **Anger would cool faster in enclosed rooms.**  
   Not erased, not denied—just shortened, as if a window had been opened in the mind. Fewer feuds would survive the night.

---

In exchange, the power would ask for no temples of gold, no blood, no obedience to spectacle.

Only three observances:

- **The Day of Rechecking** each thaw: every household inspects one thing that is “probably fine.”  
- **The Third-Bucket Rule**: when drawing water, one share in every three goes first to common stores in lean weeks.  
- **The Open Ledger of Repairs** in each district: not for shame, but for memory—what failed, what held, what was learned.

These are prayers disguised as civic procedure, which is exactly how such a place prays best.

---

Over generations, people would argue whether the power exists. Sensible people would say: “No, we simply got better at coordination.” Mystics would say: “No, coordination is its name.” Children would leave tiny offerings anyway—twine knots, smooth pebbles, first spun yarn—tucked into wall niches near dangerous turns.

The city would not become easy. Winters would still kill the incautious. Walls would still crack. Caravans would still go missing. But collapses would be fewer, recoveries quicker, cruelty rarer, hoarding harder to justify.

And perhaps most importantly: the people would remain themselves.

Because a wise benevolent power, here, would not solve the mountain.

It would strengthen the habit that already lets human and animal life endure in young stone:  
repair before collapse,  
store before hunger,  
read before reacting,  
share before winter.

In the end, if anyone asked what the power did, the truest answer would be:

**It made care slightly more contagious.**
