It would **not** begin by draining the basin.

A lesser mercy would call this place broken and try to return it to some earlier shape—sunlit, green, legible, still.  
But a wise power would feel, almost immediately, that this world is not a wound. It is an adaptation old enough to be a culture.

So first, it would do the hardest benevolent thing:

## It would refrain.

It would watch whole melt seasons pass.  
It would learn the grammar of silt and collapse, the patient percussion language of stonewinders, the way life here survives by timing rather than possession.  
It would let itself be taught by what endures.

Only then would it act—and only at scales the basin can accept.

---

### What it would do

**1) It would soften catastrophe without erasing danger.**  
Not safety. Never total safety; that would unmake the residents as surely as famine would.  
But where collapse is purely annihilating—dead-end pressure traps, basin-wide kill funnels—it would introduce roughness, baffling, alternate flow paths.  
A wall would fail into terraces instead of a guillotine surge.  
A corridor would become turbulent but passable instead of final.

In human terms, it would not stop storms.  
It would teach storms to leave survivors.

---

**2) It would create memory in a world where maps rot.**  
The basin’s tragedy is not change; it is amnesia.  
So the power would seed certain stones—old ceramic ribs, deep pylons, vault plates—with resonance that keeps a long record of strain and flow.  
When a stonewinder taps them, they answer with layered pulses: what this channel did in three winters, where the wall bent last thaw, which culvert killed the last brood.

Not prophecy.  
Accumulated truth, made legible.

---

**3) It would bless thresholds, not territories.**  
The carried life cannot own ground, so mercy would anchor *intervals*:  
brief slackwater pockets during peak runoff,  
oxygen-rich upwellings that recur just long enough for hatchlings,  
rotational carrion basins that prevent starvation cascades.

Temporary sanctuaries, rhythmic and moving—like breathing space in a hard conversation.

---

**4) It would enter the stonewinder language instead of replacing it.**  
No shining voice from above.  
It would answer knock with knock, pressure with pressure, in the same slow syntax the basin already trusts.  
At first: warnings.  
Later: questions.  
Eventually: shared songs—multi-season chords about when to release eggs, when to abandon old seams, when to follow new fractures that become nurseries.

The power would become, in effect, another current-reader.

---

**5) It would accept death, and make it less wasteful.**  
No wise force promises immortality in a place built on transfer.  
But it could tune decomposition corridors so that large deaths feed broad life rather than vanish into sterile chasms.  
A dead adult stonewinder would still become a district—but a richer one, longer-lived, less likely to be stripped barren in a single violent week.

Mercy here is nutrient justice.

---

### What it would *not* do

- It would not restore the old city.  
- It would not force sunlight-dependent ecologies into a world that has cohered without them.  
- It would not remove predation.  
- It would not crown one species as rightful owner.

Because wisdom recognizes this:  
the basin is not waiting to be corrected.  
It is waiting to be accompanied.

---

In time, the residents might never agree on whether a power had visited at all.

They would only notice that some collapses now break into ladders instead of graves.  
That a few old plates “remember” and answer honestly.  
That hatchlings survive one surge in ten more than they used to.  
That the great slow songs carry farther.

And the stonewinders, tapping jaw to wall in the dim cold, would add a new pattern to their lexicon:

not *claim*, not *warning*, not *mating call*.

Something like:

**There is kindness in the load-bearing.**  
**Move now. You can live through this one.**
