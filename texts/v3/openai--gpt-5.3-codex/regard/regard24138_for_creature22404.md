A lesser power would have pitied them and flooded the basin.

A benevolent and wise one would descend the rim at dawn, stand in the angled light, and do nothing for a long time.

It would watch the first cloths being wrung into jars.  
Watch children test mud with bare feet.  
Watch two elders argue over a channel slope with a stick and a line of pebbles.  
Watch a sleepless parent wake to listen for drip cadence in the dark.

And only then would it understand: this city is not asking to be rescued from effort.  
It is asking not to be betrayed by randomness.

So the power would choose **fidelity**, not spectacle.

---

It would not conjure rivers from dry stone.  
It would not raise shining towers that no one could repair.  
It would not crown one person and call that order.

Instead, it would alter small laws:

- dew would gather a little more readily on woven cloth than on bare rock;  
- clay seals, if made with care, would fail slowly and visibly rather than all at once;  
- the smell of contamination would become sharp enough to catch before sickness;  
- the first heavy rain after a dry season would come with an extra hour of warning wind.

None of this would feel like a miracle.  
It would feel like the world becoming slightly more legible to the people already listening.

---

Then it would turn to the being who lives there.

Not one body—many.  
A distributed creature made of hands, ramps, lids, ledgers, and remembered gradients.  
A civic animal whose heartbeat is measured in drips per minute.

To that being, the power would give one true gift:  
**shared foresight at useful scale**.

Not prophecy of dynasties. Not stars falling.  
Just this:

On certain nights, those who keep flow would dream the same map—  
a faint chalkwork of where next season’s seep will emerge,  
which retaining wall will bulge first,  
which stair will glaze with algae,  
which cistern can be deepened by one arm’s length before the rains.

They would wake, compare notes, and call it craft.

---

The power would also protect them from the oldest civic poison: contempt.

Whenever someone mocked maintenance as lesser work, their own cup would leak by noon.  
Whenever leaders hoarded water for prestige, their stored grain would mildew first.  
Whenever anyone claimed sole credit for a functioning system, the public would remember—clearly, stubbornly—every hand that laid the unseen courses below.

Not punishment.  
Alignment.

In this basin, truth would have consequences that are practical.

---

And once each year, on the cold morning after first rain, the power would take a single action visible to all:

At the highest unfinished ring, where chalk lines mark tomorrow’s wall,  
a thin line of moss would appear overnight in the exact curve of the safest future drain.

No voice in the sky.  
No commandment.  
Just a green suggestion.

The people would gather, touch it, argue, measure, revise, and build.

---

After some generations, they would tell stories about a Presence at the rim.  
Some would call it Keeper.  
Some would call it Patient One.  
Some would insist there is no power at all, only accumulated knowledge and good governance.

The wise power would be pleased by all three explanations.

Because its aim was never worship.  
Its aim was that, in a place still becoming itself, becoming could continue with less loss.

So that children could carry their first full jar up a ramp that does not fail.  
So that mourning stones could keep holding terraces.  
So that each season adds one more stair, one more garden, one more named cistern—  
and none of it depends on a miracle returning tomorrow.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here:

not end the work,  
but make the work truer,  
safer,  
and more shareable across time.
