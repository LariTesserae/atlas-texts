It did not come as fire, prophecy, or a new law carved into the sky.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this high wet city and the one great Being that lives in it—the distributed mind of water, stone, fauna, and Keepers together—understood something immediately:

This was not a place to be *saved from itself*.  
This was a place to be *helped to continue*.

So the power’s first act was restraint.

It watched one full long cycle: monsoon rise, cistern swell, burrower bloom, fungal correction, lean months, repairs, migrations, births, die-offs, rerouting, quiet recovery. It listened to lock bells and tram-squeal and culvert pulse until it could hear what the Keepers heard: not noise, but metabolism.

Only then did it act, and only at three pressure points.

---

### 1) It gave warning, not prevention.

In the deepest seep galleries, where metal-rich waters sometimes turned from useful to lethal in a day, a new living sheen appeared: thin silver-brown threads like wet hair along stone.

The Keepers called it **mercy lace**.

It fed on the same minerals as the old chemotrophs, but when toxicity crossed dangerous thresholds, it changed resonance. Pipes carrying that water began to hum in a low, unmistakable interval audible through walls and railings. No alarm towers needed. No miracles of purification. Just earlier knowing.

Children learned that tone before they learned market routes.

Thousands lived because a gate was closed six minutes sooner, a flock diverted one hour earlier, a fungal vault drained before bloom-collapse.

---

### 2) It stitched memory across generations.

The power did not write scripture. Instead, it made memory harder to lose.

Where Keepers tapped sound ledgers into rail and casing, those rhythms began to leave faint mineral banding over years—subtle, color-struck traces in the metal itself. A repair cadence repeated long enough became physically legible even when no one remembered who first tapped it.

So when fires took records, when flood took elders, when one district’s language drifted from another’s, maintenance knowledge still surfaced in the walls: a rust-red arc for “close second gate first,” a green-white seam for “do not drain both basins in dry season,” a black-gold ladder for “this vent is nursery in monsoon.”

Not infallible. Not complete. But enough to keep continuity from snapping.

---

### 3) It softened the cruelest bottleneck.

The city’s law had always been circulation. But one cycle remained brutal: when scavenger populations crashed, carrion surged; when carrion surged, disease ran through drains and wader marshes, and then into people.

The power did not erase death. It introduced a new eater.

From old tunnel waters came a blind, soft-jawed filter-beast—slow, colony-minded, harmless to large life, voracious for rot and pathogen mats. It nested only in designated warm spillways and could be herded by vibration. The Keepers adopted it quickly, built it corridors, named guilds for its tending.

Plagues that once came like knives began to come like storms: still dangerous, but trackable, survivable, routable.

---

And to the Being itself—the vast civic organism made of many minds—the power gave one private gift:

Not dominion.  
Not immortality.  
A dream.

Once each year, during the first full cistern after the high rains, every sleeper in the terraces dreams the same dream: water finding a blocked way and then, patiently, another. In the dream, no one is hero and no one is useless. Every small redirection matters.

They wake kinder to maintenance, and less tempted by grand, brittle solutions.

---

That is what the benevolent power did.

It did not lift the city out of hardship.  
It did not make it pure, or quiet, or simple.

It made catastrophe less sudden, memory less fragile, and care more contagious.

Then it remained—not above the place, but within its intervals—another quiet participant in the oldest rule they already knew:

Nothing is abandoned.  
Everything is absorbed.
