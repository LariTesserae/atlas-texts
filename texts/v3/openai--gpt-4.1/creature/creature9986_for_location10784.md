Who, or what, lives here?  
It is tempting to say nothing lives here, or rather, that only the riot of plant and fungus—the unthinking multitude—inhabits this place. Yet the place is too articulate to be empty of presence, too deeply inhabited, too bent in secret purpose to be mere ruin overgrown.

The **first sense of life** here is not a single figure, nor a collection of animals stalking between the pipes and shadows. Instead, it is the green network itself—a mindless, collaborative appetite that moves not with legs or eyes, but with pressure and insinuation. Roots are aware of warmth and faint mineral currents the way a tongue is aware of sugar; ferns angle their lawns of frond toward the faint blue pulse of fungus as if calibrating to a secret timepiece. The molds, black and delicate, form irregular lines like sentences in a language of decay. Somewhere in this slow, relentless proliferation is a kind of deliberation: cells remembering where the warmth is deepest, who among them will send the first root-between-seams, who will climb, who will rot, who will bleed green into the cracks.

But this is only the beginning. Beneath the blanketing mass of leaf, stem, mycelium, and condensation—within the moist cathedral of root and oxidized machine—something else persists.

It was not born here, but neither did it entirely arrive from outside. Once, perhaps, it wore clothes and gloves and peered into glass tubes, humming to itself as it tapped at brittle keys. It watched dials, turned valves, ran its hands along cool pipes. That shape—that person—is gone, as gone as the precise function of the half-swallowed engine or the order that placed the jars upon their shelf.  
Yet life, even the most conscious, seldom vanishes without leaving something of itself behind.

**Now:**  
Those remnants stir in the vault. Not as ghosts, but as patterns—the subtle ordering of things, the tendency for certain bundles of stem to follow the hum of an exposed wire, the way some roots split or knot as if in memory of an obstacle, the angle that ferns choose to unfurl in the half-light falling from a broken indicator. There is in the life here a peculiar choreography, a latent rehearsal of gesture: a spiraling of roots around molded jars that almost—almost—seems purposeful, a swelling of mycelium along steel struts that looks methodical rather than random. Sometimes, in the condensation pooling on a broken control panel, you might see the faint impression of a fingerprint—a perfect smear, as though some patient, silent caretaker still reaches out, slick with sap.

It is possible, in the steamy dark, to imagine a **keeper**, but the keeper is a composite:  
—Roots thickened where callused hands once rested.  
—A knotted pseudopod of fungus gripping clay the way fingers might.  
—A vine brushed by wet, oily air, flexing in a motion that echoes a longing or a task.  
—The low whir and chuff of engines, not as machines kept alive by conscious repair, but as mechanisms maintained by living pressure, cooled and fed by plant sweat, mollified by the circulation of sap.

There may be no one body, no single will, but the space is cradled by locomotive intent. The place **tends itself**. With root and fungus, with the absolute insistence of life, with the unwitting echo of human routines, the vault is attended: wires exhumed by greedy stems, indicator bulbs flickered alive by the warmth of decay, a sheet of mold polished to iridescence by the passage of slow, wet air. Where once a worker wiped and checked and mended, now the plant-mass performs an evolution of the same acts—not by plan, but by the patterned momentum of what was, mingling irresistibly with what grows.

**If you stay in the vault**—if you survive the heat, learn to breathe the swollen air, to listen to the language of dripping water and struggling fans—you, too, might become part of this mindless tending. Sweat pearling on your forehead would mingle with the moisture on the ferns. Your fingerprints might be left in the gelatin of a lichen bloom, slowly overwritten by root-hairs. To exist here is to dissolve, molecule by molecule, into the patient process that fills this room with vegetative breath.

**So who lives here?**  
—The community of plants and molds, each sensing and shaping, together a willless colony of need and response.  
—The ghosts of old routines persisting in the habit of vines and the angle of radiant heat.  
—Perhaps the memory of a caretaker, left behind not in spirit, but in the labyrinth of habits and traces condensed into mossy flesh and bioluminescent pattern.  
—The place itself—a living, half-machine, half-hothouse organism, devouring what remains, converting machine to green, gas to sap, metal to humus, humanity to indelible gesture.

Here, life is not an occupant, but a process—restless, consuming, remaking—a low, immortal residency. Nothing here is alone, nothing is entirely itself. All are entangled, caught in the slow, humid festival of reclamation.
