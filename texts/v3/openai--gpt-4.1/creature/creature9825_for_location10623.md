Who, or what, inhabits such a chamber?

To ask what lives here is not to ask for inhabitants in the sense of citizens, but of presences—beings shaped by necessity and bent to conditions utterly unlike those of our own moist, shadow-haunted world.

### The Non-People

**They were never quite human, or, if they were, humanity melted from them slowly, replaced by mineral patience and exquisite tolerance for glare.**  
If you could see one, you would not at first recognize it as alive. These denizens are slight, neither tall nor short, and narrow as a seam of ore, their forms lost in the phosphor brilliance. Their skin is not skin as ours, but a flexible armor—a mesh of translucent plates shot through with veins of something between bone and selenite, glowing faintly with the day's accumulated radiance and thrumming to the ever-present sound. They are hairless, earless, their faces a compromise between mask and instrument: hollows for eyes, shuttered to mere pinpricks, mouths little more than clefts. Their lungs and throats are dense with ossified folds, adapted to sieve music from the air instead of vapor from water.

They move lightly, deliberately—a dance of tension and pause, each action a negotiation with light and vibration. Their gestures are spare, learned from the need to avoid excessive friction or abrasion in an environment where even dust is precious. Fingers are long and jointed, ending in blunt, flat tips—made for fitting stones together, weaving mats from glass-fibre stems, or polishing emblems of crystal.

They live by what grows here, and what grows here lives through them. Scraping nutrient-crust from a mineral grass; gathering the crystalline filaments blown free at midday to grind and mix with the dust-bed they tend within their dwellings. Eating is a slow absorption, part chemical, part ritual—the exchange of brittle things for brittle strength. When daylight is too intense, they retreat to their shaded alcoves not for comfort but to shelter the youngest, more vulnerable plantings.

### The Chitinous, the Borrowers

But they are not alone—the chamber is shared with smaller, harder life.  
Across the tessellation, small creatures scurry—each the size of a curled thumb, sheathed in muted, earthen shell. They have too many legs for mammals, too few for insects—a compromise for moving over sharp stones or burrowing beneath mats of mineral-filament. Their minds are not higher than necessity, but they are clever in their way: they know where the sonic resonance grows dangerous, where the brightness turns killing. Young ones hunt in the cracks, licking residue from flaked salt, avoiding the touch of the "Not-Quite-People" who sometimes sweep an enclosure clear of dust, disturbing their complicated social maps laid down in feces and burrow, in pattern and pace.

They, in turn, are prey and participant in the ecology. Sometimes a burst of spore—sharp glass rattling—draws a swarm, which is drawn in, shredded, and in turn fertilizes the filaments.

### The Vegetation: Half-Mineral, Half-Memory

And then there is what lives while standing still—the vegetation, exuberant in stillness, frantic in how it leans.  
These plants are not passive: their awareness is one of gradients, of competing for vantage and vibration. Rootless creepers fuss toward the nearest seam of prismatic radiance or the thrum of a stone vibrating just right. They pulse, not with sap, but with the migration of ions, their fibers flexing minutely, guiding brightness deep into their cores. Their "leaves" are narrow, bristle-like, and brittle; their "flowers" are small puffs of glassy dust, quick to rupture when the air's pressure waves crest in the right pattern.

Their growth is responsive, coiling or releasing depending on cycles of sound and silence. Some push root-like anchors into seams of coppered dust, drawing not water but thin films of chemical reactant, the taste of ancient hands working metal and salt into the soil.

### The Social Memory

But the truest inhabitant might be emptiness made animate—the echo of those who came before.

The city was built for a purpose: perhaps ritual, perhaps shelter, perhaps simply to mark territory in a world that has little but relentless light and competing noise. Its meaning is written not in inscriptions, but in use—each low wall a suggestion of privacy, each hollow a map of seasonal gatherings. The very dust is thick with history, wrapping remains of crude tools, the fossilized pattern of people's pathways.

Sometimes, on cycles dictated by sound or solar intensity, the Not-Quite-People gather. They do not speak—speech would be vapor lost to brightness—but instead perform rituals of arrangement and alignment. They adjust stones, sweep passageways, rearrange mats and slabs precisely to shape the sonic ecology of each chamber. Each act invites a new note, a new echo, a changed resonance: a stone placed here to harvest a standing wave of brightness; a wall bent to refract a howl into something bearable; a mat woven anew to alter the balance of dust that accumulates.

Their rituals are observances, half religious, half ecological: celebration of persistence, of sound, of the thin line of survival with neither water nor shade.

### Rare Visitors

Even rarer, though, is the *outsider*—a creature less adapted, drawn by hunger or thrown by chance. These do not survive long, burning out in the brightness or going mad in the ceaseless sound. Their bones, their leavings, become another vein for creepers, another patch of settled memory in the tessellation.

### In Sum

**Who lives here?**  
Things which learned endurance; shapes half-made of light, half of dust and design. Not humans, not beasts, but inheritors of resonance and glare, as deeply part of the city’s mineral life as its salt-rooted plants or dust-shelled animals. The greatest inhabitant of all is the ancient, curated emptiness: a silence never silent, a memory never fully extinguished, a presence overwhelming in its absence.  
Within the blinding gaze of this chamber, it is emptiness—and the small, strange lives which thread through it—that rules.
