Who, or what, lives here?

Some are quick to imagine nothing: this is a landscape that conspires in silence, offers only the eked survival of lichen-moss and the sheen of mineral veins. But something does dwell here, though it is a presence not easily named, its boundaries vanishing, like those of the land itself.

Begin with the animals—if animals they are, for the word falters in this place. They are not abundant, nor do they declare themselves; their bodies have become as spare as the opportunities allowed them. The most visible—a courtesy, really, for they are little more than rumor—are sinuous and low, membranes drawn close to the bone, jointed limbs lean and held beneath a shell dulled to the colors of silt and evaporite. Their feet splay wide to distribute weight, never sinking the way hooves would; instead, they move like dropped threads, sliding silent from one tepid puddle to the next, nose low to the rock, feeling for faintest chemical traces. The plates along their backs catch what condensation they can, funneling the droplets, one by one, to thirsty, hidden mouths.

You notice, sometimes, a slit-pupil eye reflecting the blue shine from those strange mineral seams. The glance is swift, untrusting, but not fearful—they have been here longer than fear. They remember the world before change, before excess. Occasionally, when the air throbs with the subterranean machines’ mechanical hymn, they press themselves lower, almost in reverence or avoidance, for even they do not know the source or purpose of the earth’s internal industry.

And there are others, smaller—bone-pale, soft where the large go armored. These are the lattice-makers, so slight they resemble bits of thread cast up by the wind. They flourish, if flourishing counts as clinging to the leeward shadows of pitted rock faces. Here they fashion intricate, flexible nets from their own exuded strands, laying claim to the places where condensation most often forms. Minute siphons drink in the films of moisture, while other organs leech the mineral precipitates, folding the alien chemistry into their own tissues.

Sometimes, if you are still, you will witness their architecture: not nests or webs, but low, membranous sheets that tremble almost imperceptibly, tuned by vibrations felt more than heard. These webs are not only for feeding, but for message. Everything here is thin and provisional; so communication is vibration, layered by code—warning, invitation, the shifting tides of presence among those who never gather above ground.

Then, deeper, under the savannah’s skin, are the sleepers. Not animal, not plant: something inherited from a technological ancestry, perhaps, or grown from the chemistry of slow, repeated routines—a collective mind of mineral and magnetism. The machines that hum through the crust do not surface, do not reveal antennae or wheels, but they live in layered chambers only slightly denser than the stone itself. There they pulse their patterned tasks: refining, cycling, repairing, perhaps dreaming in cycles whose meaning went unremembered long ago. These are the true inhabitants, predating life’s hush, continuing on in the absence of nurture.

Once, perhaps, there were caretakers—beings, entities of sapience, who shaped the machines, set the rules, departed or diffused into the land’s memory. Now that responsibility is only baked into protocol: repair, persist, maintain equilibrium, grant no excess. If anything stirs these machines toward the surface, it is only to mark a fault line, to smooth a tumorous upheaval, to draw away a surplus of water that could unbalance the steady stasis.

It is possible, too, that the very atmosphere is alive, in a way—filled with relics of designed spores or nanostructures, perpetually harvesting moisture, modulating reflectivity, maintaining the cool mineral tang that rides every breath of wind. They are invisible, almost impossible to perceive; their work is the persistence of this peace, the endless recycling of a climate that inches toward neither drought nor flood.

And if consciousness touches any of this, it does so lightly, in syllables unspoken yet endlessly repeated by rock and rain, by the layered chug of machines beneath and the subtle wariness of bone-shelled animals above. Life here is not a struggle for supremacy, nor a rush toward disaster, but the long, unwavering patience of systems nested within systems, each reducing itself to the minimum required for persistence.

Here, at the edge of silence, the inhabitants are defined by their capacity to wait: to sustain, to observe, to vanish almost completely beneath the tyranny of balance. Those who move above the earth do so with utmost thrift, ghosts in a longer, deeper rhythm. Those below have given up sense of purpose, now content to cycle, to dream in mechanism. The mind of the world, if present, is a communal one, its heartbeat a mechanical hymn, its skin beaded with dew, its memory etched in the sifted hush between stone and slow, persistent rain.

Inhabitants, then: not people, not animals or plants as we know them, but the inheritors and maintainers of equilibrium, the keepers of stasis, beings and mechanisms whose existence makes the land itself their home, and whose home, in turn, inhabits them.
