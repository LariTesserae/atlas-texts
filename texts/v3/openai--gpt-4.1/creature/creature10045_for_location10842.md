What lives here?  
Not “who,” not in the way you would expect. The city is not a stage for actors, not a shelter for familiar mammals walking upright or an echoing frame for borrowed terrestrials. It is itself alive, but not with any beating heart you know. To begin at the largest scale:

**The City is the Great Inhabitant**

The first and final organism is the city itself—grown, accreted, never finished. It is not just architecture, but a metabolic system: feeding, breathing, shaping, expelling. Beneath its rising spires, deep within the fused ridges, are countless nodes of sensing and intention. Maybe not minds in the mammalian sense, but distributions of attention, monitoring pressure, heat, mineral flow. The city decides where to bud a tower, when to let a scaffold collapse, how to conduct its networks deeper into the heat. It dreams, if at all, in cycles of tectonic disturbance and the patterning of mineral veins.  

In many ways, smaller life is an extension of this great body—things spawned and loosed, then recollected or shed. The city’s purpose is not shelter, but emergence: an endless negotiation between order and dissolution.  

**The Patient Architects—People, Perhaps**

If there are “people,” they are not native so much as naturalized—co-evolved, annealed into the city’s pulse. What lives here must be as patient as mineral weathering, but sharp and clever in the windows when change happens. Perhaps they are long-limbed, body shapes elongated to reach across gaps or clamber between vertical shelves. Maybe they are shrouded in layered fabrics or armored with engineered glass-ceramic carapaces, channels running along their skin to gather moisture from every damp trickle, filters to distill water from the scalding air. Their hair, if any, filaments out to detect currents and vibrations, never decor but always sensor. The palette of their bodies: copper, graphite, salt-pale, where pigment protects from the ultraviolet sunslaughter.  

They are not quick; their metabolisms are slow, deliberate, keyed to the under-drum of geological tempo. They sleep in brief intervals when rain is forecast or when the lower vents thrum with the pulse of rising mineral steam. Work is never rushed—construction, maintenance, harvesting rare condensate, all is performed in long shifts measured by the slow swelling and cooling of the city’s skin.

Language is sparse, indirect—a gesture, a scribed mineral glyph, the pitch of a dropped pebble echoing off a chasm wall. Shouting would dissipate precious fluids. Instead, meaning is inferred from the pattern of movement, observed over days or weeks, like the reading of a weathering strata.

Their societies—if they are societies at all—are as distributed as the city’s own neural net: loose federations of practitioners organized around the tasks of negotiation with the living terrain, the mediation of growth and collapse. Authority emerges from age, patience, and the ability to read the city’s next becoming.

**The Mineral Fauna—Naiad Machines and Elemental Feeders**

True animals, if such a term applies, are never soft: they are glossy, faceted, adapted for the crags and vents, slow-reacting, hardy to heat and thirst. The shelled creature glimpsed in the vents may be a scavenger, scraping thin rinds of salts and rare minerals. The multi-limbed, silicone-bonded beings are not chitin or muscle, but composites—organisms bred for thermoregulation, clocked to creep or dart along energy gradients, sometimes drinking from electrical fields, sometimes harvesting volatile gases before they dissipate.  

Others hang in shadowed crevices, their bodies exhaling flickers of heat as they rest, leaching energy from thermal differences, never bothering to compete—their only predator is accident, their only fear, collapse.  

Some are ancient machineries, original to whatever hand first seeded this vertical world—drifting drones, single-minded extractors, half-feral as their tasks have blurred across epochs. They are mistaken sometimes for fauna, sometimes for environment, depending on whether they move when watched.

**The Ghosts of Vegetation—Synthetic Mycelium**

Where one expects green, there are “roots” of glass tubes and veinous filament—never entirely machine, never entirely life. These networks pulse with a slow, stubborn awareness: their only function is to gather, hold, and distribute moisture. If anything like thought flows between them, it is as slow as tectonic shifting, as difficult to parse as the memory of a starved oasis. They are tended, sometimes “gardened,” by the resident people, but also grow of their own accord, slowly thickening in promising cracks, retreating where the city’s heat browns them to dust.

**Who else?**

Perhaps, rarely, there arrive other shapes—outsiders or travelers, drawn by rumor or chance collapse, their presence smoothed away over seasons. If they come hoping for welcome and warmth, they are met only with slow acknowledgment, an offer of anchorage in the city’s patient memory, not in its embrace.

**All life here is**:  
— *Enduring,*
— *Measured,*
— *Attuned to the birth and decay of stone,*
— *Unhurried,*
— *Expert in patience and thirst.*

To dwell in a vertical city of mineral and heat is to become trace, to braid one’s purpose into the rising and eroding mass. What survives emerges by letting go of haste, finding comfort in the brief gleam of rare water, the faint echo of distant call, the satisfaction of having aligned one’s heartbeat to the mineral tempo—*the pulse that outlasts memory*.

What lives here is not what began here, nor what will always stay. What lives here is what endures verticality and learns how—by patience, by condensation, by deliberate becoming—to be neither wholly alive, nor quite stone, but a dweller in the interval.
