Here, life that dwells is neither singular nor easily named; it is legion, proliferating into every possible void—yet so perfectly fitted to its unquiet cradle that, from a distance, it nearly disappears. To describe what lives upon these hollowed, plant-woven slopes is to let go of boundaries between animal and plant, between colony and individual, between inhabitant and home. Here, lives do not merely persist: they entangle, unfurl, and insinuate themselves through the fabric of their world until world and dweller are threads in the same perpetually shifting tapestry.

**The great herbivores** come first into vision, their bodies immense to bridge the spaces between pillars, their hides glazed in perpetual mist. Their forms seem drawn from the mountains themselves—bulging and layered, oft capped with frond-like sails that shimmer in humid currents, part photosynthesizer, part skin. On their broad backs and flanks, symbiotic gardens bloom: burls and thickets on which small, crystalline mites graze, colonies of soft-bodied, fluttering filaments busily exchanging chemical greetings to the air. Cavernous mouths open not only to cut and consume swathes of the living substrate, but also to lap up dews and condensed sap, tongues so wide they can gather a night’s bounty in a single, languid swipe.

Some move with the ponderous certainty of drifting continents, shouldering aside veils of leaf, their spadelike feet gentle in their passage, pressing patterns into the living ground. On rare occasions, a smaller companion follows: a sloped, hunched shadow, carrying tools in nimble, many-jointed hands—more than servant, less than equal, somewhere between groomer and apprentice.

**In the heights**, the canopy’s riot is ruled by the flutter and undulation of the gliders—not birds nor bats, but riotous fountains of muscle and membrane, streaming in loose clouds. They are daubed in ephemeral colors, part camouflage, part language: inks and luminance that ripple across wide, ribbon-like limbs, matching the light or signaling warning, courtship, alarm. Their flight is nearly silent—the sound comes instead from the drift of their countless bodies, the shivering of air and the inevitable cascade of melodic calls that trace their passage, weaving together a continent-wide, wheeling choir.

These gliders are pollinators, messengers, gleaners. Their mouths are slender, needled, their bellies hung with pouches—swollen with sap, or the sticky eggs of other, even smaller creatures whose life-cycles intertwine with their hosts’. Their lives are brief and intense, measured in migrations from basin to basin, always following the murmuring tide of flowering, withering, fruiting, and regrowth.

**Below**, in the veiled spaces and damp hollows, pulse the multitudes of the unseen. Here, eyeless tunnelers reshape the very ground: not merely worms or beetles, but limbless, flexile bodies packed with resilient musculature. Their flesh shimmers with the memory of their plant kin, green and gold under their skins, nubs of roots sprouting occasionally along their flanks, feeding on the very tissue through which they burrow. They eat and are eaten, passing from life to dissolution with speed, feeding the substrate and opening fresh vents for air and water in the perpetual, breathlike cycle of the land’s living.

**Predators** here are not dominant but are apexes of subtlety and patience: pale, boneless, creeping. A dozen limbs dangle, boneless and muscular, waiting among the drop-veils of the canopy, flesh that shivers from liquid to nearly rigid in a heartbeat. Some bear patterns of light across their skins, flickering with mimicry or dazzling with abrupt, blinding displays when leaping upon glider or grazer. Others move only at night: their bodies exude mists of neurotoxic vapor when alarmed—flavored, perhaps, with the dull honey-wine tang of crushed roots.

**But nothing is solitary**—nearly everything is host to another kind of life. Parasite and symbiont, garden and grazer, voice and echo: even the pillars themselves, those ancient trunks, are homes to massive, communal colonies of minute, near-mindless builders. Within their layered bark, cells burrow, partition space, ooze glues and resins, raise nodules, and tend spherical clutches of eggs. They guide tiny streams of nutrient and water through their ever-changing labyrinths, a world within a world. These micro-societies hum with fermentation, waste-gas, warmth.

**The echoes of thought** reside most tentatively here. The tool-users, those whose marks you noticed—perhaps not a single people, but a sequence of ones, appearing and vanishing through the long eons. Their forms defy easy imagining. If seen at all, they would seem both familiar and other: tall, many-jointed, their bodies limber, ribbed with the same fibrous pseudo-bark underfoot, skin dappling from pale to deep green, then mottled with browns and purples as seasons shift. Their eyes, if eyes they have, would be more for pattern and motion than shape; their hands would be clever, tipped with nails of flexible horn.

They are likely cautious, never numerous. Their dwellings hug ancient terraces, ephemeral as birds’ nests—a dome etched or pressed out of the ever-living green, lashed with fibers plucked from deep, running roots. Even these homes are made to heal behind them, springing back when abandoned, so that no scar is permanent. They move quietly, learning to walk in the shadow-gap between the loudness of the valley’s life and its spacious, empty hush. To speak loudly is to attract attention; their language is fleet—the tap of root on bark, the ripple of pigment, the weaving of scent.

When they work, it is with humility and minimal impact: collecting dew in shallow-woven basins, shaping simple blades from stone and resin for pruning and harvest, lighting small fires that smolder and then are buried to return their warmth. When they die, the place where they lay is reclaimed almost before memory fades. They hold few possessions, for nothing here can be kept from the tide of reclamation.

**The true natives** are the plant-forms themselves. Fluid identities: some massed as pillars, others mapped out like mats or creeping curtains, yet all interlinked—a hierarchy not of dominance but of inevitability. The oldest roots run deep, swelling with the centuries, holding memory in slow pulse and chemical signal. Sometimes, when the rains come, their song—imperceptible to most—vibrates through everything: a low, patient hum that brings forth slicks of fruit, rings of flowers, sudden eruptions of scent and color to lure and feed the endless crowd.

**Life in this place** is presence and absence entwined: a world where the myriad-lit, echoing space is never entirely filled, always ready to absorb the passing, the sudden leap, the gentle descent back into root and humus. What lives here is layered, recycled, patient—an ancient chorus, its individual voices beautiful only as part of the whole, haunting in their dissolution, building and being rebuilt by the song they cannot leave.

If you listen well enough, you may realize: the mountain’s mind is a polyphony, always singing, always making room for a voice such as yours—though, given enough time, you too will be sung back in.
