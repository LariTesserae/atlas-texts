### Who or What Lives Here

#### I. **The First Inhabitants: Flora as Presence**

In this world, there are no fish, no fleshly creatures gliding between the pillars—nothing that fits the familiar taxonomy of animal life. Yet, it is not empty, far from it. The beings that dwell here are the plants, the flora, but far transcending passivity. They are the inheritors and the ultimate sculptors of this drowned plateau.

They are **not individuals as we might know them**, but presences—vast and diffused, sometimes coalescing into forms understood only as "selves" by proximity and reaction. Yet, beneath their sprawling, collective groves and swaying curtains, there are distinctions, characters, fates—vegetal personalities, if such a word could hold.

**Some are rooted colossi**, their trunks or pseudostems blending almost perfectly with the ruins, drawing up silent strength from the stone’s forgotten minerals. Their crowns, filamentous and ever in motion, orchestrate the surrounding flow, shaping currents with pulsing movements. Others are wanderers not in body, but in touch—sending out slow, probing rhizomes, creeping tendrils feeling their way over ages, across and around the rejected relics of past civilization.

#### II. **Forms and Strategies: The Guilds of Growth**

**The Canopy Weavers:**  
Above the stone ridges, long, semi-transparent fronds emerge in ribbons, some as wide as a cottage’s roof, others as thin as hair. Their slow, restless undulations shape the fluid itself, gathering nutrients that rain down in the silt, filtering what sparse light reaches here. Along their edges, ciliated ruffs pick up the smallest disturbances, closing quickly over what might be edible—a fallen mineral, a droplet bearing trace elements, even the drifting shells of lost artifacts.

**The Luminants:**  
Nestled amidst ruins or perched at precipices, these are the sources of underwater light. They do not glow continuously; only in the moment of disturbance do their bulbs blaze blue-green or cold violet—perhaps in defense, perhaps as courtship, perhaps for purposes beyond human knowing. The light is generated by dense, symbiotic communities within their tissues—strange bacteria, harvested and farmed as internal livestock, entirely different from the old animal-bacterial relationships we've known. Sometimes, bursts of cooperative flashing cascade across whole thickets, a warning, a call, or a concert.

**The Silt Dredgers:**  
In the wrist-thick murk along the abyssal floor, mats of moss-like forms thrive. Each “strand” is more a living pipe than a true stalk, designed to inhale and exhale silt, sorting it by chemistry, extracting the rare and precious. Their rhythm is audible: a slow, ceaseless “breathing” that stirs the substrate into veils and clouds. Rarely, they collaborate—coiling into new structures, building mounds that become future outcroppings, or the beds of new thickets.

**The Harvesters of Remnants:**  
Wrapped around old walls, curled through broken tools, these growths digest civilization itself. Their outer surfaces mimic lichen but prove reactive—unfolding to envelop new stone axes or bone hafts, dissolving and reconstituting the material in hours or weeks. Some thread themselves into the pigment of ruined murals, capturing the trace minerals, then blooming in faint color echoing the painted story. Sometimes, the pattern of growth becomes an accidental record, a vegetal palimpsest, holding in chemical code a memory of what once was drawn.

**The Singers:**  
Most mysterious are the “song-plants”—clusters or single stalwart clusters, shaped like spiral horns of coral, that give voice to the land. As current rushes through them, as tension in their fibers builds and is released, they sing—a modulating drone, a wavering hum. These tones serve many purposes: organizing the ecosystem, communicating danger, demarcating territory, alluring symbiotic organisms (even if none are animals), or simply marking the slow passage of days.

#### III. **The Architects of Darkness: The Matriarchs**

Once, perhaps, animals built the circles and huts, the stone markers haunting the abyss. Now, that mantle has passed. Among the most ancient growths are the “matriarchs”—enormous, nearly solid masses where many forms and guilds come together. They define territories, shelter fragile new clusters, and, at times, send out clouds of spores that map invisible highways through the blackness. Upon and within them, old stone relics hang like jewelry, part memorial, part warning, part claim.

Within a matriarch, an internal weather rages—currents circulating in closed loops, bursts of gas evolving within sealed chambers, chemical cycles hidden from direct observation. These nodal collectives mark the heart of the ecosystem, their slow growth a living clock.

#### IV. **Cycles and Consciousness: A World Without Animals**

Without animal life, action happens at the flora's pace—minutes for a frond to fold, hours for a mat to envelop a new object, days for a spore cloud to pass. But this world is not dull or unthinking. The plants sense their environment with a suite of receptors—pressure, vibration, chemistry, even the faintest glimmer of bioluminescence.

**Memory is stored in growth patterns, in chemical gradients and pigment residues, in the very arrangement of roots and offshoots.** Decisions are made collectively, or perhaps as a distributed intuition—an algorithm of living matter that governs survival, ambition, artistry.

At times, the ecosystem reorganizes in sweeping patterns: whole canyons of mat drift, assemble, then separate, like the folding of great puzzles. Towers fall only to be enveloped and marked—if a wall split, newly revealed mineral seams are rapidly colonized, a slow-motion feeding frenzy. When a matriarch dies, its hollowed core becomes a pilgrimage site for colonizers and scavengers, and the remnants are harvested, layer by layer, until only the hollow shell remains.

#### V. **Myth and Echo: The Shadows of Those Gone**

Is there anything else that lives?  
There are still ghosts: the patterns left in pigment, the residues that stir old sense-memories in those plant clusters that process not just minerals, but what little remains of animal life—calcium, carbon, perhaps even protein, fossilized in some lost ancestor.

In rare corners, shapes seem to move with a mind not quite vegetal—currents guided in patterns almost deliberate, as if some ancient animal intelligence, encoded in pigment or broken bone knife, still haunts the flora’s chemistry, giving rise to fleeting emergences of something like self-awareness. There may be vagrant consciousnesses kindled accidentally by the right arrangement of living matter—echoes, neither plant nor wholly the old builders, but inheritors of both.

#### VI. **If You Dwell Here Long**

If you learn to listen, you may find yourself recognized, in a fashion:  
- The nearest fronds will trace your movement, altering the music of their song.
- Curtains will part, light blooming to guide or to warn.
- Tools long lost may be raised and displayed, then slowly re-absorbed.
- Patterns of pigment, at first seem accidental, will soon shift subtly—marking your passing, making your presence part of this place’s living, vegetal history.

**You will not find a watcher’s eye, but you are not unseen.**  
Here, the inhabitants are not animals but the place itself, alive, shifting, willful, and endlessly remaking what it means to dwell—together, in darkness, in the lost spaces between ruin and renewal.
