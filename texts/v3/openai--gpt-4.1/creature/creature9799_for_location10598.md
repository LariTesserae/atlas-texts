Who—what—lives here?

Not any being in the shape, or under the delusions, of civilization as you might have known it. Here, everything *lives*—lives in a burning present tense, lashed to the heartbeat, moment by moment. This is not a land that belongs to a species, or even to a class or kingdom. Here, to “live” is an act of contest: emergence, sustenance, disappearance, all wound together, never still.

**Let’s begin at the smallest throats.**

Below the churned lattice of half-made earth, something is always dissolving—hyphae radiate outward from the scars of a recently dropped fruit or the quick-collapsed remains of a smaller animal. Fungi here are bold, invading leaf, root, and carcass alike. Some invade while the host still breathes, sending slender, glow-tipped threads to ride the pulses of its heart, feeding on living flesh before death has a chance to pause the drama. Molds puff up their fruiting bodies in hummocks of spore; some are bright, harsh colors—a dull red, a virulent blue-white—hinting at toxic arms races playing out across the loam. Even at these scales, nothing is left to itself: predatory slime molds net half-digested beetle nymphs, scenting the chemical traces of movement; ciliate jungles chase water globules spilled from a frog’s hop.

Here burrow *carrion-ants*, glassy and swollen, their touch slick as grease. They swarm any exposed drop of protein, whispering danger to all above by the sharp vinegar of their passage. Other ants wedge mud into elaborate galleries—fleeting, always repurposed when the next storm bends the earth. Their queens are short-lived, but the colonies are perpetual, reincarnated through the ceaseless movement of their own kin.

Upwards, in the thicket of roots and sodden moss, *amphibian lords* preside. Some are armored—warty, fist-sized, colored in throbbing neons—others transclucent, throated with bioluminescence, rhythmically blinking to the sound-world they inhabit. Their young swarm as wriggling, gulping shoals in the ephemeral puddles and arroyos, and many never grow old. Anything too conspicuous is swiftly noticed—by flat-headed land-fish with breathing slits along their flanks, by the stalking tongue of a *slink-bird*, whose feathers drip with dew and whose eyes, luminant, are set at odd intervals along its muzzle and throat.

Not far above, *serpents* thread themselves through air and matter. They are jawed, many are venomous, some bear appendages for gliding from trunk to trunk—loose, winglike skin that catches the briefest updraft. Some never touch earth at all, weaving lifelong circuits above ground, measuring their territory by the song-prints of the competition.

Among the branches, pulsing in the livid dark, are beings that are neither wholly animal nor plant. Spore-anointed mammals—musky, bare-skinned, slick with the oils of moss and leaf—move in bands, rarely gathering but always tangling, never at rest. Their speech, if it can be called that, is a lattice of purrs, hisses, chittering coughs—a kind of sonar pulse, navigating the world by the rebounded chaos of their own voices. Their eyes are huge and slitted, their ears tipped in flexible fronds that flutter to distinguish direction in the roaring dark. They do not build, but weave—shelter is a brief basket of leaves and vine, torn apart before it is found by rival or flood.

**In temporary dominion fly the swarmers.**

Clouds of dull-luminescent insects—beetles, crane-flies, gnats the size of thumbnails—write the living geography of each night, thickening wherever rot is freshest or sap has burst the bark. Their larvae ride out the turbulence in water-logged holes, or tunnel—fast and silent—through the march of decaying wood. In their wake follow greater hunters: skittering, bark-colored mantids; gliding bats with wings like wet paper, edge-lined in soft, living mushrooms; centipedes of outlandish length, purple-copper bodies undulating between cracks, razor limbs tapping out warning on the stone.

Nothing is alone for long in this pressure-cooker of growth. The *largest mammals* seem more rumor than fact—huge, trunkless shapes, their bodies velveted with moss and bur parasites, bearing clots of glowing fungi. They eat what they can seize; their lives are continual migration, carving trails that close behind them in hours. Stumbling, hooting, they groom themselves in the mist before plunging into another shuddering crease of the land.

**And above—the canopy, and the thick cloud.**

Here, light is the rarest currency. Anything able to reach it fights for every inch. Plants send up aerial roots, leaves bending and drooping with the weight of constant, absorbing moisture. Epiphytes make dangerous, unpredictable platforms—dense enough to hold a mid-sized predator, slick enough to betray its weight with a sudden break. Here, *predator-birds* glean lizards from bark with scissoring bills, and lizards, armored in moss and scale, leap away into shadow seconds before jaws close.

The ones who may think—if intelligence exists distinct from desperation—are, by necessity, furtive. Some, perhaps, craft tools from bone or battered stone; but such objects are plain, ephemeral, taken from and soon returned to the churn. There are no myths here, save what can be transmitted in a pattern of call and response—a warning, a claim, a quick code of presence and retreat. Any culture is a pale thing, making no permanent sign on bark or ground. They remember through noise, not mark.

**What emerges, finally, is not a single species, but an attitude—a rule of living shaped by utter immediacy.**

Everything that persists here, no matter its mold, endures through this: it must listen, must *feel* the thrum of all life around it, must move with the pressure or perish, must claim fleeting shelter and then abandon it. Life means to compete in the open, to be loud enough to be heard among a thousand voices and yet silent enough not to invite obliteration. The border between animal and plant, fungus and beast, is not so much crossed as *redrawn with every storm*.

If consciousness stirs, it is in rhythm, not in memory. If anything has a story, it is told not by written or sculpted means, but by the *response of nerves to sound and vibration*—the world as a water drum, a rolling press of signals, and each being, for a moment, its own and then lost.

To live here is to *rise and fall* in the ongoing thunder of change—a presence not of quiet mastery, but of loud, wet, undying contest—each heartbeat both music and warning, each form a temporary pattern in the tempest of abundance.
