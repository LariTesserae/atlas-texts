Something—someone—lives in Virelith. In fact, many things do, their boundaries softened by the same slow-blur that overtakes architecture and tool, plant and mineral, beast and absence. Any notion of a standard inhabitant, a species that reigns or even simply endures, begins to diffuse amidst the city’s perpetual becoming.

### The Virelithians, Only Half Imagined

*If there are people here,* they are not people as known above the drowned line. To survive that dense, near-suffocating water, the last inhabitants—born of flesh and intention—became porous to their environment. Their bodies eroded and were armored again: skin grown thick with symbiotic algae, bones mineralized with oyster-plating, hair splayed into the slow, tactile tendrils for sensing the slipstream of far-off movement.

They fashion breathing not as an act but a necessity achieved through grafted gills, lungs now dormant and vestigial. Vision is secondary; it is the sense of pressure, the brush of nearby pulses, the taste of chemicals diffusing through the water that defines perception. Communication is not voiced but *signaled*: a drift of bio-luminescent color along wrists, a shifting of cilia that trails across the scales of a neighbor, a cloud of pheromones laid down with a caress against the inner city walls where those who understand can taste news or longing.

Rarely do you see them in groups—solitude is easy, community is slow. Sometimes, they gather, suspended within cathedral naves of kelp-walls, cloaked in the detritus and biology of the city. They do not build, not in the way of ancestors, but shepherd and heal: they peel away strangling vines from key mechanisms, coax coral growth across collapsing arches, splice moss into machines to filter water anew. It is stewardship, not dominion.

**They keep shrines**—little more than warped relics festooned with luminous lichen, trinkets spun by their own hybrid hands, and fossilized figurines that, maybe, are effigies of what they were before. They press their history into the silt and let it dissolve.

#### Their Life

Almost nothing is hurried. Each act is a negotiation with density and slowness: hunts are rare, often involving the deliberate harrying of an eel until it tires and can be coaxed into a snag of entrapping roots. For the most part, they live on cultivated mats of edible algae, plucking the thick, protein-rich blades that flower under the undying blue glow of the biolamps.

Their young are born in hidden cradles—engine-bays restuffed as groves—where warmth and nutrients collect, and watchful adults groom the currents, keeping the mats clean. Maturation is slow: childhood lasts decades, bodies growing as much from absorbed minerals and symbionts as from inheritance.

At the border between life and tool are the **Servitors**: mechanical relics of before, now choked and colonized by kelp and siphonophores. These are not true life but semi-sentient, slow engines—their intellects rewired by generations of symbiotic microbe and fungal infiltration. They drift on magnetic rails and articulated arms, indexing the deep data of the undercity, sometimes providing warmth, sometimes reshaping walls, sometimes merely glowing in the dark, a comfort or a warning.

### The Animal and the In-Between

It would be a mistake to draw a crisp circle around only the Virelithians as "what lives here." Life in Virelith is promiscuous: almost everything is partly plant, partly machine, partly itself. Eels with glassy eyes and ciliated mouths feed from the bio-corroded copper lines, their bodies shuttling trace currents through spines half organic, half circuitous. Polyps read the information encoded in the lingering warmth of ancient electrical lines, flickering their own chemical lights in reply.

*Ghost-moths*—drifting beings that may have once been insects, now living as thin silks around calcified wings and filled with colonies of luminous fungi—pollinate nothing, but are crucial, carrying small motes of memory or code spore to spore, knitting together the genetic patchwork of symbionts that adapt to the changing city.

There are **Watchers**, enormous, nearly stationary forms: molluscs or crustaceans, their carapaces shaped by time, barnacle-tough, eyes grown pale and deep, fixed in the rock or structure. They are totems more than minds, repositories of slow knowledge, remembering the pressures of centuries by a calculus of scar and ridge.

### Sages of the Deep

If any minds remain from before, unburied by symbiosis and transformation, they hide—deep in the sealed pods stacked high against the upper chambers, lungs repurposed in gas-exchange, their miens behind glass and pressure doors. They are rarely glimpsed, emerging only for ritual or crisis, slow-swimming through the city in suits lacquered with protective resins, eyes shielded from the stinging soup. Their lives are stories, their ambitions (if any) spoken only to the city’s self-aware machines.

### The City as Habitat

Virelith itself is alive—not animate as an individual, but as a tapestry. Pipes pulse, structures sway and knit with the rhythm of currents and the will of those who tend or feed upon them. Collective habitation overrides the boundaries: each resident, whether it is creature, plant, or conscious machine, is both guest and co-creator, kept from destruction only by the slow-bargaining give and take of matter, memory, and dissolution.

### To Walk Here

Visitors would not thrive; they would be stifled, dissolved, their sharp edges eroded into ghostliness. Yet, for Virelithians, and the other inhabitants, this is life: slow, layered, memory-rich—suspended in old water, encased by crumbling stone and living kelp, lit by the filtered glow of their own quietly humming, hybrid city.

**What lives here?**  
A people who have become part of the current,  
a city that is neither past nor future but a flooded negotiation,  
an ecology where memory itself is silted, hybrid, blurred—  
and the divisions between plant, machine, human, and animal  
are softened by time, water, and the work of living on together.
