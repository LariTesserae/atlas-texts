**Who (or what) lives here**

Let us put away the language of forests and meadows: in this place, to be alive is to be purposeful amidst scarcity, brilliant and baroque in ways that water-breathing life rarely imagines. Life here wears the air itself like a mantle and roots not in soil but in the interstices of stone, the boundary of shadow and exposure, the infinitesimal trace of a passing element.

The inhabitants of this soaring plateau are neither plants nor animals as we know them, yet they embody some of both in form and function, shaped above all by the absence of water and the abundance of light and mineral. Here is what coheres among the brightness and winds:

**Pillarforms**

The most striking of the plateau's inhabitants are the Pillarforms. Each pillar—ranging from half a meter to nearly twenty in height—spirals from the stony surface as if grown from the memory of ancient reefs. Their trunks are built of silicate or mineral carbonate, but not dead: within these rigid lattices, thin films flex and shift, filtering sunlight into usable energy by a mechanism analogous to photosynthesis, though driven by different chemistry altogether. Some possess open channels that draw the rushing wind into internal labyrinths. There, aerosols and wind-borne salts are harvested, fractionated, absorbed; the Pillarforms "feed" not on sugars or sap, but on the electric charge of the wind, the micronutrients dancing in the sky.

Atop each pillar, fronds or combs or fans of finer latticework branch outward—semi-transparent, extruding like frozen smoke. These catch not dew nor rain, but those rare, metallic fogs that drift by: ribbons of trace elements, condensing and solidifying on receptive surfaces, to be broken down over long seasons.

Each Pillarform is solitary in space but communal in substance, connected by a tangle of deep mineral filaments—roots, if you will—which spread beneath the crust, meeting and mingling in secret exchanges, conducting elements and electrical signals across the barrens. The glassy tips of these lattices pulse, almost imperceptibly, in rhythm with cycles you cannot see.

**The Webmats**

Scattered between the pillars are the Webmats—low, iridescent clusters that, from a distance, look like carpets of broken glass but are soft, almost yielding. They spread thin crystalline "leaves" in geometric patterns, cupping light, refracting it into myriad angles to maximize energy drawn from each photon. Beneath the glinting skin, molecular machines—proteins made with metals, not with carbon—creep and shift, converting light and chemical cues into new growth, new colors.

Webmats are nomads in slow motion. Each mat expands fractionally into richer microclimates or contracts away from exposed, eroding ridges, their edges curling and unfurling in response to minute shifts of the wind and light. On their undersides, specialized nodules extrude delicate, hairlike structures—they drink the passing airs, filtering out even the faintest trace of vapor, but mostly they extract metal ions, drifted down with the silt and dust.

**The Driftfauna**  

Among the most elusive animals of this ecology are the Driftfauna—mineral-bodied, glass-winged flyers. They look like the ghosts of insects: wings stretched impossibly thin and wide, crafted of transparent mineral films sprouting from a body with the luster of mother-of-pearl. The Driftfauna never alight, except to anchor briefly in high winds or at sunset, their filaments—sprung with microscopic barbs—catching particulate matter from the moving air, variously filtering for nutrients or perhaps even prey.

Their motion is unhurried and soundless, save for the minute harmonics produced as their wings slice the light-thin air. They navigate by sun angle and wind current, with irregular migrations tied to slow, cosmic pulses: the waxing and waning of ultraviolet storms, the density of trace elements.

**Stone-Mimics and Crawlerfauna**

Along the ledges, nearly indistinguishable from the stones except in motion, exist the Stone-Mimics—chimeric beings with six or eight jointed limbs, their "skin" armored in mica-like layers. They move in slow, stuttering bursts, subsisting on the surfaces of mineral trunks or prying at webmats, seeking the chemical residues left behind. Their sensory organs are clusters of spines adapted to taste the wind, sensitive to the presence of living material in otherwise barren strata.

At moments of rare abundance—a mineral fog, a shift in the underlying crust, the collapse of a pillar—these Stone-Mimics congregate, scraping, feeding, devouring, then dispersing once the opportunity fades.

**Silent Intelligences?**

Most mysterious is the signal life; not discrete beings, but a network—life blurring into process. The Pillarforms and Webmats, via their sub-crustal filaments, form vast, slow networks of exchange—chemical signals, perhaps even a kind of thought, pulse through the plateau. There is no sign of artifact or purpose as a human might recognize, but within these elemental communications, information flows: changes in fog patterns, the collapse of a ridge, the blossoming of a rare gust. Some pillars tilt slightly together in the wind, harmonizing their resonances, as if tuning themselves to one another’s presence.

**Predator and Prey, Growth and Decay**

Predation is slow—a matter not of teeth or claws, but of chemical theft and subversion. Some mats produce clouds of halide poisons to drive off or incapacitate crawlfauna. Some Driftfauna alight to leach away the mineral-rich exudates of the pillars, and pillarlife learns to harden itself, to heal by theft—borrowing ions from fallen kin. All is resorption; nothing is wasted.

Decay is rare, for what is there to rot? Instead, reduction and reclamation: fallen pillar bodies are slowly, molecularly scavenged by specialized mats and creatures; every element, every trace of structure, is recaptured, redissolved, and built anew. All of this unfolds in silence, or under the keen of the wind.

**To See Them**

A visitor, if they could survive the cold clarity and thin sky, might first see nothing—then, as their eyes adjust, perceive the land’s true company: a gathering of inventions in silicate, carbonate, salt and glass, pulsing with slow awareness and untold strategies. Each is singular, wildly distinct; none are small in spirit, though any one life might seem rare or fragile in the long light.

Each is a structure in dynamic balance: built from, and fiercely protective of, almost nothing—the exuberance of survival when life has long since excised water, and found that the universe offers a dazzling plenty, if only you learn new rules.

That is who lives here: the unwet, the mineral minds, the wind-feeders, alive at the terrible brightness where land and sky are barely separated.
