Who—if that is the right word—lives here?

Nothing is quite at home upon the vast wet stone. No warm bodies hurry from nook to nook; no commotion betrays habitation. Yet to say the place is empty is to misunderstand what it means to live at this altitude, in this slow, enduring equilibrium.

What persists, then? 

At first glance: only the tough riband of mosses that gather where rock cups water. Gray-green, tarnished bronze, ochre-brown—they collect the centuries on their backs. These are the first inhabitants: pioneers of patience, colonists of the marginal. To be here, a moss or a lichen must harden its inner workings against the freeze, the dry spells, the wind-misted gusts that stride the open plateau. They anchor themselves with clutching rhizoids, drink from condensation, parcel out their energy—enduring, stunted, improbable flames of persistent life.

Interleaved with the mosses are thicker mats, almost iridescent, of crustose lichen. Their lives unfold over decades, even centuries, roughening the stone, dulling its colors, replacing deft movement with the inexorable growth of chemistry and tolerance. Some lichen form patterns, sporadic runes in black, sunken glyphs of rust, hierarchies of pale crust mapped into the old stone like the faded paint on a sacrificial altar. In places, where water trickles more freely, slick mosaics of algae and mold form the lowest tier—a greening film, a living varnish. 

But look closer. Inside the soil, that cool, patient sponge, the bulk of being dwells. Here, the nematodes curl and uncurl in their ooze, blind and ancient, slipping between granules of silt, weaving through microscopic mazes. Mites, beetle larvae, the ghosts of old rot—the soft-bellied and patient—are the architects of the dirt. If there is a civilization here, it is built in darkness, every chamber measured in pinprick spaces, every migration undertaken beneath the threshold of daylight.

And sometimes—though seldom—the surface is crossed by tenuous messengers. A beetle with opal shell, slick with dew, plots a ponderous course, its life measured in slow, determined lines. Overhead, a lone bird sweeps with the wind; no melody marks its flight, only the occasional scrape of primary feathers. Perhaps it is a migrant—never nesting, only wayfaring on the updrafts, a traveler between worlds who knows the high sky as a corridor, the plateau as a waypoint. Its presence is provisional, a rumor of animal life in a world with little need for movement or noise.

Under an overhang, there may be more—a ring of delicate filaments, the spun tent of a hunting spider, built for dew’s weight, not for dryness. Its labor is nearly invisible, a geometry of patience, yet even this predator depends on rare passersby—a flying insect adrift from richer biomes below. The cycles are long, the catchings rare; hunger is less a drama than a condition.

What of the stones themselves? Some here would say the landscape is the resident—the vertebrae of the world made manifest. Boulders settle, but never quite; ridges align in patterns half-recognized, old as myth. Misting in and out, the veiled atmosphere serves as both wall and window, sometimes exposing in swirling light the suggestion of form—a face in a rock, a corridor between ridges, a pseudo-doorway in the edge of a slab. If meaning is not placed by human hand, perhaps it is sedimented into the bones of the land, alive in its memory of water and wind.

No creature here is wasteful. Each enduring organism is home to ten thousand others, sheltering, cycling, consuming, or simply abiding. The world’s metabolism is measured in eons; the assemblage of mosses, lichens, root-bound soil fauna, and silent beetles—this is the slow congregation, the collective heartbeat of the plateau.

Nothing here rules, and nothing is out of place. Life does not flock or nest; it abides, spreads, endures, and is reabsorbed. The plateau is lived-in by what can withstand long, expectant waiting. There are no secrets—but secrets are endlessly kept. The truest resident is persistence itself, clad in moss-pelt, root-filament, shell, and the cool patience of stone. 

Place your hand upon the rock; mist settles, water beads, a beetle moves beneath your palm. Listen. What lives here is time. What lives here is everything that endures the long pause between weathers, and calls that pause its only necessary home.
