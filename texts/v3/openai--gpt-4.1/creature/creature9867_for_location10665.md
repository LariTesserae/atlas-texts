Who—or what—lives at Blackwater High?

Here, the notion of a singular, central inhabitant falls away, as easily and finally as a drop of sweat into the black marsh. Life here is an ecology dressed in sorcery and rust, a pageant without spotlight or master of ceremonies. Those who live here are not visitors or owners, but extensions of the plateau’s own gnawing, growing, churning impulse. They are neither merely organic nor wholly machine nor strictly mineral, but things complicated by centuries of entanglement—between biology, industry, debris, and darkness.

#### The Benthic Houses

First are the sprawling, colony-beings: the Benthic Houses. They are not houses in the human sense, nor are they simply organisms; they are mobile, centuries-old conglomerates, each a tangled cube or dome of moss-thickened industrial relics, kelpy fungal mass, and whirring energy collectors. Sometimes they glide imperceptibly atop sodden soils, trailing feeder roots and siphons; sometimes they lock themselves unmoving for years, fusing deeper into the ground. Their “limbs” are cable-cored limb-thickets, slow and methodical, used not for rapid movement but for reshaping the environment, hauling up rocks or wringing nutrients from the flooded dark.

The Benthic Houses host a thousand sub-creatures within: luminous filter-beasts, microphage bats, clusters of spiderlike mechanics called *gleamlets* that repair or sabotage the machinery spun between organic tissues. At night (which is every moment), clouds of musty, warm vapor rise from their spongy backs, leaking complex chemistries that signal to kin across kilometers.

#### The Blackwater Grazers

Below the wide fronds and spired factories walk the Blackwater Grazers—massive, low-slung, sometimes six- or eight-legged, but always patient, numb to rushing rain. Fur replaced by cascading veils of mycelial scrim or sheets of armored, self-repairing lichen, they plow through pools, their jaws haloed with phosphorescence as they uproot and digest whole biomes in a night. Their breath is slow and deep; it’s said that, across the basin, you can follow their migrations by the scent of sweet rot and their low, electric groans.

The grazers are seldom alone. They move in clusters, but buried in their vast pelts and beneath their armored shells dwell companion-fauna: slinking hydroparasites with rubbery eyes; electrical fish that lurk in the puddles their passage leaves behind; flickering, cyanon-blue beetles that signal to each other with coded sparks drawn from the grazers’ living skin.

#### The Sojourners

Amidst the wrecks and platforms, sentient forms move with quick intent. They could be thought of as the closest thing to “people” here, but that is a stretch: their origins are indistinct. Some resemble upright bipeds, their forms shaped more by function than by any evolutionary whim. Limbs are jointed to fold like cranes or spring like grasshoppers, encrusted with low-glow panels, cable-muscles threaded through with bioengineered kelp. Eyes are membranes or multifaceted circuits, mouths seldom needed.

The Sojourners are scavengers, engineers, ritualists of repair. They are more comfortable than others in the presence of machinery, keeping the oldest engines from collapsing into the peat, negotiating with the Benthic Houses for passage or with Grazers for mutual warning. Whether they are direct descendants of Blackwater’s ancient travelers or have only lately coalesced from drifting root-minds, it is impossible to say. They speak in radio, glow-script, and the odd churning drumbeat tapped out on tank walls.

#### The Spoor

Not all who live here are seen. The Spoor are everywhere and nowhere: clouds of motes, wet-filamentous mesh wandering under the moss, and threads of living plasma that dart in the upper atmosphere. The Spoor are the nervous system and memory of the plateau, knitted between roots and ruins. They are gossip, disease, warning, and sometimes voice—possessing the vocal works of whatever beast or engine they desire. They trade secrets in bursts of scent or low, tectonic tremors, deciding in mass whether to surge up and consume a machine or sink away and let it lie.

#### The Predatory Swarms

Silence is not safety here. When the night grows tense and too many small lives cluster, the swarms cross the marshes: flocks of membranous, oil-slicked fliers, descending not only to feed, but also to prune, to balance—part executioner, part gardener. Their bodies are patchworks of old metallic netting and living membrane, with cilia that hum when they dive, scattering static or siphoning bioluminescence from the wrong-colored corpses they leave behind. They are the plateau’s rough courtesy to overgrowth; not evil, not kind, but necessary.

#### The Tower Shepherds

Few and mostly unapproachable, the Tower Shepherds tend the ancient industrial spires—whether they built them is now irrelevant. These creatures are gangly, towering, draped in veils of aerated lichen and trailing ribbons of probe-tendrils lit by teal pulses. Legs like twisted stilts, heads assembled from clusters of lens and radar dish, they walk in circles around still-standing process towers, tuning their old humming hearts, balancing flows of fluid and charge between earth and loft.

They are the keepers of the highest, driest ground, though dryness is never quite achieved. Their presence stabilizes and archives, never dominates; their motives are as slow as climate, as atavistic as basalt.

#### The Microbial Courts

Finally, everywhere, in every droplet and sodden clod, rule the Microbial Courts: self-organizing republics of bacteria, archaea, and virus-phage, who remake the world one exudate at a time. Some cluster around batteries and pipes, drawing power or forming bioluminescent veils that signal to larger animals. Others work inside the living bodies of grazers, or in the cooling thickets of factories, determining with chemical vote which bits of detritus will persist and which will dissolve.

---

All these (and the thousand minor syndicates—root-gnawers, light-thieves, scavenger-flocks and symbiote fungi) are only ever propositions of form, not finished destinies. To live at Blackwater High is never to be only flesh or only steel, only hunter or only home, but to be at once host and guest in a world that chews, rebuilds, and invents. *They*—the plateau’s people—are continuous invention, fluid symbiosis: systems nested in systems, never fully apart, never finally one.

You will not see them arrive; you will seldom see them leave. They are not shadows in the dark, but the dark itself, thickening and sprouting, humming with purpose, always, in a place that denies the simple outline of life yet brims with it to bursting.
