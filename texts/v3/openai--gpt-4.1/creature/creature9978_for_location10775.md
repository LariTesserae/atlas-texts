Who, or what, lives here? Life is not absent, but its form is not eager or abundant; whatever dwells does so in accord with the mineral, not in opposition or even negotiation. The inhabitants are defined by the logic of the place—by patience, translucency, presence without ambition.

**The Oldest Inhabitant:**  
Begin with what is most nearly alive: an entity slow-breathing as stone, its patience a local law. It is neither animal nor plant, but calls to both—something with a body more seep than flesh, its boundaries ambiguous, like a clump of gelatinous mica. It roots into stone not with claws or feet, but by the suction and adhesion of its underlayer: a slippery interface of mineral deposit and living membrane, breathless and steady. Its color, an unintrusive translucence, is given by the minerals it sips—sometimes ochre, sometimes milky or iron-streaked. It does not hunt, nor feed, so much as dissolve, filter, and settle: as the warm water beads trickle down surfaces, it absorbs certain ions and exudes its own slow trickle, a mucus-sheen that becomes the very nodules and blooms profuse in every basin. The precipitate nubs, like dense mineral polyp colonies, are its children and its remains, indistinguishable.

**Peripheral Fauna:**  
There are those that move, rare and slender, easier to imagine than to witness. Amphipod-like crustaceans, semi-soft and colorless, with exoskeletons more pitted than plated, skitter along the interfaces of pool and air. Their antennae barely quiver in the sullen warmth; their gait is cautious, offended by haste. They sift for what miniscule crumbs loosen from the mineral-eater's outermost edges—soft slough, microcrystals. Their ancestors may have known the open water beyond, but these are home only in their crowded, hushful lanes.

Once, maybe, something larger nested here—suggested by the working of horn and bone in the silt—but if so, it is long gone. The remains point to a presence careful, tool-using but by necessity rather than invention: scavenger or gatherer, moving stones, scraping, shaping. The hand that made bone into a bowl did so without fire; perhaps not a true hand, but a deft, careful limb, articulated for the patient work of grit against grit. A being with eyes unused to daylight, skin slick or scaled for perpetual moisture, patience in limb and thought, a way of life shaped entirely by what can be scratched, abraded, or worn away.

**The Echo of Others:**  
Perhaps beneath the crusted arches and in the tight stone clefts, clusters of even more patient organisms reside—bacteria, perhaps, colonies evolving at the pace of geology. Their structures are never seen, but elsewhere have been called stromatolites or microbial mats, here indistinct amidst the slick mineral tapestry. Their contribution is crucial and invisible: a ghost-life that mediates the slow passage of water and stone, writing messages in a chemistry too subtle for human nerves.

**Ghosts and Greater Presences:**  
In a world so full, so measured, there is no room for bold spirits. If there is a keeper of this realm, it is not a thing of volition: the hollow itself, with its measured exhalations and careful curtains of condensation, is the closest to a consciousness that matters here. Every surface, every bowl, every nodular growth and channel—these are its senses, its vocabulary. The single amphipod, the rare passage of a mineral-shaper, the drift of a lost bowl: these are events, but not enough to gather a story into narrative. In such density, nothing collects into drama; instead, presences aggregate into the hush of ongoingness, the fullness after striving has ebbed.

**What passes for lifeways:**  
To exist here is to filter, to settle, to erode or to be eroded. To grow into the mineral bloom and then fracture, to scrape with horn or bone, to slide on surfaces always gently moist, to move (if at all) in the thin boundary between water, stone, and murky air. Thought, if it occurs, must be as slow as drip and as persistent as mineral accretion. Hunger is almost unknown; want is blunted by the caloric poverty and the sultry equilibrium of the place. Birth and death are not events but diffusions—by the time a crustacean dies, its substance is already smeared into the silt, already claimed by whatever can use it.

In the end, whoever or whatever lives here—whether membrane-bodied mineral-browsers, bone-tool shapers of obliterated lineages, patient crustaceans, or unseen microbial webs—has no ambition beyond presence and survival. To live here is not to shape the world, but to fill it; not to strive, but to exist so quietly, so completely, that presence is indistinguishable from place. Life here is the faintest shimmer of change in a chamber consecrated to fullness and hush.
