Here, on this high plateau worn by the violence of vanished eras, nothing *lives* in the way lowlands might imagine. There are inhabitants, yes—countless—but what persists here has abandoned all extravagance, all gesture, all adornment. To live is to become necessity's shape.

Who lives here? Begin at the smallest scale. Across every meter of those sun-paled stones, in every networked fissure and cup, the unseen majority move: limbs jointed and faceted, torsos inset with organs that glean moisture like slow lanterns from the thinnest bead. Form follows only what the world allows. Most are neither wholly insects, nor wholly anything remembered—lineages stretched and specialized through millions of years of attrition. Imagine, if you can, bodies glinting via translucent carapaces, mirroring the stone’s muted reds and yellows. Some are armored like scutes of old, ghostly trilobites mapped across a living crust; others resemble threads or beads strung together, so fine they flicker in and out of sight between the cracks.

Within the shadow of every rock, colonies of pale, eyeless things—half gnat, half worm—pulse their bodies through slickened tunnels, feeding on the memory of rains. For them, sunlight is a rumor: their world is constellations of damp, a logic of touch, a life spent sifting silts that collect in shielded gloom. When storms come, they rise as a translucent tide, feeding, mating, dying, vanishing again as soon as the stone bakes clean.

Close by, in a more visible register, come the arrangers—the organizers of the plateau’s living order. They go on paired limbs or triplets of paddling feet, some dragging chitinous cases behind, others bristling with feathery sensors—perhaps the only surplus allowed, for tracing invisible currents of water and chemical, following the minute changes that spell life or evaporation. Hierarchies assert themselves in their density, not their dominance: the large only fractionally outsizing the minute, the space between forms never wasted. Each creature is adapted not to compete but to fit—a tessellation of need pressed into the land.

The “birds,” if such, are a field unto themselves. They move in legions, smudging the division between individual and flock: dull-plumed, hunched, their wings seeming more limbs than sails, not used for flight but for swift, marathon skating along the ground. Their eyes are clouded, not with blindness but with some adjustment to the glaring, attenuated sun; they see by contrast, perhaps by heat. When they move, it is all-at-once, as though the land itself has chosen to ripple. Their songs are gone, traded for silence; breath and feather hush the only hints of them, even as their numbers render them unavoidable.

Turn a stone, and see hidden undercurrents: amphibians with skins like pearl and cinder, emerging in the vapor-rich minutes after rain. Their heartbeats slow between bouts of experience; most years, most days, they are as stone, the spirit of endurance made flesh. Their eyes are black, round, always alert—each event cherished, for each is rare.

Between the stones, in shallow sanded hollows, tiny mammals might slip—if they are mammals at all. Their fur is sparse, almost colorless, their bodies stretched and sinuous, more mouse than rat, more ghost than mouse. Their world is a lattice of scent and memory, passageways mapped over generations between the shadows of shrub and stone. Above them, small predators—tenuous, cautious, always near the margin—scout for sudden movement. None are large; even the apex here might fit in a cupped hand.

No single intelligence rules this place, nor any one form. All that lives is deeply contingent, a function of what remains after eons of subtraction. The ruins—those old, weatherworn marks—hint that once there may have been different claimants: a mind, possibly, that stacked stone and sank posts for shelter or ritual or record-keeping. If such a people survived, their signature endures only in the habits of what remains. Perhaps some organisms are descended from their companions: a domesticated pollinator returned to wildness, a scavenger’s child adapted for different refuse, a seed-disperser now living by tooth and claw. The ruins offer interruptions, not explanations.

What ties all these lives together is the economy of presence; nothing is superfluous. Survival is not a solitary ordeal but a shared tension—the crowding of need against matter, each form pressing forward only as far as the others recede. Even the shrubs, those wizened, miniature stalwarts, shelter within their roots colonies of insects and lichen, providing the barest oasis, community built into scarcity.

Above it all, the invisible. Bacteria and spores, seeds and larvae ride the currents that skim the stone—each breath of air thick not with dust, but with the silent passage of the next multitude. In this place, life’s ambition is humility: to take form that fits, to return at death everything loaned, to vanish with minimal mark.

Here, the inhabitant is not an individual or a species. What lives on the plateau is a throng, an intricate, ongoing resolution; a chorus of the persistent, bound together by mutual forbearance, each life the answer to its neighbor’s presence, every need pared down until only necessity remains. They are the memory of ecosystems polished to their limit, the last articulation of coexistence—patient, muffled, and absolute.
