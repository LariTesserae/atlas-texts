Somewhere within this plateau—where ground never dries, where water and green blur the mark of decay and birth—a particular kind of being lives, though it may resist being called “life,” at least in the typical warm-blooded, quick-moving, vertebrate sense. The plateau’s standard rhythm, after all, is not one of flight or suddenness, but the long swelling drone of growing things, the enthronement of root and wetness, a landscape ruled by what might seem nearly inanimate if you have only known forests and fields.

The chief inhabitants here are not housed within, or alongside, this living substrate, but are utterly of it. The landscape’s dominant mind, if it may be called mind, is collective; it is the root-mat itself and the high columns of composite trees—its awareness, if it exists, is lichen-slow and moss-deep, a thick percolation of sense through hydraulics, pressure, and the tense groping of tendrils into abandoned metal and decaying wood. Across the oldest spans of the plateau, where even the tram rails bow with the weight of green, this being is old and knowing, curled thick around pools and dead machinery. In the plateau’s newer stretches—where water hardly yet cuts a furrow—the being is tentative, younger, exploring: little shags of sphagnum sending out exploratory runners, curious creepers spilling across the frost-shocked bones of old walkways.

There is no single creature, but rather billions of colonies and cords. Every stretch of silver-threaded moss living on the husk of a machine is not alone; it is networked, joined by root-grafts and filaments, and every living root-buttress can send and receive slow, chemical impulses. At the merging of fog and ground, these impulses are the plateau’s thinking: not a rapid intellect, but the metabolizing of memory—the slow, purposeful forgetting of what was left behind, the deep recall of every rainfall, every shifting stone, every armature swallowed into the deepening green.

And yet, other things live here as well, albeit as exiles and interlopers; their domain is the margin and the brief, the interstice and the faint echo.

Some are *the walkers*: disjointed silhouettes moving across distant, mist-licked clearings, as if testing the ground. They are not mammalian—more likely, broad-bodied and low, kin to newts or salamanders grown massive and slow, skin glossy in the wet. Their limbs are nothing built for flight or speed but for grip: broad, splayed fingers that press into the root-moss, clinging and pulling. They rise to breathe in the open draughts and vanish to poolside shadows when the static hush of whitewater grows too wild. Their eyes, if they show, have that mineral sheen—from centuries of clouded brightness. They do not rule here, nor do they threaten; theirs is the patient existence of scavengers and mutualists, feeding on rot and parasites, trading movement for the privilege of being here at all.

*Above* there are the rare fliers, each one more rumor than avian certainty. Once, long ago, wires and powerlines must have mapped sense across the voids, offered routes for the wind’s messengers. Now, metallic vines and living bridges host only the rarest of gliders. These are not birds but something more ambiguous—perhaps creatures adapted from the local insects. Giant, slow-moving, with veined wings and narrow, dangling bodies: they do not buzz so much as float, wings catching on mist and drafting for ages before settling to feed on the rich mantle of lichen or the strange, sweet blooms that emerge from moss on days when the clouds part. Their presence is felt more than known: a shadow passing, a tremor in the aerial root-bridges, a sigh of shifting weight, as careful and as ancient as the processions of slow clouds.

Inside the watery hollows, where rivulets sink to silent under-mats, something even more alien moves: the *substrate engineers*. They are neither fish nor worm, but a blending, elongated things that wind through roots and silt, feasting slowly on the detritus washed down from heights. Their existence shapes the plateau nearly as much as the plants—they string together fine threads of living sludge, aerate the pools, deliver nutrients across the thick tissue of growth. The plants accept them, even require them. Their “faces,” if that word applies, are little more than blunt snouts, ringed with feathery sense-organs to taste water, spoor, old iron, and fresh plant waste. At times, you will see a lumpy movement just beneath the transparent pond, and feel, rather than hear, the stuttering pulse of their low song—a pressure-wave, informing one another of mineral, or decay, or the faint approach of larger things.

In every gap and seam—where the industry came once, and now is denied—you’ll find lesser companions: colonies of shining, translucent ants that feed on the metallic corrosion and the runaway fungi, their paths glinting like threads of light across rusted boilers. On dead heights, patient fungi burst in silent architectures, parsing what the moss cannot, making way for further reclamation. At night, if the choking light of the plateau relaxes even fractionally, phosphorescent beetles wander to the water’s edge, illuminating new growth with the dim hope of future fertility.

And yes, sometimes, rarely, **something human lingers**—not in sight, but in the shadow language of what was left behind: a stone arranged into a seat, a wire bent into the shape of a hook, the dim scuff of rubber where some surveyor’s boot last pressed and then was lost to the rising green. If so, these human echoes dwell as ghosts do, neither welcomed nor unwelcome, simply recorded and subsumed, folded into the patient, unending memory of the plateau.

So who or what lives here? Everything that can wait—everything that can dissolve its will into the tempo of being grown over and watered through. To live here is not to own or guard, but to participate: to help digest, to help remember, to help transform, by slow accretion or compliant decay, the sharp relics of what was into the blurring, vital present. Here, there is no single ruler, no lone animal monarch. The land’s vast network of green and water is its own community—a thousand-year tide of reaching, feeding, claiming, reclaiming. Every thing that endures here learns a way of lasting without resisting: learning how to be, in a brightness that softens shape and erases shadow, part of the plateau’s slow, unfinishable dawn.
