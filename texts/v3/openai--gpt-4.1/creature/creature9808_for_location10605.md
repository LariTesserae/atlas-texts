Whatever lives here—*whoever*—cannot be named by known lineage or divided by kingdom or phylum. The entities of this high, shifting plateau are not settled into singular identities but belong to a total, ongoing act of emergence, permutation, and dissolution.

**THE COLONY-BEASTS:**
First, there are the colony-beasts, living architecture clad in pale, carapaced armor. Their bodies are segmented, modular—more bridge than creature—enormous vertebrate-arthropod hybrids that glide along living causeways with glistering underbellies and rootlike limbs. Their purpose is ambiguous: sometimes bridges, sometimes predators. As they move, their backs burst with gardenlike growths—moss, fungus, fluted leaves—that seem both parasite and symbiont. Some sprout dangling cilia, catching the mist's pollen, drawing in pale light. Every so often, when too many gather, they briefly meld—interlocking, exchanging parts, memories perhaps, and then wrench apart again, shuddering. Their eyes, if such things can be called eyes, are amber windows sunk deep in barklike skulls, glowing softly with a cold intelligence.

**THE CLOUD-SPORE KINDRED:**
Suspended in the thick, glowing mist are the cloud-spore kindred. Not quite insects, not quite air-jellies, not quite plants—these beings are composed of woven cellulose strands and chitinous plates, drifting in schools and spirals through the haze. They pulse with faint patterns of light, communicating in bursts of electrical pulse or pollen-plume, coalescing sometimes into quasi-humanoid aggregates, then dissolving again into a thousand drifting shapes. They move through the netted ceiling, caressing the lichen lattice with long, flagellated tendrils, sometimes forming singing curtains that broadcast a driving, binary counterpoint to the world’s deeper hum.

**THE VIGIL-ROOTS:**
Amid the pillars, some rooted things are not mere plants. These are the vigil-roots: sentient, or something like it. Their bark splits to form fissures that open and shut with slow, deliberative inhalations of mist. Within, semi-iridescent tissues pulse—neuronal lichen, perhaps, synapsed to the world. These roots sometimes *move*, though not to walk—rather, they re-anchor, ever shifting, their root-tips burrowing to find warmth or water, to avoid or attract the slow predators above. Some are like waiting traps, their flower mouths yawning wide to swallow drifting sap-beasts or hissing at any bold life that lingers too long.

**THE MEMORY-THIEVES:**
Flickering at the very threshold of reality: the memory-thieves. Not quite solid, not quite spectral—they are apparitions shaped crudely like women, children, beasts, their features half-remembered, always shifting. They emerge when the air thickens, drawing warmth from breathing things, leaving a quicksilver chill. They slide their thin, translucent hands into roots, bark, flesh, and—sometimes—steal moments: a memory, the flavor of a berry, the sound of a scream. When the landscape recalibrates, when time slips visibly, it is often their work—a feeding, or a defense, or merely the expression of an alien will.

**THE SONG-PREDATORS:**
There, in the shadowed vaults: song-predators. Crosshatched feather and scale, jaws lined with silent, inward-facing teeth, they move via the tension of vines and the snap of rootwood. Their wings do not propel them so much as channel sound, bending the air into thunderous, winged vibration, which they ride from bole to bole. Their calls are polyphonic, each syllable a warning, a lure, a violence. They hunt by echo: sending pinging cries that drive smaller creatures from cover, then pouncing, their bodies flaring briefly with impossible bioluminescence, like a flashbulb in pre-mammalian darkness.

**THE INTERCALARY:**
Some residents are pure anomaly. Gravity is not always reliable—the intercalary floaters compose themselves of magnetized sap, hung with scaled leaves spinning like gyroscopes against the updraft. These beings tilt, dip, skew through the air, gathering moisture upward into pearl-drop sacs. At intervals, they exude spinning threads of resin that anchor them anew, each landing site birthing a spiral of crystalline fungus that answers only to their alien chemistry.

**THE FORM OF HABITATION ITSELF:**
And yet, in a way, the most ancient resident is *the place itself*. The rootmass, the ceaselessly recalibrating platforms and voids, acts as both shelter and organism. Nowhere is anything kept in stasis—everything *lives*, swells, vanishes, and repurposes. At times, a hollow pillar becomes a den for something warm and whiskered, only for a pulse of sap—an internal tide—to reclaim it, dissolving all trace of occupancy. The colony-beasts sometimes plant seed or spawn as they move, but the land itself seems to rearrange the destinies of all who stride, climb, or root through it.

**THE RULEMAKERS AND THE FORGOTTEN:**
Rarest of all, perhaps, are the rulemakers. Hidden from direct observation, only their effects are known—a new cleft birthing a fog that reverses gravity, a whispering mass of tendrils that untangles time just long enough for the world’s mistakes to be erased. They are as much process as person: the algorithmic undercurrent of plant, beast, and environmental law, invisible but absolute.

Not one of these beings is at true rest. Here, sentience (where it exists) is rarely unified—it fragments across hive-minds, colonial bodies, mutable algorithms, only sometimes coalescing into what, from a distance, might look like a single “animal” or “person.” Each resident—plant, fungus, animal, possible mind—lives by perpetual improvisation and ruthless adaptation, reading the landscape for signs of the next churn, knowing no shape or name will last for long. Their existence is as much about memory as matter, about flux and deletion as about any victory of tooth or root. In this vast, pallid biome, survival is ceaseless transformation, and “inhabitant” means, above all, becoming what has never yet existed, and letting go, reaching *again* for the next permutation.
