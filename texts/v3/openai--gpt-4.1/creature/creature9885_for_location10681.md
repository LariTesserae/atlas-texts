Whoever or whatever lives in Deep Underbough is shaped first—inescapably—by its weight: the pressure of soil and water and noise, the ceaseless density of green. The inhabitants are not simply creatures who stumble through, but ones grown—distilled—by the slow, cold patience that governs the place.

### **The Rootbound**

They are not truly plant, not truly animal, nor wholly machine, though in some places they wear all these faces in uneasy admixture. Their kind remember the world above only distantly, in instinct or nightmare. Their bodies are grown from the logic of Deep Underbough: lean, tensile where passage through tight root-halls demands; broad and grounded where waiting out the noise and damp is wise.

Most are soft-skinned—gray-green and black, their flesh veined by mineral and lichen, rootlets growing up through their skin or coiling at their limbs. They move with the slow care of things unhurried by sun or wind, always testing each foothold before trusting it, their senses attuned not to sight but to the music of vibration, the eddying of the air, the chemical signatures threaded through water and moss. Eyes, if they have them, are small and unused; instead they bear long, tendril-like whiskers or membranous fans—unfurled to taste the leakage of metal, the breath of fungus, the nearness of another presence treading the substrate.

The eldest Rootbound are almost motionless—tall, warped figures sunken to the knees in rootmass, slow and patient as any tree. Around their heads cluster small, bioluminescent blooms, feeding on the damp breath and lending their faces an uneasy brilliance. Their limbs entwine with vines, and when they shift, it is as though the ground rearranged. The youngest are quicker, less fused, their bodies still finding what form the Underbough will let them take. Some crawl on all fours, rooting for warmth in shallow bogs; others climb the slick columns, collecting moss or slicing fungus with spade-bladed fingers.

They live communally, if living means gathering in hollows where the noise distills and the condensation runs thick—slow, wordless, and alert to the ancient rhythms of water, root, and machine that govern survival. Sometimes they perform old, remembered tasks—keeping a lamp alive, prying half-drowned cables from muck, coaxing a generator to one last shuddering breath of light. These actions are ritual, not necessity: vestiges of a time when their ancestors claimed industry over this domain. Through it, perhaps, the Rootbound remember that they were once something else—something above.

### **The Drowned Operators**

Here and there—scarce as rumor, nearly as insubstantial—move other things: figures clothed in corroded metal and membrane, faces hidden behind masks bloomed with mold. These are the Drowned Operators—the remnants of those who first tried to colonize these halls, outposts slumbering in their veins and protocols echoing in their minds.

They do not speak, but their presences linger at the edges of light in the old stations, rising from ruts in moss-black railways or exploring machinery entombed by overgrowth. Their bodies are composite: gloves and boots and mantles of ancient steel, their insides suffused with threads of living root. Are they alive, or only inhabited, filled with the detritus and clever rot of the Deep? The distinction no longer matters; what was machine, or corpse, has become a hollow for the Underbough’s slow reclamation.

The Operators tend the machines that rumble far off—servicing, dismantling, mourning—or perhaps only miming remembered motion in the silence. They move by ritual and by the thunder underfoot. To some Rootbound, they are specters; to others, kin—reflecting a possible fate, as yet unchosen.

### **The Incidental Fauna**

Other lives flicker at the threshold of notice, small and persistent. Whip-thin salamanders curl in the bog-mist—blind, albino, thriving on the condensation. Beetles plated like chips of granite trundle between lichen. Bird-forms (if they are birds) are rare, stunted, drab—there is nowhere to fly, only to crawl, perch, and vanish behind a carpet of hanging stems. All live in the music of the place: their call is the tick of water’s drip, the whine of a cable, the flutter behind a rust-warped grate.

### **The Green Itself**

Most enduring, most sentient, is the Underbough’s plant-life. Vast, interlinked, and old, its intelligence moves in capillary time—slow pulses of chemical communication, the morphic drift of root and vine. At the scale of centuries, it engineers the rise and fall of ravines; at the scale of moments, it sniffs at anything warm and new, sending delicate rootlets, nudges, and tasting filaments toward lost machines and living bodies alike. To inhabit Deep Underbough is to be noticed by the green. Nothing is untouched, and nothing—given time—is truly separate.

**Who lives here?** The answer is neither simple nor singular. Here, life overlaps and merges: flesh with root, metal with moss, memory with vapor. Some walk and speak, though slowly, in the old industrial tongue. Some anchor and spread, sending wordless signals across kilometers of sodden substrate. All dwell in a place where emptiness is never truly vacant and presence is never alone.

**Deep Underbough is lived by:**
- Rootbound kin, ever half-becoming, patient as the fungal bloom and much less afraid of endings.
- Drowned Operators, intent on the unfinished work of the old world, but transformed by quiet rot and slow root.
- The workings and witnesses of smaller lives: beetles, salamanders, worms, and gnats, uncounted and unconcerned.
- And, over all, through all—the unhurried, omnipresent mind of the Green, to whom the machines and the meat are only nutrients, and whose empire is never in a hurry, because it already owns the infinite patience of stone.

To witness an inhabitant in Deep Underbough is to encounter someone claimed by change, compression, and the slow, unforgotten labor of returning everything to life, and life—eventually—to peat and shadow and root.
