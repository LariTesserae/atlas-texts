Those who live here—the ones who crowd the silent stone, wrap themselves into the fractures and flow of this endless, eroded plateau—are beings that, in another age, you might have called animals, but here the word is a poor translation, a brittle fossil.

They wear no names in your tongue; even if you could sort them into shapes and kinds, each would refuse to settle under any label. Some are wide and low, bodies sprawling over the stone like mats of leathery petals, each edge frilled with feathery cilia that slowly taste the ancient dust. Others cluster themselves into vertical tangles, barbed stalks climbing up along the broken faces of amphitheaters, their bodies jointed, translucent, prismatic when caught at the perfect angle—but most often dulled under centuries of spilled color and silt. There are crawling shapes, slow as mineral growth, with shells like smooth, nodular agates, each small oval opening ringed by sensitive, fluttering tendrils that test the air for information no visitor could parse.

Height does not belong to any single species. Some forms hang, limbless, from the underside of overhanging ledges, their obsidian-sheened bodies rippling with a quiet, inner illumination that pulses once every few hours—or perhaps years. Many are filamentous and colonial, a living tapestry clinging to every exposed inch, made of billions of almost-identical motes with the slow intelligence of lichen and coral, working together by chemical whispers.

There are eyes here—but to call them "eyes" is an imposition of habit. Lusterless clusters of gem-like orbs, or single, lidless abysses, or lattices that glitter for a second, then vanish into the muscle and membrane beneath; some are all eyes, a carpet of unblinking senses, forever tasting the plateau's monotony. Faces, if you can call them that, are composite, ambiguous: slopes encrusted with leaflike plates, mouths that are simply thin membranous slits, needle-quiet and patient, devouring nothing, merely sampling.

Some of these dwellers exist on the knife-edge of being, half in this world, half in some other or in some technicolor remainder of the past. They interpenetrate each other, oblivious, as if their atoms never quite learned how to repel. Observe a moment too long and a "creature" that sprawled across the ground unpeels in layers, showing itself to be a stack of parallel existences, each drifting slightly out of phase, each unaware of the others’ full reality. Sometimes these overlappings resolve for a moment into a single more-present organism: a spined arch, a dome bristling with root-like appendages that dip hungrily into the cracks of the stone. Other times, your sight slides, slips, and you are left with nothing but a ripple in the settling dust, a suggestion rather than a presence.

There is little fear among them, nor hunger as you know it. They do not predate, do not flee, do not pursue. Life is too thick here to allow that luxury. Where one cluster settles, another merges and wraps, nudging for space that is only half-available, shapes jostling for nothing but the tiniest arrangement of themselves among the neverchanging masses. If there is conflict, it is not expressed in movement or contest, but in infinitesimal adjustments—a slow rearrangement, as if the whole city of beings were a great breathing puzzle, eternally settling, never solved.

Beneath, within—wherever “here” means in the layered ambiguity of this realm—their substance sometimes flecks away into spore or silt or shimmering bits of anatomy, only to drift down and re-form as new plating, as another languid tendril stretching toward a neighbor. Their life cycles measure in centuries, perhaps longer, accretions and dissolutions so gradual that the ground itself seems to pulse in slow, unending respiration.

Amidst this living crust, there are ancient ones: beings so thoroughly compressed into reality that their shapes have become geological, living monoliths standing amongst the others like weathered statues. Their surfaces remain lumpy, barnacled with younger organisms who braid themselves into the ancestor’s skin, mining secrets from patient chemistry and deep memory. Inside these massive elders, the subsonic vibrations are strongest—a silent, ceaseless humming that can almost be thought-language, a broadcast in frequencies of patience and presence.

Yet nothing here is truly solitary. Some life forms extend only partway into visibility—not ghosts, nor hallucinations, but acts of biology that simply do not care about your kinds of physics. They are both present and absent: a swirl of colors where the mind says “something is,” but the eye cannot resolve. When two such beings meet, neither asserts dominance; they shimmer through one another, their existences intersecting in the same air, the same stone, the same cubic inch—neither erased, neither merged, just mutually real.

And beneath all this, threaded through the breathing hush of the plateau, is a layer of awareness that is not quite personal. The uncaring patience of these beings bleeds into the world itself. The difference between life and landscape is undone—the mineral ground, the thrum, the ghosts of tool and shelter, all are home to this new order; the ghosts of former builders, now barely distinguishable from the life that consumes and preserves them, are reminders that everything here has always become something else.

So those who live here are the sum of time in its slowest, densest, and strangest form: a multitude on the verge of solidity and dissolution, not separate from the place, but its very expression. Life that is not so much alive as ongoing, so thick that it has begun to forget, or surpass, the boundaries between individual and world, body and ground, moment and eternity—a layered, omnipresent coexistence that resists every final description, content to remain itself: patient, layered, infinitely dense, neither going nor arriving, utterly complete.
