Those who live here are kin to the place itself, molded by the rough geometry and patient dryness that define their world. Life is old—at a glance you sense the tremendous age in the lay of every limb and shell, but life is fierce, too, crammed and jostling near every edge and hollow.

**The People of Stone and Skin**

The principal inhabitants are neither plant nor animal in the familiar ways, but a union of both—mineral-bodied, biologically clever, each grown as much as born. Imagine dense mats of muscle and chitin armoring broad, flattened torsos, articulated around a skeletal spine of petrified crystal filaments. Their outer layers—shingled plates or ridged, snake-patterned carapace—gleam with ancient oxides, ever-so-slightly iridescent, tough as obsidian and proof against the abrasive, chemical-laden air. Limbs are numerous, some for grasping, some for anchoring, yet each joint is soft with a mossy, insulating pelage that seems, impossibly, to photosynthesize or absorb mineral light.

Faces are low, cuspate, crowded with swaths of small, glinting eyes—some mineral, some soft, all faceted. These eyes shimmer with more than mere vision, reading the slow stutter of lichen spores, the color shifts in dust, the tiniest tremors in stone. Mandibles or gently undulating cilia cluster around mouthparts adapted for scraping, sipping, or crushing, for here food is not hunted but gathered, grazed, coaxed from walls and mats in endlessly inventive ways.

They are not silent. The skin of these beings is an instrument: ridges and plates rub and knock out code—calls and warnings, invitations and histories. When pressed together in council or quarrel, their bodies thrum in a percussive counterpoint to the constant, subtle shifting of their city.

**The Smaller Kin**

Among them crawl the lesser folk—soft-skinned quarry-clingers, spiral-vent grazers, whiplashers that slip through lichen mats on a hundred flickering legs. These fill every scale of being between the dominant two-meter dwellers and the unseen, dust-sized architects that etch microglyphs beneath the surface of stone. Some are smooth and worm-like, transparent enough to see the dark grains grinding through their guts; others are crystalline, angular, flaring out in whorls and crests that both collect precious light and advertise territorial lines in radiant, mineral pigments.

Some of these are symbionts, threading in and out of living structures, helping filter trace elements, patching fractures in return for warmth or shadow; others are pests or prey, and attract a slow-motion chase or sudden flick of a forager’s dexterous grasp.

**The Builders and the Built**

Their city is not planned so much as fermented, grown as a thickening of purpose and habit through time. The largest, oldest among them—those too heavy to ever leave the shadowed halls—are architects almost by inertia, their bodies wedded to the stone. Their hands, broad as shovels, press and stack slabs chipped by generations of smaller kin. Their tools are grown, not forged: sharp, resin-tipped claws or implements slowly secreted over decades, used to chisel tunnels or inscribe the codes that narrate the yearless passage of life.

Each household is a nest and a workshop, woven through with lichen curtains and fungal mats; ventilation shafts flicker with pale pseudoleaves that flutter in air currents. At the core of every warren is a domesticated cluster of living fungi—bioluminescent in their slow contentment, exhaling chemical comfort, hoarders of the rarest vapors and trace crystals distilled from deep within the stone.

**Life’s Boundaries**

Birth is slow, seldom, a staggered broadcast of spores or eggs pressed into crannies grooved by prior generations. Offspring are swaddled in resinous, mineral-rich cocoons, kept moist not with water but with secretions thick as honey, pooling the local wealth.

Time here is deep, counted in layers, not moments. The living do not rush, yet their lives are busy—maintenance and foraging, slow repairs, negotiation of space. There is no privacy: proximity is survival, civilization is the press and grind of life enduring together, generations watching the pace of change measured less in disasters than in the subtle weft of growth, collapse, and reclamation.

**Culture of the Old Place**

Their memory is tactile—a tapestry scored into stone, a living archive of touch and pressure. Their stories echo in the layout of tunnels, in nicked rocks and grooves left by hands long gone. Music is a combination of rubbing and humming—the most valued art is the patterning of mutual touch, the slow dance of communal grooming, cleaning, sculpting, their city shaped as much by tradition as necessity.

They do not dream of elsewhere—the outside is inimical, the void is not for them. Every being is folded into its neighbor’s shadow; every surface is earned and shared. When one passes, their body slips quietly into the layered mats, nourishing the lichen, the fungi, the children. Thus, nothing is lost; the city grows denser, every death another strata, lives stacked upon each other, held aloft in their cold, shining aerie—a testament to unspectacular, unbroken continuity.

**To sum:**
Those who live here are life as mineral, community as necessity; they nest in and with the stone, patient as the slow world’s pace, voices ringing through darkness—thriving where nothing else would, their abundance the slow, perpetual answer to the void.
