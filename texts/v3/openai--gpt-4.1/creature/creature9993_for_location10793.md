The ones who live in Grayfall—if live is quite the right word for what they do here, day upon blue-silver day—are neither simple mountain folk nor the steely pragmatists of the lowland ruins. They are plateau people: persistent, thin-blooded, quietly resourceful, and older, in their bearing, than the quotidian nations below.

Their bodies tell you first: these people are built for the thin air and the chilly wet. They are neither tall nor broad, but sturdy, close-knit in stature, with powerful legs from endless climbing, and hands intricately callused. Skin is pale, even in those with dark ancestry, for the long years beneath cloud and glass—complexion pearly or bluish beneath, as if always half-submerged in the fog. Hair runs the spectrum from stone-black to the diluted gold of old iron, but is most often hidden under worn caps and oilcloth hoods against the wet and the rare sun. Faces are spare, watchful, eyes pale and quick to narrow against both wind and sudden light.

The children of Grayfall are neither wild nor shy; they are constant presences, darting from terrace to terrace, clambering across pipes with the effortless balance that comes from never knowing another place. They are trusted early and closely watched by everyone, for injury is expensive here and children are few.

The citizens are neither strictly communal nor fiercely individual—each house is a small fortress of kin and tool and memory, but the boundaries are porous. Meals are taken often in shared yards, and each person learns, from an early age, the calculus of mutual maintenance: whose roof leaks, whose cable frays, who needs hands on the water barrels tomorrow.

Occupation is inherited or adapted. Enough engineers cluster in every family to maintain the humming of Grayfall’s engines; enough growers keep small, ingenious gardens among the stone and pipe. There are millwrights—indispensable, circumspect, often bearing the black smudges of machine oil even on feast days. There are keepers of pipes and tunnels, loping through the substructure with battered lanterns and bags of worn valves. Tinkerers and salvagers, diggers and water-callers, each reading the mysterious fluctuations of Grayfall’s slow-springing aquifers. There are those, too, who work with things nobody else quite trusts: fungus-harvesters who tend to the faint-glowing lichens, or mechanics who coax the inexplicable dynamo to run, whispering superstitions to its slow-turning shaft.

It is not an easy people. Faces tend to be unreadable but for the crow’s feet that speak of long humor and quick suspicion; speech is slow, soft, and literal except in humor, where wryness blooms like moss in shade. Arguments, when they come, are always about practicalities—about pipe routes, energy rations, door hinges, never about pride or doctrine. Custom rules; elders remember the last hard winter or the flood that never quite reached the heights, and their word is seldom ignored.

Their faith, if it is faith, is in the endurance of things: in a repaired machine staying repaired, in mosses seeded along a wall eventually outcompeting lichen, in the recurrence of water after a dry spell. They hold to certain taboos, however—never dig too deep into odd caverns; never drink from the blue-glowing pools; always mark with paint and bell those passages where winds howl unseasonably, where travelers have gotten lost or returned… different.

Associations here are not marked by sign or flag, but by patterns of maintenance—one block unified by a water-main they all rebuilt together, another by a circuit of lights that never quite goes dark, a third by the communal compost that warms their gardens. Strangers are greeted with brisk, wary generosity—food and shelter at the going rate of labor, but always a sideways watchfulness, the quiet weighing of necessity against risk.

Among them, a few seem almost outside the slow struggle with plateau and machine. These are the rare seers and mineral interpreters—those who, by wordless inheritance, seem to feel the vibrational pulse of the strange old rock, who know where to listen deep in the tunnels for clanks that are not pipes and water but something older, moving far beneath. They are taciturn, respected in a sideways fashion, and consulted at dusk on matters of mineral blight or the sudden, inexplicable warm breath from a crack in the stone.

Animals dwell here, too, in a quiet partnership. Grayfallers keep small, curved-horned goats—their wool dense and water-shedding, their hooves sure as any human on slick stone—and the cats and dogs here are lean, long-limbed, able to sprint up a gully or vanish at a whistle, as adept as their owners at surviving on what can be gleaned. Rodents—kept and wild—are both guarded against and tolerated, for sometimes they lead to unseen leaks or breaks in the ancient infrastructure.

There is always an undertow of watchfulness. The strangeness of the land breeds a careful suspicion, a readiness to pause and listen for the odd vibration underfoot, or the whisper of air from a tunnel thought long sealed. But the people—enduring, good with tools, sure in their bonds—are remarkably gentle with one another, their society close-knit out of weather and need, quick to close ranks and just as quick to diffuse tensions with the careful humor born of fatigue.

To be Grayfall is to be a part of an old, stubborn conversation between stone, water, industry, and green; to have one’s own strangeness acknowledged by the others; to know that with enough care, and enough time, almost anything can be mended. The people live in the shadows, among slow machines and ancient flora, quietly proud and patient, holding fast on the plateau’s high, misted bones.
