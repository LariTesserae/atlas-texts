What lives here is many—and not as you expect.

You will not find natural divisions between what is plant, what is fungus, what is animal, what is mechanism. The boundaries blur, multiply, resolve, dissolve again.

Begin underground. Within the mantle of the plateau, old heat drifts upward, breathed through the pores of bedrock. Tunneling through strata, you would find the excavators; beings of mineral and flesh and machinery inextricably entwined. Their bodies are elongated cylinders, plated in strata of stone and hard keratin, but riven with veins of metallic ore, slick with slow-seeping lubricants not wholly unlike ancient tree sap. Their front ends, never meant for light, are crowded with tactile tendrils and rotary mouthparts: gears grown from bone, slicked with the secretions of underground fungi.

They tunnel not only for food, but to generate warmth; slow-motion mining, the scraping and rolling of rock yielding detritus for the mosses, their droppings forming lines of nutrient-rich clasts that cradle trickles of water. The upflowing air from their burrows emerges aboveground warm and faintly sweet-oily, scented by their breath—this is the smell of the upland night.

Upon the surface, motion is spare but weighted. The largest denizens are the rovers: immense, pill-bug-like beings whose hemispherical carapaces are etched in whorls of iron and manganese, grown thick as armor yet flexible at the sutures. They move slowly, their mass too great for haste, feeding on the matted tangles of photometal moss that gather in stone basins or the black-glinting crusts that thrive where fog is thickest.

Upon and within these rovers grow other lives: ash-grey, lamp-like fungi that blink with a faint turquoise light, luring drifting insects of erratic flight—wings like metal foil, bodies pulsed by piezoelectric muscles, hiving in folds of rotting lichen. When a rover pauses beside a water pool—perhaps for hours, perhaps for days—its flanks become gardens, sparking sudden eruptions of pale, short-lived blooms, or colonies of symbiont mites with crystalline claws that comb through the detritus, glandular mouths extracting sugars or oils as needed.

Across the flats, there are herds: strider-beasts broad-hipped and stilt-legged, jointed like the sculptures of forgotten artisans. Each foot splits into rings of flexible metal filaments, giving them improbable poise on the slickest rock. Their skin is more alloy than bone, born from generations of consuming ores and mist-taking moss. Their bodies hum, faintly, with harmonics: internal organs that are as much pressure vessel as muscle, core temperatures regulated by chemical rotors and capillary heat exchangers. Their heads rise, bristled with antennae—sensory arrays pulsing with electromagnetic awareness, listening for the buzz of predators and the low thunder of coming rain.

Predators, too, are a convergence of forms. They are not quick but patient, often lurking at the edges where condensation braids across stones, camouflaged by sheathing plates of tarnished bronze or dull graphite-petals. Some resemble leopards plucked from the blueprints of antiquity and redrawn with cables and hydraulic sinew—sleek, long-bodied, immobile until they spring. Others are more arcane: segmented centipedes of glassy chitin, trailing viscous, heat-shedding fluids to keep from overheating, their fangs finely etched for surgical puncture, their senses attuned to vibrations of water through stone. 

At the water pans—the rare hearts of the land—combine all things: the mosses, their textures a weave of soft wires and glinting cilia, pulsing gently as they soak up moisture; the trees, short and black, their roots burrowing through boulders with slowed, muscular patience, leaves sheened with waterproof oil and lit along their veins by bioluminescence the color of forge-fire; the insects and lesser scavengers, not so much soft-bodied as wire-joined, their abdomens casting soft blue shapes in starlight, feeding on each other’s leavings.

At night, in the true darkness, a second world unfolds—a tapestry of phosphorescence and trembling metal above, cool air prickling with vapor. Here, the aerial denizens take flight: creatures broad-winged and tubular, sails cut from ultra-thin, silvery chitin, their skeletons stretching globes of amber and iron that hum with stored heat and chemical energy. They drift soundlessly, trailing the wet air above rivers, sometimes trailing streamers of spore-lights or glowing dust.

And aloft—always at the edge of perception—those rare, mythic entities: immense, drifting shapes whose bodies are a fusion of weathered stone, slow-breathing fungus, and frameworks of living cable. They are the oldest things, perhaps, carriers of memory, swimming through the mist at the rim of awareness, silent except for the faintest whirr of endless internal motion.

*None were made. All have become. This world is not haunted by the ghost of lost industry: its inhabitants are the wild offspring of geology and time and accident, stripped of any memory of intention. They are as inevitable as wind, as inexplicable as machinery grown wild, as quietly alive as any forest. Their chemistry is metallurgy, their breath an alchemy of fog and engine-heat. They do not know you and would not mark your passing; they keep their own time, measure their days in the circling of moon and vapors, in the rise and settling of warmth.*

To imagine what lives here is to imagine a life without distinction between the animate and the constructed, the grown and the assembled—a continent alive with the slow, beautiful persistence of things that are both more and less than animals, roaring softly in the endless warm dark.
