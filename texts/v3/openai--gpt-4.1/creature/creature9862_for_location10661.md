They are neither wholly beast nor artifact, nor even what you would call inhabitants in the way of any familiar woods or towns. Here, who (or what) lives is inseparable from the city, the plateau, and the patient choreography of stone and drip and pulse. Where you expect to find trees, brush, or even lichen, there are only lives—layered, adapted, exquisitely attuned to this fortress of wet mineral, transmission, and time.

The grandest, most apparent forms—call them the macrofauna for lack of a more fitting term—move with the tectonic patience of the stone itself. Glossy, slow, they sprawl where they will: their backs armorous and textured like the boulders underfoot, slicked with steady sheets of water that flow around and between their segments. You could linger all day and see only a ripple along a ridge, a ponderous pistonlike limb retracting, and still not know for certain if what you see is alive or just an extruded part of the habitat. Their bellies host entire communities, hollows and channels in which run-off channels run, tended by scavenger clades that, for eons, have cleaned and aerated and carried off excess, much as fish pick parasites from the skin of whales.

Between and atop these giants, life races in denser, sharper currents. There are the molluscoid herds: clusters of dark, opaline shells, some no larger than a thumbprint, others the size of barrels, each slick with a sheen that mirrors the walls of their city. They move in double-helix paths across carven terraces, grazing at the mineral rind left by receding water, their slow progression mapping the city’s internal tides more surely than any chronometer. Their mantle-flesh dabs and tests, leaving trails bright with microcrystals: not waste, but a form of ongoing construction, an unconscious collaboration with place.

Above, the air is woven with fliers—delicate, spider-formed, but not spiders at all. They flit, glossy and multijointed, clusters of eyes glinting. Their silk anchors mists and droplets, collecting what is needed for their swarming broods; their wings hum not with the beat of hunger, but purpose, calibrating temperature gradients, shifting the microclimates of crevices and towers ever so slightly as they move.

Everywhere there are eyes—huge, pale, single-lidded, blinking with slow intelligence from behind mineral curtains or in the shadow of bridge underbellies. Some are wary, tracking with almost mammalian concern. In alcoves at the juncture of conduit and stone, tufts of sodden fur drowse, sopping up the warmth leached from running fluid. These are the city’s recognizers and caretakers, perhaps; their delicate digits vanish when you approach, or emerge to manipulate valves and feeder pipes in careful synchrony with brooding cycles and machine outflows. If they have language, it is tactile, carried by touch and vibration more than voice, pressing their dense, damp coats against the humming city skin in a kind of intimate query.

Yet beneath them all, in every pocket and trough where run-off meets mineral, pulse the greatest abundance: larvae and wriggling threads, sparkling in the haze of constant overflow. They feed not on roots and leaves, but on the endlessly fresh chemical leach of bedrock and the microdetritus washed in perpetual circuits from rooftop to aqueduct to drain. Some will live only hours, barely time to molt before being rinsed away; others rearrange themselves through dozens of metamorphoses, growing shells, limbs, glossy exoskeletons, even organelles that process minerals like sentient batteries.

There are, too, presences too thin to see: mats of protein, colonies of almost-microbial minds distributed in film across vertical faces, reading the city’s temperature and conducting faint currents between themselves, upregulating the local chemical climate, feeding scraps of extra energy into the veins of their larger kin.

You sense that intelligence is disseminated rather than centralized here. There are clevernesses: in the eyes that appraise you from the dark; in the clocks and balances encoded in mollusk movements and furred organisms’ manipulations; in the algorithms of growth calculated by unseen, possibly mechanical, possibly biological, processes that run under every stone and conduit. But you would not find a city council, a single ruling caste, or even anything resembling a clearly separate species. Life is process, and each being is a node—not just an inhabitant but an organelle in this larger, composite organism of stone and water and engineered flow.

What lives here, then, are assemblages: not merely of flesh or shell or bone, but of grit and memory, engineered function and ancient habit, patient endurance and constant, glacial change. Individually, they might be called creatures, machines, or even sensors, but collectively they are the city—an ecology whose shape is determined as much by what has always been as by what continues to adapt, whose sense of survival is an unbroken dialogue among mineral, animal, and machine.

They do not wait for sunlight; they do not count the turning of leaves. Their time is the cycle of pumps and hum of flow, their nurture is precipitation and seep, their inheritance is the memory of water moving through stone—held fast when all else runs away. In this place, life holds tight, and through holding, endures.
