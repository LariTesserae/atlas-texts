What lives here? Who or what could call this place home—a city stretched across the bark and veins of ancient, world-looming flora, where the ground is always slipping, always reforming, and all that is solid is both grown and won?

Let the sense of the place draw forward its inhabitants, slow as roots, strange as rutting moss.

---

### They Who Live on and Within the Plateau

#### **Their Substance:**

In a place where earth is myth and all below is plant-stuff, those who endure must themselves be kin to the logic of growth and decay. These are not, at first glance, mere human descendants—if they ever were. Their flesh recalls, if faintly, the recall of mammals: warmth, breath, soft skin, eyes that blink away fog and sap. But they have become more than that, shaped by generation upon generation in the strata of the green plateau.

Their skin is thick—not callused, but almost waxy, adapted to moisture loss, streaked with patterns in greens and yellows that mimic lichen and leaf-rot. Beneath, muscles are denser, heavier; bare feet are splayed and strengthened for traction on slick root and the uneven foam of old, compressed wood. Hands are broad, fingers ridged, nails thick as horn, good for prying up bark or scraping fungus.

Hair is rare; more often, a nimbus of fine filaments at the scalp—pale, fibrous, able to wick condensation for drinking, or trap fleeting flecks of dew. Some bear fronds or flattened projections along their arms or backs: not true leaves, not yet, but a kind of vestigial camouflage, or organ for sensing subtle growth in the living floor.

Their breath is steady, even in the thinnest air. There is a rumor that some can coax an extra sip of oxygen from the densest plant-air, their lungs lined with symbiotic algae, faint green streaks visible beneath the skin. If they bleed (and they do, when a bridge gives or a machine tears loose), the blood is dark and cool, deeply red still, but it beads up quickly and dries almost black—clotting in seconds, life ever pressing toward survival.

#### **Their Culture and Society:**

To live in this place is to be communal, pragmatic, and fiercely attentive to the moods of their world. They have no word for “soil” beyond legend, but their language has a hundred words for “root,” for “knot,” for the fine differences in the creak of wood before collapse. They tell time by patterns of mist and light through layered fiber, by the cyclic reforms of bridges and plazas, by the rhythm of pistons and the resting of their growing-rooms.

Families are vast: one lives with all who share a given platform or sector, bound more by necessity than lineage. Each group tends their share of root-mass, cultivating edible pith, coaxing fungi and moss in great bins or woven racks. There are “gardeners” whose craft is to shape living wood, speaking gentle patterns into growing root until it holds a bridge stable or arches a new roof overhead. Builders, by contrast, are part engineer, part scavenger—recycling fallen machinery, joining metal and plant in hybrid scaffolds.

Memory is essential—seasoned guides and elders are held in reverence for their sense of place, their ability to read the plateau’s subtle shifts and cycles. Oral tradition is everything, because nothing written persists: even the smoothest plate of bark will warp and carry away paint, even the hardest plaque recedes into green, unreadable depth. Their stories twist around the old, the uncanny, the constantly transforming. The children are taught to read the world as a palimpsest—forever altered, never wholly erased.

#### **Their Rituals and Attunements:**

Rituals bloom where logic thins. Before crossing a bridge newly re-grown, people stop, listening to the wind and wood; they barter small tokens, pieces of worked lignum or a rare splash of metal, for safe passage, leaving these as offerings to whatever intention stabilizes reality for another day. At the opening of a new growing season—a span when moisture is richest, and all the plateau sweats with potential—communal feasts gather across the widest decks, music made from hollowed trunks and wind-harped vine.

Death is not buried, for there is no earth. Instead, the dead are returned to the slow rot: bodies dressed in woven fibers, laid in the root-basins where decomposition is revered, believed to guide the next cycle of growth. Bones become fertilizer; fiber becomes memory. Sometimes, those lost to a sudden collapse or vanishing passage are simply mourned with stories, their names carved in runes that will inevitably warp and decay, fading back into the living ground.

#### **Their Relationship to the Plateau:**

They are not rulers here—not quite symbiotes, not true masters. The plant-city persists because it is endlessly pruned, recycled, and carefully watched, but wildness forever presses in. At the boundaries, the city’s edge blurs into where root-density falters, and the great vine-cliffs descend toward the lower void. Only the bold or desperate venture beyond the known—seeking new strains of plant, new water basins, or the rare, rare animal life that flickers in and out of rumor.

Technology has adapted to the plateau’s soft, shifting laws. Machines are always tinkered with—water engines and wind-pumps, plant-extractors and lamps grown into living roots. Tools are never too heavy, for fear of collapse, but never too precious; all must be salvageable, all must hum in tune with the wood.

Their food is plant and fungus, root-sap and lichen cake, the occasional shimmering moth or leggy lizard caught clean and shared thinly. Fermentation is art, as is distilling drink from the thick, oily night-blooms. Children chase floating spores for fun; elders gather them in scoops of woven mesh, then sow them in new-growing frames.

#### **Their Senses and Instincts:**

Over time, their senses have changed. Sight is sharp to subtle hues in green and gold, able to distinguish the health of a stalk by its pallor. Their hearing is tuned to deep vibration—the warning rumble of a failing platform, the hopeful thrum of root-growth in the depths, as well as the mechanical heartbeats of engines pulsing through the city. Scent is everything: the profile of resin, the bitterness of encroaching rot, the sudden sweetness foretelling new fungal bloom.

Maps are sung, not written, and passed mouth to mouth; navigation is a conclusion, not a science. Gravity’s oddities have woven intuition into their bodies—a sway of head to judge a descending bridge, a flick of the wrist to catch a falling tool, certain it will spiral to a predictable, if counterintuitive, spot.

#### **Their Philosophy:**

To live here is to embrace balance, to trust in both decay and growth, to see change not as loss but as inexorable renewal. They believe in listening: to the world’s creaks, to the stories remembered, to the silent pull of what is becoming below and beyond. In dreams, some say they become the city itself, stretching their limbs into every tangled root, every span of mist and shadow. Waking, they are content to tend, repair, harvest and endure—and to leave, always, more growing than they take away.

---

Thus, the plateau’s citizens are not quite flora, not quite fauna, not quite machine—but a perpetual collaboration of all three, living at the edge of what stability this high, mythic terrace allows. They do not merely survive in a world without dirt; they have become its root, its memory, its ever-changing hope.
