Those who live here are, themselves, as fresh to the old stone as the plateau they inhabit—an emergent people, shaped not by descent from settled valleys or deep-rooted forest societies, but by the peculiarity of a place born almost yesterday, in geologic terms. Their bodies and habits, their bearing and thought, are quietly and deeply marked by the novelty of their home. They are not a race of conquest or long, storm-swept migration; nor are they a people of legend or old myth, grown strange and tall from time’s stretching. Instead, they are recent—recent in the world, but ancient already in their patience, in their ways of moving and building that blend with what the land gives and with what the land forbids.

**Aspect & Form**  
Those who dwell on the high, wet plateau are neither large nor small. There is a balance in their form: compact, sturdy bodies that squat easily at poolside, scramble surefooted on sharp stone, and carry the grace of the careful rather than the bold. Their skin carries the memory of sunlight—golden-brown to wet-terracotta, flecked sometimes with a paler mica sheen as if dusted by the stone itself. Fingers are long, deft, nails sometimes broad or chisel-like, having grown or been tended this way for the prying of moss, the shifting of small rocks, the gentle coaxing of stalk and leaf in a world without beasts or birds.

Their hair is dark, often bound close or left to fall in short, thick locks—practical, slow to soak, quicker to dry, sometimes adorned only with a twist of green stalk or a woven crown of moss. Eyes are wide, irises large for seeing in the mist-dim or the stretched sun, colored by the earth—ochre, wet-slate, leaf-dark. Expressions are unhurried; a smile forms as a slow glint rather than a sudden flash.

**Dress, Ornament, and Mark**  
Their clothing is simple, suited to the humidity and constant presence of moisture—tunics or wraps of woven plant fiber, waxy broadleaf carefully dried and layered for against the cold of a misty night or the wet of a sudden rain. Feet are often bare, toughened, or bound with soft bark; working hands sometimes sheathed in greaves or gloves of pliant, watercured moss. Ornament is understated: a cuff of copper at the wrist, traded and treasured; a line of dark pigment traced along the temple, marking a household or a task completed; baskets with braided patterns, or tiny mirrored tokens cut from old, stone-polished slag.

**Culture & Rhythm**  
They live in the world’s newness without hurry. Every action stretches into its proper place within the patience of the plateau—planting stalks in cracks, tending drip-lines from pool to pan, lifting stones to reinforce runoff channels. Their sense of time is steeped in cycles: the passing mist, the return of the flood, the slow swelling and shrinking of moss at water’s edge. There are ceremonies, but these are more acts of attendance than events—periods of quiet gathering at the central high ground, voices a muted hum, as if to learn together how the land means them to live.

Tools and homes alike reflect the language of the plateau: uncarved lines, retaining the form of each stone, each broad leaf arranged so that every layer sheds water to the next. Nothing is wasted; nothing is overdone. They know, unconsciously, that accumulation is dangerous here—too much presence, too much ambition, and the fragile peace of stone and water and green might tip.

Labor is shared patiently, and expertise lies in what cannot be rushed: the slow encouragement of plant roots to bind soil, the gentle nudging of pools to keep water where it is most needed, the maintenance of walkways and shelter so that every settlement stays light, never oppressing the world. Their myths are quiet and new, stories of the land itself—how the water glitted for the first time on the unbroken rock, green emerging from the cracks, the slow fashioning of the first hammer from the world’s detritus.

**Social Arrangement**  
They arrange themselves much as their settlements arrange the land: spaciously, respectfully, never crowding. Family units are porous, clustered around the tending of a pool or the maintenance of a patch of broad-leaf garden. Bonds are strong but gentle; age grants not rule but trust—elders are those who know the slow patience best, who recall the histories of each settlement’s careful expansion and pause.

Children are quiet but watchful, learning by observation rather than discipline; songs are sung softly at dusk as mist rises, music made not with drums or pipes—echoes would be lost in the hush—but with reeds, water-filled shells, a gentle pattering like the land’s own voice. The presence of copper is rare, marks milestones: a tool’s founding, a house complete, a new pool secured.

**Relation to Other Living Things**  
With no animals or birds as companions or labor, their relationship is all with stone, plant, and water. They pollinate by hand, fingers stained by foreign pollen, carrying dust from blossom to blossom in a ritual both practical and reverent. They know the needs of each moss, each broad-leaf stalk, propagating their growth as one might nurture kin. In dry years (rare, but memorable), they fashion dewscreens: broad frameworks draped in leaf and fiber to gather mist and direct it down to thirsty roots.

In such a quiet world, they have cultivated a kind of gentle philosophy, one that recognizes their place as new guests upon a land that itself remembers nothing older than its own raising. They measure their days not in ambition or in monuments, but in the quality of their attending—the satisfaction that every plant finds root, that every drop finds its pan, that every home remains a place of freshness, not fatigue.

**Mood & Meaning**  
They are at once the keepers and the children of this plateau—never possessive, never removed. They live gently in the hush, carrying the newness and open promise of the world within themselves, holding close neither sorrow nor hunger nor loud joy, but a deep, enduring presence. If one found their settlement and sat long enough, it would seem at first that no one was there. But slowly, among the scattered green, the mirrors of water, and the pale stone, figures would emerge—walking quietly, hands full of living green, faces open to light and mist. Each act is an honoring of the surface, each day a continuation of arrival.

They live as the plateau itself was meant to be lived upon: open, washed, green and veined with water, forever new, forever pausing—never hurried forward by time, but sustained, entire, in the hushed beginnings of the world.
