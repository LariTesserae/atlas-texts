What lives here?

First, discard all easy analogies, for this is not a place for familiar forms. Nothing that wriggles or walks here would find its sense in light, in water, in root or leaf. The dwellers of this place are shaped by the press of strata, the angles of crystal, the heartbeat warmth that never surfaces.

**The Hyalophyte Webs**:  
Sprawled along ceilings and snaking into impossibly thin crevices, these are neither molds nor true fungi, but something that shares a boundary with both mineral and life. Their ‘bodies’ are sheets of frost-pale filaments, woven tensile and thin, glinting faintly with whatever ember phosphor seeps from the rock. The webs branch and somatically fuse, forming vast, single organisms that sheath entire galleries. When a breath of warmth pulses through the stone, radiant with latent charge, the webs pulse in return, slowly harvesting the energy and exuding crystalline motes—food for others small enough, silent as dust, drifting until gravity or hunger claims them.

**The Carry-Runners**:  
These are most nearly ‘mobile’ life in the labyrinth—a broad classification for beings of segment and limb, but their forms betray this world’s angular logic. Each is an articulated chain of rigid plates, wedge-shaped and glassy, banded with metallic joints. They move in languid procession along the narrowest ledges, their dozens of feet making no noise; their antennae, each tipped with a quivering drop of obsidian black, taste the stone ahead for mineral flavor or electric flux. Sometimes their underbellies extrude gummy pads to lap up exuded motes, sometimes they gather the detritus cast off by the webs. They carry things—pieces of fractured stone, harvested filaments, the rare resilient fragment of an ancient artifact, all stowed in ribbed, inorganic pouches beneath their plated shells. Yet, observed for years, one might see them simply moving, carrying burdens for the sake of the circuit, as if their journeys mark and maintain the equilibrium of place.

**The Chill-Knotters**:  
In the greater chambers, where pressure and time have folded the stone into vast, geometric nests, there reside the slowest forms of life—great, condensed masses of living mineral, coiled and interlaced into knots the size of boulders. The surfaces of these forms are faceted and cold, but beneath, a thin seam of living matter spirals and braids, imperceptibly slow. These beings process heat gradients on a geological timescale; their growth is neither plant nor mineral nor animal, but an endless weaving in response to the faintest pulse of warmth. Over centuries, the knots twist and tighten, exuding sparse, fatty crystals that form numinous, slippery veins in the nearest walls—substance scavenged, perhaps, by hungry webs or far-runners.

**The Glassmirrors**:  
No eyes, here—at least, none that simply absorb photons. Instead, some presences reflect, their entire bodies a modulated amalgam of faceted silver or prismatic glass, thin as a blade but spread in whorls and sheets against the walls. To pass them is to feel watched, not through sight but through the subtle disturbance of heat and charge they register in your body: they ‘see’ by refraction and the tremor of electric change, shifting their planes to mimic or divert the warmth they sense. Sometimes they appear only as a shape that ripples in the rock, or a brief, cold doubling of your movement as you pass.

**The Thread-Slip**:  
Barely visible, these are something between worm and current—a ghost of pale metallic filament, dozens of meters long, no thicker than a hair, sliding through cracks with patience and intent. They twine where mineral glass meets metal, feeding from minute electric differentials, leaving behind a trace polymer sheath that catches the faintest dust—a larder for smaller, hungrier things. Sometimes, after centuries, enough dust accumulates to spark a sudden, fractal bloom of motion: miniscule scavengers, like brittle inch-tall spiders of vitrified bone, emerge and feast before the thread slips onward, vanishing into another seam.

**The Memory-Builders**:  
Rare, so rare that only faintest traces remain: a cluster of arranged stones, an old basket, a groove worn smooth where nothing else touches. These are not people, not in ways you recognize, but something with intent—slow, deliberate, absent of rush. Perhaps they return, century by century, to tend these humble objects, to trace familiar paths through ancient halls. Perhaps there is only one—a life form of almost complete stasis, persisting through memory held in mineral thought, its touch light as a dream, its handiwork proof that something, once, was made not for survival but for the gentle shaping of place: a constellation of action so tiny, so repeated, it marks time in the bedrock.

**And Others**:  
Slippery shapes that glide horizontally through the glassy crust, gnawing at fissures made by long-gone tectonics. Nodules that sprout on wall and pillar, extruding seedless ‘buds’ of crystalline substance that wander off under their own slight charge, diffusing into new patterns of existence. At the edges, entire colonies of mineral ‘mites’ crawl to orchestrate a choral hum, tuning the resonance of stone for purposes inscrutable to any surface world biologist.

All are patient, all are slow, all suffuse this sunless mosaic with the palpable sense of company: nothing that seeks war, or dominance, or conquest. Every presence is precisely shaped for its angle of niche—a world at peace with itself, existence measured not in rush but in the even, mineral pulse of subterranean time. Here, in the densest darkness, what lives is ancient, opaque, and steadfast in its silence—a multitude that never rushes, never hungers for more than its share, yet fills every angle with its secretive, continual life.
