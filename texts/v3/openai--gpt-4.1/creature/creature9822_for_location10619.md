Somewhere between cold-blooded and cold-hearted, and yet not without a surfeit of life, the denizens of the High Runnels at Day’s Edge are less a people than an unfolding—an ongoing, sleepless urgency more than an identity pinned down. To merely call them “inhabitants” would be to mistake the effect for the cause, for this is a place thronged with not one shape or mind but many—a crowded, overlapping, anarchic cloud of life. They do not “live” upon the plateau so much as participate in its nervous, kinetic, relentless being. Yet something we might call “people” exists here, though the word wavers at the edge of strangeness.

**Who—or What—They Are**

Neither fully animal nor quite spirit, neither strictly natural nor wholly constructed, the dwellers of the High Runnels seem, at first, to be small, furtive, and legion. Their forms suggest adaptation spiraled to an edge—bodies etched by the calculus of cold, hunger, friction, concealment. They go by no single name; even among themselves, any notion of enduring lineage, tribe, or standing seems as momentary as a frost-breath at dawn.

Look, and you might see them—many-limbed, their bones close to the skin, their movements a staccato negotiation with chill and glare. The most common form is low to the ground, torso compact; two to four legs, with a weak suggestion of hands, not feet, their digits splayed, webbed, callused. Their heads are angular, face drawn into a mask of peering crystal-gleaming eyes, beneath which fronds or pale tendrils quiver at each inhalation, sensing flow, scent, vibration. Their skin is strange—neither furred nor scaled, but a shifting sheath: clammy, reflective, patterned with pearlescent nodes or chitinous patches, always slick with condensed water, always half-lit by refracted sun.

Others rise higher from the communal mass: crested creatures who balance awkwardly on jointed haunches, their limbs triple-jointed; some fewer, taller still, ride the line between animal and wraith, hollow-boned, draped in capes stitched from scavenged pelts, drifting rather than walking—perhaps the eldest, or those temporarily ascendant in the ever-mutable patterns of status here.

They modulate their forms, season by season, moult or bud off appendages, swap skins with the living garments woven from moss and pale fur, so that what is “the same” individual may seem, from dusk to dusk, a belonging to a different caste or family—a community in ceaseless recombinant flux.

**How They Live**

For these are not solitary, territorial beings, but—out of necessity and the tyranny of geography—social, if only in the way lichens are: a sociality constituted of friction rather than fellowship, of coexistence, confluence, crowd. Their history, if they keep history, is written in the incremental swelling or contraction of their “city”—cities, truly: flowing colonies that vanish and bloom again depending upon meltwater, drift, brief daylight. They cluster in the low-built labyrinths—always in motion, adding, demolishing, patching, tunneling.

In groups, they move with nervous choreography. They never stand fully upright—always crouched, leaning, ready to leap into a crack or under a frosted slab at any sign of disturbance. Their communication is tactile and chemical, conducted in swift pulses of touch, pheromone, kinesthetic shuffle—a society of immediacy, of swarming. They live in frenetic proximity, their labor a collective rush toward the next meal, the next patch of precious warmth or hidden drift of scavenged wood. Here, everything is about capture or escape, about the maximization of fleeting opportunity.

And yet, in the deep blue-lit threshold before each sun’s waning, you might glimpse a change: a hush greater than silence; figures gathered around a pit of thawed water, not in predation but in a momentary repose, as if to share the day’s gleanings or recall, in whorls and tap-patterns, the shape of absence that the night will bring.

**How They Think**

Theirs is a mind built for the margin, for the almost-too-late, for the improvisation required when all plans shatter on the cold stone. Their memory is local, immediate, distributed—held not in stories but in the shapes of tools, the arrangement of shelter, the churning profiles of frayed, flint-tipped implements dropped in the peat to mark a claim or warn of danger.

Ideas are not spoken but worn, imitated, sniffed out: a gesture, a carved whistle, the dab of moss that signals “unsafe,” the quick flash of a pelt meaning “here is sustenance.” There is wisdom here, but it is wisdom of flux, pattern, and riddling symbol: the knowledge of ten thousand daily crises endured through communal adaptation, of survival through abandonment, through the welcoming of loss as simply another phase.

**What They Want**

What are their concerns? Warmth—the rare luxury that pulses down from the sun for a handful of brittle hours, pooling in dark stone. Sustenance—the ever-vanishing nutrient in lichen, crab-things, the pale-crowned scavengers. Shelter—against the pricking wind, the slicing cold, the predatory forms that slide on too many jointed legs.

But want here is not hunger alone. There is, in the way they build and rebuild, in how they return again and again to what the storm and sun have nearly obliterated, something of yearning. A reaching for pattern, for recurrence, for continuity in a world of nothing enduring. Their “art,” if such a word fits, is the layering—of textures, found objects, stones arranged in careful spirals—which catches and holds the sunlight in certain ways at certain moments: a fleeting beauty, an affirmation that even here, the world can be shaped, if only for a minute.

**Their Strangeness**

They are what the High Runnels have made them: not singular, but recursive; not stable, but roiling. You could not say if these people are the same this year as last—they molt, divide, some ancient ones may persist for centuries beneath the stones, others blink out of being in a day, replaced by their own offcuts or the merged remnants of a dozen kin.

There is no lord nor chief, only a council of shadows: a shifting coalition, its voice a composite of scent, hive-murmur, and ritualized collision. To be a leader here is to be a rhythm—the one whose pattern others unconsciously follow for a time. When they sleep, if they sleep, it is in short, unsatisfying bursts, uncertain whether they have truly fallen out of the stream of motion at all.

Some outsiders claim these are not people, but a kind of living city—the dwellers and their labyrinthine hive inseparable, each the outgrowth of the other, neither possible without the ceaseless, uncaring cold. Others whisper that here, even the stones are alive, that the thin people are merely masks of a deeper mind.

**How They Want To Be Seen**

If they know of the world beyond the cliffs—if, in fact, any have ever left—they do not speak of it, nor remember it, nor yearn for it. To intrude here is to be mistaken: you are not prey, nor kin, nor threat, but anomaly—quickly adapted for, skirted around, possibly imitated, possibly ignored.

They do not welcome nor repel; if anything, they absorb, as glacier absorbs the stone—a slow, gelid patience, intent on its own inscrutable purpose. If you remain, you will learn to move as they do, until you too ripple with the flow, and your story is no longer strictly your own.

**In Essence**

What lives in the High Runnels is a people of the pulse: protean, glimmering, communal out of necessity, clever in famine, adaptive in cataclysm, artists of impermanence. Their world is the quicksilver moment—before the water freezes, before the sun declines, before the silence breaks—and in this tension, in this nonstop becoming, they are most entirely themselves: creatures of the high, bright, breathless edge, where even survival is a wild, uneasy artistry.
