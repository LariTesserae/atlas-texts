What lives here is not alive in any sense you recognize, not at first. There are no hearts, no roots gulping water or mouths thirsting for light. What endures here is the patient assembly of existence at its thinnest margin—life that is less an actor in the world than a quality of the inorganic world itself, a halting, mineral verse that almost forgets to recite itself.

These columnar beings, these mineral flora, inhabit the close and flawless dark like thoughts too slow for any waking mind to follow. Their bodies are made not of wood, leaf, or flesh, but of a crossbreed substrate—half stone, half something secreted from within, a compound gleaned by molecules pried from ancient resting beds and fitted together by glacial laminae of their own exudate. Their anatomy is neither open nor closed, neither clearly plant nor properly mineral; each rises bluntly from rock, with no visible mechanism for absorbing, breathing, or even growing.

But grow they do, though to notice it would require aeons—an inch of height per thousand lifetimes, a thickness gained in increments as fine as talcum slipping from finger to palm. Each time the powdery deposit settles anew, each time some chemical favor tips the calculus, their surfaces accrue, the shell thickens, and the column’s apex becomes infinitesimally firmer, rounder, more complete.

So they live: not by pulse or spark, but by the steady patience of chemistry at the coldest edge of possibility. When atom or molecule drifts close—perhaps carried on an exhalation of stone, perhaps simply attracted by the insistent deficit of the column’s outer layer—there occurs a meager exchange. The “roots” of these entities drill into the substrate not to drink or draw in, but as anchorage against time; their intake is more akin to osmosis through skin of glass, a sunless photosynthesis fed on voltage and sweetness generated in the meeting of mineral and dark.

No eyes, no sensory fronds, no nervous system. Yet each being harbors a slow inwardness: a registering of pressure, of warmth, of the nearness of a sibling column perhaps several feet distant. Some periodicity of expansion and retreat, at rates so slow that even centuries would struggle to comprehend, could exist, a gentle “breathing” in the rigid dark—tiny clicks bloom as inner chambers adjust the fit of layered matter.

Do they know each other? There is no communication as we know it, but perhaps a chemical vapor, heavier-than-air, trickles from one to the next when conditions are right, and for a span too long to observe, a nearby column’s composition subtly shifts—adjusting, responding, tending toward a static harmony. In one spot, where two columns almost brush, you might rest your palm and sense a glimmer in the waxy surface, an almost imagined pulse as molecules jostle just a micron beneath your skin.

There is no upward striving, no blind quest for light—only the satisfaction of being sufficiently present, sufficiently fused to this one patch of the ever-smooth basin. In their ridges and blunt points, their textures change with eons: a measured roughening where powder accumulates, a renewed glossiness wherever a rare exudate covers old wounds. They have no ancestors, not in the way animals or plants have—each is a product of solitary, iterative patience, the environment’s slow and unhurried dream of itself.

Their existence is absolute in its economy: when a spike fails, when an abnormal pebbling betrays a flaw, it sloughs and is left to fossilize where it falls, slowly merging back into the pock-marked plates below. This, too, is living—a rounding off, a surrender to silence, an unresisting part of the ever-worn floor.

There are no predators; nothing competes. Survival is not a struggle but a matter of persisting within the boundaries set by stone, warmth, and the dark. Occasionally, if the rock surrenders a trapped bubble, a sussurus of warmth traverses the stilled air; in that moment, the columns may respond with an imperceptible shift—growing almost, breathing almost, in solidarity. The community of these beings is not a web but a field, a spacing-by-choice or necessity that keeps each momentous in its reserved place.

To approach is to feel at the threshold of recognition. If you sit quietly, palm against a column, you become part of their ecology not by being noticed, but by consenting to the hush. Heat leaks from your body; you find your own breath answering the silence; powder collects in the lines of your skin.

Once you leave, their patience closes behind you, and in the never-breaking dark, what lives here returns to waiting, to the slow transaction of mineral and time, to the realization of life as absolute equilibrium. Not lifeless, never dead, always abiding—anchored under the invisible, immutable weight of unending night.
