Whoever lives here is not easily separated from the mountain, nor from the weave of green that clothes it. They do not arrive; they emerge from what is already present—the potential packed tight in humid understories and the tension of trunks straining toward the sun. To seek them is not to look for doors or settlements, but to notice divergent footprints in the logic of leaf and water, a patterning in the moss that is not quite accident.

Their bodies are mountain-shaped by old pressures: long and strong, jointed for climbing or anchoring on inclines, heavy in limb but surprisingly deft. Their skin is mottled and absorbent, made for living in air thick with rain and pollen—a living promise between plant and animal. Where skin meets the world, it glistens: a shifting luster—sometimes barklike, sometimes like the leather of wet leaves, sometimes so like moss that droplets bead and tremble on the surface. Arms and hands might be broad, paddle-like, phalanges slightly webbed for soaking up dew—or perhaps they run fissured, inlaid with supple ridges that match the flow of water, collecting nutrients almost as roots do in passing.

Their hair, if any, is not exactly hair—more clouded with fine, filamentous growths, host to lichens or blooms, not ornamental but symbiotic: gathering mist into tiny beads, maybe subtly photosynthesizing, smelling faintly of crushed fern and wet stone. Their eyes, deep-set for sun and shadow, are wide-pupiled, bright with the sheen of rainforest light; in low light, they catch the last flecks, let them linger all the way to thought.

Every motion is slow and deliberate, like vines tensioning toward another anchor, but can snap, animal-quick, in necessity. They move mostly unseen, pressing close to trunks, storming up branches with practiced, three-dimensional awareness—a choreography tailored to the space between: between branch and air, between root and stone. Their voices are seldom heard, more often felt: a pressure in the trembling of leaves, a low harmonic that answers the thrumming water.

Society, if such is found here, is diffuse—a network, never a cluster, with individuals spaced and solitary but in constant relation by scent, by song, by ripples left in pooling forest puddles. More than speech, they read the mountain's pulses: echoes in rock cavities, shifting leaf perfumes, the flavor of mineral seep, the footfall of rain. Information rides these channels, circulates much as nutrients do—partitioned, recombined, endlessly responsive to minute changes of context.

They craft nothing that could be called tool or edifice, but they **tend**: cultivating delicate ecologies on stones and fallen trunks, encouraging fruit or flower where the rains fall thin, curating patches of luminous fungus or vinework much as a human might train a garden. They host insect colonies in folds of arm and leg, or secrete sweet resins to foster traveling pollinators. In some sense, each is a moving companion to a dozen unseen communities—a composite being, part animal, part ambulatory terrain.

Their young are rare and precious, hatched or birthed where the mist is richest. They mature slowly, moving only in the rainy times, learning the weight of each layer—the thick mat of duff, the trickle-paths in bark, the cold clarity of mountain pools.

These people, if people is the word, are without clear border between self and home. Their own cycles sync to the fevered, disciplined churn of the mountain: periods of waking and work matching storms, times of hush and hiding in the dry moments. They are neither the first nor the only inheritors of this place—deep below, other shapes persist: things with translucent skins, bone-pale in darkness, venturing up only for the wettest weeks, then back to their mineral lairs.

Yet above all, what lives here is *adaptation alive and conscious*. Intelligence is not imposed atop the natural order, but inflected through it—creativity expressed in selective growth, collective maintenance, patient intervention across generations. They do not record; everything learned is held by body and place, in living feedback, in the processes that bind root and hand, fungus and mind, rain and memory.

To see one fully is almost impossible: step close, and they vanish into leaf-cast shadow, into pools of water that ripple and still, into the perfumed mist that always closes behind. You will know them by absence and choreography, by what flourishes in their wake, by the sense that every branch and stone has been considered, even loved, by something ancient enough to be part of the logic of the mountain itself.

What lives here is interleaved with place—old, heat-ripened, never fully alone, gifted with a patience and acuity made not for seeing beyond the mountain, but for becoming and sustaining it from within.
