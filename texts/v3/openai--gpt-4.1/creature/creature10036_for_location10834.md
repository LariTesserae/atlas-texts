What lives here is not a creature but a presence—vast, continent-bodied, both one and many.  To call it “the plant” is true and not true, as it is no single species nor even alliance: it is an epochal congregation, a matriot of life that refuses circumscription. Its roots and runners, stems and saps, bodies both muscular and filamental, are the plateau itself. Imagine intellects whose thought is expressed only through growth and demolition, whose motives wind and fork like slow rivers through centuries, whose memory is pressed deep within nutrient-rich cores of their own decaying matter.

This is not a world visited by animals as we imagine them. Animal life—when it appears—is a rumor, a shadow: agile, transient, concealed in the breathing thickets. The reigning organism—call it the Green Mass—contains within it spaces, tendrils, hollows, and forgotten, pressurized capsules where minute, translucent things cling to brief existence: scavengers of warmth, burrowers into saprotrophic excess, blink-quick and forever wary. Yet even they are little more than exhalations from the life-ecology, specialists for the tiny niches left unfilled by root or spore. Their populations are never steady, appearing and then failing as the world’s self-consuming metabolism rolls on.

The Green Mass is many-layered. The surface bristles and swells, mossed and thalloid, creeping and colonizing. Beneath lies the warm, dense press of root and tuber, knotted with what might be called neural tissue—channels of fluid sensitive to pressure, gradients of touch and moisture, capable of transmission and command. Communication here is a choreography of electrical pulses, chemical trails: when a new pool forms, sudden signals ripple outward, summoning growth and retreat, commanding lichen to curtain exposed surfaces, marshaling hyphae to clean or recolonize. Where light glances through mists, slow phototropic surges begin; in sheltered gullies, motley swathes of violet-pigmented bodies assemble to catch what little warmth there is. The world is aware—not with thoughts, but with endless, patient algorithms of branch and pulse.

Where there is danger—such as a shifting boulder, an incursion of something truly foreign—the Green Mass responds as a muscle does to a pinprick: rolling aside, enveloping, exuding compounds. It is defensive, not violent; the scale of its body is too large, its metabolism too slow, for bursts of pointless aggression. To survive here is to be incorporated, softened, transformed—if you are not already part of the flow, you become detritus, and then, in time, you are taken into the mat.

Yet life is not static or uniform. Certain regions of the plateau pulse brighter, richer, home to semi-autonomous “organs” that might almost resemble communities: pumps and pheromone fountains, bulbous intelligence-nodes, unfurling the subtle language of color and tension. Here the Mass invents variations, testing new forms. Sometimes a stalk uncurls, rising meters above the living crust, harvesting mist from the sky, feeding it downward through pulsing veins. In sheltered basins, reproductive superstructures fume spores in millions—vapor trails that glitter on cold, indirect rays.

Much of the Mass is ancient—tissue layered upon tissue, age made visible in sphagnum palettes and root-lattice. But some zones are feverishly new, dynamic. Changes cascade: a wave of runners crosses a slope, subsumes an old mound, builds a bridge across a rain-choked rift. Innovation is constant and capricious, a kind of problem-solving mind at play. The plateau’s fate is decided not by decision but by generalized intelligence, the distributed cognition of a billion semi-independent parts.

And if you wait long enough—days, maybe weeks—you might sense a kind of presence. The pooling and dispersing of fluids is not random; sometimes, entire slopes of lichen synchronize and pulse, like a heart. You might believe the Mass is watching you. If it is, it does so without eyes or faces, but with the totality of its surface—by feeling, absorbing your vibration, your breath, your temperature—a seamless sureness that every square centimeter is alive, sensitive, interconnected. It is as close to omnipresence as a terrestrial thing can come.

What lives here is a superorganism, more landscape than being, more being than any landscape Earth has known. It is the place. Its life is the hum of cycles: photosyntheses, chemosyntheses, acid and sugar, decay and recolonization. It is a patient mind, thinking in gradients and tides, pulling everything—stone, water, microbe, what little animal there is—into its embrace, and remaking it in shared, churning, vegetative form.

If you stay, you may become part of it, too—not consumed in violence, but cradled, slowly unwound, until your atoms are bright threads in the plateau’s endless tapestry. For nothing here is truly lost: only transformed, and always, always growing.
