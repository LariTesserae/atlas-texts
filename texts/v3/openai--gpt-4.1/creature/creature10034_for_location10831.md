Those who live here are not a people, not in the way of parliaments or pageants, but rather an eruption—an outpouring of animality, pressed from the clay of new earth and the urgency of untested need. They are a multitude, segmented and faceted, armored and soft, woven from possibility’s first draft. Theirs is a civilization without legend or memory, grown not from inheritance but from pressure, condensation, immediacy. 

The city’s inhabitants are present in numbers you cannot comprehend, as if every space must be filled; for if any nook is left unoccupied, the place itself fears it may dissolve back into the earth from which it is so hurriedly wrought. They swarm and wriggle and cluster, bodies matted against stone, wet against wet, chitinous exoskeletons smeared with the city’s perpetual condensate. Their movements etch out the city’s own rhythm: the rapid scuttle, the slow heave, the abrupt withdrawal. They never move tall—there is nowhere to rise—so they move wide, sprawling, pressed, splayed, bellies grazing the cold warmth of the stone.

Some are squat, bearing too many legs for comfort, torsos low and armored, their mandibles busy with wet crumbs of stone or scraping through the veils that dangle uncertainly. Their eyes are small and beady and sensitive, reflecting the city’s hungry twilight. Others are all spindles, delicate limbs folding triple beneath bellies like pale knots, their skin translucent, webbed by blue veins, salt-and-mineral sweat glistening on flesh. There are creatures with ovoid bodies and trailing, featherless antennae; round, moist beings who shuffle through the perpetual puddles, leaving mucus traces before being busily erased by a multitude of following bodies.

Their young are born not of eggs, nor by live birth, but by splitting—a colony-matter instinct, a fissure along a seam in the stone, out of which half-grown imitations wriggle. They peel away like wet bark from a surface, mottled and malleable, and immediately crawl into shadows, drawn to the ceaseless chorus. Growth is a matter of accretion—each creature thickens on the mineral film, drinks from the secretions that drip tirelessly, and adds to itself by digesting the layers of veiling, dimly biotic matter that clings to every hem and corner of this city.

They do not trade, for there are no things of desire outside survival; they do not war, for there is no territory outside the next available hollow. If there is hierarchy, it is fluid and brief, shifting with who blocks which passage, who arrives first at the freshest moisture, who bridges a gap with the greatest expanse of limb or shell. Life is a crowded negotiation, a game in which every participant is both piece and board. They do not mark time, but are marked by it—the only clock is the mossless, unfading rawness of what surrounds them.

If you glimpsed faces, you would see at once the intensity of presentness and the impossibility of memory. Their features are built for touch, taste, shimmer—palps and feelers, microhairs and slits that can sense heat from stone, pick apart minerals in the dark, sweep condensation from the air. All are designed for contact, rarely for sight, and all are marked by a certain unfinished symmetry, as if each was pulled from a mold still soft and learning what it should become.

They do not speak—not in air or gesture or song—but the city drowns in their communal sound: the rustle and scrape, the drip and squelch, the collision of many bodies with stone and each other. It is communication by friction, chorus by necessity, a thousand bodies pressed together sounding out presence by sheer vibration. In this, perhaps, their greatest communion is with the city itself, which holds, echoes, and contains their thrumming. To hear this city is to hear them all—never a lone voice but a saturated multitude.

Daily life is the maintenance of the city’s unfinishedness—constant movement, constant brushing and pressing, a smoothing and breaking, piecing and shuffling. Every creature leaves behind the smallest imprint: a pock, a scratch, a sheen where the body wore at the stone. Their civilization is sedimentary, layer after layer not of time but of press and pass and fleeting occupation. Their bodies polish the stone, shape the corners, and breathe out new humidity into the air, making the very stuff of their habitat perpetually fresh and unsteadied.

Even as the space is dense with their presence, there is a haunting sense that more are always being born, that more will crowd in, quicken, split, and flow through channels too narrow for the imagination. Yet this is not plague, nor collapse, for everything is held in a kind of miraculous stasis—a rush of being without progression or dissolution, an endless crowd just steeped in the city’s newborn air.

There is no story here, only arrival, only arrival, only arrival: a city and its countless makers, becoming themselves and their world in the silence before any other history, in a multitude articulated in stone and water and unyielding, humming breath.
