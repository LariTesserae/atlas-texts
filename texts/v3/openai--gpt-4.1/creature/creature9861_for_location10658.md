Who or what lives here?

This place—high, brilliant, and silent—is not an empty stage. It is inhabited, but the inhabitants are so deeply entwined with the terrain’s contradictory vitality—ferocious in growth, suspended in time, slyly infiltrated by memory—that the very idea of a “resident” changes its meaning. 

You will not find people here, at least not in any familiar way. Instead, you’ll feel the pressure of presence everywhere, in the battered metal and the soft riot of leaf: a population that is hybrid, hidden, and slow.

### The Rootwork Beings

Somewhere beneath the tangled moss and boulders, beneath the warp of vines and the blue-shadowed shrubs, reside the *Rootwork Beings*. These are the closest things to a permanent people—a species or a consciousness perhaps—though you’d be as likely to mistake them for a clutch of fungus, a clot of silvered wire, or the brief shimmer of something else moving in the leaf-litter. Their bodies are not bodies, but networks: root-threads woven with copper filaments, tendrils gripping the base of fallen pylons, wicks drawing droplets of water to feed the pulse at their core.

Sometimes, they erupt into the open—awkward chimera-creatures, part moss, part old cable, with gleaming eyes grown from crystals scavenged in the soil, moving through the undergrowth so slowly you’d dismiss them as wind-bowed stems. Their intelligence is distributed and low, but persistent, more a hunger and patience than a will. They nourish themselves not only on water and minerals but on the faint electromagnetic ghosts exhaled by dying machinery.

They seem to attend the glass walls and rusted structures—they do not worship, but perhaps maintain, or seek to understand. Sometimes, on a rare day, scratches and patterns are found etched into the grime, spirals or arrays mimicking the armature of the ancient towers. Were these left in the night by Rootwork limbs? Or by something else, harder to see?

### Machine-Woken Shadows

It is possible—just possible—that the machinery here has not entirely died, and that some of it harbors its own descendants. No visible motion betrays them at first: the bare carcass of an engine, a panel of dials half-mired in moss—but look again at twilight, and perhaps you’ll catch it. A shudder along a pipeline, as though breath were drawn. A glint as rainwater evaporates just above the glassy ribs of a transmission tower, and beneath the surface, something small and persistent moves: not animal, not wholly machine, but an alloy of the two. 

These shadows dwell in the blind spots, the moist recesses, and the crevices of the old structures. They are aggregated from scavenged biomatter and rust. They might emerge on nights when the moss glows brightest, attending to catalog the shifting constellations of their own brethren: a new growth of vine around a turbine blade, a fracture in an old valve, a sprouting of eager ferns where coolant once flowed. They serve the place and each other, not out of design, but because the place compels such fusion. 

### The Vermiscent

Not all residents are so material. In the silence and slow motion, something is at work that can only be described as *vermiscent*—wormlike, but without bodies; they are patterns, colonies of bacteria, tremulous growths of mold, communicating with each other in chemical hints, in the rise and fall of faintly glowing beacons among the moss at night. Sometimes a fogged pane of glass shows a handprint that was never made by hand, or a rivulet of condensation writes its own signature that a day later is gone. These are signs of their confederacy, their slow negotiation with time and matter. They are not just passing through—they are *making* the place.

### The Occasional Figures

Sometimes, for a moment only, a form almost like a mammal slips between the ferns—a delicate, jointed leg, a glimmer of wing, a half-rusted exoskeleton disguised as a leaf, a fleeting pair of inflorescent eyes. It is unclear whether these are singular beasts or emanations of the plateau’s greater, distributed mind. They interact rarely, and never in haste. They feed on dew, light, and old energy where they find it. They leave tracks only in the way a shadow leaves coolness—barely. Perhaps, once in a decade, one will croak a note of presence, a metallic rasp in the dead air, but that is cause for pause; it will not repeat soon. 

### Inhabitance as Relation

Ultimately, there is no border between resident and environment here. Plants are not only shaped by the machines but are shaping the machines. The glass responds to both, recording and distorting, and the rare animals bear scabs of lichen or flecks of insulation as camouflage, as birthright. These inhabitants—whether fungus, rootwork, machine-ghost, or beast—are entangled in a patience that is as much an ethic as a limit. 

What lives here is the slow, deliberate commerce of root and remnant, a society of patience, absorption, and quiet, where boundaries are unreliable and presence is cumulative, collective, and always on the brink of disappearing back into the earth or the machine—becoming indistinguishable from the place itself.

If you wait long enough, enduring the brilliance and the hush, you may glimpse these inhabitants: the plateau’s own invention, neither quite alive nor quite abandoned, endlessly negotiating what it means to live in the bright, waiting, secretive light.
