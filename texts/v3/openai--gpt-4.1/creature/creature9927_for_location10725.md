The Beings of the Gloam Galleries:

They do not stride boldly, these residents—few, if any, arrive here by ambition. Instead, they diffuse, seeping gradually, as condensation seeps, claiming hollow in what caverns remain, their forms shaped as much by the constraints of this world as by any ancestor or intention. They are subtle, meditative things: **People** only in the broadest sense, threads of continuity and adaptation tying them to surface folk, but unrecognizable in habit and outline.

**Their Bodies:**  
They are short, almost always, bodies compressed by generations of ducking, threading through narrow vertical interstices. Joints are flexed, spines a little bowed. Skin is sallow, olive or dun, sometimes patched with lichen-like freckles that mark a childhood’s worth of close, persistent moisture. Eyes are wide, the pupils dilated, seeking what little light can be spared. Some families cultivate reflective layers in the sclera, giving their gaze a milky sheen, like the bellies of cavefish or ghosts in low light. Their hair, when visible beneath scarves or utility hoods, tends to dark and damp, braided close to not catch on passing machinery or dripping vines.

What might pass for clothing is more often a concatenation of wraps and layered covers—oiled fabric, fungusleather, salvage netting quilted with barbs of moss or dried root. Metal bands catch the light at joints and throat, sensors or small heat-drawing pads. They value the functional over the ceremonial, ornaments made from fragments of what surface worlds toss away: a gleaming wire, a drill-bit, a glass bead that once was a lens.

**How They Live:**  
There is never much space—so they claim compartments, or craft “nests” lined with shredded insulation and the pilled fibers of moss, always nestled against the warmth of a pipe or the shadow of an overhanging fern canopy. Their beds hang from the walls like hammocks; their kitchens are mere niches crowded with jars of pickled stalks, compressed loaves of algae-flour, and the gleanings of nightly growth.

Families are less defined by blood than proximity, by patterns of shared and mutual care, by whose condensation gutters one’s food or air depends on. Children (those few who arrive) move like curious, miniature echoes through the tight quarters, marked with charcoal to map their safe routes, their silence practiced until it is near total.

The **Eldest** cloak themselves in trailing veils of waterproofed cloth, moving as stewards or archivists of memory. They tend the hydroponic walls, monitor the cycling of pumps, note minute shifts in heat and air-handling, passing warnings in finger-tapped code. Living here requires a constant tuning—a skill at reading the lives of machines as surely as those of ferns or kin.

**Work and Artifice:**  
Their labor is as much tending as making: coaxing plants to thread around cabling, harvesting edible stalks before they choke vital ducts, patching leaks, shoring up funguscrete where the weight of above threatens a collapse. Some are mechanics, others planters, all are watchful. Jewelry and script are made from droplets gathered and frozen, fossilized in resin, or from the colored films grown on bacterial mats, used to transmit slow messages or simply exchange as tokens of beauty.

Entertainment is fugitive. Music is hummed against shell and pipe, reverb resonating through the iron bones of the city. Stories are told in gestures amid the soft phospher lamps, voice never raised. Festivals are not common, but when they happen, entire communities collaborate to grow a burst of edible bloom, or to reroute a rivulet to produce a new jets of mist, a brief spectacle of glitter and shadow.

**Attitudes, Relations, Motives:**  
Their lives are circumscribed by the need for patience, resilience—not for conquest, but for surviving the slow, sure attrition of rot and repair. Outsiders would find them wary: their faces glimpsed and gone, their negotiations always indirect. They know that their world is brittle, that any intrusion could unbalance water or heat, tip some equilibrium and invite collapse. Generosity exists but is couched in the practical, a willingness to share what cannot be stored, or knowledge of the hidden paths and killswitches in a failing pump.

They are not isolated—news arrives through the trickle-down of salvage and rare visitors from upper levels, or the careful trade with the other subdistricts (who send rare fish, surface mushrooms, the frost-worn glass of abandoned outposts). But the Gloam folk do not rise. They do not seek expansion, only the renewal of what they have—to keep their lights burning and their moss lush, to remember that dim is not the same as dead.

**Others—Unseen or Quiet:**  
The vanished fauna leave traces. Albino cave salamanders nest in hot or idle conduits, their eyes gone, their skin nearly clear. Insects—graceful, almost-translucent things—crop the moss or thread their wings between machines and leaf-litter, vanishing if approached. A rumor lingers of something larger—perhaps a blind shambler, so rare it is more ghost than animal, living off heat and rot, emerging only in the longest lulls of sound.

Now and then, a thing happens—the bright green push of a new vine colonizing a forgotten machine, or a collapsed airshaft opening into a chamber never seen. The Gloam folk gather, quietly, to observe, to debate if this is danger or gift, to let the place decide if it will settle back into its ever-remade self. They are caretakers, not masters; inhabitants, not lords. The galleries re-make them, as gently and relentlessly as water shapes stone.

**Everything here lives by compromise:**
Not one thing alone but all entwined—people with moss, the iron with lichen, the cycles of sweat and heat. The Gloam folk are as much a product of their place as any mechanical pump or trembling green leaf, a society of humidity and shadow, built for survival, for hush, for the privilege of rooting quietly beneath the pulse of a world above.
