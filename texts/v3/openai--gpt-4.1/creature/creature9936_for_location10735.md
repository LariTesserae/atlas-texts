What lives here? Or perhaps: *what does it mean to live, here*?

Some things may simply *be*, barely distinguishing between living and unmoving—so patient, so circumscribed by the deep rules of the place, that all distances between life and structure have worn down with age and friction. Still, let us walk slowly, senses striving, to find the ones who belong most uniquely to this black, bound, endlessly metamorphosing garden.

---

### The Inhabitants: The Indwellers

#### I. **Body and Form**

They do not walk—at least, not as humans or animals understand walking. There are bodies here, but they manifest more as an *event* or *process* than a finished thing. To see one clearly, you must wait: over hours, a surface peels itself from the wall—a trunk’s tough bark opens, exhaling a *crisp* exhalation of dry, resin-scented air, and reveals a limb. Not a hand, nor a branch, but a *limb*—containing the intention of both climbing vine and cautious craftsman’s finger.

Their bodies are not single, whole organisms, but intricate assemblies: knots of articulated woody fiber, interleaved leaves and bulbous resin eyes, thick cords of root and lichen, their “skin” a composite of ribboned cambium and whorled hyphae. Each individual is an *accretion zone*, a place where organizing will gathers clinging fragments of plants, fungus, dust, and crystal, knitting them together with a viscous exudate that cures to brown-gold opacity in the dry air.

No two are the same in shape. Some are tall arching arcs, like the rim bones of a leaf. Others sprawl and flatten: mats, rosettes, or coiled forms with a single sensory stalk. There is neither face nor eye, but arrays of delicate, flexible filaments that sense vibration, scent, and the faintest changes in air pressure.

#### II. **Perception and Presence**

Sight, here, is a thing remembered and abandoned. The Indwellers “see” by pressure, by subtle oscillations of air and substratum, by odors carried on minute breezes—each sensed through radiant antennae grown for the purpose. To step into their presence is to be caught in a net of awareness: they feel your footfalls in the outermost layers, the way a root feels the fall of a tree leagues distant.

Their attention is vast but slow. What a mammal knows in a moment, they gather over hours: scents, the geometry of a moving presence, the tick of a tool, all marked like weather on the fabric of their extended bodies.

#### III. **Language and Memory**

These are not silent beings. Their language is the *sound* of the world—the arrhythmic groans, the percussive pops, the rattling shivers as wall and vault are flexed, manipulated, tested. They “speak” by leaning weight or torsion against the bones of the city, producing long, branching harmonics that arc from one occupied root-bundle to another. Some meanings are ephemeral—an alert or invitation. Others are records: a complex, repeated crackle pressed into the vault remains, echoing for days, instructing those who pass after.

Memory is communal, held in the architecture itself. Need to know how a rib vault was raised? Sample the under-strain harmonic with a split tendril, and the “taste” of that event, centuries old, will fill the dry air.

#### IV. **Activity and Art**

Indwellers shape the garden continuously. Their lives are works of architecture: they join and part roots, encourage the growth of certain bodies, stifle others, polish resin into reflective panels (not for sight, but to scatter scent), braid stiff fibers into slings and baskets, shape hollow vaults for storied sounds to linger in. Every movement requires weeks of preparation, every new chamber is a *season* of activity.

Their art is not separate from labor: decorations are vibratory lattices that sing when the city flexes, arrangements of blooms that open only in the low frequencies of certain communal gatherings. They cultivate phosphorescent blooms in hidden pockets—instruments for ritual, warning, or rare celebration.

#### V. **Sustenance and Reproduction**

“Feeding” is transmutation. They draw not sap or flowing water, but the last available minerals wrung from the ancient substrate, drawn up with the patience of mountains. Some break down dry moss into sugars and alcohols; others derive from the air, collecting and condensing volatiles in hidden, spiral organs. Most substance, however, is gained by rearrangement: to grow is to borrow, and to borrow is to promise to return. Death brings dissolution—their bodies crumble, taken up by smaller, less articulate scavenging forms, and pressed flat into the landscape, their memories encoded as physical stresses in the floor beneath.

Children, or offspring, are not birthed nor seeded in the ordinary sense. Rather, when an Indweller grows too vast and ancient, new knots of will cohere among the youngest, most active roots. A “child” is a gathering—a collectivity of potential, which will slowly shape itself from the gathered materials and the borrowed memories in songs and echoes.

#### VI. **Society and Solitude**

Community among Indwellers is a patient, overlapping chorus. There is little urgency. Any gathering must be negotiated over days, sometimes seasons. Cohesion, such as it is, forms not around kinship, but around the shaping of place: those who work a vault, those who echo a passage, those who mark a festival with their trembling blooms.

Solitude and integration are not opposed: an Indweller may subside, curl into a dormant knot for a century, then emerge to join or lead the construction of a new archway. Loss is rare—there is little that can erase their memory from the city, so long as the place endures.

#### VII. **Presences Besides the Indwellers**

There are other, smaller minds—fleeting, flickering things: scavenger-crusts, courier-tendrils, shadow-moss that answers only to the most ancient harmonics. Some occur briefly—mobile lichens that flake in and out of union, scribes that “write” histories in the subtle arrangement of moss and resin. These are not strictly People, but belong to this ecology’s pulse.

---

### In Essence

The beings who live here are not the absence in the “garden,” nor its memory, but its continuation: enduring, endless shapers, less like a species and more like a verb—the slow, dry, patient act of organizing living matter into city, communion, and self.

To meet them is to feel the place itself rearrange, a hush thick with ancient intent, the press of remote, inhuman patience, and the sense that if you waited long enough, you too might be woven into a careful, ringing geometry of root and fiber—a presence added to the city’s ceaseless, unending harmony.
