Who lives here?

They do, so many kinds of them, so densely that to try to single one out is a kind of violence against how this world lives — a mistake as severe as mistaking a coral reef for the single polyp, or the human tongue for just its taste bud. Nonetheless, you can feel them pushing against you — in the darkness, in the pressure, in the sound.

Their bodies are domed, padded, swaddled against one another in damp armor. Chitin is their masterwork, but not as Earth’s beetles wear it: it runs in overlapping, glistening scallops, in plates that ripple with every movement, flexing not just to locomote, but to speak. Some are no bigger than a clenched fist, others broad as seal mothers, a few rare bodies vast and slow as boulders. Their segmentation is total: bodies braided and jointed so that each narrow part can grasp at a film of mineral, lever against slick stone, or find crevices of shelter in the perpetual swarm.

Eyes — if any — are sunken, glassy beads, rueful vestiges, used to sense only the nearby glimmer of blue mineral phosphor, or to track the feeble chemoluminescence in distant, shimmering gels. Far more important are their listening plates: thin, fanlike membranes ringing each armored shell in layers, vibrating in the ever-present music of noise and pressure. They hear with their bodies, taste with their legs, savoring every shift in the density and flavor of the moist, mineral-rich air. Some bear rows of ciliary fronds, filtering what little nutrition floats; others rasp and grind shells upon stone, wearing grooves in the blue-pale mineral beds, exhaling clouds of invisible spores or breath-murmurs over the films that grow there.

Some live as gatherers, slow and inexorable, never straying far from pools where condensation gathers thick enough to drink. These ones are armored in matte gray, stippled with the patinas of generations. Their children trail behind in mute trains, clutching with sticky pads the way a barnacle’s spit holds to hulls. Occasionally, a larger one will part the mists — a behemoth, leathery and stolid, its progress sowing temporary chasms in the otherwise crowded expanse. These giants are grazers, their mandibles built for scouring stubborn films, tongue-plates brute enough to lever off entire crusts of mineral and gel.

Others are wanderers — more delicate, limb-rich, vulnerable to the tread of the larger herds. These have elongated bodies, with trailing vent filaments and clusters of hardened, clattering knees. They dart among the bodies, or swing up to dangle from overhangs, feeding on the trembling glows or siphoning the nutrient exudates the larger ones leave behind. Some are quick-footed, some seem to ooze with a boneless grace, rehealing themselves as easily as a film re-forms when brushed by water.

Predators stalk within this host — if stalking is the right word in this press of beings. They are not swift, but sudden and suffocating, built to sense weakness in the current of sound: a rhythm out of phase, a vibration that marks out injury or dying. These coil-bodied things bear thickened, pointed limbs, like awls, and instead of hunting alone, they work together, communicating through coded knocks and pulses, closing together on their prey before the swarm re-absorbs them into its anonymity.

All these forms are bound in their living by cycles older than their own bodies. They mate in choruses of density: not with visible congress, but with pulses, phosphor, and exhalations, spawning clouds of microscopic life that sift through the perpetual mist and settle on any surface left momentarily bare. Juveniles are pale, jelly-bodied, glistening as the film they feed on, and they swarm toward warmth — a warmth itself only another shimmer of crowded mass in this cold, rain-laden dark.

Yet there is, somewhere, individuality in the pattern: isolated, ancient things, older even than the scraping herds. In deep pockets beneath stone, coiled in their own secreted capsules, survive the rootless monarchs: hulking, many-limbed things with gristly, luminous carapaces, hoarding heat and chemical light in their bodies, barely ever moving, their slow, seismic booms setting the pace for the rest. Some say even deeper creatures exist, living almost mineral lives, more silent than their kin — crystalline larvae that, when conditions tip, burst into ephemeral, crawling columns before dying off and becoming only the next film, the next patch of glow.

In such a world, there is no empty space. The air is full of drifting, nearly invisible plankton-creatures — miniature versions of the armored swarm, feeding on whatever is lighter than itself. The silent films and mats are not just food, but kin — ancestral colonies, the patient, glowing under-layer out of which new forms always bud and where the dead are returned, parted out by raspers and dissolvers until only mineral and memory remain.

To visit here is to be pressed and suffused, hemmed in by breathing, feeding, ever-shifting life. Nothing is permanent; everything is process. There are no kings, no tools, only the unbroken congress of motion, layered upon itself, voices echoing out in the pressed and swollen dark.

Who lives here? All these, in forms too many to name, too alike and too varied to count. The endless multitude. The kingdom of pressure, shell, and sound.
