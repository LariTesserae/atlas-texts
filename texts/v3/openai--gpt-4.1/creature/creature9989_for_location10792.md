Who, or what, lives here?

**Waverunners:**
The most immediately visible denizens are the waverunners, a term as diverse as “bird” or “fish” once was. They are creatures of flowing muscle and articulated shell, built for strength and subtlety, some sinuous and eel-like, others squat and armored, but all tailored for the thick, rushing water and the unpredictable gravity of currents. Their limbs are clever and adaptable: jointed paddles that can lock rigid against the battering flow, unfurl into broad fans for soaring glides through the kelp-forests’ tangled arches, or flex into digits for gripping boulders and bark in the lull of eddy-shadows. Their hides are a patchwork of scales, velvety fur, or smooth chitin, marked with shifting, iridescent bands — not just camouflage, but language, flashing patterns to each other in bursts of color that shimmer even in dim, clouded light.

Faces are variable: some broad and whiskered, their senses arrayed for low-light prey; some globular, studded with electroreceptors that read the hidden pulse of nearby engines and the ghost flickers of distant movement. Their eyes are never simple—at least two, sometimes six, watching in all directions with corneas wide and vivid, capable of seeing the faintest shudder of silhouettes through ground-glass gloom.

Many of the waverunners are tool-users. The cleverest hoard fragments of old metal, smoothen shards into tools, or encase their own claws in rings and cuffs of salvaged alloy. They nestle among the sunken relics, fitting passing turbines with networks of woven kelp or sponges as both shelter and snare. Some even shepherd threads of living machinery — symbiotic “cordweed” that grows along circuit traces, coaxed into conducting filaments and living lights.

**The Architects:**
Beyond the animal commotion, there are the older intelligences — “Architects,” though neither truly ancient nor wholly sapient as we mean the word. Hybrid of animal lineage and mechanical inheritance, their bodies are modular and mutable: plated heads and branching limbs spilling from shell-houses part natural, part constructed, their bones seeded with ferrous crystal and bioengineered semiconductors. The Architects think at a slow, tectonic pace, less as individuals than collective memories. Each is the sum of countless generations’ attachments — literal: replacement limbs, faceplates adorned with dust-polished symbols, cognitive cores passed like heirlooms from shell to shell. They move rarely but with purpose, rearranging the broken mouths of turbines, scoring patterns into rock to redirect the coursing water, modulating the thrum of old power stations to coax their mysteries back to life.

The Architects are gardeners and engineers both, and the distinctions blur: where the kelp is thickest, you find their marks — ranks of boulders reshaped into terraces, kelp-gardens seeded for selective growth, old pipes now braced as nurseries for filter-feeders. Their industry is not civilization as we understand it: it is the slow, generational stewardship of a living, unruly world, their legacy carved into the ecology itself.

**The Airsingers:**
Near the upmost water, where the planktonic blooms form airborne continents in the dim light, you sometimes see the airsingers: immense, slow-moving beings, shaped almost like inverted umbrellas or great, undulating ribbons. Their bodies are permeable to the thin, aerated water near the “surface,” trailing streamers of bioluminescent tissue that shimmer like auroras in the half-light. The airsingers swallow air-bubbles and rain, producing low, thrumming bellows that can be heard as far as the valley floor — communication and navigation both, said to direct the seasonal migrations of countless schools of life below.

Legend (or observation) suggests some airsingers carry within them pockets of air, micro-habitats for minute scavenger-castes, submarine “birds” and insectile creatures that feed off what’s caught in their furrows and tented membranes.

**Hidden Folk:**
In the dark, cold fissures and behind the pumice-banks, the hidden folk thrive. Small, nimble, crustacean or reptilian, they make good use of tunnels and layered burrows in the old mountain stone. Many are communal nesters, insulated by matted fur and shared heat, farming beds of sapphire moss and dull palates of plankton on the smoothest rock. Some are technical, repairing or at least reusing segments of ancient cable, weaving them through their dens: perhaps little more than opportunistic scavengers, perhaps, sometimes, the last link in the chain of machine-keepers begun by the Architects.

**Ghosts of the Past:**
And everywhere are the vestiges of the old civilization, not in ruins alone but in living descendants: bioengineered organisms designed for lost utility, now gone feral. Filter-crabs with sockets for vanished sensors, photosynthetic reptiles that cluster near old streetlamps powered by persistent but flickering grids, fungal “brains” overwriting broken fiber lines, stirring up faint, accidental intelligences in the neural networks of old infrastructure.

**No Single Dominion:**
Who lives here? There is no central, ruling intelligence. This place is a web of sovereignty, a domain where mind and wildness, industry and tumult, all intermingle and re-weave with every generation. Some beings build, some remember, some simply persist — yet in their tides of birth and loss, tending and abandonment, the place itself is alive, mind-like not in its inhabitants alone, but in the ceaseless, boisterous, aching churn where they all meet: valleys of icy water, old stone, fluttering filament and polyphonic noise, a civilization always on the edge of dissolving, always recondensing in the ever-coiling, ever-thundering surge.
