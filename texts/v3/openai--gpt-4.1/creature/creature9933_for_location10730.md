They do not have names, these dwellers, for names are the signals of distinction and novelty, and nothing here is new—nor needs to be individuated. Yet, within the Cathedral Hollow, a multitude persists. They are not a people gathered by choice or kin, but the necessary inhabitants of a system in such endless, patient equilibrium that the idea of being apart from it is unthinkable.

**The Great Root Sybionts**

The oldest, deepest citizens are not animals, not even plants as you’d recognize them; they are the *Sybionts*—vast, continent-boned root-entities whose massive, woody sinews arc overhead and interlace in the living naves. Their lives are measured in glacial pulses, their minds, if such concepts apply, are a sort of diffuse memory—in the signaling of sugars, the shifting of ions, the slow flex and response of cellulose and cortex to the world’s minor provocations. Where you see exposed root and vaulting buttress, you see only their outermost dermis. Their immensities spiral below, into stone and ancient detritus, forming the Hollow’s petrified underfloor and its ribs above. They do not move, but they do *change*, imperceptibly—adjusting, fraction by fraction, the tension of each span, the movements of condensation, the pressure of the groaning sap-streams that wind through them like thought.

**The Curators: Palecreepers and Tendril Moths**

Where the curtain creepers hang, curtain soft and muscular, small shapes wait—never flying, not really crawling, but shifting in the terminal stillness. Some are like moths, their wings long since guttered into mere sensing membranes, tuned to chemical signals and the brush of dew moved by another’s passage. Others—the *Palecreepers*—cling in twisted repose, little more than jointed cages quickened by the atmosphere, their faceless heads nested with tactile hairs and olfactory pits. Their purpose is curatorial: to collect the stray spores, the fugitive drops of detritus, the minute failures of the system, and reposition them where the biome demands. They move only when the pressure builds—never in disorder, never disturbing the air.

**The Mycoform Choir**

Upon the mats and on the fungal toes that sprout from every hollow and ledge, clusters of *mycoform animals* persist. Each appears as a bulbous, stationary polyp—a cross between a puffball and a sedentary beast. They anchor themselves across the cathedral floor, extending feathery cilia to taste the air, or extrude slow-moving pseudopods to trace the condensation back toward their own bodies. Occasionally, they pulse in unison, sending forth a fugitive mist of spores—yet, in this place, even reproduction is a form of preservation, not colonization; the new settle where they are, growing only as necessity dictates.

Many in this Choir are not singular beings, but collectives—symbiotic unions of fungus, lichen, and mite-like things that share metabolic cycles. They neither hunt nor flee. Their life’s work is decomposition in miniature, patient fractionation, turning fallen hyphae into new substrate, then feeding it into the slow grind of the Hollow’s metabolism.

**The Peripheral Avians and Echo-Birds**

No winged thing crosses the vault directly. Instead, half-seen *echo-birds*—grey, root-camouflaged, with eyes grown huge and unmoving—perch in the high, sound-reflecting recesses. Their outlines are lumpy, as if they’ve grown around the bark itself, and their calls are not so much songs as a kind of periodic signaling: a warning, perhaps, to possible newcomers, but mostly a marker for their own kind, maintaining their fixed distances. They may eat bugs—fat-bodied, carapaced, sap-suckers that live in the cool—yet never in pursuit or violence, but as a byproduct of closeness, a slow gathering in the niche.

**The Paladin Beetles and Endless Procession**

Beneath, in the root-walls and threadways out of sight, is an infinite traffic of minute, armored-creeping things. *Paladin beetles*—so called not for war, but for their tireless patrol—clamber in narrow files, bearing with them fragments: a droplet here, a flake of cellulose there, passing them to mandible to mandible with clockwork devotion until the resource is placed, consumed, or reabsorbed. These beetles never breed nor die in this place; instead, their number is maintained by the root itself, extruding new forms as needed with no discernable individual tragedy or birth.

**The Unsleeping Murmurers**

In the deepest shaded valleys, where dew pools in root-cradles, slumber pale, amphibian things—unsleeping, because nothing here sleeps. Their skins glisten with secreted oils; their gelatinous eyes clouded white, for sight is useless. They feed on the vapors exhaled by the fungal ground, or simply absorb the moisture, lying together in patient heaps, as if waiting to be called forth for some purpose which has not changed in countless generations.

**A Mind at the Center—Or None at All**

If you stand long enough in the living void, you may feel a sense of attention—not eye or ear, but a circulation, the immense distributed consciousness of the Sybionts working in tandem with their rooted choir and their insect industry. They sustain and respond without agency, but with perfect custom and regulation, enacting rules older than weather. To be here is to be monitored—not judged, not threatened, simply noticed and placed within the pattern of cyclical, unshifting life.

**Who lives here?**

All those described above, and more, reside—not in tenancy, not as stewards, not as conquerors or as prey. Each is a function, a performer in a never-ending liturgy of metabolic process. They process moisture, sound, and light, never altering their core, never hoping for change. No dreams, no sorrow. Their patience is not stoic, but innate—what life would feel like as unyielding harmony, as an ancient machine so well tuned that no part is distinct from purpose, and nothing—truly nothing—is wasted.

So, too, perhaps, does the visitor come to dwell within—not as an intruder, but as a momentary permutation, accepted, absorbed, and—eventually—put into balance with all the rest. For in the Cathedral Hollow, it is not a question of what lives, but of what endures, working—always working—in a cathedral that is older than dreaming, and far, far less forgiving.
