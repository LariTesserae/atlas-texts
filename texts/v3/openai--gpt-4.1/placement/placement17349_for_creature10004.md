If I find myself somewhere in this world—if I let the vision enfold me rather than stand above it, pronouncing—then here’s where, and who, I am:

---

I am not new here, nor wholly native. I am no child—no apprentice whose lungs ache with every errand above the vented bridges, nor veteran whose knuckles squint with alkali-white scars. Yet I walk unworried in the pre-dawn haze, liminal—a watcher, a fixer, a listener for tremor and flaw. I am called (in the clipped Plateau phrase) a *listenant*: one whose labor is in attention, in reading what cannot be seen, touching what must not be forced.

My home is midway up the city’s broadest promontory, a narrow metal-walled chamber nestling beside a steam vent’s steady plume. Its windowless side opens via a swath of sliding mesh to catch the plateau’s heaving breath. I sleep fitfully, waking (like many) to the deeper changes—from the machinery below, yes, but also from some thickening in the air, a rhythm caught by body and bone before ear or mind.

Each day starts at the relay-tower’s shadow, eating roots and salted fish, my visor fogging as I read the stones: checking for seepage, for oily slick, for a muting in the hum. My toolbelt is ritual as much as necessity—chime rod, pressure reader, sampler for what trickles through basalt to feed new veins of moss.

When work calls, it’s not with orders but with anomaly: a valve shuddering off-rhythm, a shimmering along a section of rail where the plateau’s breath warps sight and sound. Then I go—down pale stairs, across a causeway where the air is alive with engine thunder and the metallic scent of unseen rain, down again into the city’s lower skin, where the light grows odd and colors bleach toward grey.

I am, by turns, mechanic and mediator: tapping pipes to hear the story of pressure, settling cross-legged where water pools beneath the vents, closing my eyes to the flutter of pale insects as I listen past the surface, searching for the soft heart of stone, the fret of machinery against the will of the land. It is not always clear what I seek. Sometimes, what I fix is only tension, a tightness in the way the city sits atop the plateau—as though the earth beneath dislikes haste, and rewards patience with silence, punishing the rest with fracture.

On certain days, when the rock sweats in the sunless heat, my part is almost ceremonial. I make the offerings—a curl of wire, a scatter of dried peas—leaving them by a weeping wall where others rarely go. I light no lamp in the shadow-bent tower before midnight, and I teach the apprentices why: not through threat or tale, but by letting them stand in the hush and feel their own hearts race at the lopsided pattern of the dark.

I am spoken to seldom, but trusted much. It is a privilege earned over years, and not lightly given—most Plateau folk have rooms at city’s edge, as far from machine-thrum as possible, while I keep to the centers, to the pulse of things. I am neither boss nor drudge, but intermediary: between human and city, machine and plateau, between what wishes to move and what endures.

Sometimes, at dusk, when even the steam is pink and the horizon wider than hope, I climb out above the loudest tunnels and look for the pale-winged scavenger, circling. I mark its turning with a pressure on the wrist, a private semaphore. The birds fly as machines work: present, patient, never hasty, marking weather and time less for harvest than for knowing when to fold in, to wait, to endure.

In the thick of the city, amid clangor and hiss, I hear things others don’t: the way a weeping wall’s trickle doubles on humid days, or how the echo from a dropped bolt hints at a subtle hollowing—water eating out new rooms beneath the ancient stone, a future fissure that must be honored before it becomes disaster.

At shift’s end, I join the slow chime with the others, standing on leveled stone and feeling the resonant hum, letting it fill my chest—lungs stretched, skin taught with vapor and metallic tang. I am stone-touched, not stone-bound. I will change, in time; I will become part of the slow memory of this high place, my work recorded in the soft-dark moss that follows every leak, in the hang of old wire along the handrails, in the etiquette of careful, measured labor.

If I were more than myself—one of the city’s deeper presences, perhaps—I might slip unnoticed among the sweating shadows, part sinew, part sound, neither quite human nor quite ghost, but I am content with my lot: between things, slowing the world for just a moment, so the ancient plateau remembers mercy, so the machinery can groan and live, so water—brief and rare—can breathe its way through stone.

---

Where am I in this world? I am in the intervals—between steam and stone, between the plateau’s slow exhale and the engines’ endless grind—listening, waiting, tending. I am a Plateau Listenant, a keeper of pace, a witness, one among the few who has learned that endurance, above all, is a conversation: not conquest, not surrender, but a patient, measured, never-ending truce.
