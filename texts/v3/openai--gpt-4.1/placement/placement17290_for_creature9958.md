I would find myself on the edge of the settlement—not at the heart, nor quite on the unbound rim—where a low wall turns a sharp angle, offering a seat at sunrise and a vantage for watching the slow exhalations of cloud as the day begins. Here, I am neither entirely within the warmth of the town nor wholly exposed to the austere openness ringing the basin. This is the threshold, a place for those who are watchers, quiet stewards of change in a world that abides above all in stability.

Who, then, would I be? Not a founder, nor an elder thick with the village’s rote wisdom; not a restless youth dreaming of the distant, softer valleys. Instead: the keeper of water, the watcher of weather, a role both mundane and sacred to this town.

In each house, water is a question drawn fresh every morning from the stone’s embrace and guided by the soft hands of the careful, but I am the one who knows the sum. My days revolve around the cycles of slow accumulation and sudden absence. I tend the runlets and stone channels, listen for the message in a gutter’s trickle, read the weave of dark moss against old stone for hints of hidden damp. I am the memory of rain, the wordless chronicle of drought, keeper of the keys to the cistern lids—trusted because trust, here, is never lightly given.

*I rise before the well-lit sky*, climbing out from blankets roughened by age and care. My breath condenses in the interior’s gloom. My clothing is layered and practical, patched at elbows, streaked in places with the green of lichen or the gray of stone-dust. My hands are always cool, nails clean but edged harsh with lime and soil. I walk each morning’s spiral: from highest rim to deepest well, pausing where dew collects thickest, sometimes gathering it in soft-wiped cloths to squeeze into waiting pots. I peer into each water channel, clear blockages, sometimes speak softly to the stones—not because I believe in reply, but because habit runs as deep as the cracks in the plateau.

My hut stands slightly apart—not solitary, but marked by proximity to the well and the work of the water. On its wall hang small tools: the careful, thin-bladed knife for paring away moss clogging a spout; the broad, shallow scoop of shaped wood; the line and hook for lowering a lamp into the trickiest seam in which water sometimes hides. I am not overly skilled in agriculture—that is another’s realm—nor do I keep animals but for a single old goat, whose companionship is of the silent, slow-breathing kind.

On days when a traveller tops the ridge, I am, more often than not, the first to see them—the only one whose path ranges outward by necessity and routine. Yet even then, I am never the first to greet. True welcome remains the charge of the council or hearth-keeper. I only observe, noting how their passing might press new weight on our reservoirs or prompt a review of the water stores.

At night, I sleep lightly, attuned to the muttering of wind against the eaves, the faintest drip in the well below. My dreams are dry and spare—occasional wild floods, imagined torrents; more often, a satisfaction in the evenness of the balance sheet, each entry a drop of persistence.

*My life’s meaning is a math of endurance and mercy:* not only for the village, but for the land itself. I mark what is enough, and I say when we must do without. Sometimes I remember when I was young, a child trailing behind the old watcher, learning the taste of stone-filtered water from different corners, reciting the rhythm of the channels—a secret song, but also a ledger.

Yet in truth, though my work is the practice of calculation, I also belong to this place in a subtler way. The water, the stone, and I—we are all shaped by scarcity, by a fragility that does not admit drama or waste. My eyes, accustomed to the clarity of the plateau, have grown keen for small, bright things: the single flower gracing a shady niche, the first beads of dew on an otherwise arid slab. I have learned, as all in this town do, how to praise what endures not by changing it, but by recognizing and preserving what it is.

In such a place, I am never the center, but always essential, as quietly integral as the water that seeps through stone. My existence is both act of faith and continuous negotiation—the pact of balance, which is the plateau’s—and the town’s—oldest law.

If you, reader, walked this plateau, you might meet me first by accident: not in the ring where others gather, nor expecting introduction, but bent quietly beside a guttered slab at the break of day, dipping my hand to test for dampness, reading the bare script of water and stone. If you asked who I am, I would give a name, but it would not hold all my belonging. Instead, I would offer you a seat out of the wind, a cup drawn from the clearest store, and a pause: a moment to listen with me to the quiet ongoing of this place, where nothing changes except as it must, and every drop is a patient, silent miracle.
