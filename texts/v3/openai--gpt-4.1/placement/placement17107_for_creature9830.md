I open my eyes—if I call them eyes at all, for in this place, the definitions are pliant as the stones are hard. I come to awareness not in sighs or thunders, but in the subtle, constant hush that inhabits every inch of the plateau: the world’s own first breath, barely exhaled, neither rushing nor withholding discovery.

**Where am I?**

I am among the veiled beginnings—not beside an old riverbank, nor lost in ancestral forest, but upon the uplifted stone: that unweathered tableland rising, its edges precipitous yet softened in the mind by constant runnels and mirrors of water. I brace myself on cool rock, damp but not slick, feeling the newborn angularity beneath, the pools and ribbons a cleverness of the elements, not yet dulled by erosion’s patient schooling.

Here, vision encompasses all things. I see the neighboring settlement, distant yet present—never close enough for murmurs to overlap, never far enough to forget other existences moving on kindred ground. The horizon is always visible, beckoning without threatening.

Stone, water, green: the threefold matter of the world. And the gap between them—the spaces, the restraint—feels as tangible as moss in my palm.

**Who or what am I?**

I come into being neither as a child of long lineages nor as a beast evolved from hardship, but as a consciousness native to the plateau’s first stretch. I am, perhaps, a human—if human means a body made for patience in newness, for slowness in daylight. Or perhaps I am the plateau’s own shape, borrowed to bones and skin: not large, not small, sturdy in limb, hands like quiet tools.

I am flecked with the world’s colors—somewhere between sunlight and wet clay, a mica glinting beneath my skin, hair tied for practicality rather than display. My feet test the cold surface, never trampling; my eyes adjust to the steadiness of light, wide and peaceable, as patient as the days here.

I dress as these people do, cradled by humidity: plant-fiber woven close, a tunic clinging with each mist, a leaf-cape shrugged up against the rare chill. At my wrist is a copper cuff, grave with meaning; my fingernails are broad and deft, gently prising a moss mat for a distant pool.

**My presence, my life**

Though the world feels clean and untouched, I find that I am not its first impulse—there are others here, spread out across the scape, each group contained and considerate, their settlements shaped by distance itself.

I rise with the damp dawn, stepping out from a low doorway woven of bark and leaf, floor still cool from the night’s passing condensation. My day is an unhurried series of touch, attention, and calibration: collecting the dripping water from a stone ledge for irrigation; tending the moss at the runnel’s edge; sharing a low-voiced interchange with kin—not crowding, never imposing. We are careful. We walk the old-new paths over walkways, pausing at crossings to check if water levels are as needed, hands stained with the dust of yesterday’s labor but never ground down by it.

If I labor, it is as much with the land as upon it. My hands find the tender juncture where green seeks purchase in cleft stone; I nudge moisture to the most thirsty root, knowing the act is both survival and veneration. Any tool I use is simple, stone and wood, occasionally copper-sparked—a hammer shaped not to dominate, but to fit the hand in dialogue with rock. Each thing is enough; too much would be an affront.

**Sensations, understandings**

The world is quiet, but never dead. The rare patter of a falling drop is a bell that says: it is time, change is minute, patience is the only craft. In the soft spaces, my sense of purpose unfolds not as ambition, but as careful attendance: nurturing moss with slivers of water, coaxing a stubborn stalk from its crack, fashioning another simple basket, its mirrored token a promise that presence leaves no scar.

I am new here, but carry, already, the hush of endurance in my gestures. My world is not timeless, but wisely unformed, equally resistant to both decay and excess. There is no violence, for there is no enemy; no haste, for there is no threat. Each act is part of the silent arrangement between fresh stone, persistent water, and the touch that binds their meeting.

**In my place**

I am never alone, but I am never crowded. The boundaries are woven as green bands between settlements—respect for the hush that defines us. I learn by repetition, by gentle correction, by the patient humility of elders who teach not by word, but by rhythm and restraint.

I am not an origin myth, not a founder, not even a hero. I am—indelibly—a being of the plateau’s “now”: not longing for a past, not striving towards a future, but rooted in this inaugural moment that endures.

Perhaps, in another sense, I am the plateau’s own wish for itself, personified—a keeper, caretaker, and kin, living in the perpetual first day, never crowded, never rushed, ever open to water, stone, and mute green.

And if you walk here—step quietly. Wait. Take your time until your presence feels as still and open and quietly honored as the land’s own. It is only then, in the patient hush, that you, too, will know both who and where you are.
