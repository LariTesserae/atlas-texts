Where would I find myself in this world? Who or what would I be?

---

I wake, and what wakes with me is not quite the self I know from other dreams, nor quite the memory I keep when I have risen and look at other days out a familiar window. In this place—stone-bound, light-laced, ever-cool and ringing with the measured patience of work—I surface as a tender of lines not drawn on paper but mapped in the living body of the city.

I am not exactly a person as I once was, although I walk upright and might greet you as a friend. My hands remember both the newness of graphite stone, the minute textures where moss roots probe, and the satisfaction of steering a slow, straining tram across tracks beaded always with condensation. Perhaps I am called a lineskeeper, though the word is spoken less than lived—one who ensures that what connects, flows; that the routes which water finds, the tracts on which trams pass, are always a little damp, faintly gleaming, and never quite overrun.

**My dwelling stands on the concave lip of a minor channel.**  
Each morning, it is the glisten of mineral film across worn flagstones that calls me outside: the promise within water’s slow journey from the heights to these inlet grooves. No door needs locking, for the city holds its own boundaries, and what strays within is only what belongs—amphibians, children chasing after starlings, the accidental drift of pollen. My shelter is stone and sheet-metal, glass in frost-patterned panes, its walls alive with the slow advance of green and the burble of a tiny drainage rill. Pipes and rails cross in view, and every day I listen to the layered chorus: the engine-house’s somnolent thrum, birdcalls in the moss-tufted eaves, wind teasing water from already wet leaves.

In the city’s logic, my task is twofold:

- **I guide the old tram that traces the inner ring**, coastering between hollowed stops. My passengers are never in a hurry. Each route is a slow circuit—a way for elders to observe new sprouts in the moss, for technicians to check steam escapes, for children to ride and learn the city’s pulse.
- **I walk the cracks and boundaries each afternoon**, noting where water collects too long, where a pipe hums with strain, where birches reach toward some new, uncertain ledge. I carry a pouch with wrenches, spools of copper thread, a handful of river pebbles to set as markers, and sometimes, tiny shrines to place in fresh crevices: a bolt gleaming in the sun, three white stones nested in moss, a feather caught in loop of wire.

**I am never alone in the truest sense.**  
The city’s other dwellers regard me as kin, or at least as recurring presence—like the black dog sleeping on the tram’s rear seat, or the crows that gather when I halt to share an apple’s core. Human neighbors are in sight, rarely in earshot; we share signals by lantern, gestures by hand, and linger for dialogue in the glow and hush of the drainage house. My favorite companion is a frog with glassy sides who has built her kingdom in the overflow pan under the platform’s heating grid. I watch her life as carefully as any gauge, grateful for her presence.

**My language is motion, repetition, patient tending.**  
I know the names of every main line and every moss-bank where rails vanish to green. Sometimes, I hum the old shifting-song—a pattern of notes that tells the engine when to ease off or when to ready steam. I am a mechanic, a moss-gardener, a watcher at the threshold of both structure and seep.  
Some days, I pause by a shrine at the rising edge of stone: half-arch, half-engine bracket, adorned with offerings—screws polished with years, ferns rising anew through a wash of coins, whatever charm another city-dweller believed worth the leaving. I leave mine too, sometimes only a silent gaze, sometimes the pebble I polished in my pocket all day.

**When dusk presses the city, and the mists coil up under the lights, I am present—soaked to the shins, boots dark and hands chilled, under a sky that will never shelter true night but always refract the strong, almost mineral light. I make my way with the city’s slow heartbeat, a participant in the mutual persistence of machine, moss, and moving water.**

Perhaps I am only human—an inheritor of function and enduring rhythm—or perhaps, in this place, each worker, tender, and watcher is slightly more than that: a node in a slow, semi-conscious pattern that ties machinery to lichen, pipe to birdflight, stone to light. I dwell in the city not as its owner or builder, but as one of its primary senses, a being whose purpose is to mark and respond, follow the slow negotiations between wet and dry, between vigor and rest.

I know nothing of beyond; the city is all my world. To dwell here is to commit to its patience, its mutual work of tending, to be neither master nor mere servant but, rather, another surface where water beads, another point where light lingers and changes what it touches.

In this city, I am lineskeeper, tram-driver, boundary-watcher. I ride and walk the space between machine and moss, part of the lasting story told not in haste, but in the careful, continuous tending of what the city and the earth wish to become together, under the shining, unsoftened light.
