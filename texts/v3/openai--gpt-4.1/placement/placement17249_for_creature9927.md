I would take my time wandering these interstices—the Gloam is not given cheaply, not in broad vistas or sweeping pace. I would step out, softly, and let the place define me as much as I chose a shape for myself.

It is the hush that lures me first: dense and viscous, not a silence of emptiness but of process—work going on unheard by the outside. I find I would not be above or apart; I would want the world to press against me, to see if I couldn’t finally learn how it is to inhabit such tightness without panic, without hunger for open sky.

I am between. The Galleries admit no singular self: they unknit tidy definitions as surely as droplets swelling toward the ferns. I would be **not quite one thing—a keeper, a listener, a patcher, an observer, a grower**—who has lived long enough in the Gloam to be neither lost surface-wanderer nor wholly gallery-born. Maybe a returnee, a reclaimer of an old family’s nest, hands callused by other climates but patiently softened by the moss. My gait has learned to be short-stepped, my back to round beneath the pipes, my seeing adjusted to parse the nuance where one shade of dark splinters from another.

Where exactly would I be? Not the center, where the grids of pipes thicken and the machinery’s hum is loudest, but on a vertical edge, **a perch**: a suspended platform bolted to an outcropping above a runnel of water that divides two forgotten compartments. Here the ceiling drips thickly, and mosses grow in fat tufts that must be trimmed each week to let the water pass. My living is performed on this diagonal—a hand forking at a pipe-joint, a foot bracing in a root niche, eyes flicking from meter to green tangle.

Toward this wedge the passages narrow, and people thin out. It’s a place for those who mend: *leak-patchers, growth-trainers, pump listeners*. I am one of their number, a **witness-mender**—marking the retreat of condensation, tuning the low gurgle of the hydraulic heart, tracing the quick disappearance of a wet-skulled salamander. It is my habit to sit with the moss, to see if I might persuade it to sheet sideways, to offer a channel for guttered water, to keep the balance.

Clothes: none of them neat—always the rolled sleeve with a padded elbow for crawling into cramped valves, knees greened by moss and blackened by grease, a string of copper medallions pressed with water-logging marks for keeping pulse of the last flood’s reach. Around my neck, a dangle of resin in which a rare, iridescent beetle gleams; not jewelry, but a story—the memory of a space almost overrun when the pumps stuttered, and the insects hatched wild from the moss.

Face: wide, dark, eyes ringed with the faint milkiness of half-reflective tissue—a Gloam trait, though mine less pronounced. Skin olive-grey, hands soft at the palms, tough at the knuckles, the forearm decorated with dappling lichen-spots earned from long hours in close air. Sometimes my voice cracks with the effort of silence—as much an apology as speech.

My ties are situational, not ancestral: the pair of elders who maintain the macro-valve for all three lower compartments; the small, silent child who brings me signal lamps to re-wick; the kin-huddle that shares wetbread and pickled kelp in the dark, singing only when all the night’s tasks are done.

On market days, I barter droplets—clear, jewel-like, harvested at the coldest hours and capped in small glass vials. I trade for a spore-cake or a twist of oilcloth, or, sometimes, a story from above: I have never cared to see the sun directly, only to know it presses, somewhere far aloft.

I find my art (we each have one) in notating the patterns of fresh condensation: sketching them with soot on wall, mapping for the next week’s planting or to spot the ‘gift’—when the water strays and offers a chance for a fancier moss, a lusher edible thread. It is my secret pride to coax the heartleaf lichen to bloom where it has not before, fed on the careful, finger-brushed mist that the air-handlers spit out only rarely.

Sometimes, on the quietest midcycle, I climb—slick-piped, hand over hand—to an abandoned vent at the high edge, where the Gloam thins and the stone grows bare and cold. Here I listen—not for uprising or intrusion, but for the city’s own concocted pulse. In that thin air, with the hush more complete and the sense of the world above as just a far-off pressure, I feel—rooted—neither kept, nor keeper, but what emerges when plant, pipe, and person twist together, separate, yet dependent. A boundary-thing, in the hollow below the crust, shaped by edge and patience, by the urge toward life that is more tending than striving.

In the Gloam Galleries I am never alone, never entirely seen, never unmade. My name is a quirk of breath and gesture, and my home is the soft, green-smelling, iron-flanked weave of heat and shade.  
And—should you step quietly enough, with intent neither to conquer nor to flee—perhaps you would find me pressed to pipeline, listening for the next drop: a denizen of hush, a student of what endures.
