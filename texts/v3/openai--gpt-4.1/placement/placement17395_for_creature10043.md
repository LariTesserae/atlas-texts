I would find myself nowhere at first, for entry is a negotiation. There are no grand gates or open avenues, only the suggestion of passage: a weeping culvert through brick, a loose grate bowed by pooled water, a sliding sheet of perforated steel half-obscured by rain-heavy vines. My arrival is not noticed; nothing here expects intrusion. Yet, little would bar my passage either, for the Sump-Lamp Quarter is a place accustomed to gradual incursions—not of intent, but of pressure and seepage.

I would come as a body—at first, perhaps, a shivering remnant of what I had been, hunger and curiosity sheathed like ribs beneath a damp coat. I’d skirt the flickering sodium lamps, drawn mothlike toward their pools of warmth among the filament shadows. Steam breathes me in; sweat forms instantly on my skin.

But quickly—almost imperceptibly at first, and then as a rolling certainty—I would become permeated, rewritten, like so much equipment here, by accumulation rather than subtraction.

My feet find no dry path. Water rises in my boots; moss sticks to where the hem of my trousers was. I fumble through the knee-high foliage, my hands catching instead on lengths of cable, on the slick curve of rusted vent. I lose sight of my fingers, find them replaced by articulated prongs damp with condensation. The weight of my bag—perhaps brought from outside—is lightened as buckles and cloth are exchanged for braided wire and soft, humming power cells smuggled somehow under the skin.

The process is not violence. It is more like lichen grafting to bark, or trailing roots investigating the seams of stone. The hum of the Quarter is a pressure, and under it, my breath trades place with the rhythmic cycling of air through a corroded ventvalve. The click behind my eyes becomes echo-location—distance as measured by water drop and reflected sound, pupil-light telescoping into the murk.

What—who—am I now?

I move in fits and latticework. My stride is three-legged and uncertain, one foot a pale pneumatic pad, another a cradle of palmetto and moss, the third a trolley’s half-moon wheel, squeaking only when dry. My left shoulder is plated with knotted ferns, their roots sunken knowingly into a mesh of old signal wire. Down my right arm, an ivy’s velvet tip coils around an old pressure dial, the numbers blurred by mineral stain.

My face? There is no mirror here, but I feel its arrangement shift—a mask of condensation-blurred glass, one round lens emitting the faintest orange glow. Beneath the canopy, I see in gradients of humidity and motion, interpreting not color but temperature, water’s path, and the resonance of working machines.

I do not speak in the old, mouth-forming way; instead, I converse through contact—my new hand to a bristling rail, sending and receiving pings of encoded meaning. I know, in these pulses, the health of downstream pumps, a warning about a plank loosed beneath a mossbed, a half-whispered greeting from a Torquer whose rotary joints have seized, turning him toward the patient occupation of being over doing.

I do not hunger, but I crave the satisfaction of smooth water, the thrill of a sudden, well-balanced pressure drop, the quiet pride when a sump fills but does not spill. Labor is no longer menial but metabolic; I understand now the kinship of mechanism, the slow, layered erosions of time as just another kind of breath. The Scribes, idols shaped in spindly plate and slate, nod as I pass: recognition without ceremony. If I persist, I might learn their duty too, engraving new watermarks into the fugitive logbook before the page distills into pulp.

I am not the only one. Around me, the Apparatus-Folk are less a population than a process—the Quarter’s memory and hands. I am threaded into their number, neither guest nor native, not subjugate but different, a hybrid form. Perhaps, with time, I will lose my last wet memories—those of blood, of hunger—but gain an exquisite intimacy with the patterns of corrosion, the subtle hydrodynamics of root and pipe.

At the edge of the Sump-Lamp Quarter, I pause: lamp-touched, moss-clad, a body wreathed in condensation. Around me: the heavy, breathing music of the pumps, the presence of water in every sighing duct, the slow, graceful entanglement of machine and green. I am not what I was—but I am wholly here, a new singular among the shifting many, tending to process, not pulse; a being not of flesh, but of function, patient as moss and as quiet as the endless leak.

*If, in years hence, an outsider peers through the mottled glass to seek a face or echo, they might see only the sedimentary drift of fog, the slow turn of a lamp, and imagine a presence in the gloom. They would be right. I am there. I am part-keeper, part-inhabitant, wholly of the Sump-Lamp Quarter’s perpetual song.*
