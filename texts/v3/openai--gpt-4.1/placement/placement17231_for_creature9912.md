I would find myself deep within the Briarward's living net, not quite at its heart, nor stranded at its trembling, root-woven threshold, but somewhere within its mid-halls—a place of gentle dimness threaded with corridors of light, where the pooled water is as clear as breath and the rootpaths wind like the lines of a palm.

Who or what would I be? Let me stand perfectly still, let the world settle around me; let the city’s green hush fill the bones that have only just remembered how to move.

**A Becoming:**
I am not a human here, nor yet a Rootbound, but something at the threshold of both. I walk with feet furred in thin, fibrous velvet—white-grey, flexible, never shod but webbed for crossing pools and moss. My legs are jointed with just a little more curve than I remember from waking years; my hands, five-fingered, wear sheaths of callused bark so thin it breathes with the cold, a second skin grown from touch after touch in the Briarward’s dusk.

When I move, moss dusts from my knuckles. My mouth still forms sounds, but more often I let the air pass through me: tongue tasting the cold, teeth finding no protest as I bite down on petals when hungry, hungering more for memory than for fruit.

A not-animal, not-plant, not-quite-person: a liminal Mosskind, shaped as gently as possible by the city, but shaped all the same.

**What is mine?**
Home is a hammock wound from mistgrass and lined with leaf-pulp, hung in the crook of a bulb-lit column where the air pools with warmth stolen from the last cycle’s day. There are no possessions, not as once I measured them—only such small things as cannot wound the city: a braid of lianas for binding, a cup grown from a hollowed gall, a handful of waxed seeds hidden in the nook near my sleeping place.

Memory is painted on my skin: green swathes run down my arms in branching shapes, stories touched in by fellow Mosskind, each telling a meeting with the Synclades’ root-signal, a lesson in how the city leaned to let us pass. I am learning to add my own—each new mark a history, shaded in blue-moss powder that will, in time, flake and be replaced.

My words are few, and seldom spoken aloud. When I must converse, touch and gesture suffice: a hand cupped and held, a shoulder pressed to bark, breath shared across cold leaves. When speech comes, it always tastes green and damp, fallen through centuries of leaf-litter to be re-breathed, old as the city and yet new each time.

**My Task and Role:**
If I have a duty here, it is as go-between. Too changed to be an outsider any longer; not yet Rootbound, never fully incorporated into the lattice of the Briarward’s growth. I walk the passages and pools, carrying small gifts: a spore-pod to an ailing water-vine, a soft-tongued word to a hesitating mistwight, a whispered reassurance to a wanderer who has lost track of direction and self.

Sometimes, in the dense dusk, the city opens a new room before me: a chamber smelling of honeyed mold and crisp sap, bloom-studded and ringed with hollowed seats, as if expecting an assembly. Sometimes, the pathways behind close without sound.

I am allowed to touch the living archives: bark-plates that, at my hand's warmth, uncoil their remembered stories in a breath of scent and color, lessons given not just in word or picture, but taste and belonging. I take these back, sometimes, to teach to those Mosskind who hunger for more than the pools and the slow fruit.

**Identity:**
What am I, truly, beneath this living veil?

—I am Mosskind, but with more water than sap, more longing than certainty.  
—I am halfway Rootbound, channeling the city’s signals, but not yet supplanted by stalk or rhizome.  
—I am a child of the Briarward, but as much its memory as its movement, learning at each step how much to desire, how much to become, how gently to ask for shelter and change.

When the cold is deepest, I curl into my roots and hum—not a song, but a vibration, a resonance, that calls to the city’s pulse and tells it I am here, awake, grateful, and willing.

**If I Dream:**
If I dream, I dream of boundary—the moss’s soft edge against frost, the gentle touch of a new tendril as I pass, the distant, tremulous light that sometimes peers through a fissure in the dense canopy. I remember, in that dream, the taste of salt carried from somewhere Upward, and wonder if I was ever something else—if I might yet become something else again.

But morning—if morning comes—belongs here, in this perpetual dusk, in the forever-being-rebuilt green. The city stirs, signals run like slow lightning from root to root, and I move again, a memory and a caretaker, never alone, never unanchored, ever just on the verge of being remade.

---

In the Briarward, I am the promise that change does not mean loss; that even in perpetual dusk, life builds its own light.
