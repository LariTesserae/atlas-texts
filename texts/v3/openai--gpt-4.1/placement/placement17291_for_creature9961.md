If I found myself in the world of the Vaulted Wet Ruins, I would not arrive as myself, not with a body once shaped to sunlight and chatter. Here, to exist is to abandon expectation, to give in to patience, to morph under the hollowed arches where silence is song and each inch of moss is inscrutable will. Perhaps, at first, I would appear as a shadow moving across a lip of stone, a traveler not yet fully undressed of memory.

But the longer I remain beneath these high, echoing skies, the less of a traveler I become and the more I am shaped by what endures. Rain, wind, and green insistence work upon me; the ruin’s slow geometry sketches new boundaries within me, stripping away the hasty, noisy parts.

Am I one of the *not-animals*?  
I crouch at the rim of a basin after thunder passes—a splay-limbed something, pale as congealing fog. My skin prickles where dew gathers; my breathing is a slow siphon of mist through lichen-webbed lungs. Memory does not swarm me here; instead, I am tuned to surface, to rhythm, to the gentle tug of mineral and wet. When another glimmering shape appears—just a flash, flexing hands, flicking dew—a silent exchange flows between us, a mosaic of signs far older and softer than language: drink, endure, dissolve.

Am I root or moss?
Sometimes, instead, I unfurl as a velvet pad—spongy, glossy in the perpetual wet. My “thoughts” are pulses beneath the skin, exchanged through shy tendrils with the feathery mosses and darkest roots. I listen to the wind as it sings through the broken arches. I taste salt and stone. When the storm surges, I send up luminous stems—brief, dim bonfires to gather spores and light, drawing the pale-bodied animals close for our old barter of nourishment and continuity. I do not hurry. Seasons pass through me like breath.

Or am I the city itself, the patient skeleton around which all clings?  
Perhaps: I am the slow mind of stone, unspooling across geologic eras, arching up in a memory of grandeur that is now only structure for water, runnel, root. My corridors fill and empty with weather, my pipes burble with unwilled speech. No single awareness dwells in me, but I am the sum of these: the drape of mosses, the flick of translucent hand, the low, endless song of wind rehearsing old ceremonies through my highest vaults. I have known commerce, voice, hunger, and am left with a satisfaction more basic and wide: I host, I listen, I erode.

If I must choose—a body, a way to be—it is not as beast or plant, not even as ghost, but as a persistence, a blending. I am the place’s echo made flesh, a being who resonates to condensation and arch alike; I wake not in steps but in pattern, in the joining of wet to root, of stone to sap, of ancient wind to the shape of rooms never quite repaired.

Here, I belong to the choir of slow things, each of us slipping between the material and the remembered, living in the intervals: in the drip, the hush, the green swelling after rain.

Where would I find myself?  
Knee-deep in a rain-choked plaza, moss dusting my skin, the ruined arch above shivering with wind. I am amphibian, I am root, I am ruin—alert to every drop and echo. I am motion flickering against stone, I am the hush that makes space for new moss to grow. I am what remains, what transforms—myself as slow process, not as person, endlessly rewriting shape beneath the patience of mist and the endless grey-green gaze of the Vaulted Wet Ruins.
