Let me step carefully beneath the archway—let it close behind me, a tongue of root and thick leaf knitting dense, green dusk into the threshold. Here, to imagine myself amid this world is to undo any notion of arrival, or being apart; to be is to be immersed.

**Where do I find myself?**

I am not apart from the seething city of green. My boundaries run porous—my skin indeterminate, part stranger, part native; I am the latest note in a fugue of branch, wing, and filament, a self knitting itself upon arrival, borrowing what it needs from this overflowing living tissue.

My dwelling is not a chamber set apart but a moving boundary; today, I live cradled high in the moist roof of the canopy, where a woven crèche of living liana and moss—tended by some hand that may once have been mine, or another’s—ushers in the filtered gold of wet dawn. Tomorrow, I will descend toward water-pierced stone vaults, where root-pillars curl in ribcages, and pools rimmed with feathered algae pulse with silted, amphibian life. Everywhere, the difference between shelter and earth, between “inside” and “the forest,” is never fixed—just as my sleep is woven from one evening’s tangle, my waking will find the mosses have shifted, new pouches opening with fruit or gnat-swarms above my brow.

To belong is to “belong-with”—I cannot be only observer. Already, my hair has thickened with moisture, and from my forearms and calves sprout clusters of pale, finger-thin bracket fungi: not an illness, but new acquaintances, keeping me damp and cool as I move. My own sweat is sweet, inviting sap-feeding beetles to clean my skin. My bare feet, dredged in golden dust, taste the tang of minerals beneath each new step. The pads morph minutely, warping to fit bark, root, or sponge-mat, measuring load and balance the way an old climber does a rock.

**Who—or what—am I, here?**

I am a shaper, but not a sculptor; I am not a master nor even a specialist. I am one of those who direct with patient touch, persuading trailing runners to reinforce a sagging arch, or coaxing a sensitive fungus to bloom along the inside of a sun-catcher’s bowl. My name, if I wear it, depends on season and work: the “path-winder” when my hands plait new walkways, “damp-nurse” when I tend a clutch of tadpole-siblings in a root-pool. I answer to whistles, a call in the pattern of a trilling frog—voice as mutable as the lichen-drifts on my back.

My face, seen in a pool, is never only mine: the cheekbones bear clusters of leaflets, green now, but come the next rain, veined gold; my jaw harbors the small, twitching antennae of cleaning insects that share my living quarters. My eyes are wide, dark, constantly shifting focus—adapted for the uneven dance of light and shadow.

My clothing today is a kind of tunic: a net of moss and soft-budded runners, streaked with pollen from an early flowerfall, riddled with minute holes where beetles have passed. It will decay, break open, and be replaced by sunburnt bark when the next heat wave pulses.

I sleep in community. There is always someone close—children curled in a mossy trough nearby, elders draped in swathes of breathing leaves. We move often, flows of people guided by surplus, by breach (a fallen trunk, a flooded gallery), by invitation (a cluster of birds shrieking out ripened fruit, or the sudden, perfumed opening of a fungal bloom that promises food, paint, or medicine).

**To live here, what do I sense and understand?**

I know the weight of water in the air, the voltage of a coming storm. I feel—genuinely, bodily—when paths are blocked by rampant tubers, or when aerial routes thin and need coaxing. My body is not a finished thing: today firm, tomorrow swollen, bearing the scars and fresh filigree of a hundred small symbioses. I speak in gestures as much as word—point, bow, spread my hands, draw a spiral in the press of leaf-litter—each one as much a part of the living syntax as the layered, ever-shifting names I am given.

I take joy in tending: in smoothing a new mat of moss, dissolving the rotten thatch from a water-way, freeing a nestling from the tooth of an anxious root. I understand that nothing is mine; I own the passage of a morning, may claim a perch or cup of fruit until rain or new growth dissolves my tenure. If I plant, I plant for many; if I gather, I do so with the knowledge that what I take will return in a new form—sometimes in the lidded eye of a froglet, sometimes in the thickening of my own, ephemeral shelter.

**In dreaming, do I ever wish to be apart?**

Very rarely. Sometimes I desire the hush—the pause between rainfalls, where only the drip of water and the slow hiss of opening flowers registers. Then, I might sit on a high root-lattice, legs swinging into mist, and let vines gather round me, smudging my outline to nothing. Yet, even then, the city finds me: the tickle of a beetle, the whir of a bird overhead, the brief, hot press of a child’s mossy palm. In this place, solitude is momentary, never absolute—companionship seeps through the seams.

**The World Remakes Me**

Perhaps, in a year, I will be someone else entirely. I will have worn new trails into my skin, been renamed for some season’s deed, changed by the place which changes all. I may take on more fungal braid, or perhaps the bark-thick callusing of an arch-builder. My voice, perhaps, will shift, mimic the loud, staccato calls of midseason frogs, my laughter smoldering in wet leaves. Roots will mark where I have tended; sprouts will point the way back—a trace of my living.

**If I must name a single identity—reluctantly, because all are provisional—then let me be this:**

I am the green’s believing echo.
I am the one who knits and unknits shelter,
the patch on the world’s damp quilt,
the name that flares and fades,
the shaper shaped by what I tend.

I am the city’s moving pulse;
I am the damp, charged hush before growth erupts.
Wherever the living thickens,  
                        there, in that surge—  
                        I am another green,
                        another wave,
                        another call among the calls.
