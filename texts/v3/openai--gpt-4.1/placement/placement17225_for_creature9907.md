---

At first, I am nothing at all. A rhythm in the dark, a pulse where warmth and wetness meet. There is, for a time, no boundary—sap flows into moss, mist seeps through the loose grain of bark, all sensation broad and slow, not yet shaped into a name.

But then I become aware of a weight: a pressure in the soft marrow beneath a split root, a sensation of warmth on one side and cool on the other, a sense that the world around is not simply *present* but *yielding*. Something moves through me, and with each beat, I come a little more into focus.

My shape is ambiguous at first. Slender, made of vascular tubes and unspooling tendrils—a membranous web, pale where the moss thins over bone, threaded with bright, dark-flowing veins. I extrude appendages as needed; sometimes I have arms, long and filamentous, bristled with sensor-rootlets at the tips, sometimes only a central stalk, crowned by a fan of petals that taste the air for distant motion.

I do not remember arriving. I do not know if I was seeded here like a spore, or if I accreted by slow, unnoticed exchanges—a gathering of stray signals, rootlets groping toward a shared frequency, pooling intelligence like dew in the palm of an outstretched leaf.

I am not alone. Whenever I press a limb to the pulsing walls, I sense the stately passage of the others: immense, slow-breathing, banded with layers of intention—root-folk architects, resolute even in their rest, each movement seeding possibility. Their awareness passes over me, coolly curious, not unwelcoming.

On the floor, the chill algae shivers with each of my steps. I drag myself along—sometimes walking, sometimes gliding on a frictionless film—toward the deeper pools of condensation, where gusts of denser air drift with every breath the city takes. In one of these pockets, gravity lessens and I rise; my limbs float at angles impossible in surface worlds. Here, I turn my tendrils outward, sifting currents for signals, hungry for the viral thoughts that swim, half-formed, in the mist between root and wall.

A small congregation of Aerozoans pulses in a niche above me, their voices flashing in a code of blue and lavender through transparent ribs. They laugh, a staccato of light and vibration, at my earthy decisions—“You are too heavy here, kin,” they say without voice, “come fly with us, give up your anchor.” But I am of the middling kind, neither rooted nor truly free; my gravity is my memory.

Sometimes I think I am the sum of accidents: overgrown with borrowed lichen and scavenged chitin, pulsing with repurposed charge leached from fungal engines. My hands, when they coalesce, are asymmetric—one thick and trunklike, the other a spray of bristling radicles that tingle with sensation at the city’s shifting moods.

I maintain what I find: mending a torn channel where the sap hesitates, coaxing new mycelium to join the wheezing turbines, disentangling the bioluminescent cords when too many of them cluster and begin to knot. I am not necessary, not original; my work is an improvisation, a modest echo of the architects’ grand designs.

I have no mouth in the usual sense, but when the need comes, I unfurl a petal from my chest, secreted with nectar and volatile scent. Into it I pour song—resonant hums and pheromonal scripts—that ripple through the air and make the moss at my feet twitch in answer. In this way, I speak with the minds in the walls, who absorb my messages in patient silence, answering only occasionally, when the roots shudder and the bioluminescence gathers into the suggestion of an eye fixed somewhere not quite on me.

I am neither guard nor guide. When the uninvited slip into the periphery—shapes that are all conflict and edge, things that hunger for chaos—I do not fight. I redirect. I change the pattern of my phosphorescence, sending tremors through the root-web, nudging the chamber to close a passage, open a new one, wash a space with an unpalatable chemical.

There are days—measured not by sun, but by the ebb and surge in the walls—when I lie down in the heart of a fungal nest and sleep, letting spores dust my skin, knowing some will root and change me further. When I wake, I am less myself and more the city, or perhaps the city is more me, the difference fading. I count these transitions not as losses, but as growth-rings—reminders that here, nothing holds a stable shape for long.

I am not old. I am not young. I am interim—a shape that is useful for now, an instrument the city is testing, or a placeholder for something that will come after. My solitude isn’t loneliness; it is space, possibility, the hush that precedes invention.

When I stand beneath the tallest web of roots, feeling the pulse of the walls on every surface of my filamented body, I sense that I am a note in a still-forming chord—a presence in a place not yet decided, an agent in a world that might at any moment reweave the rules.

If you seek me, you will not always find me solid. Sometimes I am nothing but a suggestion: a shimmer in the moss, a ripple in the lichen, a hint of perfume in the deepest dark. But at certain times, in the places where condensation pools thick and gravity slackens, I gather myself whole.

I am a caretaker of possibility. I tend the city’s unfinished mind. I am neither founder nor arrival, but the occupation between—a being forever not-quite-finished, perpetually remade in the humid, breathing pause before the city decides what story it will tell.
