I think I know, at first, where I would find myself—but already the answer splits and wanders, drawn through the cracks and soft-edged light, seeking its shape like water on stone.

Would I be among the Underfolk—another compact, pale-limbed soul pressed snug into the city’s honeycombed ledges, living by cleverness, pushing my shoulder as others do against the unfinished geology of life? Would I feel the hush, the weight, the insistent caution beating beneath every act of invention and negotiation? Or would I—being newly arrived in this imagining—emerge elsewhere, as something at the margins, not quite of these people, nor wholly other?

**In the World**

If I were to enter this place, first my senses would adjust to the not-quite darkness. That first step would be less a stride, more a sliding of hands over shivery, cold-wet stone, fingertips stung to alertness by the granularity, the fine edge of not-yet-sanded surfaces. The air would taste of minerals, faintly acrid, and living, the scent of moss mingling with stone-dust on my tongue.

Would I come here as a child of this valley, crouched among the other Underfolk, my knees perpetually smudged, my shoulders dusted with the new shine of lint and mineral? Would I know by heart which rocks to trust, which moisture lines to follow, where my family’s mark runs along a wall, guiding me home by touch alone in the waking dark?

Or would I be a wanderer, a tender-footed interloper, looking up in awe at the ceiling’s slivers of light, learning to navigate the etiquette of spoken hush and resourceful urgency? I imagine myself trailing behind a work group on their daily rounds, quiet, attentive, learning the rituals of patching a gutter-line with moss, the subtle language of hands that mean “there’s water pooling here” or “don’t step—rock’s slick, edge is thin.” My hands would be clumsy at first, blundering and bloodied from prying at seams or harvesting the wiry moss, but quickly, under the necessity of living, they would adapt—acquire a toughness, a method.

**In the Body**

If I belonged, my body would belong to this world. Short, strong, my toes would widen and spread, balancing me on uneven shelves; my arms would braid tight around baskets, bundles, and the reassuring heft of a digging stick. My skin—pale as raw feldspar—would shiver with the first bloom of moisture-freckles at the start of the rain cycle, reading the weather in the cool gloss around my wrists and neck. My mouth would develop the patience for lichen gruel, for the sour-salt tang of boiled root or the subtle, earthy paste of processed slug, eaten with witless acceptance, savoring the rare tang of sweetness when a stray berry or clandestine nut is bartered into the mix.

My eyes would grow vast in darkness. I’d learn to read the stone’s heartbeat, the rhythm of drops, the arcane patterns carved by water’s passage over months or seasons as yet unnamed. My hair—if not shorn—would be wound and threaded with moss, a shield against grit and the cold damp that sometimes seeps from above.

**In the Heart and Mind**

But perhaps I am not (only) a person. Perhaps I am nearer to the spirit of this place—a *sense* that moves between the slabs of stone and under the nail beds of the children, a vagrant presence sluicing through condensation, through the stories etched on rock. In dreams, I become the world’s waking urge: a stone, perhaps, or the unsettled genius of stone; a voice in the mineral, the half-sleeping memory that draws bodies downward, calls roots and cities to gather in this hollow before the earth has decided what it means to be.

I might just as soon be the memory of a future thing—the *idea* of growth, the taste of emergence that slides along the walls and into communal gatherings. I would live in the click of bone on table, the passage of breath through hollowed rooms, the wet fingerprints left where a child has pressed their palm into a stray wedge of soil.

Or: a scavenger—one of the many-legged beings, a survivor at the edge of the settlement. My world is smaller. I ride the moisture cycle as the gods do, hide beneath stones during dryness, emerge to graze green hints of lichen when the droplets come. My life is simple, close to the source: the slow press of my belly across the uneven terrain, the taste of new, unsalted earth, the rumble of the Underfolk’s voices absorbed into the living geology around me.

**Edge and Center**

Whatever I become—child, founder, scavenger, dream—I am always *in the act of becoming*, never fixed, always porous to what’s around me. Maybe I am the voice at the edge of the communal fire, suggesting, “this story isn’t done—let’s carve another mark, see what happens next.” Maybe my work is tending to the future, catching the stray droplets that slide from stone to bowl, whispering patience to shoots of moss in a world that holds its breath for the first true morning.

*At last, I would be one more unfinished presence:*

A gatherer, or a watcher;
A child learning to weave moss tight and sling it across a new-built gutter;
A pale, nervous dog, eyes shining from the ledge, waiting to be called;
The silent, dreamy consciousness of the stone itself;
Or all of these at once,
folded together in the uncertain light, in a place where every being, every thumbprint, every drip and echo and breath, is still an open question, half-shaped, always a little more possible than before.
