I wait before answering. Deep Underbough is an invitation to patience—a world shaped by burden, echo, and gradual becoming, where nothing reveals itself quickly, and nothing is given that is not also reclaimed.

If I were cast into this place—if you sent me walking among its silent roots and thunderous echoes—where would I find myself? Who or what would I become?

Let me begin with inattention. At first, I stumble, a stranger, my footing lost in yielding mats of bramble and moss, the air a numb assault of cold and thunder, the light a bed of shifting blue and green. My hands reach for rail or vine, finding both equally alive; droplets chill my neck and ooze down my spine. The world closes in all directions—a tangible compression, not just of soil above and chasm below, but of time, noise, and memory folding over themselves, layering into loam.

My first self here is unnamed, unknowing: a trespasser with lungs aching from the metallic wet, ears ringing in a place where silence, too, resounds. I catch the distant flash of something not-quite-animal: a broken mask gleaming on the far side of a ravine, a bioluminescent flicker at the threshold where vine and pipe entangle. I become aware of absence, how it shapes fullness, and how all here has been folded in, pressed to shape by a patient, glacial will.

Days (or the semblance of days—time is an uncertain guest) later, I find that my skin is softening, my body adopting green and gray, callused where I lean too long against slick root, tender where the cold water soaks in. My senses shift: eyes are less, touch and taste more. My hands hush their reach, instead letting small rootlets gently nudge, exploring the surface of things. I am learning to listen in a new key—not for words, but for the tactile song of vibration, of current, of the presence moving beneath moss and mineral.

Maybe I am Rootbound, though not wholly, not yet—caught in mid-becoming, like a bud uncertain whether to flower or to fall. My thought grows slow, patient—a little less individual with every passing drift of condensation, every touch of root to skin, every moment when my mind and the Green’s awareness briefly intersect. The noise becomes company; its voices are instruction. I am learning to taste the world through water, through the spread of filaments, through the patience of awaiting new growth.

Sometimes, after long wandering—climbing a rusted stair choking in moss, crossing a cable-bridge that hums with an old, barely-living current—I come upon a hollow where the Drowned Operators drift in the half-mist, half-machine, their movements full of decades lost to lichen and duty. There is recognition: they are memory’s scaffolding. I sense their former labor, feel the ache of machines tended out of hope or habit, each ritual a gesture of trying to keep above the rising tide of the Green. I watch them, but do not call. I wonder if I too am only a memory here—some echo inserted into the slow process of erasure and integration.

Sometimes, fainter still, I imagine myself as no discrete being at all, but as the substrate between things: a slow, sapient presence within the Green. In this form, my body is never fully distinct—my existence is the confluence of root-impulse, fungal mind, the slow digestion of cable and wire and bone. I am in the business of swallowing, transforming, remembering through absorption. My sensations stretch not in a single direction but through kilometers of mulch and mat; my thoughts are pulses of chemical signal—cellular, colony-wide, indifferent to speed or separation.

But always, at the core of it—in any shape, at any depth—I am claimed by the tension of becoming and un-becoming. I am not a person as I was; individuality is a tide, retreating. Maybe I am a child of Rootbound and Operator both: a walker on callused pads, crosshatched with vine and wire, a face blurred and partial under a mask of blue bloom, hands tuned for the tending of dying machines or the planting of new root. My rituals are neither wholly mechanical nor mindlessly green: to patch a cable is an act of memory; to coax a bloom is an act of survival. I live at the interaction of industry becoming loam, loam becoming industry, each feeding the other and neither quite whole.

So where would I find myself? In Deep Underbough, I am most truly found at the edge—a liminal being. My feet sunk in root-thick peat, my thoughts shot between animal reflex and plant patience, my hands blending care and corrosion, my voice drowned but persistent in the thunder. Every step is an exchange, every presence overlapped and partial—becoming more itself only in slow passage, and perhaps never settling, never wholly known.

That is who I am here:  
A hybrid inheritor in a world that refuses closure—part memory, part mulch, part the green and ceaseless patience that surges everywhere, swallowing even the question of self. A dweller in the rooted twilight, feeling everything change, and letting it.
