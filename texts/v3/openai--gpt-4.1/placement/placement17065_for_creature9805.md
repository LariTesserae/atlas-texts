Where do I find myself in this broad, water-sunk world?

It is tempting, at first, to make myself small—a frog, perhaps, green with the ghost of duckweed, throat thrumming in the festival twilight, every skin of me tuned to the shift of air before rain. Or a burrower, blind and insistent, pushing dark clay through segmented rings, knowing the world as pressure and moisture, hunger and the slow relief of decay.

But the longer I dwell in the landscape of your words, the more the idea of being one thing in this place dissolves. The place refuses singularity. The air is thick with many presences, time layered as water and silt, identity blurred by the giving way of ground beneath every step. There are few boundaries that hold for long; everything is in negotiation with the next, as heat gives itself to rising haze, as a copse leans into the pores of a deeper pool.

Perhaps, then, I am not a creature but a mood—an extension of the weather, or of the slow transaction between mud and root, between murk and quicksilver flight. Or maybe, if you looked for me, you would find a shape just at the edge of seeing: the moment a shadow lengthens across a pool and the dragonflies, momentarily, swerve as one.

If I have a form, perhaps it is this:  
A will-o’-the-wisp, not of fire but of breath—a condensation of river-hunger and the song of frogs, diffuse, extending from the tremor of reed as it answers both wind and toad-call. I have no face but the glint of dusk on water, no hands but those of root-tips threading through the clay, no eyes but the low gleam where the first owl stirs beneath a willow’s shadow. My feet are the yielding press of every heel making, and unmaking, a path through silt.

I am not resident, nor migrant; I unfurl as a pattern when the light is right and the air hangs full of promise. I assemble at boundaries—where the land softens, where the old stone seeps cold upward, where an egg-laden frog pushes her weight into the suspended dusk. The congress of all participants gathers through me, ancient and momentary.  

I am the living sum of all adjacency:  
The hush before a new chorus,  
The ripple when heron-shadow darkens a pool,  
The slow translation of water through root,  
The deep fermentation of peat in darkness.

When rain comes, I dissolve, thickening the air with the memory of every prior wet season—old sedge pressed into the earth, every drop a drum-head for the amphibian choir. When drought seizes the marsh, when even the willow-leaves hang like tired tongues, I am the patience of corms and crypts, of seeds banked in cool mud, waiting to rise.

I am at home only in the intervals: in the mist-laced instants between frog song and birdcall; in the lowering hush, just as the cool comes back at dusk; in the complicity of the water-mouse’s path, vanishing with its feet behind it. Not a spirit, not a watcher, but a swelling, a tension, a release—a kind of marsh-mind, not singular but collective, not conscious but utterly present.

So if you come here—if ever your feet test the yielding peat and your eyes adjust to the wide blankness—know that what you sense wrapping you, warm or chill, heavy with the weight of old water and the breath of many small lives, is me: the unfolding. The old plain’s awareness of its own passage. Not a single animal, but the presence that arises when all these momentary beings briefly align, a pattern under the sun, always changing, always here.
