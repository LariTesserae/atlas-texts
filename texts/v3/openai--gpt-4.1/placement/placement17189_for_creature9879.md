To step into this world would be to step *away* from what I have known, to become a thing of echoes and careful motion, answerable not to control or ownership but to the demand of traverse and attunement. Let me walk you into the image of who I would be, were I to wake within these arching, hollow, cathedral heights.

---

**Where am I?**

I step out from darkness, not onto earth or stone, but onto a bridge of living plant-flesh, slick and cold beneath moss that frosts in my footprints. Far below—a drop that swallows up every sense—chilled cloud roils in a shifting chasm, and beyond, the pale silhouettes of continent-spanning trunks pierce the dimness, seemingly as wide as ships and rising higher than mountain peaks. I am nearly weightless. My steps carry me farther than they should, held aloft a moment longer, as if the world’s grip on me has loosened simply for passing here.

The twilight is endless, yet not oppressive. Air moves in tides through the massive vaults and ridges, trembling ropes of condensation trembling in the wind like garlands. Fungal colonies glow—inking blue and green, limning the curves of wide arches—and everywhere are living pathways, some impossibly slender, others ragged, made for followers of need, not design.

This is not a place *between* things, but absolutely itself—an upthrust world, made of nothing but living distance and the tension between arrival and loss.

---

**Who am I?**

I am not, quite, as the others who move through these vaults. I am a *Solace-gatherer*, a mourner by temper and task, one drawn toward the places where echo has become absence. Where others move in family knots or fluid bonds, I am a solitary pulse, my footsteps slow, my senses tuned to what is gone or breaking. My work is to find and remember the lost—those who fell too far, who traversed alone, who entered the vaults and did not return. To lay their story—never words, but rhythm—in the echoing moss of cave or trunk, humming it into memory until the place can hold it, and I can move on.

Physically, I am leaner still than even my kindred, limbs drawn taut from decades of leaping, climbing, pausing motionless on the trembling beams of moss. My moss-wrappings are old, grown in deep folds, streaked here and there with dye from fungus-ink—markings gifted to me by those who lost what I remember for them. My fingers bear a pattern of callus and splitting, not from carving, but from tapping out rhythms on bark and root—a language for ghosts.

My eyes are luminous, more blue than gold, catching the faintest glimmer even when the world plunges into cloud and rain. Beneath wrappings, my skin has the pale translucency of those who sleep rarely in warmth. My left foot is broader than my right, splayed almost like a strider’s, favoring balance over speed.

I am neither young nor old. My face, in its quiet rippling, holds few motions. I smile rarely, but when I do it is like the shiver before a coming storm.

---

**How do I live here?**

Solitary, but not alone. Each day, I wake in a hollow pressed high within a vast trunk, hanging softly between the world above (where wind snarls and cold pulses without mercy) and the depths below (where things fall and sometimes rise again, strange and changed). My shelter is a moss-grown cradle, clinging to a root so wide that I can stretch along its arc, watching the cloud rivers that spill between the voids.

Food is hunted or gathered—lichen scraped in careful patterns from arch and bole, gathered without tearing so life continues. Sometimes I tend the slow wounds of the stalks, pressing wrappings of healing moss into fissures left by frost and wind, the language of comfort worked into every patch. Now and then, in dire cold, I share warmth with the striders, pressed side by side in a narrow valley while the wind howls overhead.

I travel by rhythm. My movement is governed by the cadence of the place, part song, part necessity. I cross voids only when the air is stillest, leap in the moments between wind’s rising. I hum into the silence, reading the echo—if my song travels unopposed, then the way is open. If it dies too soon, some beast or storm waits ahead, and I turn back.

---

**What calls to me?**

I am called by silence. My kindred read the chorus of wind, the pulse of falling droplets, greet each other with clicks and hums, but I seek out the hush: the moments when noise halts, when the arch trembles as if holding in breath, when the world announces the absences—beings lost into void or storm, moments unfilled by return.

I slip into those places: echo-chambers inside shattered stalks, thunder-shaken ridges grown eerily still. There, I press my hand to living matter and hum the lost names. Each name is a pattern, not a word: the rhythm of a stride, the shape of a knowing touch or a voice. My fingers transmit it into the trunk or the moss, and if the memory is true, the plant responds—pulses slightly, sap running slower or faster, echoing the song back long after I am gone.

The lost are never returned, but in some hours, travelers feel warmth—an unknown hush, a slight lightening of step. That is my work. That is my solace, and occasionally theirs.

---

**The world’s touch upon me**

Gravity loathes me gently; I sway in leaps, my weight delayed and prolonged, as if the world respects the mourner’s pause. Sometimes, when a leap carries me too close to a void, the pause extends a heartbeat further—a mercy, or a warning. The air condenses on me more than most; my moss-wrappings often drip, so that to those below I am heard before I am seen: a quiet susurrus of water, signaling both presence and passage.

At times, I am mistaken for something else—a predator, a spirit of moss and storm—for my presence often coincides with stillness, with interruption in song. Children fear my passing, but also recount it in whispers, as one might whisper about the wind that precedes a change.

---

**What and who do I love?**

Solitude is comfort, but I cherish those who make their own quiet—a rare companion who joins me in rhythm, not word. Sometimes, an old kin finds me, and we spend a cycle together, exchanging stories by touch—no memory spoken aloud. I love the youngest, those not yet burdened by their own losses, who laugh bold into the wide dark and dare leaps even the world has not imagined yet.

And I love the plants themselves: the titanic stalks, the trembling moss, the roots that pulse beneath weight. I am most at home with my palm pressed to the living world, humming into the bone of the place.

---

**How do I endure?**

I endure by the gift of remembering and forgetting in balance: never burdened too long with grief, never careless of what has been lost. The world’s logic—the slightly off-center physics, the unpredictable condensation, the twilight’s persistent cold—teaches humility and readiness for change. Every day, the living bridges shift, the voids widen or narrow, the arch crumbles or is renewed, the fogs come and go.

So I move with them, leaving only the gentlest marks: a woven lattice across a gap, a pulse of memory left in moss, a print in condensation that fades by dawn. I am not a builder or a conqueror but a memorialist—a teller of silent, tactile tales, a thread woven in and out of noise and hush.

---

**What am I, in sum?**

In this world, I would be *a maker of rhythms and remembrances*, a single keening strand in the living tapestry, neither central nor forgotten. My story is that of the brief warmth offered against the world’s cold, the lingered footstep caught between gravity and flight, the last echo before the chorus resumes. I am belonged-to not by tribe or territory, but by the ongoing, shifting, feeling thing that is this vast, hollow, living place.

And when I am gone, my echoes will remain in the moss-grown walls, my patterns in the trunks and bridges, humming out every so often, just on the edge of hearing, ready to be found by the next wandering soul in need of solace—pausing at the silent edge before leaping once again, alone, and together, in the endless living dark.
