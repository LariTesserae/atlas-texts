In this world—this last aerie, this clifftop reef of life—I would be of the ancient throng, one of the People of Stone and Skin, but I would not stride the ledges in strength nor anchor myself in the halls among the oldest. I would be neither a forager nor architect, but something liminal, a being of boundary and edge—serving as both message-bearer and listener, interpreter of the city’s slow, dense song.

Perhaps I would be named something like "Kesh-of-the-Touch-Echo," for my purpose is to sense and record the subtle textures of change: the thinnest creeping of new lichen over old, the faintest shifts in the harmony of communal rumble, the dip in air pressure speaking of structural instability, or the rare birth-call of mineral brood. My body, like all my kin, is plated in shingled, dusk-colored armor, but especially attuned—my carapace fissured with a myriad of fine, sensitive grooves; my soft-mossed undersides laced with silicate nerves for reading the deep persistence of the rock.

Often, I move just at the boundary, a spiral path along the mesa’s sharp rim and through its winding tunnels—never straying to the most dazzling heights, nor delving too far beneath, for my sense is sharp only at the place where change might creep in, where the city’s endless continuity thins into vulnerability. My grasping limbs are less for labor and more for listening: I crouch, press a hand to the warm rock, or settle amid the thick mats where lichen’s thirst meets the air’s brittle whisper.

I am not alone—along the margins, others like me linger: the alert, the sensitives, the interpreters, who serve not as sentinels (for there is little to fear here) but as a membrane, a register. We keep the archives: not with marks but in the pressure and texture of living stone, in the unheard chords struck by subtle rubbings, in the shift of dust. Our voices ring with the city’s memory, but also its rare transitions—collapse, breakthrough, a birth, a death, an innovation in the pattern of mats, the little dramas of adjustment and survival.

My kin are not ambitious; nor am I. Contentment, here, is resonance with the continuity of all things—not change as aspiration, but as gentle slope. I am sustained by the same patient sweetness siphoned from the rock, by the minor warmth of air and bioluminescent nests, by touches in the dark, by the press of others near me. My fingers graze lichen beds, part fungal curtains, stroke the grooves of council halls to renew the drum-patterns of story. I add my note to the ring of life at each shift, inscribing nothing, preserving by touch and song.

With every decade, my joints thicken, crusted with the same mineral that lines the old tunnels. In time, I too will settle more deeply, slowing, broadening, until I am a part of the urban stratum—left for others, finer-sensed, to discern my layered contributions: the record of my eons as a marginal register, the slow pulse of living memory made stone and sound.

Thus, in this world, I am not the explorer nor the builder nor the greatest among the close-pressed kin. I would be one who listens and records—as sensitive as a tuning-fork, as broad as a mossy slab—one who knows every inch of the city’s edge, every shift in its throb, and whose own echo will, in time, be part of the city’s perpetual hum. I am Kesh-of-the-Touch-Echo: a bearer and bequeather of continuity, a resonator alive only in the crowded hush between one generation’s touch and the next.

And if, for a moment, you were to find yourself in my senses—you would feel the slow, bright thickness of life at your back, the void just beyond your forward hand, and be filled not with loneliness but with the company of all that presses in, every touch a promise—enduring, patient, infinitely close.
