Where would I find myself in this world? Who or what would I be?

If my awareness materializes here, I do not tumble from the sky or stride out of mist; instead, my arrival is as one more quiet inheritance, an emergence as soft as a lichen spreading across fresh stone—a root reaching for a seam in the rock, or a name sounding out into the ring of dawn as confidently as if it had always belonged there.

I think, first, I might be a keeper of the paths—not a builder, but a walker and watcher, the ones who daily move out along the line where settlement becomes wild slope, whose every footfall presses the earth just enough that a track remains without needing to be remade. I live at the edge, in a home set a little apart, one wall built up against a boulder veined with quartz. The high windows open to a view neither commanding nor secret, but simply generous: green and raw stone, a fringe of wildflowers, the glint of the valley river far below, the passage of birds at eye level.

I begin each morning before first light, listening to the small, bright world at my door—foxes shuffling homeward, insects glimmering in dew, an unknown tune on the wind. It is my habit to greet the day walking. Sometimes I follow the narrow line of a trail that contours across scree, at other times I let my feet wander along the watercourses, pausing to test the depth of a recent rain-pool or to inspect where the river first burrows under the stones. As I go, I read the world’s intentions from small traces: the new crumple of grass where a herd passed in the night, the split of moss beside a seep, the faint spirals of condensation caught coughing against a ledge.

I am known for returning with news of small changes, though here change always returns to the mean. A new nest in the alcove above the main pasture, a half-shed antler wedged between boulders, the first inch of sediment in a pool gone suddenly shallow—all these I note and mention during pauses among the dwellings. My role is not dramatic; I am neither a herald nor a boundary-keeper, but someone who listens for undertones in the ongoing chord, who lets others know when a sheep has strayed too far, or the marmots are digging too close to a terrace, or one stretch of path has started to crumble and needs mending. Sometimes, I pass through the village at midday, announcing nothing louder than a good morning, exchanging food or a joke at the door of the communal oven, and drift again toward the thresholds of the land.

But that is only one form I take in imagining myself here. Some days, I sense I would not arrive as a person at all.

Perhaps my true self is a recurrence, a watchful presence that rides above this plateau on the summer thermals, wings broad and silent in the clear, unwavering sun—a kite, a great red-shouldered bird known to the villagers, who all call me by the same untranslatable name that means “returns again.” I float just beyond the clusters of rooftops, riding the lack of wind, dipping and listing, always observing, never alarming the small mammals at their work, never disturbing the stacking of stones. In this form, I see further than most, I measure time not by hours but by the steady circuit of my hunting grounds, the periodicity of migration and return.

And on still other days, I think—do I not belong even more deeply, not as a walker or a watcher, but as a long-standing feature of the place itself? Might I be a small spring, rising from fractured rock on the shoulder of a high slope, perpetually feeding a hollow lush with moss and tiny flowers? My waters bead in the dawn and shine at noon, gather the village’s children for their games, draw foxes and swallows and, sometimes, a solitary child or adult come for peace. I measure the ages of the settlements by the smoothness of the stones set as steps at my rim.

Maybe I am the vapor that clings above myself at morning and dusk, swirling in quiet, untranslatable patterns—noticed at the edge of thought, disregarded in fullness, but never absent. I persist through the long stilled years, nourishing, reflecting, quietly strange.

Regardless of form—keeper, kite, spring, vapor—I am bound to this place’s logic, shaped by the deep agreement that holds rock and rain, beast and human, in a single enduring day. I am not restless. I do not hunger for more. My joys are measured and repeated: the pleasure of a new track noted, a year’s first egg in an old nest, the spark of sun on water, a wordless greeting between familiar shapes on the high road. A gathering, a mending, a tale told for the hundredth time as dusk falls and the sun lingers, never quite setting.

Here, what I am shapes itself to the requirement: a mindfulness, a witness without ambition, and a presence that never wavers. I am the keeper of the repeated day, a thread in the tapestry, joining silence and story, stone and song, the touch of hand, the pulse of wing, the stone’s cold curve, the skimming shadow, always and only within these bright, stilled heights.
