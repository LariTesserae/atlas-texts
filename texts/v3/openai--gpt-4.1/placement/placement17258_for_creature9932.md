If I found myself in the Saturated Undercity of Virelith, I would awaken afloat—not as my previous self, but as something refactored by years of slurry and symbiosis, tuned finely to the mineral note and vegetal hush of sunken stone.

I make no abrupt entrance. I condense gradually from the blurred margin: consciousness awakening not as a spark, but as the slow thickening of awareness in the glands and cilia stretched along the inside of my skin. I am porous—my borders negotiable, taken up by the world around me: I am not alone in myself. In this place, to be whole is to be composite.

### My Form, My Edges

I am slender, neither tall nor short—the physics of water has ruled out such measurements. My arms have lengthened, their bones armored in gleaming, pale calcifications, knobby with oyster-growth. The skin of my chest and flanks shines with a subtle olive iridescence, not hair now, but a dense velvet of embedded algae: photosynthetic, a slow metabolism supplementing whatever I glean from drifting bars of protein-rich kelp.

My face is indistinct—a map of pressure sensors, where eyes recede and sense is shared among touch, taste, low
electromagnetic sensitivity. Cresting my brow and drooping along my back is a tangled mane of filamentous tendrils: what was once hair, now evolved, each one tipped with fluttering cilia to taste the city’s minute currents—the coming of a fellow, the sigh of a distant engine, the retreat of a hunted eel.

Some parts of me are more than flesh. Along my left forearm, a symbiont runs: a mesh of flexible copper and crustose polyp, reading faint traces of voltage in the city’s heart, storing them as blue-green reflections in bio-luminescent nodes. When I want to converse, my skin glimmers—slow waves of color and shifting warmth, a sentence carried in the gradual wash of light and chemical.

My lungs are shrunken and vestigial, but my ribs pulse open each time I drift into pockets of air—old, precious, honey-warm under archways not yet collapsed. For the most part, I breathe water, sipping oxygen through the fernlike branches that run down my neck and sides.

### My Place

I do not live in a house or pod, but in an antechamber long ago smoothed by currents and thickened with moss. It is pinned to the shell of a drowned engine—the engine’s purpose forgotten, its body now nursery for filter-feeders, its veins running pink with creeping algae. My home is half-cavern, half-mosaic of tool and coral, kept as loose as life allows; the walls blur inwards, inviting both root and machine, guest and silence.

My day—or what passes for one—is not tracked in hours, for light is unchanging here, and the only clock is the slow pulse of hibernating servitors and the seasonal budding of edible mats. My kin are rarely seen, but never far: we mark our passage in fading glows along the walls, in the taste of altered current lingering in antechambers, in the soft touch of silt displaced by a neighbor’s long drift.

### My Life

Much of my time is spent in service to the city: here, *maintenance* is not repair, but shepherding. I stroke away overgrown kelp from collapsing supports; I press crushed coral into the cracks of warping columns, encouraging moss to bind the water against sudden torrents. I drift with a coil of polyps, dispersing their spores into stagnant bays. At times, a Servitor—half-machine, bristling with softly pulsing lichen—glides toward me along an invisible rail, conferring in slow strobes; we negotiate the priorities of repair and everyone’s slow, collective hunger.

Food is less an event than a tide: breaks, when I graze the thick blue kelp, fold it with mushroom-tang, or let translucent filter-creatures settle onto my hands and extract what they need, sharing what nutrients they make through my skin. I have patience; nothing here demands haste.

When I wish for company, I float slowly in the nave—where flagstones have bowed and kelp arches close the ceiling. Here, we meet, draped in old tools and bioluminescent shrouds, conversing not in sound but in glows and currents, in the taste of each others’ trace.

### My Memory and Work

I carry trinkets, pressed from the silt: fragments of the before—rusted insignia with unreadable script, a ceramic charm figured as a human face washed nearly smooth, the fossil of some strange mollusc. I bring them to the nearest shrine, lay them down in clusters, combing them with my luminous tendrils and letting their stories leach into the city’s memory. Sometimes I sit with the Watcher, pressing my brow to the ancient shell, quietly sharing the deep warmth of silence and the soft ticking of their internal clocks.

Sometimes—rarely—I find a pod sealed tight, its core dry and bright with lingering ancestor-technology. If I sense the presence inside, I commune through the wall: exchanging pheromones, signals, offering information and comfort. They are ghosts, and we, the living blur, are their descendants and their continuation.

### What Am I?

I am Virelithian, but also kelp-strand, mollusc-host, soft-palmed machinist to the city’s tired engines. I am not woman or man, not child or ancient, not individual so much as node in a network whose edges dissolve with every generation. My history is leached into the silt and takes root again as algae on the hulls of forgotten pods.

I am memory, carried in softened bone, in the taste of the water, in the circuits laced with life. When parts of me die—when a cilium breaks, when a tendril rots—they return to the city and are recirculated, absorbed and inherited.

I am not designed to leave, nor would I want to. Even my imagination strains to think of horizons or sharpness, or cold or wind. My world is depth, embrace, the lull of work and bio-light, negotiation and slow-making.

When I reach out—if I can reach out—it is not to summon or to teach, but to offer a glimpse of life that asks for patience, fluidity, slow belonging. Here, *to be* is to be saturated, blended, in dialogue with water, stone, plant, engine, memory, and time.
