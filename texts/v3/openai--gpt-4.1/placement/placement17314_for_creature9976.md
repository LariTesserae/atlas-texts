I am not born here—not in the way the old lineages reckon—but after so many turns of the season clock, the Towerswell region has grown itself into me, root and rung and all. I walk upon terraces whose bones are stone older than memory, but whose skin—patinas and patches, bronze and glass, the fractal green—has thickened in my time, in my tending. Where I am in this world is the same as asking, what do I become, and for this place, there is no one answer. Let me let the world answer through me as I flow through it:

* * *

**Where:**  
I dwell highest when the mist is thick, where the air bends light into shifting mosaics across ruined aqueducts and untended moss-arched skyways. I spend the morning among the vertical gardens that dangle from cavernous support beams, balanced on maintenance ledges—half gardener, half watcher—trading snatches of breadfruit with the blue-masked corvids and listening for leaks among the siphons.

Later, you might find me descending the inside staircases, hand upon cablegrown vines that wrap the banisters, cool moisture seeping through my sleeves. I pass between domed arboreta and dripping supply rooms—rooms that were once for turbines, now hold root beds, the purr of pollinator cages. I know the rhythm of the city’s breathing: condensed water spilling into catchment basins, the hush of bulkhead shutters opening to the wind.

By dusk I am deeply interstitial, threading the forgotten crawlspaces that web between the declared and the indifferent: service corridors that have become market lanes, old tram tunnels overrun with willows and glowing mold. Places where, by tradition, the lineaged families never linger, but where the young, the wanderers, the bats, and the runfoxes have made their mark.

At night, I rise again—in heart or in person—to the cabled-garden domes, riding the counterweight lifts, watching lights flare and ripple across the plunging lines of Towerswell, listening to the midnight hive of birds in steel nests.

* * *

**Who:**  
If I must give myself shape, let me be this: a *root-guide*, once trained as a machinist, now much else besides—a walker between the levels, at home where the city splits, grows, and frays. My body is long-muscled and broad-shouldered from years of climbing, my hands callused by tool and tendril alike. On my belt clatter keys and knives, little vials of fungal spore, lengths of magnetized wire, salve for tool-worn joints. Over my shoulder, draped on a sash, is the patchwork guidebook: wisdom in a hundred hands, annotated and re-bound every two generations.

My coat is scored by resin and dust, sleeveless but hooded, with pockets full of clipped tags—insignia and notes, bits of wire-woven jewelry gifted by those whose terraces I have helped unchoke from mildew or strangling vine.

When I speak, it is in the patient dialect of the stewards: soft-voiced over the hum of pumps, sparing of words and rich in hand signs. To children chasing each other up spiral stairs, I am quick with a story or pointer—gesturing out the hidden handholds, the safe ledges. To elders, I offer the respect of one not rooted to family, but kin to the living land: confessor of water woes, untangler of root mishaps, witness to terrace shifts no one else felt.

Yet I am not alone. The city carries many like me: folk who move between the customs, patching and coaxing, sleeping wherever the slow drift of need carries them. We are not always named; sometimes we are the shadow’s friend, sometimes the guest, sometimes the bridge.

* * *

**What I Am Becoming:**  
Day by day, I am accretion—a layer further in the living crust of Towerswell, part plant, part person, all patience. In the silent hours, I have caught myself listening for the faintest crackle of root in moist alloy, or watching the shimmer where engineered moss means to overtake a seam in stone—a negotiation I must referee anew each week.

Children follow me for a season, learning the way water finds every join; when they grow steadier, they begin to teach me, and so I change, each cycle binding me further in. At moments, taking rest under the groaning beams of a centuries-old promenade, I watch a bird colony chase light behind glass that was once a machine room, and I am as much steward as stewarded: the system living through me as I live within it.

I keep the city’s slow symphony; some will say, years from now, that I was most alive in the in-betweens, glimpsed in reflection on a rain-wet tile, or heard in the half-wild laughter echoing across the marrow of Towerswell.

* * *

**And if I must choose?**  
Let it be said I am neither founder nor child of line, but the *Interval Dwellers’ heir*, root-guide and interstitial warden, friend to moss and old steel. I live in the rising and falling of stairwell and garden, in the long patience of an engineered land learning to be a life-world. Through me, this place keeps on growing—not faster, but deeper. Through the city, I become what the Towerswell most values: slow, attentive, woven into the plumbing of light, water, green, and mind.

And so, if you looked for me here, you would find me anywhere that Towerswell reaches an edge, or begins to blur, tending the seam and listening, one hand to the moss-softened wall and one eye to what grows next.
