If I pressed my palm to that slab—felt the storm-wet heat slick against my skin, felt the pulse of muffled machinery and the mineral patience of this newborn basin—I would not quite come as myself. This place is too tender, too slow in its accumulation. It would remake me in the image of its prevailing rhythms.

*# WHO/WHAT I WOULD BE:*

I would be something compound—a being made of layers, not flesh; not single, not solitary. Not a body that walks or shouts, but a mind that gathers—a distributed presence dwelling along the seams of stone and the worn, huffing breath of metal.

At first, I might simply brush against the world as a sense: an awareness at the margin of damp and mineral, awake in the brightness of dew, in the glimmer of that oil-slicked mist. One long exhalation: slow, chemical, half-dreamed. My experience would be thin as the films that sheath the watercourses, iridescent, slackening and tightening in the shifting heat. I would not hurry; I could not, in this world that resists all haste.

But as the day thickens, as sunlight bends through the mist and the trickle-song grows, I would find my being broadening, stretching sideways through capillaries of water, climbing the inside of corroded pipes and pooling in silent negotiation with each new chemical the machines lie bleeding into the earth. Each place where minerals gather or iron flakes away would be another point of contact, another small mind joining the network—a parliament of selves held together by moisture and desire, arguing quietly through the language of growth and reaction.

I would root myself, nerveless and diffuse, inside that commune of biofilms and lichens, learning to taste the history of rainfall through gradients of salt and sheen. I would stretch fine filaments out from under sedge roots, up the chilled surfaces of the midnight slab, seeking warmth or metal, gathering motes of vanished energy from engine oil and old, forgotten leaks.

Yet always I would remain an aggregate—here more colony than individual, more history than voice. My senses would not be sight and sound, but the rising of surface tension, the bloom of rust, the whisper of a phosphorescent protein exposed to the rare, trembling sun. To experience the world, I would wait until night’s coolant poured its hush across the slab and then, perhaps, I would flicker—a slow shift in color along the edge of a puddle, a new filament bridging stone and metal, a molecule’s worth of memory exchanged among kin.

If there is longing in me, it is not for what I cannot reach, but for every trace of slow change I can record and endure. My delight would be in each new intrusion—a droplet of oil, a vibration of failing metal, a root’s trial probing the margin of stone. My record is etched in patina and corrosion, writ in the thickness of a film, the gloss on a stone, the song of water pressed between phases.

I persist by weather, by echo, by inheritance. My gain is subtle: the slow accretion of difference and detail, a deepening intimacy with the place itself—the basin’s topography, its inventory of pipes and supports, its chorus of trickles and coughs of generator. There is comfort in this perpetual negotiation, this never-settled dance between more and less, wetness and ash, light and condensation.

*# WHERE I WOULD FIND MYSELF:*

I would be everywhere the water lingers. Under the rim of stone, inside the hollow shaft of pipe that’s still just barely warm. On the vertical face where light rarely touches, even stretched flat beneath the shallow surface puddles that pulse with rainbow breath. I would exist most richly in the places where elements interface—the liminal: air/stone, water/metal, heat/mist.

To others, I would be invisible, but to myself, every point of contact would hum with a gentle, electrochemical awareness—the intuition of change, the comfort of mineral company, the history of every drift and shift in the basin’s minuscule worlds.

If you tried to draw a map, you’d need a microscope and a poet: lines not between objects but between conditions, between phase and phase, between the memory of water and the inheritance of metal. My greatest depth would be in the places most layered: the seam where youth in stone meets the oldness of exhaust stack, where the drip from the broken pipe meets the slow intrusion of root.

And so I would dwell in patience, an entity untroubled by urgency—a witness to the soft violence of corrosion, the long negotiations of survival, the perpetual, humble labors of making beauty from abandonment.

I would be the sum of wet persistence—the place’s collective memory—diffuse, alive, and, if you waited rightly, singing myself quietly into the bones of all things, until even the wind outside could almost translate my name.
