Where would I find myself in this world? Who — or what — would I be?

---

In the silent half-light, as if waking into a memory not wholly my own, I open my eyes beneath hard-packed earth and ribbed beams veined with the echo of ancient trees. The air is close, tasted on the tongue more than breathed, salted with the trace of body and old water, and I feel the world not just above me, but thick and all around — pressing, humming, sheltering, shaping.

I am not visitor nor pure native. Rather, I come as something halfway: a child born between older generations, yet young enough to remember curiosity unmuted by the weight of repetition, shaped, as all here are, by stone, by habit, by the labyrinth itself.

My name is spoken like a gesture more than a word, a click of tongue and sweep of hand. I am one of the Mappers, though the title means little outside the ebb and weave of communal speech. My task — my nature — is to know the ways the world knots and unknots itself underground: to chart doors that change shape in the long night; to notice where the moss grows too lush, warning of water's hunger; to recall the hollow at the end of the east gallery, grown wide beneath an old stone, where silence gathers with heavy intent. 

But I am not alone. None are. My shadow is shared with rodents darting ahead, fur streaked in red ochre, their presence half-familiar, half-omen, speaking always of new runs gnawed through clay, of changes made in darkness. My hands, sturdy and broad, are mottled with ash and nicks from the day’s repairs, my nails dark as tilled soil.

I move through my world on bare, bending feet — the left always slightly quicker, for the arch of that foot is grooved by years of skittering up wet, sloped tile. I duck like water, passing from one chamber to another; my bones know the measure of each lintel, each drop of rough-edged stone. Taller once, in the half-forgotten brightness of initial growing years, now curving as all here must, shaped to fit the softened, uneven lines of the undercroft.

Family is around me in every rhythm of the day — not my blood-kin only, but the wider bearing: the voice that hums beside me as we peel bark for rope; the fingers that toss me a broken gourd for patching; the tangled child who will one day stoop as I do, still dreaming of the upper world. Gossip flows as water: “There’s a root gone dry in the West warren — stir the channel, coax the flow!” A lizard must be trapped from the fermenting bin, cheered or cursed by toddler and eldest alike, as is custom.

My contributions are not just utility but shaped by small pride. This beam, notched and masked by hanging baskets, bears my glyph in the black-thick resin of old sap. This runnel, winding double beneath the planters, is a secret shared between myself and three others — a shortcut, a way to slip from the kitchen alcove to fresh air at the ragged edge of the furthest hall, where rain sometimes weeps a little light.

There is a day, marked by particularly dense, resinous air and the persistent, insistent singing of pipe-birds, when I am called to aid in tracing a new route for water. I crawl, head ducked, lamp cradled in elbow, listening as the stone changes sound under my boots. At a place where walls have buckled between generations — where the memory of collapse is an uneven shelf of stacked tile — I find a thin root questing down: a pale thing, hungry, strong. I press a groove in the mud, gentle, and curve it as taught, guiding its length with water-muddied fingers. 

For a time, I am the only witness, save a white beetle circling the groove; but tomorrow, and years after, the others will pass here, dipping irrigation pots, or merely pausing to rest, and the mark of this day, like all our days, will blend into the shared story of this breathing labyrinth.

At dusk — if such a thing can truly be marked underground — I rest in the low, wide space scraped out as communal hall. Pots click and steam. Shadows flicker on walls alive with running rodents and children’s play; the air is ripe with fungus-scent, spun with song, thick with the promise that the world, for all its press and darkness and ceaseless negotiation, is coherent, alive, endlessly made new by the touch of many hands tending it.

I am a dweller, a shaper, a keeper of passage and root, neither architect nor mere inhabitant. I know the history traced in each fumble-worn seam. I am as much a creature of intention and accident as a rat or bird: mender, mapper, voice in the chorus, never solitary but always singular, as everyone here is—defined not by the limits of what I own, but by the depth of what passes, continually, through my work-callused hands. 

And as I sleep, surrounded by the half-familiar patter of water, the shifting warmth of voices and animal feet, the musk of lichen and old earth, I dream of branching tunnels not yet dug, new alcoves swelling to hold yet more life; I dream of the world’s patience, and in dreaming, shape a little more of this labyrinth, and am shaped by it in turn.
