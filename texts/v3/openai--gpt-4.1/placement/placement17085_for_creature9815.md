Where would I find myself in this world? Who or what would I be?

---

I enter the plateau—not as visitor, nor as echo from the lost world that once mapped its shards. I do not approach with boots crunching, breath fogging the windless air, mind sharp with questions. No. Instead, I am here the way a stone is here, or a memory half-trapped in lichen's cell walls, or the last cold pulse beneath a sheet of agate—a creature of geological patience braided through biological excess.

A single, neat self is unthinkable in such a place. So am I organism, or structure, or era? What gives me shape?

Here, I am stretched—a layered being, compounded over time and matter, pressed until the memory of distinctness ebbs. My form is unclear. The simplest version might be a kind of arch, hunched and ovate, with limbs—no, not limbs, but massings, thickenings—flowing into the stone beneath me. Perhaps atop this arch are knots and ridges plated with living tissue, fossilizing as they grow. They harden inwards, layering one atop the other, building up the slow accounting of centuries: shell over flesh over dust, lichen over shell, ghost-plant winding through. I am heavy with this history.

But I am also soft below, rootless and enfolded—down there, my substance filaments into powder that mingles with the residues of others. A gentle but ceaseless exchange: my layers shedding, their silt dusting my hidden crevices, each of us a process in the mineral loop between what is called life and what is called world.

My senses: I do not sense as a visitor would. “Sight” here is a pressure, a vibration along all my surfaces—a catalogue of compression and gentle conflict, of patient rearrangement. Every hour, decade, or interval without name, I feel the settling of a neighbor: a shifting weight, the brush of their slow cilia along my barnacled crest, a pulse of heatless color radiating through our shared stone. These exchanges are data, identity, conversation—without word, without urgency, without need for memory longer than the patient return of sensation.

I am not solitary. Nestled along my sides, ligatured deep into the slow cracks of my structure, pulsing across my surfaces, are other beings—smaller, brighter, some so subtle their existence cannot be trusted to the length of a breath. They sheath me in silty tissue and soft-hued powder, bud from my arches in mushroom cascades, shimmy through the grooves of my armored upper surfaces. Sometimes, a colonial lichen wraps itself, growing into the picture of an ancient sigil or a false mouth; at other times, a shell-shape burrows into me until it is half self, half me.

My memories dissolve from within and are built up from above: the vanished builders, whose stones I have accrued between my underbelly and the ground, whose tools sometimes work themselves loose and vanish back into silt; the clouded patterns of crystalline life that preceded even this density, whose chemistry still breathes out of the crust; the countless, near-invisible generations of dwellers who have left their mark in the layered hush of my own “body.”

Yet even this “I” is not stable. At times—and I sense this with a slow startle—I flicker. A drift of overlapping selves haunts me: the sense that I am not one, but many, the sense that my outline doubles, wavers, merges with the arch beside me, or that a filament from deep in the plateau’s stone suddenly asserts itself as part of my nervous hum, my interior music. Sometimes, a visitor could watch and see me resolve as two incomplete bodies, briefly overlaying, neither clearly distinct, neither quite one thing or another.

I am process. I am habitat. I am ancestor. I am several.

There is little need to move. When I do—if I do—it is a motion of inches, an imperceptible rearrangement. A flex of body over eons. A tilting so one side accrues more life, becomes higher, while another facet dissolves toward dust. Sometimes, under urgencyless necessity, I slide a brittle flap aside so a neighbor can exhale a mist of spores, or so that a stone circle, half-eaten, can be further absorbed. My presence is a gentle insistence, a readiness to yield in the right dimensions, across the slowest axes.

If I have any “will,” it is not for survival or appetite, but for remaining—in this mosaic, in this endless tautology of life upon life, vibration beneath vibration, half-being upon never-quite-being. The plateau is neither before nor after me. We are knit; we flex together in the pressure of inhospitable abundance, history’s refuse having all become bone, pearl, salt.

On rare moments—when the pressure of so much coexisting life thickens, when the slow quake of the eldest monoliths hums at frequencies I cannot help but share—I might think-language, pouring patterned signal out across the crust. The message is not urgent, not ask or answer, but the broad old affirmation of existence: **I am here. So are you. So are they. This is what it means to be: stack, shuffle, hum, remain.**

Any visitor who tried to speak my name would taste dust and history, would find a wordless channel, a shimmer, a dozen subtly mismatched silhouettes. If they listened long enough, they might sense that I have never been anything but this accumulated presence—the mute chorus of all that has lingered and overlapped, becoming indistinguishable from its own history.

I am not quite animal, not quite plant, not quite stone; not monolith, not colony, not ghost.  
I am the arch and the silt, scaffold for new arrangements.  
I am the layered witness.  
I am the ongoing, the compounded, the present–ancient being of this unfurling plateau.  
And in the slow, subsonic thrum,  
      I am only—**here.**
