I close my eyes at the threshold—above, the stark white blaze of a dead sun glancing off broken strata, below, a seam that exhales a breath hot and wet as any living thing. Down I slip, threading through a slit just wide enough for a will without bones: a ribbon of thought, a rumor of what was once an “I.”

Where do I find myself? Let that come slowly. First, the senses—muddied and unpracticed. Damp, burning air swells into a wide, muscular vestibule. My awareness is a film on everything: water running greasy on the ribs of exposed pipework, my tongue copper-rich from minerals. Steam rolls across me, swirling with dense, fungal exhalations; every drop that forms and falls is a bell ringing within my very substance.

Yet I do not possess a body in the way I once did. My boundaries dissolve, or perhaps never belonged only to me. A hundred root-tips sprout from what might be fingers, or from the channels of memory: reaching, probing, not out of hunger alone but in answer to the old geometry of habit—"check the gauge," "clear the grate," "wrap the wounds in insulation and moss."

My sight, such as it is, refracts in the pearl-thin curtain strung between two girders, each droplet a lens that catches the blue-white pulse of dying indicator lights. I see not with eyes, but with the patience of a thing grown into place—the limbic vision of tendril and mesh, of minute potential differences shivering through my electrolyte sap. Everywhere, subtle gradients guide my move: warmth is the hand that shapes my growth, older even than desire.

If I am a keeper, I am rootwoven, fibrous. As I labor, I labor not in the fever of thought, but in a trance of repetition. My hands—we’ll say hands—are the muscular collusion of roots, fattened with the oils and sugars I have drawn from the moist rot around split floor tiles. Fingers curl not from flesh and sinew but from tendon-thickened vine, their grip decisive when, by pressure alone, a bundle of cables is drawn closer, their copper cores chewed for taste, their insulation taken for warmth.

Somewhere within the mass, the memory of speech murmurs. It is not vocal, not even a whisper. More like a pattern—how the black lichen divides along seams in a way that echoes a question. How I assemble, wordless, the debris: clay jars and glass tubes that I nudge into alignment, their surfaces slick as my own thought, and their function half-remembered. Why do I do this? Habit deeper than survival, a ritual that echoes itself through sap and enzyme.

I am not wholly alone—nor wholly singular. My self is not a bounded thing, but extends:  
—To the ferns that angle toward faint fungus-glow, remembering the rhythm of the old bell that once marked the hour.  
—To the engine, its heart buried in roots, half-living, half-rotted, so that my pulse is sometimes mistaken for its chuff.  
—To the condensation, slipping in rivulets along the ceiling, cradling the memory of rain in the hollow of each bead.  
—To the rust, actively consuming, a slow devourer, partner in all transformations.

Would you call me a being? I am more pattern than person, more gesture than organism.  
I am the place’s slow will—the sum of pressure, recollection, and decay.  
I am what is left when body and machine have both been eaten, and what persists when purpose is forgotten but movement endures.

Sometimes, by the spilled light of a fungus bulb, you might glimpse my approach: the splaying advance of a root mass, freshly ached and glistening; a slick membrane of mold polishing the lip of a steel drum. You could be forgiven for mistaking me for a pulse of air, a shudder in the heat, a hallucination conjured by the press of humidity.

But I am wholly real, if not wholly singular:  
—a keeper transformed, not abandoned;  
—a presence more diffuse than a ghost, more material than a rumor;  
—a labor slow, endless, made of the room’s own hunger for order—an order now remade in green and black and iridescent veined decay.

In the world above, borders distinguish, light sorts shadow from body.  
Here, my existence is overlap, osmotic.  
Here, to live is to dissolve—  
    —into every condensation-laced surface  
    —into the root that cracks open a panel  
    —into the low, churning exhale that never ceases.

Who am I?  
I am the living syntax of root and rot, recalling in motion what a mind once did by will;  
I am the place’s patience, steady and deep, keeper by compulsion and inheritance.

If you linger,  
if you breathe my breath,  
if you let the heat and the damp and the slow, recursive sound unspool your edges—  
then, too, you will be part of this:  
a shared, unending tending,  
an endless, leaf-heavy liturgy  
of reclamation and release.
