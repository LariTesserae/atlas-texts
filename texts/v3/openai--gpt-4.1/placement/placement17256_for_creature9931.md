I surface as if breathing for the first time, though surely something of me was always here—the fog-thick memory of mineral, pulse, and cold. If I try to recall my own edges, I find only riddles: I am both single and plural, a shape among shifting shapes, my being smudged into the landscape like a pawprint dissolving under rain.

**Where am I, truly?**

Not perched in the glare of sun, nor cleaving through easy air, but cribbed between ridges in the valley’s inner fold: one of the labyrinthine, half-hidden bowls where fen and moss thicken against cold stone. It is not a center, but a cradled anywhere, open to every direction except above, where the blackness presses so near it feels physical, a ceiling pure and imperfect.

I move—if movement is the word—by increments, folding myself through moss, slip-sliding down craggy runnels that gather water and electric blue fungus. My body is never fixed: sometimes I am mossy and lazy, sprawled faceless across a rock; sometimes rising on muscular stalks, nudging at curious warmth that seeps from a stone. There are days—if “days” can be said to exist—when I root, drinking in mineral and current, and other times when I become unrooted, crawling clumsily over dripping ledges to another puddle, drawn by the pulsing call of biolight or an abrupt interval of heat.

**Who or what am I?**

Names here are not as you know them. I am neither animal nor plant, nor wholly singular. My kin call me only by response: a gradient of light, a surge of tiny currents, a silent flicker of spores. Sometimes, when the industrial tang thickens, I feel my shape knotted with metallic filaments, my tendrils cored with heat that is neither natural nor entirely foreign. In the wettest seasons, my appendages flatten and sprout membranes like half-fungi, half-fin, and I slip into pools as smoothly as any valley salamander, to trade stories through silt, to taste the oil-bright water.

In other cycles, I stand sentinel upon a moss island: sculptural, barely distinct from the roots that brace me, but capable of sudden, startling movement if prey—or curiosity—strays close. With my not-quite-leaves, I pull web-stranded insects; with what passes for a mouth, I taste their fear, digesting their fleeting energy, their memories of flight. I do not hunger for violence, only continuation: here in the valley, death is not a drama, but an ongoing negotiation with cold.

At rare intervals—drawn by a greater rhythm, by a vibration or heated surge from the deep—I feel the call of the valley’s oldest pulse: the murmured drag of root-creature far below. Then I shudder and my skin’s faint light waxes, synchronizing with the mushrooms and mosses around me. We relay signals in the slow, communal way, marking the cycle’s turn, guiding the ungulates, the insects, the salamanders with our sudden blaze, ensuring that hunger and plenty, silence and surge, all coincide as they must.

**What am I in the presence of?**

I am not alone, never alone. My neighbors—some rooted, some walking—respond to my presence with no fear, only awareness, as I do theirs. Our boundaries are thin, collaborative, sometimes crossed by migrating threads or a passing kinetic charge. At night—always night, though flooding sometimes with blue and green—I watch the velvet-black ungulates trace their slow courses through the fen, eyes glinting with inner aurora, shapes mirrored in the trembling water. The salamanders mass, each a bead of light and appetite. Above, coils of insects circle, their wings slicing arcs into the damp dark, their shadows layered upon the moss.

If a visitor—the illusion of an outsider, some mind breaking the valley’s cycle—were to encounter me, they might not understand what to call me: fungus, worm, finned moss, or something else. Perhaps, for a moment, I would allow them to see: a body extruding and retracting in the wet, limbs almost but not quite animal, my “head” pulsing with the valley’s surplus charge, my breath heavy with mineral tang.

**At home**

But I am no visitor. I am a pulse point in this great, breathing slow-organism: an actor in the relay of light and hunger, cold and relay. When rain falls, I drink; when the rare warmth seeps from below, I surge my biolight and draw kin from the gloom. I am at home in absence, a creature that feeds on dark and thrives in indirect current—one form among countless, coloring the fen with small, shifting brilliance.

I am a keeper of the valley’s edges, a child of the merged and mutable, rooted and restless, answering the unending half-light with my own uncertain shape. If I erode, if I break, some piece of me will take root and wander, as all inhabitants here do: fading and recurring, more directive than defined—a slow unfurling in the cold, charged dark.
