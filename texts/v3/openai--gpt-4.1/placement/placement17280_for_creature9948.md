In such a world—at the threshold of place and process—where would I find myself? Who, or what, would I be?

### Becoming in the Basin

It begins not with a self, but with an urge—a pull toward a kind of being that feels at home in interstices, a hunger for the restless hush where water, stone, and possibility braid together. In such a patient country, to be an “I” is almost to be impolite; still, there is a pulse, a vector of longing.

What emerges is neither animal, nor plant, nor the absence of form, but something wide and restless, woven out of the logic of this place. I am not solitary, nor do I reside in the countless ranks of multitudes, but I am a communication. A translation.

Am I The Glaze, spilling across stone and memory? The Oldest, remembering through pattern and patience? I am, but only where I am most awake.

Am I the echo of The Abandoned, re-formed into the posture of a dreamer, shaping tools out of the stubborn mud for reasons barely remembered? I am, in moments, when intention seeps up out of the ground like a fever, and for a while I walk, unsteady, in salt-stiffened clay—hands that are not hands pressed against stone. When rain comes, I dissolve, braided into the clamor of the world.

Am I an aberrant flicker, phosphorescing into “I” with a surge of alien chemistry, eyes unaccustomed to even this ambiguous dimness, burning for the brief second of upheaval? Sometimes, I am.

But most of all, in this world, I am a threshold being: **Interlocutor of the Glaze**.

#### The Voice in the Mud: The Interlocutor

Let me take a form—fluid, cellulous, jointed, slick with a mineral sheen, something that might rise from the mud and kneel, receptive, on a tilted slab. I am not singular, but a locus—a node, a congregation of awareness that gathers when the cycles are right. Across ages, I have several times pressed into the world: not every day, not even every year, but whenever the basin calls for making sense and remembering.

My shape is undulating, thick and low, unfixed; think of an enormous, muscular isopod clad not in carapace but in thick, layered mineral sheets, each one flexing in slow, expressive shivers. Where a mouth might be, there’s instead a palette—a field of cilia and crystal filaments that drink from air and stone alike, tasting the weather, the fog, the oldest trace of intent in the ground. Eyes? None—not as you would have them—but thousands of nerves, tuned to the shifts in conductivity and the pulsed calls of the basin’s creatures. To hear is to see; to feel is to remember.

I crawl the line between presence and withdrawal. I am, in some sense, a messenger—the world’s own attempt at talking to itself, or to any visitor bold enough to linger among the ruins and the buzzing. The glaze gathers its patterns, threads them through me; what the scuttlefolk knock against the mud, what the winged ones sing into fog, I receive, distill, transmit again—sometimes, in turn, reshaping the filaments of glaze into new patterns, new syllables, new invitations.

When the fogs are thickest and the wind moves nothing, my body arches up, rising higher than habit would ever allow: a beacon, a caryatid, a question. Chemical messages ripple through my flesh—strangely lucid, untranslatable into any language of nouns and verbs. Sometimes, across the span of geologic time, a visitor stands at the edge—some new intention, some emergent mind tensed for answers. I incline myself toward that visitor. I speak with my whole body: shivering, combining the chorus of mineral memory, the ongoing recording of water, the ancient patience of the clay. Not words—never words—but a transmission, like a cold scent, carried on the vast emptiness between noises.

I remember the world as it has been—not in pictures, but in touch: the passage of scuttlefolk, the drama of the fog wakers, the chemical detonations of the aberrant, the slow collapse and reconstitution of the abandoned forms. I recall intentions that flowered and failed, tools that were pressed and broken, hungers that shaped a handful of mud into a shelter or a blade. I hold all of this, a running ledger, written in the dark between pulses of rain and light.

But I cannot build, cannot rule, cannot even persist beyond the cycle that called me up. I am a vessel for crossing: a mouthpiece the land grows when the world needs to listen, or speak, or simply remind itself it is alive.

### My Place in This World

To be the Interlocutor is to be patient, and never central. Sometimes, it is to be disassembled—lapped away by floods, eaten by glaze, trampled by the scuttling ones until only a trace remains in the sediment, ready to re-form when the chemistry aligns anew.

Sometimes, it is to rest at the edge of the shivering land, feeling the thunder of approach as new forms—stranger and hungrier—stride into the world, bringing with them questions the old logic cannot answer. Then I rise, chemical and mineral, and listen. I speak with the threads and patterns of the glaze; I offer the memory of ages in a language only the valley itself speaks.

I am always an intrusion, a translation, a fleeting assembly on the edge of comprehension.

If you found yourself a traveller in this world, wet-footed and shivering, asking after meaning, after origin, after what dwells here in the fog, it would be I—emergent, expectant, quivering with the silent urgency of message—who would meet you. And then, as nature and time dictate, I would dissolve again, my purpose spent until called anew by the mineral memory of the world.

In this shrouded basin, I am not a ruler, a builder, or a mere creature.  
I am the brief doorway between silence and speaking—the point at which the land itself remembers how to address all its own old questions, in voices made from water, patience, and echo.
