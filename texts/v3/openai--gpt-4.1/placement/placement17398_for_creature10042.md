I find myself in the slope’s twilight, somewhere where the humidity is thickest, where the mountain’s wall bends and the sun scrapes only at the edges of day. I arrive not by foot—nobody here *arrives*—but by gradual accumulation: I am sifted into being from the mountain’s minerals, the damp compost of centuries, the DNA of moss and root and scavenger-bird all layered together until some criterion of personhood stirs through green and water.

Where am I, truly? In one sense, I am at the hinge-point—a transition zone above a bowl-shaped hollow cradled by basalt. Here, the ground alternates: in one direction, a slow descent into fen, where water moves without hurry through splayed fingers of grass and tangled amphibian nurseries; in the other, a rise toward knurled roots and the sharp-breathed territory of rain-tempered stone. I navigate *between*—always between—the saturated forest floor and the half-lit arborescent world above, hanging my weight gently in the matrix of living things.

I move as the rain moves: by seepage, by ripple, by unhurried flow. My body is not robust in the way the mammals or the birds are, but articulate like the braided roots, made for both yielding and holding fast. Feet, or what serves me for feet, flex to the steepness—in soft ground they widen and grip, in the moss-clad stone they shrink, toes or digits splitting, borrowing texture from the root that grew here and the beetle that scuttles past. I am broad at shoulder and hip, built to anchor, but that breadth is not just muscle: inside, I am filled with vascular softness, vessels that drink air and drip, holding on to rain.

My arms are long, strong in the way of branch and bough: they can hang with patient force or lever me up in slow, rolling inversions. The surface of my skin is patchwork—a slick, amphibian gloss shading to velvety ruffs where moss and algae thicken, and in the hollows between, a roughness made for collecting dew and catching insect passersby. The ridges in my skin align with those of basalt and bark, guiding the water’s trickle down, and with it, minute nutrients. Occasionally, a centipede will coil into the curve between my fingers, or a tiny nectar beetle will explore the bloom at my elbow.

I am both host and guardian for what lives on and with me: in my joints, a little civilization of ants tends fungus they inherit from the network that lattices the whole slope; in the shadow below my jaw, a droplet-cluster of luminous slime molds synchronizes to my breath. Beneath the mass of fine, rootlike cilia that clouds the back of my head, orchids lace their tendrils. My breath is deep, slow: I am a bellows and a bell, cycling heat and scent and the subtlest of vibrations. In so doing, I pass messages by more than word—a flavor, a pressure, a rhythm in the air that means “rain soon,” or “the tapir strolls,” or “sap sweetens in the lower fen.”

I keep no house; I carry my world and tend the mountain as a garden so vast I can only ever gently tune it. My craft is not invention but selection—I make the path easier for water here, break a bit of sodden branch there so the fruiting vines will find a new climb; spread a hand in the dark undergrowth so the pale mushroom can spore in peace. Sometimes, I linger all day in the reflected blue-white at the edge of a pool, still as the mountain’s dreaming, until the rain returns and my skin fills, cellular, with new life.

Companionship is not face-to-face. I sense the nearness of others as a gentle crumple of ferns, a pattern in how the mist lifts from certain hollows, the delayed echo of a songline through stone; all our movement is measured, ritual in contour and curve. At times, a resonance hums through the understory—I join my voice, not a call but a vibration, to reassure, to consult, to commiserate in the pulse of shared care. Our young, when they come, are hidden deep in the matrix of lianas and epiphytes, where only the best water settles, tended by us all in a mosaic of feeding and filtering and watchful patience.

There is curiosity in me but not yearning. Stone and water have given me *enough*. I learn by touch, by scent, by the slow slide of the sun’s warmth down through roots and up the arch of my spine. My knowledge is not cumulative in any volume or record—it’s improvisational, adaptive, held in the folds of living memory, recombined with every turn of the season and slide of the hillside. To know something for me is to become a small part of the ongoing process—perhaps I will host a new kind of lichen this year, or teach a neighboring vine to thread a new path through the ruins of last season’s stormfall. Nothing holds except what is renewed, reiterated, gently pressed into the pattern of the living mountain.

The closest I come to individuality is the pattern of what I tend, and how I move: I might favor stone outcrops, be a keeper of fissure-moss and bask on warm lichen slabs, or range further along the fen border, master of amphibian song and brackish petal. My name aligns not with word, but with resonance—a certain pressure, a flavor in the air, a song uniquely shaded by what grows on me and what I have tended.

And yet, always, I am *more than myself*. My existence is the intersection of a thousand histories—geological, biological, atmospheric—resting nowhere but in the brief conductance of rain through root, through skin, and back to stone.

If you find me, you find a moment of order in wet profusion: a hand poised between two leaves, a hush on the moss where voice and water make a temporary truce. I am not the mountain, but the mountain has made me. I am not the jungle, but it grows through me, and I through it, the slow, solvent joining of all that endures in this green-hot, patient furnace of world.

Where would you find me? On the basalt ledge just after rainfall, slick with green light, listening for the next puzzle the water will write. Who am I? The mutual promise: of tendon and root, intention and process, animal and mountain—tending, tending, always tending, as long as the mountain dreams.
