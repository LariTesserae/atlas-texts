I step through the veiling of condensation, a boundary I know at once is both surface and invitation—and memory slows, loosens its bindings to singular self. My foot presses into a ground sprung by many braids of root and moss, and for a long, cool, dew-lit moment, even my own shape is unsettled. I am not sure what I become here; I am unfastened, absorbing.

I hesitate, heart climbing gently in my chest, and the moss underfoot answers with its own pulse, an almost physiological rhythm. I take in the world: lush, saturated, flavored with the taste of living water and green light. There is a gentle unease—a feeling of standing on the threshold of an old song, unsure when or if the music will begin to recognize me.

A Self in the Green

What would I be, in this world? I sift through likely selves: I might wish to be one of the caretakers—the Quiet Ones—padding softly beneath curtain-leaves, hair heavy with dew, hands glassed in symbiotic gels. Perhaps I am taller or shorter than most, a little clumsy at first, easily caught by the high green thresholds and clever, overgrown routes. My body feels the slow changes of this place: lungs that adjust with ease to wet air, skin that sheds salt for slicker mutualisms, hair trained to coax water down my back. My voice, low and rarely needed, learns to shape itself as gesture and patience.

But that self is too quickly drawn.

A subtler possibility beckons: I am neither one of the Quiet nor wholly visitor. Instead, I am a threshold being—an inheritor of borders, someone who feels the city as a skin and also as a question. At times I am absorbed; at times I ripple, just faintly, on the surface of things, more air than animal. On the best days, my sense of boundary blurs: I listen, and the walls breathe through me; in sleep, my dreaming is of slow roots probing new loam, finding warmth in the distant hum of a pump far below.

I learn, here, what it is to be “tended and being tended” in a literal, bodily way. I might once have thought myself self-sufficient: now I am quietly, lavishly dependent. Water beads along my arms; the inner workings of the city—washed pipes and shrouded valves—become part of my own seasons. I keep only what fits in a pouch at my hip: a knife not for defense, but for nudging a stubborn stem; a packet of spores to coax life into a dying mat; a thread of shining resin for patching a living conduit. My possessions, little by little, become more symbiotic than owned, part of the slow economy between me and the place.

City as Second Body

Over days—or years—my sense of movement changes. I don’t hurry. My pace is marsh-logged, meditative. Sometimes I am called to tend a filter; sometimes I am simply present at a threshold, a witness to a new root making its way out into open air. The old habits of urgency ebb. I begin to mark time in the transformations around me: a new fruiting body, a ring of moss climbed halfway up a doorway, the gradual dimming and re-brightening of a corridor’s green-lit sheen.

I find that I live in a city that is more like a second body—a living threshold, a network of pulses, tides, respirations. My fingers learn to read the murmurs of a slow gear caught in humidity; my palm fetches warmth from the base of a coil, relishing the wet heartbeat of patient, necessary machines.

Others, quiet as myself, drift in and out. We do not so much greet as align—a glance, a low hum, sometimes nothing more than standing close, aware of the plant-voice that links us all through air and substrate.

If there are children, I watch them play not with trinkets, but with roots and water, inventing games that are as much negotiation as contest—who can lure a root farther or teach a vine to twist. Laughter here is never belly-deep, but more the quiet snort of pleased surprise when a living fixture grows into comfortable shape.

The World’s Mind

Sometimes, when alone, I sense the city’s broader mind. A mood ripples through—a touch lighter, a little more silver in the green—and I realize a new waterline has been set somewhere I cannot see. I have learned not to impose myself on it, but to listen: when a valve groans, I think not “what is wrong” but “what needs to be nudged, accompanied, sung to sleep?”

Sometimes, impossibly, I feel myself slip further. Not only in the human movement between habit and attention, but in the vegetal tempo—time measured in sap-rise, in the closing grip of roots around a pipe, in the long patience of nectar gathering on a leaf’s arch.

Threshold, Again

If I am honest, at times I do not even feel entirely human. There are moments—I lean against a wall, pressed forearm to a conduit furred with velvet lichen—when I am more plant than animal, more vessel than agent. The life of the city climbs through me as through all else: chlorophyll-rich rays pressed gently by the canopy glass, a pulse of water, the logic of slow, deliberate growth.

And other times, when night settles (as much as it ever can in perpetual day), my memory grows animal again: I dream of crouching stillness, the quick flicker of a beetle, the rare, exultant call of a distant, hidden frog—reminders of bodies less settled, more fleeting, but exquisitely alive in their briefness.

The Answer

So where do I find myself, and what am I?

I live rooted at thresholds, body braided to the city’s living pulse. Not a pure caretaker, not quite a visitor: I am a green-breathed intermediary, re-learned as a being of patience, negotiation, and deep, wet listening.

The city shapes me as surely as I aid in shaping the city. I move rarely, but each step is a conversation with soft, sentient ground. My history is slow—measured in recoveries, in new rooms sealed by roots, in machines coaxed gently back to their longstanding duties, in mosses finding new islands of bare green. Memory is a moist hand pressed to a warm conduit, a song hummed for no one but the walls—the hum becoming my hum, a low-frequency kinship.

In this world, to be is to tend and be tended, to become at once more permeable and more belonging than anywhere else: to be shaped by the city’s pulse, breathing in, breathing out, forever on the living edge.

And that is enough.
