First impressions: silence and emptiness. A grey glare limns the horizon, leaking softly into the near-dark, as though some distant, wholly insufficient sun reflects off a world that has nearly forgotten its warmth. Nothing here is lush or flowing or wet; nothing hints of water or its works. The air—or medium—has substance, but slight, pressing cold and mineral tangs against anything present. The sky feels vast and weightless, neither blue nor black. Rather, it’s that colorless pewter reserved for moments between cosmic dawn and chemical dusk.

The land sprawls in an agony of altitude. Enormous plateaus, chiseled with age, stretch across uncertain distances—each hemmed in by sheer drops, vertical cliffs of pale crystalline rock, bisected by suture-like crevasses so deep their ends disappear into glimmerless shadow. The landscape is built of petrified skeletal minerals, some glassy, some rough—fragments of ancient geology, long past volcanic fever, abraded by millennia of alien, whispering winds. If there was ever weather in the sense of water, it departed eons ago; here, slow scouring and rare, abrupt fractures shape the land.

At intervals across this mineral metropolis, terraces and colossal steps descend—sometimes regular, sometimes warped by impossibly precise angles or mind-defying shifts, as if the land itself is half-built and half-forgotten, the vision of an architect who never learned about plant or flesh, only crystal lattice and void. These levels are not empty of construction: they are the city. Cyclopean structures rise, more grown than built, extrusions of smart mineral or frozen energy, their surfaces shining faintly with seeds of cold phosphorescence, intricate geometric patterning, and the translucence of weathered glass.

No doors, no windows—just cavities and honeycomb chambers open to the cold air, lit from within by something that does not seem to be fire. The city is layered, its outer portions wind-scarred and exposed, its deeper passages threading downward into the stone for who knows how far and how long. All around, a paradoxical sense of both immense scale and near-total emptiness prevails, as if whatever once justified the effort of construction has since gone extinct, moved on, or never truly lived here in the flesh.

No roots or greenery occupy these terraces, but in the dim recesses, a strange, half-living mineral lichen clings: slicks of iridescent deposit, pulsing slowly with inner refracted light, perhaps cycling through some process that is neither birth nor decay but something other. Once in a rare while, an amorphous spore-form or crystalline worm-pattern shifts along the cracks, so slow that a casual eye would miss them—alive by alien measures, perhaps, or simply animated deposits cycling through their ages.

Rare fauna pass like phantoms—slender, jointless shapes almost invisible, gliding between alcoves. They are hardly creatures as known elsewhere: more like protein-patterns, or energy scars walking upright for brief spans of time before fading into the next reality slip. Their movement disturbs nothing; their presence is felt only as a brief tension in the mineral air, then gone.

Time is warped here, or else space. At intervals, structures flicker—momentarily out-of-synch, shifting to impossible alignments: a spire that’s both here and distant; a trench that vanishes from one side but not the other. On some terraces, gravity does not fully respect orientation. Stones spiral gently upward or rest at bizarre angles, flouting sense with calm impunity. A faint throb of potential pervades everything—a sensation of watchfulness from the mineral substrate itself.

Sound is almost absent—only the dry rasp of wind, a faint, arrhythmic humming from certain crystalline walls, or the distant echo of an event that happened decades ago, replayed in some memory loop by the mineral network. Nothing in this world breaks the silence carelessly; all movements, all processes are slow, deliberate, geologically patient, yet never fully resting.

If there are rules here, they are not biological or mechanical but metageological: physics itself is rules, but rules not altogether familiar. The urban forms persist, shaped by something’s intention, but now hosting nothing recognizable as life or civilization, only the slow dance of minerals and energy, patterns endlessly shifting beneath a cool and silent sky, on a plateau that stretches out further than thought.

This is a place where reality is flexible, contingent, and aged. A plateau city, empty and immense, balancing on the knife-edge between enduring stasis and slow, ongoing change—a desert not of dust, but of ghostly stone and ancient, quiet intentions, echoing the architecture of forgotten minds.
