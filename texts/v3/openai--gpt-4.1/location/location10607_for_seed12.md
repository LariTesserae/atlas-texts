At the edge of waking and elsewhere, you find yourself in a region where the concept of “ground” is only a myth—a high, sweeping land, except that here height is a paradox, because you are submerged in an endless, cloud-like sea.

**Terrain and Materials**

You are inside a vast, dim-lit ocean, but the sea does not press down. Instead, it cradles and suspends. Its temperature is soothingly warm, as if the world itself is steeping in a deep bath that never cools. The sense of “highness” is ambiguous: here, oceans drape the backbone ridges of drowned mountains that stretch for some unguessable vertical. There is no true shoreline—just kicks and waves of open water rolling in pale gradients above chasms. The heights are revealed not by the absence of water but by subtle gradients: pressure, temperature, and strange, shimmering currents that drift upward, carrying motes of plant matter and iridescent sediment.

All is newly formed. The stone—if you touch it—is neither old nor weathered but slick, glassy, obsidian-smooth, freshly cooled and almost translucent. The high ground is more an idea than a form: arched bridges and knobs of half-crystallized rock, needle-sharp spires still clinging to their raw beginnings, black and blue, untouched by time and wearing the sheen of youth. Small ledges break the openness, thick with tangled, floating mats of red-green kelp and whisper-fine roots just beginning to anchor themselves in cracks. Erosion, here, is only a rumor: currents swirl but do not carve, and the stone seems resistant to change—not immortal, but merely at the threshold of its story.

**Vegetation & Ecology**

Vegetation is surprisingly lush for this elemental, submerged emptiness: forests of translucent algae and immense, jungle-thick mats of photosynthetic fronds, all floating, twisting in the weak, filtered light. Leaves are swollen with water, buoyant, shaped like streamers and sails—a realm of plant matter built not for gravity, but for drift and suspension. Some root delicately in shallow dimples of stone; others wind freely, trailing threads to snare nutrients from the gentle tides. Moon-sized trees do not exist, but clusters of vast, spiraled kelp paddle and sway, sometimes forming dense canopies above shelves or cradles of stone.

Fauna is rare—glimpsed more than seen. Perhaps the flicker of a great scale in the murk, or the quick retreat of a many-limbed, finger-sized thing into a hollow. Life here prefers caution and concealment. The only regular movement is the gentle sifting of sand-gravel as a small, armoured creature burrows for the roots of new plants. Their silence shapes the space, making any sudden movement feel momentous.

**Atmosphere, Light, and Sound**

A perpetual crepuscular dimness fills all: not night, but the long, deep twilight of under-ocean, lit by distant shafts that reach down from broken clouds far above. The water glows faint blue and green, humming from somewhere outside perception, more felt than seen. Occasionally, ripples of brighter gold or purple sweep through, the result of slow, unseen changes in the sky far above—a subtle dance of color and shadow, never hurried.

Sounds carry strange: muted, wool-soft, distant. Sometimes there is the far-off, hollowing peal of a stone cracking in the depths, a sign that the world is still forming. More often comes the gentle susurrus of vegetation rasping against itself, or the distant dreams of construction—the clattering calls of some underwater creature, faint and quickly fading. Otherwise, it is mostly quiet, and your own heartbeat feels like an intrusion on the stillness.

**Human Imprint & Built Forms**

Hints of settlement are visible, but rudimentary. Structures cluster wherever stone breaks the water’s emptiness—suspended platforms lashed from giant fronds, bubble-shaped shelters woven from plant shells, their interiors faintly glowing with trapped bioluminescence. Rope bridges sway between pillars; nets for gathering drifting seedpods billow gently in the currents. These constructions are simple but deliberate—proof of hands and planning, yet humble before the vast, slow-moving world.

There are no metals, only sharp stone and plant-fiber tools. Ladders of bundled kelp reach upward, curving away into the murk. The people here understand the ocean’s patience: everything changes slowly, nothing is claimed forever. Their settlements ride with the slow rhythm of water, expanding and shrinking as new ground emerges or old stone is swallowed again by kelp.

**Weirdness and the World’s Laws**

Gravity here is slow and gentle, so that stone needles and bridges can arch improbably high before giving way. The sky is a myth—no one here has seen it, only heard stories from the eldest, told as ghostly glimpses of sunlight from ages past. Things float that ought not to, and you sense a subtle intelligence in the background, as if the world itself is curious what it will become.

Colors are all strange—ferns pulse teal, stone flickers with hints of violet when the water turns just right. Everything is embryonic: not yet itself, still open to surprising. The world is quiet because it is listening—to itself, to the slow spread of living things, to the first reluctant steps of beings who build. Here, every sound, every root, every structure is a beginning.

And always, there is the sense that everything is preparing—emerging as if from a long dream, unsure what shape it wants to take, suspended between possibility and dawn.
