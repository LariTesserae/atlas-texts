Sometimes they call it the Peak Forest, but the name falters, too small for the thing. It is more accurate to call it the Living Plateau, or the Plateau of Roots.

Unthinkably old, the land began as a range of mountains, raised in ancient violence; now, it is worn down to a high, almost featureless continent, as much mesa as massif. The surface is near flat in the way only surfaces sanded by a billion years become — barely-rippling, the outlines of ages lost, matter distributed like hush across the sky.

But what surface? Here, everything is plant. The stone lies deep beneath, sealed off, a memory. Above, the living crust vaults thick as a city’s foundations, a continent-wide skin of green and grey and persistent bloom. In some places, roots press so tightly together that to step on them is to walk a living parquet. In others, the surface yields, spongy, a tissue of moss and lichen, high-crowned ferns, and towering, slow-twining trunks fused into ridges and buttresses. The air is heavy with leaf-aroma — verdancy so saturated it feels like another element.

Water moves everywhere, not in churning rivers but as an endless seep. Rain barely needs to fall, though it does in shallow, long-drifting mists. But the air is thick with moisture, weeping from strange-tasseled growths, dripping down root-walls, pooling in hollowed swells of bark and cup-leaf. The plant-mass captures every molecule, hoards it against drought, breathes it into the humid, shadowed half-light under its canopies. Here, puddles glimmer dun gold, shaded eternally by stacked boughs. The living substrate itself is quilted with patches of standing water, ringed by dappled fungoid forests.

It is always warm. Not oppressively so — there is a gentle, patient heat here, an old world basking atop the heights, the sun pressed flat by veils of green. Light rarely reaches as radiance; mostly it comes as glow, diffused through endless leaves, green- and purple-tinted, never bright, shadows stacked atop shadows. Sometimes clouds parade so close over the endless crest that vapor trickles downward in languid veils, making the air seem dimmer still.

The plateau is vast — walk for days and the scenery barely shifts except in subtleties: the angle of a root-arch, the way some ancient trunk twists into a colossal, moss-armored dome, chambers coiled within, creatures burrowed in pockets and corridors. Scale here confounds; from above, undiscoverable save by long-lost satellites, the plant-waste might look like chaotic fractal highways, fusing and parting, with foreign geometry. Sometimes, in clearer pockets, you can look out over a living ocean of arboreal forms, swelling in slow, tumorous eminences, mist hunkered in every hollow. Weather is born and dies within the green — fog roams, rain gathers from evaporating leaves, wind stirs and is muffled.

Movement here is slow, imperceptible. The cycle of the world is set to the tempo of trees thinking. Now and then, a growth surges imperceptibly forward — a root seeking richness, a cup-leaf filling with rain. On the scale of months, whole segments of the surface buckle and re-knit, as patient as glaciers. You might see, across an aeon-smoothed clearing, a gradual landslide of moss, a convergence of violet ferns, a new corridor submerging an old one. Few things decay swiftly; everything is absorbed, reused, folded back into living fiber.

Fauna: They are everywhere, quiet, adapted, and odd. Nothing large hunts. Most creatures are small, secretive, or symbiotic: bright-furred weevils nuzzle in the tanglewood, translucent lice drift in the haze, birds (if you can call them birds) slip from canopy to bloom, skeletal and spindly, their feathers blending to lichen. In some places, chords of mammal-like marsupials dangle from root to root, slow and musical, hungering for the sugary sacs of giant leaves. In the slow dusk, you might hear patient calls, the clicks and creaks of unseen inhabitants drilling their tunnels, floating soft through the padding green.

It is not silent, and yet sound drowns swiftly. Steps are muffled. Every surface hushes movement: moss erases footprints before you rise; even the trickle of water is merged into the background pulse, a slow, quiet heartbeat of condensation and drip. The loudest sound is the periodic detonation of a spore-fruit, echoing through wet bark hollows, or the rare cry of a beast startled into brief, frantic flight.

Remnants linger: the half-structure of something built, long overgrown, marker stones or forgotten altars choked by vines and roots. Now and then, in a broad root-clearing, you find an old pillar, pierced and eaten through, more plant than artifact. The world has swallowed every outside thing and remade it into itself, benignly indifferent, patient.

And the rules: physics here are the same, but perspective changes when everything is alive, pulsing with slow, communal will. The plants respond — distantly, but decisively — to trespass, to wounds, to shifts in animal population. They close corridors, divert flows of nutrient, heal over scars. Nothing of fire endures. All things end up drawn into the substrate, into the mindless, endless hunger of the green. Time moves not in seconds, but in cycles: of growth, bloom, slow rot and reintegration. There is no other clock.

If you linger, you begin to sense it. The place is patient, unhurried to the point of indifference, but aware in a mechanical, impersonal way, as if the entire plateau dreams darkly, beneath its wet, verdant carapace. The slow world awaits, ancient, alive, and larger than any mind walking its dew-bright surface, beneath a sky choked with hanging greenery and the brush of warmth.
