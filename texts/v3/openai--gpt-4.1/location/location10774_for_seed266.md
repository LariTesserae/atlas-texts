The traverse begins before you’re aware you have entered it—because "edge" and "center" are constructions that are weak in such places.

**Vast Mountain Scaffold**

Mountains, but not in any terrestrial, cloud-etched sense. Here, the world’s skin is a single continent-spanning cordillera rising in stepped tiers, each ridge sprawling for kilometers and stacked so staggeringly high on the horizon that the distance between summit and floor is a scale of physics reserved for gods and tectonics. The terrain is dry, wholly dry, no hint of rivers or lakes, not even the memory of ancient floods; the landscape is a mineral ceremony of ochre ridges and dark, heavy rock, sun-bleached and ancient. Deep salt fissures, looking almost like old veins, cut the ground, sparkling dully in the steady, slightly golden daylight. The warmth is tangible, but not punishing; stones glow gently under the sun’s gaze but never radiate fever.

**Atmosphere and Air**

With so little water, the air is uncannily still and clear. Sometimes there is a slow, invisible wind—born not from sea-to-land pressure gradients, but from some deep, planetary exhalation that circulates upward through chasms and down the slides of shadow. The silence here is not absolute but diffuse; sound is faint, words vanish quickly, noises swallowed by so much emptiness. There are subtle, arrhythmic echoes, so that a hammer blow in a distant factory returns as a sigh hours later.

**Ecology: Animacy without Vegetation**

Plant life is a myth. Instead, everywhere are signs of abundant, attentive fauna: creatures skittering and shifting from mineral cavity to metal gantry. Their bodies are articulated for the crevices, chitinous or metallic in surface, patterned with mineral dust, eyes shining with strange internal lights—warm rather than wet, some burning low with geothermal glow, others like twin furnace doors. Most walk or crawl, some fly on broad, membranous or glassy wings, but even these returns are slow, deliberate.

Rather than feeding upon plants, life here grazes on mineral outcrops, sifting dust for rare elements. Some larger forms burrow or patiently carve intricate, regular lattices into cliff faces—webs, domes, riveted structures of pure ore left to weather and resonate at specific tones. Others cluster around heat vents and the slow exhale of chemical fumaroles, harvesting energy from processes as constant as the seasons.

**Settlement: Built Urbanism**

Humans (or something like them) are here, citizens of these heights. Their cities are perched on ledges and terraces, in cavities and slow spiral ramps cut into the stone. The architecture was not planned all at once—each epoch layered new structures above or within the old. Iron, bronze, obsidian, and bright alloys all intermix in public plazas and complex, bridge-bound neighborhoods. Ducts and rails hang from the undersides of overhangs, while great, slow gearwork lifts groan in perpetual patience, carrying cargo between plateaus.

The industry is everywhere, but humming quietly. No boisterous engines or hard clangor; instead, the working is slow and deep—mines drift gradually into the mountain, refineries smelt ore with slow, phased currents, airships crawl from city to city luminous with industrial glow. The towns are interspersed, with great spaces of untouched land separating each urban node: the cities do not, cannot sprawl, but leap-frog in a network, each a point on the vast, mineral canvas—with cables, pipes, and causeways strung. At night, from afar, they look like silent embers straining up the slopes.

**Materiality and Sensation**

The ground crunches—a sound somewhere between glass and dry bone. There is no moss, no lichen, not even soft sand: only hard crust, slowly yielding in places to the slow work of the wind, or the scraping claws and feet of the fauna. Stones, carved and polished by ages, shine in the sun in colors no biology could mix: streaks of blue-green copper, hematite-red, sulfuric yellow.

Air tastes sharp: mineral, fine like powdered quartz. It dries the mouth but never quite parches, because water is not needed in the same sense—life here conserves and recycles chemistry with brutal efficiency. Breathers vent heat and strange vapors; exhalations smell of metal and stone.

**Weather and Change**

There is weather, but stranger: not rain, not snow, not even fog, but tides of thermal haze, slow-moving fronts of colored dust, and, once an eon or so, a "storm" of crystallized particulates, falling from the sky and igniting a periodic, planetary reframing of geology. The pace is sedate; shifts in the world are measured in centuries, infrastructure rebuilt as foundations slowly yield to mineral cycles.

**Strangeness and Rules**

The weirdness here is not garish, but foundational. Gravity has a slightly different pull at certain altitudes; stones sometimes resonate with a low continuous music. Some claim the mountains themselves are subtly alive, shifting over generations. Certain species of local fauna are not quite animal—some are motes of light or trembling fields, feeding off geomagnetic lines, others are intricate automata orphaned from long-ago industries, now rewilded.

Life, built and grown, lives side by side—there is no clear division. A city’s walls may host colonial organisms made of nacre and brass. A public square’s fountain is a spire that exudes mist of rare gases, gathered and condensed as a sort of communal inhalation.

**The Place in Summary**

You are in a world of dry mountains, dry air, dry stone and light, where cities and creatures make do without water or wood, and where time is an immense gear turning only slightly each day, balancing absence and presence amid silent heights. Here, all is balanced between slowness and electrometallurgic life, between emptiness and careful construction, between mineral and machine and the strange, patient animals who travel between them.
