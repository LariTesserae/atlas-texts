Across a world-sized plateau, the ground rises in jagged tablets: new rock, sharp-shouldered, glinting under a cold ordinary sun. Each blade of stone still tells of uplift, fractures so young you can see the powder along their seams. The air is knife-edged, but not biting—a clarity that picks out infinite detail, rimmed with the smell of wet minerals and heavy condensate.

Moisture leeches continuously from the mantled clouds hugging the stone, making everything saturated but not drowned. Shelves of granite and black glass jut from pools slick with melt, and thin water-skins hang perpetually from the fissures: clear, quivering sheets, refracting the infrastructure that clings to each ledge. Where rain falls, it beads and trickles in runnels; where it stops, vapors swirl, condensing anew. The water seems a part of the land's bones, pressed close to the surface, summoned by the landscape’s own cold drive to equilibrium. 

Vegetation erupts without the comfort of a forest or meadow. Mosses and spike-ferns laminate the rock, thick wherever they can clutch. Fleshy pad-plants and mosses form broad, overlapping grids—artificial, regular as tiles, engineered to filter water and hold nutrients. Their lushness is not wilderness, but systems: root-webs in geometric ranks, able to harness each passing mist. There’s no wild competition, only a collaborative, built verdure, cultivated by absent hands.

Spanning the endless heights is a city not made for bustle, but for scale. Populated by no animals, unpeopled by any voice but the detonation-hum of engines and the bone-hollow groan of pipes. A net of viaducts, struts, and gravity-fed hydric towers hooks into the plateau’s skeleton, growing out into great empty canyons. Where erosion has begun to carve cliffs or cut valleys, construction has followed the raw edges, scaffolding atop the sharpest spines as if to catch the world before it falls apart.

Buildings are both temporary and eternal, their facades shining wet with condensation. Reactors keep their perpetual hum; turbines cough and roar across the vastness, shoving vapor through their iron hearts. The only music is a parliament of competing machines—clang, hiss, the arrhythmic thud of great press-hammers far away. At intervals, the air splits with the static-rumble of factories exhaling steam across the valleys, vapor columns rising as new clouds, feeding the world’s eternal rain.

The entire city, if it is a city, seems to breathe by proxy, its infrastructure the only pulse. No birds, no insects, no cry from hidden life anywhere—nothing but disciplined growth and the tireless labor of machines. Sometimes, in sheltered gullies, the kinetic din is muffled to a low chorus beneath heavy, bristling vegetation—still, all growing right-angled and in ranks.

The world has no seasons. Time here does not wander. Everything is static, save the water’s endless cycle and the unchanging drone of the mechanized landscape. Yet strangeness lingers, in the scale—buildings sized for absent titans, passageways too wide, platforms and cranes cast for processes slow as continents.

Atmosphere is thick and electric with possibility, so cold it preserves all in perfect stasis. The rules are thus: matter and process, but no birth or death. Moss does not spread, machines do not break down. Things may move, but they do not change. Each day is a repetition—water condensing, engines rumbling, light glinting across ruched mineral and fabricated form. 

It is vast, echoing, saturated, and always loud—a living planet, emptied of life, caught in an industrial equilibrium at the impossible edge of newness, with purpose humming through it but never quite explained.
