The air steams with the heavy, seething heat of perpetual creation. Above and below, vegetation masses swirl—their architecture as much a process as a thing, their splendors not static gardens but living, shifting engines. This place is high—amongst the exposed ribs of the world, somewhere above the cloudline at the roof of a vast archipelago of landforms, all recent—a landscape lifted violently and too young to have worn away much at all. Edges are sharp, swift, and clean; cliffs jag upwards. Gullies and chasms are abrupt and narrow, uncushioned by soil. Yet the stone itself is almost entirely covered, obscured by root, stem, leaf—so thoroughly overgrown that no “ground” can be found.

It is (paradoxically) both full—every visible surface made from or by plantlife—and vacant, a huge, echoing openness slashed and traversed by living structures, with immense gaps between forms. Enormous spans—arches, causeways, towers, ramps—constitute a region-sized matrix of botanical construction. But wherever you might expect a tight mesh or thicket, instead you find broad voids, chasms, hollows, spaces where air and light flood in. The architecture is all vegetative—colossal looping buttresses of photosynthetic fiber, pale-barked and dripping, twist out over shallow abysses. Often they stop abruptly, like the unfinished limbs of a growing nerve system, or plummet into nothing, their ends trailing frond-like nets that twitch and spasm in the ceaseless hot wind.

Water pours and sheets everywhere, condensed out of the saturated, swirling air, pooling on every surface for an instant before being drunk up by sponge-like mosses—then weeping down again, gathering in hanging reservoirs within the plants’ own bodies, overfull and prone to burst. Rivulets chase each other off edges in effervescent streams. “Clouds” here have no clear border—mist and fog blend into liquid and then into green, so that vision is constantly interrupted by a gauze of shifting translucence, veiling all but brilliant flashes of color: the glint of sap, the shocking white of a newly-emerged stalk breaking through a buttress, the rare deep blue glimmer of a sky far above. The air is thick with the scent of resin, chlorophyll, and warm rain.

Yet despite the fullness—every molecule of moisture and sun-thickened air feeding the riot of plantflesh—there is a sense of loneliness, as there are no animals, no insects, not so much as a birdcall. Instead, the world broadcasts its existence in other sounds, endlessly: cracking, stretching, thumping, a symphony of sap-boiling expansion and tearing wood—a tempest of photosynthetic noise. Vines wrench themselves from one surface to another, their motion almost too slow to see except when a sudden jerk echoes like a whipcrack. Sometimes, a bud somewhere above will burst open so rapidly that it thrums like distant thunder, scattering spore-clouds that shimmer as the humidity condenses on their passage.  Pools slap and overflow; fronds slap together violently in the high hot wind.

Here, everything is built. But not by hand or tool—there are no tools in this place, no evidence that anything has ever lifted a stone or pruned a branch with intention. Rather, everything is constructed and alive, made purely by the organizational will of vegetative systems, a planetary-scale plant-civilization that knows no animals, no subservient roots—just itself, surging, arching, ventilating, perpetually making and unmaking its own body. There is no rest: everything is in churn, construction, collapse. Now and then, whole sections of bridge-like plantwork fall away into mist, detaching with a devastating crack and crashing down out of sight. Seconds later, new tips sprout outward to replace the loss, greedy for light.

The rules here are other, alien: there is nothing that dies in the usual sense, nothing that is preyed upon or migrates; there are no seasons, only pulses of heat, torrents of liquid change, and the continual improvisation of form from chaos. Competition is not so much for life, but for position—who can reach the next span of light or moisture first. And always the hint that everything you see is provisional, on the verge of being unmade and reconfigured.

It is a place that moves and resounds with the ceaseless energy of creation itself, where life is architecture, where water is blood, where to stand still is to be submerged in verdant, living change—a high, wet crucible on the world’s living roof, echoing with wild, wordless invention.
