A place, small as a cathedral and no more welcoming, is held aloft in the dim vastness of a world with no water. It hangs, or rather nestles, high in a place that is all height—perhaps a pinnacle, or the flat top of an impossible plateau, so eroded by eons of wind, chemical dust, or some other agent that its bones seem both sharp and worn smooth. The boundaries are sharply defined: beyond this dense precinct, nothing but the void, or precipice, or the choking air of a gas world. Edges drop away. It is as if a single colossal stone shelf or mesa—the last tooth of an ancient mountain range—has endured, whittled into a final stable form, the surrounding earth long ago flown, abraded to emptiness.

The air glows with a dull and mineral warmth, thick and slow-moving, just enough to heat skin but never to sting. The light is scarce: a perpetual dimness reigns, as if it is always near dusk or veiled sunrise. There are no strong shadows, only overlapping umber, ochre, and bruised violet twilight. What illuminates is uncertain—perhaps some distant sun behind a sullen atmosphere, or perhaps the rock itself, bearing a deep, internal glow, exhausted but not extinguished by time.

And what life! For a place so desiccated—so old—it seethes with beings, as if life has seized and clung and filled every available pocket and ledge with layers of itself. Not classic vegetation, not with water as an ingredient—but lush, yes: minutely variegated beds of dry, mineral plants, crusts and thickets whose tissues hoard not water but some other sweetness, some other precious, slow-flowing essence absorbed from the rock or atmosphere. The growth is ancient and patient—moss-like, lichenous, or something more alien, clustered in dense, interlacing patterns over every surface. Stalking above are trunk-like towers of crystal or carbon, twisted spires wound in coiling sheets, supporting leaves made of hair-fine metal or porous stone: every gap is filled, every overhang draped.

Fauna press close: not just a scattering, but a city of bodies. Everywhere something moves—jointed, crawling, or gliding over the roughened panes, their bodies designed for clinging or burrowing, for drinking in trace heat or gases, for grazing the dense mats and thickets. Chirring, grinding, humming—sound roars and ricochets in this enclosed horizon; a thousand conversations in a language of clicks and drumming, from carapace and stone, from under mats and between fronds. Faces—multi-eyed, furred, masked, mantled—peek from every crevice, bold and alert, thriving in the crowd.

And amidst this life, the built is everywhere: primitive, yes, but unmistakably shaped by hand and intention. The 'urban' is a warren, a hive—dense arrangements of stacked stone, chiseled parapets, layered shelters built up and out, interlocked for warmth, protection, and proximity. Each construction is patched with the living crust of the place—architecture and ecology have become one, with walls crawling in patterned lichen, roofs bristling with spined herbals, paths carpeted in resilient fungal bloom. Tunnels pierce deep in the rock, marked by toolwork—stone and bone, obsidian and resin—simple, effective, worn dull by endless use. Every inch of space is in service: no void is left unfilled.

Rules here are ancient, patient. The world barely changes—processes are long, cycles so slow that one could watch a life begin and end without seeing a season turn. There is no rain, no river, just the whisper of chemistry and the slow accumulation and exhaustion of resources. No great disasters come; instead, gradual adjustments—a wall grows moss, a tunnel collapses and is rebuilt, a shelf gains a new garden.

To stand here is to feel the tight infinity of life—ancient, pressed close, never quite at peace, but never upended. The air hums with the throb of voices and bodies, the slow yield of the rock, the subtle shifting of a world where water never came, and time is measured in the thickening of lichen, the soft accretion of dust, and the quiet recitation of ancient, mineral life.
