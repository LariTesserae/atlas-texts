You stand where high stone meets sky, yet the horizon is broad and the air is thick with sound. This is not the jagged crown of a young range, but the plateaued, weathered roof of a very old mountain land: a sweep of high tablelands, creased in places with long, low valleys and shallow basins, all rough with rock, but their ancient bones softened by time. You can sense the height in your lungs—a thinness—yet the world stretches open around you, not so much an abrupt peak but a continuous, rambling expanse.

The ground underfoot is never parched, though water itself is only glimpsed—trickling from weeping walls of sheer dark stone, pooling briefly in shallow rock pans, slipping under slabs to vanish. The air tastes faintly metallic, wet, and sometimes, if your step breaks moss or lichen on humid days, there’s a sudden exhale of chilled vapor, like the mountain is breathing through old wounds.

Vegetation is less companion than afterthought here: tufts of pale, wiry grasses, sometimes a run of tough-barked shrubs huddling in rain-flushed gullies, but for the most part, naked stone runs to the horizon in undulating, open waves. The closer you look, the more you see—a salt-stained mat of strange succulents hugging cracks, tiny springs feeding inch-high ferns, white roots threading through rock, always scouring for a trace of water. Trees—if they exist—are rare and stunted, twisting against storm and stone with gnarled determination.

Amid this sparseness, humanity—or something like it—has made its mark, and what a mark it is. The plateau vibrates with the rumble and shriek of industry. Clusters of buildings perch on leveled shelves, their outlines angular, metal, almost grown into the terrain. Factories exhale steam that drifts, ghostlike, in the warm updrafts. Elevated walkways and pipelines ribbon between the structures, shining with condensation, tracing out a web that somehow feels both ancient and arrestingly present.

This city—if it is a city—does not thrum with the density of crowded lives, but sprawls, spacious and efficient, its nodes separated by long stretches of bare rock, connected by humming rails and broad concrete causeways. Tall, skeletal towers punctuate the skyline, perhaps wind-driven, perhaps broadcasting, their humming lost beneath the deeper vibration of mechano-industrial engines that echo from deep shafts cut straight into the plateau. Sometimes, you sense a whole chamber of machinery working far below—its presence known not by vision but by tremor.

It is a city for workers of a specific kind: thin-scattered, highly organized, operating by long-standing rules. There is no bustle. The pace is slow—machines are vast, cycles are long, adjustments infrequent. Patterns of smoke and steam change subtly, as if major shifts happen on a timescale too slow for a day's notice. Faces, when you see them, are quick and sharp-eyed, but not hurried; labor here is deliberate, dictated by the patience of stone, water, and weather.

Nature is recessive. Creatures may exist, but rarely breach the open—a broad-winged scavenger circling high, pale insects skimming water-born haze, the rare glimpse of something sinewy crossing weathered stone. Semaphore towers and warning walls mark places where it is unwise to linger; this land has other, understated rules. Here, the territory of people and the slow workings of the world intermingle, but never wholly blend.

Sound dominates: a near-perpetual ground-thunder of turbines and filtered exhaust, a clanging that travels—sometimes so constant it becomes a physical pressure. Now and then, the air splits with an over-loud groan: the ancient rock shifting, or some deep machine correcting course. Conversation is rare and close, always pitched for clarity, as if worried about being muffled or mistaken.

Strangeness seeps in at the margins. A glassy patch of stone that sweats even when rainless. Shadowy runnels where the air seems always to ripple, distorting sight. Slicks of oily mineral—unnamed, rainbowed—coating metal banisters, never drying. The electric lights do not flicker, but sometimes their shadows move, stretched and bent without regard for the forms that cast them.

Here, the cycles are grand. The plateau wears its years like a mantle, shifting only with the seasons, the slow drawdown of water, the grinding of machines into stone. Decay is gradual, and growth—both man-made and natural—must be measured in decades, not days. The whole space, open yet constructed, ringing with deafening persistence, seems carved into the idea of patience: a landscape that will not soon be moved, not easily tamed, and that offers only what you are willing to endure.
