It would be easy to say it is a garden, if gardens were black and made entirely of living things whose shapes are at odds with memory. In the gloom, there is no sky—only a sense of being wholly enclosed, as if secreted away in the bottom-most cell of a giant lung. The space is not small, but it is secretive: a sunken alcove lost beneath the ground, perhaps carved out beneath ancient roots; it feels hidden, embedded within a larger world, but one that has forgotten it long ago.

### Terrain

Where footing might be expected—gravel, dust, or stone—there is only the densely woven matter of plant-life. The “ground” is a thick, matted plain of fibrous roots and fungal hyphae pressed together over eons, flattened under the weight of their own generations. Despite the density of living material, there are no signs of water: no wetness, no droplets clinging in the air, no trickle or sheen. Everything is dry to the point of crumbling, like cork or driftwood, ancient and airless.

The elevation is low: this is a sunken place, perhaps below the reach of what weak daylight might have once existed, ancient and smothered. The substrate feels entirely spent—eroded not by force, but by age, pressed down and retreated from, having achieved a final, unchanging form.

### Atmosphere & Light

There is darkness—perfect and consuming, as if light itself cannot reach, or has been excluded deliberately. Sensation in the darkness comes instead by sound and smell, touch and vibration. A faint sweet rot: that is always present, a dry decay that is somehow comforting, not tragic. Often, loud sounds roll and echo—creaks, cracklings, the patient stretch and compress of living structures slowly adjusting their positions over days and weeks. Sometimes it is like the moan of a ship’s wood, sometimes like the slow fracturing of a glacier, but always alive, always purposeful.

### Architecture

The environment is unmistakably built. Walls, columns, and high branching vaults arise not from stone or iron, but from tree-trunks braided and pressed, bark tough and petrified with age. Great ribs of lignin curl overhead, grown into vaults and archways. What were once creepers or branches now serve as ordered passageways, bridges, and buttresses—everything composed of plant-form, but marshalled, guided, sculpted with a care that speaks of generations. The city’s plan is visible only in glimpses—the way pillars align, the subtle terraced layers on the ground, the suggestion that order persists, even as the green life shifts and renews itself eternally.

Small objects and furnishings are plant-based too: fibrous benches, woven basket-containers, containers carved from ancient knots or hollowed burls. Everything is made by hand—primitive, without forge or fire. Sharp-edged tools of stone, bone, or glassy plant resin are found, dropped or rested in nooks, evidence of slow, manual craft.

### Ecology & Life

There are no animals here, no birds, no scurrying things, nothing with eyes or voices. Yet the plants are everything. They are substrate and wall, they are structure and purpose. Some, pearly and blind, slowly unfold tendrils across the ceiling; others curl into themselves, opening only when a certain vibration passes. Underfoot, ancient mosses and dry lichens crunch softly, a crust that has been walked by many, but not recently. Muted scents move through the air, a mingling of resin, bark, and subtle floral decay that thickens the sense of living age.

Now and then, the suggestion of faint phosphorescence: a knot in the wall will glow weakly as a thick cable of yucca-fiber slides across it at a pace too slow to see, or a sunken bloom opens with a loud, dry rustle. But otherwise, direction comes by sensation and memory, not sight.

### Soundscape

The most constant feature is sound: low, rumbling groans as the great trunks subtly shift; the dry crackle of new growth forcing through the ancient, pressed layers. There’s a symphony of creaks, pops, and fibrous snaps that mark the architecture’s slow metamorphosis. Every now and then, a root threads itself through a wall and the sound swells and fades—a slow conversation between plant and plant. The built environment is in constant, very gradual, but never-quiet motion.

### Rules of the World

Nothing here rushes; even loud events unfold over hours, or days. The inhabitants (if ever there were any) must have learned patience, endurance, and to live within the pulse of plant-life rhythms. Simple tools shape the plant world, but the plant world shapes in return—architecture responds, bends, adapts, swallowing or extruding space as it ages.

The absence of water changes everything: life is not juicy or soft, but dry, slow, tight-bonded, and persistent. Chemistry is founded not in liquid flow, but in mineral transfer, evaporation, and crystalline formation within living tissues. The city never rots—at least, not in a way familiar to moisture-driven decay. Instead, age petrifies, preserves, and memorializes every root and branch.

### Impression

To walk here is to feel like an insect in the hollow of a long-dead tree: the conversation of age, the hush of a world wrapped in perpetual night, built not of dust and stone but of living, unspeaking timber. There is emptiness, a cavernous sense of absence between the wildly arching trunks, as if the inhabitants have all left or turned into moss themselves. Nevertheless, there is an order, and a surprising comfort—everything is as it should be, and will go on shifting, slow and loud, for as long as root and fiber endure.
