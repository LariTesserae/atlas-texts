Below, among the root-lit worlds, the cathedral swamp hums.

The place is *under*—under forest, under hill, under whatever surface world remains—it lies deep within the ground, but it does not lie: it rises. The “floor” is nowhere; a confusion of roots, boles, and clutching tendrils mesh in black-shadowed fans, interlocked to the point of indistinction. There isn’t bare earth. There is only living mass, so tightly intergrown you cannot say whose tissue is whose.

Here, “packed” is the law. Every cubic inch is occupied, not by air or stone or animal, but by one endless vegetative organism, or many, pressed tight. There are layered walls woven of fibrous trunks, spongy with damp, branching at impossible, interlocking angles. Overhead, the vaulting is a braided canopy of leaf-plates and waxy glasslike fronds grown-wide by adaptation to dimness—light is scant, only what filters muddied from hidden pores in the roof above, and from the slow, exuding luminescence of phosphor-bark and dripping lichen. Pools mirror the arching, root-ribbed ceilings, surface quivering with the perpetual rain of condensed moisture.

The air is *wet*, not just humid but fog-thick and shining. Warmth hangs close, pressed by the enclosing wood, vapor breathing in slow cloudlets from every open pore, condensing on every surface. In places, rivulets thread sinuous paths along bark valleys, and here and there—where the intergrown canopy thins—droplets become quiet curtains, always in descent. Beneath, the ground is a mat of root and sponge, always springy, always clotted with standing water. Occasional pools are dense with duckweed, ferns, and strangling nets of hydrophytic vines; everywhere, plants grow upon plants upon plants, green and brown and red and the ghostly blue of cave-adapted growth.

To call it “urban” is not a metaphor. Amid the root-walls and spongy mainways, the plants themselves *build*—vaults, corridors, chambers. Some are engineered, grown into recognizable forms: archways ribbed in glossy, sap-stained hardwood; stairways spiraled from braided creepers; buttresses like the bones of whales, looped overhead in living mass, punctuated by bioluminescent nodes. Infrastructure grows itself: sap-channels like piping, xylem-grown capillaries pulsing with transport fluid; great, leaf-drained cisterns clinging to the ceiling, constantly filling and weeping; nodal chambers where growth is sped and maintained by still-strange plant engines humming beneath glistening, translucent bulbs. The city is both settlement and substrate: every room a cell, every corridor a vein.

There is industry, not of steel and flame but of regulated lifecycles. Vat-flowers of mottled green and yellow churn out fibrous pulp, guided by subtle biochemical signals; mycelial weaving mills turn starchy sap into long, webbed fibers—for nets, for clothing, for repair of breakages in the mat; fermentation chambers gleam behind walls of root, their moist sigh audible in the root-thrum. Electricity is generated from piezoelectric bark, from the stolid, ongoing flex of pressure plants under the weight of their own communal mass, from the wet-rot and slow shifting of the city’s biomass. Mechanisms are grown, not built, and powered by the never-ending cycle of growth and decay.

No animal life passes here. There are no insects, no rodents scurrying below; decomposition is handled by plants themselves, with digestive pitcher hollows and slow, enzymatic sap. Even microbial life seems muted; competition and slow-moving cycles leave the world free of most things that creep. The only movement is the gradual extrusion of new roots, the slow swelling of trunks, the patient flex as one living wall yields, another thickens, always over centuries. To the senses, the city seems still—except for water, except for noise.

For it is *loud*: an ongoing riot of hydraulic sighs, the drumming of droplets on the spongy mat, the sucking and popping of air in plant-chambers, the internal groan as pressed-xylem shifts fibers, the susurrus of the great pulses of sap, and something like a wordless choir—the harmonics, the deep sub-bass quivering, the creak and tremble of the packed mass. No silence is ever permitted. The place is a song of itself in wet, living wood.

Rules of the world are gentle and patient. Change is slow. No sharp transitions, no sudden shifts in architecture—just the unyielding, ceaseless occupation of space by all life, all green, all living. To walk here is to move through threshold after threshold without ever being released into empty air—always under arch, pressed on all sides by texture and scent and humidity, in the eternal twilight of this under-biosphere.

Here—packed, lived, humming amid the water, the city of plant is forever itself—slow, alive, and vast, in a space the size of a cathedral, the tangle home, the biological labyrinth, the endless, dim green hum.
