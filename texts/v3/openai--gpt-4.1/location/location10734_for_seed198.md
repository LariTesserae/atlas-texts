It is a place that hums, but the hum is muffled—an engine’s drone heard through thick fog and sheets of dripping leaves. You stand in the belly of a constructed world where the land is elevated, yet not sharply mountainous—rather, rising, sloping, every surface calibrated, layered, and built up over eras of intervention. Think of a plateau, but its bones are steel and composite, dressed in coatings of living green. Terrain undulates gently, but not a patch of it is untouched: every horizontal is paved with something, every vertical faced or scaffolded, every edge notched or guttered, no stone or dirt or root left raw.

The air around you is cool, moist—sometimes almost cold in the shadowy chasms between immense edifices, walls and terraces ascending on all sides, each shingled and clad in living material. Water is ever-present, not by torrents but as a thread in the weave: it beads along cables sagged between walkways, accumulates in pans and gutters, oozes from seams in the wall like the sweat of the city itself. Thin mist snakes in at dawn, condenses on the underside of vast walkways, and falls in a soft, constant drizzle, feeding a thousand vertical gardens. This is the source of freshness, the skin of the place never dry—a fabric cooled and kept alive by rainwork and aeration.

Lushness does not mean openness. Here, life has filled every crack, climbed every engineered surface, been guided along every lattice and strut. Where you would expect concrete, you find moss-thick tiles studded into flexible mesh; where cities would offer hard lines, this place swells and bulges with root-pockets and vertical farms, with spiney creepers and swollen ferns engineered for shade and vigor. Light is scant, lost before it reaches the old highways two stories below; the sky is mostly a memory, refracted through the columns of growth and industry. Soft, green-edged glow panels poke through the undergrowth, and on higher decks, thick glass domes distil dim daylight to deeper levels—never quite enough, but always enough to live by.

The sense of containment is paradoxical: you are in open country measured in valleys and small towns—the scale of a region, sprawled not across but *within* high-elevation artificial mountains. Each valley is terraced, each terrace stacked dense as honeycomb: factories, gardens, living quarters, waterworks, transport arteries, all pressed together. A full anatomy of engineered elements, sited and worn to comfort over uncounted generations of habitation. Yet nothing is ever still.

The density is total—too many things in every cubic meter. The margins, the between, don’t exist; walls, rails, and root-mesh alternate without a break, a city-built ecosystem so jammed that if you removed a piece, the others might collapse inward like foam. Walkways teem not with people but with machines: conveyor pods and spider-jointed service automatons scuttle and climb; pulley-plant hybrids creep through ventilation gaps. Doors open into manufactories, power rooms, fermentation baths, water-capture systems, and a dozen other workshops; their engines pulse quietly behind insulated walls, spinning, stirring, reacting—never thundering, but present as a constant, soft-ticked undercurrent.

Fauna are rare, and those you see have the look of the allowed: small birds in the upper gardens, the occasional cat-shaped shadow padding over the warmest pipes. Mostly it’s a world of engineered flora and machinery, the biosphere crowded out except for these curated pets. Insects, too, are rare, with only the hardiest permitted their niches inside the all-encompassing structure. With this comes quiet, save for the faint creaks of structure, the effervescent hiss of condensers, and—like a distant sea—the wisdom of so many engines synchronizing themselves against the hush.

It is a dynamic place—turbulent, even. The surface seems at first balanced in perennial damp, but beneath, mechanisms shift at every hour: compartments repurposed, structures recombined, the flow of energy and people and freight restlessly redirected. Markets flower and fade daily in the terraces, manufacture migrates from corner to corner—overseen by signals that flicker in the mist, organizing the near-chaos. No day’s geography resembles the last, for infrastructure is built to be reconfigured, technologies replaced piece by piece, nodes built up even as neighboring ones decay and are stripped bare for their parts. There is no "original" earth here, only the long action of construction and corrosion, the self-renewal and self-composting of a city-world that will neither finish itself nor cease its growth and transformation.

Here, rules are soft but omnipresent, embedded in the pattern of use and the restraints of tight, necessary space. To live is to find a role, to not impede, to add to the meshwork—your act becomes another strand in the infinite layering, part of the next cycle of building, repair, or feeding the roots.

If you stretch your senses far, it feels like the breathless hustle of a bazaar, compounded with the moist, secret life of a rainforest, inside the sheltering skin of a machine. There is no wildness, only organized improvisation: a constructed chaos, lush and dim, with the certainty of water and the work of endless hands.
