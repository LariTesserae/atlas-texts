At first, there is only night. Not true night—a subdued, muted daylight floats thinly from a sun already exiled by clouds, lingering near the horizon if it is present at all. The light is faint and bluish, sinking into mist and old snow, struggling to reach down through vaporous swathes that drift endlessly past. Imagine vastness, not quite endless but vast enough for distances to become concepts rather than specifics. Coldness is everywhere, biting against the skin, lingering at the root of every stone and lichen.

You stand amid a kind of primordial mountain range, but not one scarred by time—rather, you sense immediacy, as if geology is happening even now, the world’s bones being pressed and folded, crumpled and raised, not yet ground by wind or water. Immense knife-back ridges, jagged, sharp-edged, bare rock thrusting into thinning sky. Yet the peaks themselves are oddly smooth in places, as if still molten within, surfaces glazed by ancient heat now cooling. The slopes are open, raw, and weather-beaten, devoid of paths or signs of passage.

Everywhere, water lives close to the surface—a thin skin between ice and vapor. In bowls and fissures, it seeps out shyly, accumulating in still pools that rim the ledges, or slinking down the cliffs to darken the stones. Sometimes, faint veils of mist rise from wet rock faces—the result of moisture condensing from the breath of clouds. Swathes of snow, sometimes fresh and powdery, sometimes hard and glinting with blue reflections, patch these surfaces. From time to time, a rill of meltwater zigzags down, threading a silver stitch along the mountain’s body, quiet but constant, gathering to charge nameless streams in the colder valleys below.

Vegetation clings on the edge of what is possible: tufts and threads and low mats of color—lichen in acid greens, rusty oranges, and dull purples, patchy mosses and stunted grasses in the rare soil pockets where glacial dust has settled. Here and there, lonely shrubs hunker low to the rock, branches twisted and limbs bare, their tiny leaves shining with condensed dew. You taste the scarcity in the air; every living thing wages a battle to exist.

Yet life is surprisingly dense wherever it can gain a footing. The rocks and snowfields are alive with motion—swarms of small, hardy creatures bustling through the cracks and patches: burrowing rodents with ever-moving noses; herds of nimble, long-limbed grazers bounding over loose scree; predatory birds circling on updrafts, waiting for a careless lapse among their prey; and, in the wet, shadowed nooks, colonies of amphibious creatures chirping in muted coordination. Though individuals are elusive and much space yawns between clusters, the pulses of this biome resound—a shape in silence, a color darting across the field of blankness, a scrabble of claws echoing between boulders.

In the distance, on a lower plateau where the ridges flatten and moisture gathers, something much stranger—a bristling interregnum of motion, whirring and chunking and grinding. Here is the hum of engines, the sigh of bellows, the rattle of chain—without visible hand or artifice, machines or industry emerge as processes of the world itself: mineral veins pulse and rumble as they are forced toward the air, sometimes flexing like muscles, at other times ejecting a mechanical shimmer of fine, raw ore into the rivulets. The vapor rises in regular spurts, condensing to lacy frost. There is neither polish nor direction to this technological outpouring—just chemistry and pressure, something midwifed by the mountain as a natural act. Tiny metallic beetles, their shucked shells littering the hollows, scavenge for nutrients, clicking and glimmering as they cluster.

No signs indicate human passage—no roads, no ruins, not even the faintest bridge or marker. Whatever industry this is, it is not one of civilizations but perhaps of the world itself: the mountains extruding their own implacable, self-assembling engines, networks of strange, metallic filaments binding, breaking, rebuilding in the stony dark, overseen by the cold intellect of geology’s own acceleration.

The atmosphere is pungent—sharp hints of ozone and minerals, the faint, tangy must of slow decay, underlaid by the freshness of water that expects to become ice again soon. Wind moves intermittently: sometimes hard bursts whistling between the ridges, scattering loose scree; sometimes nothing at all, so quiet that your own breathing becomes intrusive, too warm for the place.

You listen: mostly nothing. The watery slap of occasional landslip, the muted calls of predators on the hunt, the low, almost inaudible working of stone-grinders far off, seamlessly blending with the solid, humming silence at the heart of this place.

Rule here is the law of emergence, of becoming and perishing in the permanent birth-agony of new land. Nothing is finished; everything in flux and on the edge of forming or vanishing. The place cares nothing for presence—it offers space and raw materials, and the choreography that follows is played out by weather, stone, and the persistent, improbable rush of life. This is the world at the threshold, the forge of mountains, where all things are made and almost nothing is settled—where you can watch the planet learning to be itself, over and over, never quite the same.
