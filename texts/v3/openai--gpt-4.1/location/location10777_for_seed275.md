You descend, at first, into absence—an abyss beneath the surface where water is unthinkable, where cool air clots with ancient mineral dust and the taste of raw, unfinished stone. But this is not a tomb. It is an underland in the process of becoming, an embryonic region, sprawling further than sight in vaults and talus-throated canyons, like the unrolled folds beneath a world’s crust.

The darkness that might be expected here is, instead, staved off by a diffuse, persistent glow—a generous clarity, as though the stone arches and basins were illuminated from within or by countless softly glowing bodies. Light filters through immense botanical structures: not trees in any familiar sense, but columns, veils, and webs of plant matter, flesh as architecture, the substrate itself alive.

The floor is a patchwork of living forms: thick, succulent mats of tuberous growth, ridges and terraces composed of fibrous, pale-green plaits, bioluminescent lianas humming quietly with stored energy. Roots dangle in columns between levels, encrusting every available ledge, each strand coated with a powdery, wind-blown mineral dust—the closest thing to precipitation in this parched world. Here and there, a sense of emptiness persists; pockets and clearings where the plant-tissue has not yet claimed the terrain, revealing raw mineral or voids into deeper layers.

This is not the dense, riotous claustrophobia of a jungle. The mass of plant is total in its dominance, but not suffocating: wide avenues, whorled about great organ-pillar growths, stretch into communal clearings or through arched tunnels, giving the space a sense of public scale. This is an urban place, planned or grown according to some organizing will—almost citylike in its networks and plazas, its distinct but open neighborhoods.

You move among constructs whose purpose is ambiguous: some are clearly shelters or communal spaces, carved into living walls or woven up from root-tubes and membrane, but others bristle with engines: turbines driven by thick fibrous tendrils, silent conveyorways floating cells of raw plant matter, conduits throbbing with chemical current—industrial mechanisms, but growing and grown into the substrate, rather than assembled. Metal exists here, but rarely. A faint scent of resin, sweet and strange, signals the interfacing of living and constructed. This is a world whose “machines” are made of and maintained by plant, perhaps even consciously grown to order.

Despite the apparent dominance of vegetative mass, life is active. Audible only if you strain for it, a chorus of movement: scraping, clicking, the whisper of scaled limbs on ribbed plant-husk. Small animals—soft-shelled, pale, thriving in cool, dry conditions—nestle among the rootworks, leap the empty vaults, dart into half-grown passageways. Flocks of pale-mottled creatures wheel through vertical spaces, their wings almost like petals. Predators, sinewed with some opaline chitin, drift quietly, patient and nearly silent but for the faintest hiss as they move.

No liquid water drips or pools here. The rarest moisture—delivered by drifting mists of condensed atmospheric minerals or hinted at in the glister of crystal on plant surfaces—moves through entirely different channels: absorbed, stored, rationed, exchanged in the raw, permeable chemistry of these plants. It is a dryness so complete that to touch anything is to risk becoming desiccated, unless you bear the right hides or skins.

The world is emerging. Growth patterns repeat and fracture: some neighborhoods are dense and formal, others ragged with new tendrils reaching, architectures half-realized, unfinished spiral ramps, domes whose purpose is not yet clear. Sections are unfinished: bare vaults, scaffolds of tough white root, the sense of construction in progress everywhere, as if the city is always in the middle of becoming itself.

Rules here are strange, half invisible. The plants are city, path, and energy store; their industries are organic, spread by intention as much as design. Technology isn’t separate, but nested within and grown from substrate-living matter. Ownership, memory, and tradition seem encoded more in the rhythm of regrowth than in written law. Disputes are settled in exchanges of growth direction; alliances marked by twined tendrils or deliberately coiled root patterns.

Sound is an absence, most of the time. Only when you listen closely do you notice the creak of living structures flexing in the cool air, the soft churn of matter being moved deep in the walls, the low, collective hum of organisms metabolizing stone and light. Communication—among plants, between plants and their attendant fauna, between whatever operates or tends the industrial growths—may occur as pheromones, as shifting coloration, as a subtle dance of stalks and petals in the light.

This is not a “city” as we would know it, but a living, growing world: dry, vast, active, full of space—and always being built, by industry and nature indivisible from each other, alive in every sense, and quietly alien to us.
