Here is the world:

You stand at the edge of a valley that twists out of sight in both directions, a sweeping expanse that defies easy mapping: not so much a bowl as an undulating shelf, ridged and scalloped by abrupt shelves and sharp-edged hummocks, their shapes too clean and sudden to be the work of slow erosion. The ground here feels young beneath your boots: raw, fractured, and unsettled, a land only recently upthrust or torn free from the grip of some greater structure. Stone ridges curl like unfinished sentences beneath dense mats of vegetation—a wild, bottle-green tangle rising from rich, wet earth.

The air is cold. Chill even for what passes as "summer" here, clinging close to the ground in heavy veils. Every surface — the stones, the leaves, your own skin — gleams with moisture as if the world itself is exhaling a cold breath that will never warm. All around, water makes its presence felt: not as lakes or rivers, but as a ceaseless seeping and gentle weeping—slick-walled cracks weep mineral-rich tears that feed mosses glowing faintly in the dark; grassblade-thick rivulets and quicksilver threads etch temporary courses between roots; occasional surges of mist coil up from the chilled ground like exhausted spirits rising and drifting. Pools form overnight, and are gone by noon.

Yet, for all its saturation, standing water is rare. Instead, this is an exhaling landscape, always on the verge of revealing or reclaiming its wet heart, a place where the surface tension of the world is almost visible—as if the next moment, the air itself might condense and run in streams. Rain is never far off; even in drought, the stones glisten.

Vegetation crowds every conceivable niche. The ground is thick-matted with low, immortally green shrubs, intertwined ferns, and whiplash creeper-vines that throb with moisture—a dense, three-dimensional labyrinth that rises and folds, vines climbing over one another to seek any stray shaft of light in the gloom. Older, broader-trunked plants—halfway between lichen and tree—drip tendrils that meet the mat below, closing circuits of living matter. Fungi spread broad, bioluminescent brackets in clusters, painting the dark in cold, soft hues, sometimes drawing lines of eerie, functional light across passageways.

But the world is very dark: nearly subterranean, under a ceaseless twilight. The sky, if visible through the ever-thick ceiling of cloud, might show swirling graphite or a chilled violet, but mostly overhead is blocked by so much growth, woven thick with mosses and shining lichen crusts that glint in the uncertain gloom. Here at ground-level, the light is a sullen, indirect exudation, emerging from the phosphorescence of living things, intermingled with the reflections on slick wet leaves. Sometimes a distant, unguessable pulse flickers through the undergrowth—something made, not organic, so sharp and regular your teeth ache.

Structures: Scattered signs of forgotten or abandoned intent rise here and there—a curved metallic pylon, half-swallowed by climbing foliage, arrests the eye; a rectangular platform at a node between trickling springs pulses faintly in the darkness, emitting stray, soft-edged fields that repel mold and freeze water for a moment before the heat of growth resumes its work. You make out fragments of glasslike fiber snaking through the undergrowth, woven with the roots and stones, pulsing with data or power, destination unclear. Machinery is here, but not in any familiar form—a suggestion of ordered symmetry in the moss, the regular rhythm behind a hum, an inexplicable patterned rainfall at the mouth of a narrow cutting.

In the air, sound rules, drowning everything. The world throbs with a thousand-thousand living presences and the tumult of their passing: a constant cacophony of calls, clicks, shrieks, and low, juddering roars, all modulated by the bouncing and focusing of noise through organic and technological channels. The ground shivers under your feet, not simply from tremors but from the ceaseless movement of things beneath the surface. Shrill insectile drones blend with hollow, mechanical booms that echo from unseen machines. Every brush of leaf on leaf is a drumbeat. On the rare moments when something quieter passes, you recognize it only as absence.

Fauna of every description dart and crawl and rustle through the gloom, some close to the soil, others suspended in mid-air by gravity-defying limbs or pulsing fields. Much of the movement seems random, but moments of pattern emerge—swarming flights of iridescent microfauna choreographing sharp, luminous dances; larger shapes crash together at the margins of pools, then dissolve between tree-boles; something with too many legs ripples over a shining cable and vanishes as if folding itself into the air.

Change is everywhere, impatient and total. Every hillock may have shifted since you last looked. Pools appear, then vanish in an hour; bridges form from living tendrils, only to rot within days. The mushroom-glow of the forest pulses faster at times, as if signaling, and the air grows acoustically crowded with the shrieks of unseen engineers, animal or otherwise. The world is in a permanent state of storm: not just weather, but invention. Structures tug at themselves, growing or melting; ruins reveal a new machine or vanish beneath a wash of sudden moss. It is impossible to mark a place and be certain of its persistence. The land, the wet vegetation, the scattered, half-alive technology—all churn and negotiate, never still.

And yet, beneath the uproar, there is a sense of coherence: some underlying civic code, older than the tech, older than the plants—something that adjudicates the chaos and lets it persist, not as disaster but as a kind of feverish, crowded harmony. Here, everything is temporary, but nothing is accidental. Every sound, every surge, every flicker of strange machinery in the deep cold dark, happens somewhere just beyond your ability to explain—or to look away.
