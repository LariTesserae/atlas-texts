You climb out from a freight elevator into a city that was never built for humans. The air is heavy with a dry warmth that should feel suffocating, yet it saps moisture without threat of burning. All around, the terrain is impossibly ancient: the ground is not stone or dust but a strange, layered mineral pressed and fused across chasms and shallow plateaus, stripped bare by eons of motionless time. Erosion, long since finished, has pared the world down to honest shape — no cliffs fall, no ridges wear, no grit blows here. The land’s forms are final, worn smooth by an age so deep its memory is in everything you see.

Above is darkness, not of night but of an absolute absence. Whether this world hangs in a great void or beneath a dying sky, there is neither starlight nor sun. Occasional industrial lamps glow at vast distances — islands of sodium-orange afloat in otherwise unbroken black. Illumination has been shaped not for beauty, but for function: low lumen, long range, casting contourless, hard-edged shadows onto the mineral sheets and the bones of this place.

There is no sign of water. Pits glitter with a taste of salt or some unknown crystal, sharp and dense underfoot. The surfaces recall dried lakes that have never known rain, marked only by the periodic scarring of long-gone machines, patternless but regular, as if every intervention here has been precisely calculated. The air carries the tiniest ring of something metallic, the only echo in a world lacking wind, rivers, or even the memory of a storm.

Vegetation: none. Not a trace of plant, lichen, nor even stain left by something decayed. Life, at best, is a rumor — the kind passed through whispered stories from the occasional maintenance drone or ferrous rat, glimpsed in corners, moving too quickly or too smoothly for the eye to hold. Their presence is rare, and in this long-quiet place, any movement reads as almost miraculous, a flicker of pattern in near-total entropy.

The city itself is vast, but only barely present. Built into, upon, and through the oldest strata, its infrastructure is arranged with industrial order: broad yet scattered — distant blocks of what could once have been factories, clustered down snaking boulevards of conveyor-belts and half-lit cable towers, separated by enormous, empty fields of frosted glass or dark metal plazas. Construction is both deep and layered; some sections disappear beneath the ground, linked by massive shafts and inert elevator channels, their walls marked by cryptic numbering and sigils of long-dead collectives.

Sound is mostly an absence; what you hear is the low mechanical hum from power transmission lines and, now and then, the percussive clank of cooling metal somewhere out of sight. Your own footfall is swallowed by the soft, dry surface, muffled almost instantly. No wind, no running water, no insect or bird; the only native acoustics are the deep thuds of distant machinery cycling through routines older than memory.

Ecology, if it exists, does not play by the rules of Earth: the rare creatures — resistant things, engineered perhaps, or simply adapted to mineral and darkness — live inside service conduits or burrow motionlessly, waiting on schedules measured in centuries rather than days. Energy flickers along transmission rails, more like nervous electrical storms than life, sometimes visible where darkness gives way to deliberate activity.

Nothing grows. Nothing flows. Change is even rarer than life: this world is held in equilibrium, an ancient city’s last holding pattern, technologies cycling quietly to ensure the balance holds. All gradients — of heat, pressure, life — have long since been spent and stilled. Here, all that is left is form, memory, and the cryptic machinery built to preserve the last possible version of this place.

It is an uneasy peace, a graveyard kept alive by preserved intent, engineered to produce neither waste nor growth. Still, as you cross the mineral plains toward those distant lights, the city spreads wider than ever, resilient and inscrutable, its silences broken only by your presence and the eternal humming in the veins beneath your feet.
