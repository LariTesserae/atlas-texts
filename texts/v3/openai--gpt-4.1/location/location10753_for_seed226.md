High in the world’s early bones, barely shouldering their way from beneath the planet’s skin, there is a place you could call a city — if city means a gathering of forms and intentions, not merely crowded buildings and the drone of commerce.

You approach it through rising wisps of cloud and drifting mist. The air is cool, but not biting: it wets your breath but does not paralyze. Moisture is everywhere, not pooled so much as beaded in the creases of stone, clinging to the boughs, saturating moss and mist alike. This is “wet” not by raging river or marsh, but by constant, gentle weeping from the high, half-formed rock — as though the world itself is sweating, only recently emerged from the fire, unsure what to do with the water drawn up into this newborn altitude.

Around you, lushness: everywhere the urge of green. The vegetation moves in a slow riot, exultant, crowding wherever it finds purchase — but the scale is bounded, as if held softly to a rulebook. This is not an ocean of jungle, but a series of living alcoves, moss-girded chambers, and tight, soaring groves. Everything here presses upwards in little columns and clusters, drawn by normal, generous daylight, gathering around the geology’s abrupt emergence.

Yet the space itself feels *empty*: what has risen is not yet filled— it’s vast between what presences there are, echoes ringing through strange stone halls open to the raining sky, hung with rare, riotous gardens that haven’t had time or occasion to grow crowded. You might step from an almost-empty room — its walls no more than pale rock ribs rising and branching like frozen foam — into a hollow dizzy with vines and mist, and then, a dozen paces later, find yourself eye to eye with nothing but stone and a single, gushing root.

The city rises in fits and spurts — not of brick or timber, but of living, worked material. Humans and prehumans live here, or came through recently: at the scale of a hamlet or a holy complex. Their tools are stone, bone, shaped wood, maybe a scrap or two of hammered copper, a suggestion only of what might one day be industry. Buildings are lashed together, fitted into gaps and crannies, woven into what the stone provides: platforms of bark and bracken, ladders from sinew and sapling, walls of interspersed earth and woven grass. But it is organized — if you stood above it, like a cloud passing, you’d see a layout: lines of movement, gathering places, divides between living and ritual and store.

This city feels *embryonic*: the very mountain it is built upon is raw and new, stone thrust up in chaotic, jagged lines, porous, unweathered. It is unscarred. Very little has yet crumbled or worn; forms are crisp, edges keen. There is almost no soil, only beginning slicks of dark matter in hollows and runnels. Lichens, moss, and the first tenacious ferns claw at the rock’s surface— hopeful, lush, but young.

Fauna are scarce; here, life is mostly quiet, almost absent. No herds, no flocks. From far away, occasionally, a strange animal’s call sounds — part echo, part presence, hard to distinguish from the wind, which itself is rarely still. When something does move, it echoes through the chambers, seeming larger and more significant than the source—a lone, enormous beetle lumbers across a stone span, its carapace catching the light, or a dart of bird or soft-winged moth erupts and vanishes in a blink. 

Sound fills the space: always water drips and trickles, amplified by natural cisterns; wood creaks under foot and hand. Voices echo with strange clarity by day; at night, the sighing of wind through unfinished stone corridors, the snap and hiss of fire, the muffled breathing of clustered sleepers. It is not a quiet place, despite its emptiness — every presence booms in the absence of crowding.

And there is a breath of the unusual, the weird: vapor sometimes condenses in sheets, rolling with odd speed through the rock halls, pooling in places like an animal gathering its strength. Occasionally, stone or wood hums very softly just beneath hearing, as if the world itself is settling, learning its new shape.

Almost nothing happens quickly here: processes are slow, stubborn. The mountain’s growth is measured in the memory of eruptions, the city’s expansion governed by the slow accumulation of workable matter—each hut, each platform an earned increment. The people do not rush. The cycles of water, stone, sun, life, give the sense that time is thick, movements deliberate, nothing yet swept away or truly endangered.

It is a place of potential, dense with the possibility of becoming — the city, the mountain, the gathered humans and their allies, all on the edge of formation, not quite fixed. Everything seems to wait: poised on the brink, wet with birth, marrow-deep in cool air and echoing potential, before the weight and crowding of ages to come.
