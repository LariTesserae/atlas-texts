The place is an irregular bowl carved into high stone, neither fully gorge nor valley but an enclave—an elevated chamber cupped by jagged, unweathered cliffs that thrust upward in geometric confusion, a new piece of the world split from some older plateau by untimely force. Pale stone ribs grasp at a sky of cool mist, the atmosphere blue and silver whenever the cloud-thicket parts to probe the sparse light below.

The air is chill, sharply fragrant—lichen, damp earth, the astringency of crushed ferns. Mist drifts and condenses into slick beads on stone and foliage, scattering scant sunlight into faint halos. Some days, rain sweeps the place all at once, washing the rock faces, pearling on every leaf. Yet the bowl is largely protected: long spells of dryness alternate with sudden, greedy moisture.

Within the softened center of the bowl lies a settlement, neither village nor city—a scattering of huts and shelters, their forms both familiar and unfamiliar. Walls curve from thick branches and newly-cut timber, lashed with sinew or grass-rope, weighted with slabs of pale stone. The structures perch with strange spacing, never crowding, each with a clear buffer—ordered by some principle of absence or exclusion, giving every building a visible margin of emptiness, an open buffer between one presence and the next.

Underneath, the ground is uneven: hard-packed soil, spongy moss, fist-sized stones weeping condensation. Rough-hewn footpaths, edged with woven rushes and logs, make uncertain circuits among shelters, following the drainage or seeking dry footing in the frequent damp.

Flora surges in exuberance: great fronds and wildflowers vibrate with color and moisture, mosses coat the vertical rock, small trees root in improbable cracks. Vines slip from heights and droop across paths. Vegetation seems far denser than the human constructions—yet paradoxically, it too adheres to a pattern of spacing: great clusters separated by open ground, copses and miniature jungles set apart by swathes of just-sprouted grass or bare stone. Here and there, springs erupt from fissures, trickling into shallow, crystalline pools, too tiny for real rivers but enough to feed the sodden tangle of moss, and to steam faintly on cold days.

And all through this sheltered amphitheater, life roils in unreal abundance. Fauna crawl and leap and glitter, but rarely betray themselves with sound. The air seethes with insects, wings iridescent, so many that the boundaries between swarm and individual almost vanish: patterned beetles, moths heavy as leaves and twice as cryptic, centipede chains and minute aerial hunters tensed in impossible poise. Every patch of stillness—moss, stone, water—seems alive with trembling life: frogs with glass bones crouch in shadow, pale lizards flick through sunbeams, eyeless something-or-others slip in the pools. Larger presences pass rarely but unmistakably: a velvet-antlered thing peers from fern-shadow, crowned matter-of-factly with bone and fungus, vanishes again before step or sound can follow. The people here build with deference; their tools and food-hoards are lashed behind shuttered fences, every refuse-pile and middens heavily anchored, as if to keep the animal world out, or perhaps to prevent its overflow within.

Sound is nearly absent. Even with an ecosystem racing at a frictionless pace—predators swallow prey in silence, claws and wings barely disturbing the hush. The running water softly gurgles, sometimes a wet patter as condensation yields a drip. The wind is slow here among the stones, the bowl swallowing all but the quietest whistle. Once or twice a day, a distant, resonant click echoes through the enclosure—an unstated signal from its animal denizens, or a warning, or a game, or a rite. It is never clear.

Despite the apparent fecundity, the place feels sparse, haunted by its own empty intervals—every abundance recoiling from another abundance to permit space: between house and house, between thicket and pool, between creature and creature.

Strangeness pervades. Bright fungi grow in fractal rosettes, spirals of greenfire blue at the edge of sight. Sometimes, a hexagonal patch of moss begins to convulse, sending up a cloud of spores in answer to some inaudible pulse. At rare dusk hours, certain plants seem to move: ferns rearrange their fronds, a creeping vine flexes in patterns that mirror the silent insect traffic overhead. All things avoid the miniature springs with peculiar precision; their waters flash prismatic in dim light, and even human hands do not touch.

The settlement is old in layout, young in material—built new on stones that have not eroded, wood cut from trees that have only just caught hold. Tools are basic: hand-axes of stone, baskets of interwoven reeds, hearths of cobbled rocks never quite stained by use. Yet everything displays geometric care, from the placement of shelters to the stacking of stone to the clearings shaped not by randomness but deliberate exclusion, as though the place were held in thrall to rules predating memory.

If there are laws here, they belong first to the animal multitude and the pulse of the fertile, and the humans remain second, arranging their life in the margins granted by strangeness and abundance—distinct, always buffered, in the dim and cool and urgent hush of a living bowl not wholly owned by either man or thing.
