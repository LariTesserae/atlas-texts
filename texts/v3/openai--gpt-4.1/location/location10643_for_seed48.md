**A Landscape of Steam, Stone, and Life**

The land stretches open and low—valley-broad, easy underfoot yet giving a sense of sunkenness, as if some basin once gouged by ancient rivers has since relaxed into a permanent state of damp. There are no trees, nor shrubs, nor grass: not even the ghost of plant life stirs along the ground. Instead, the surface is dominated by slick, dark stones—rounded over the ages, pitted by chemical rains, marked by mineral veins in ochres and green-blues leaching from deep below, crusted here and there at their roots with a blush of salt or rust. 

Faint hollows and runnels filled with thin, sluggish sheets of water catch the dim light, tracing dendritic patterns across the plain. There are no roaring streams, but water is everywhere: beading on the undersides of rocks, condensed in shallow pools, seeping up in slow, sticky trickles that steam faintly in the nearly suffocating heat. Post-rain, always post-rain, though no true storm is ever seen—moisture simply hangs heavy in the air, condensing, releasing, drifting. Mist curls in ribbons low to the ground, gathering the fragile light and diffusing it into a perpetual twilight. 

The air is hot—weighty and thick, a heat that soaks into every pore, that rises from pressure and the impossible closeness of the water table beneath. Crumbling stone structures, the relics and arteries of industrial ambition, sprawl across the flatness in every direction. Once, the city planners laid out their vision: low rectangular factories, rails half-buried in silt, thick conduits running in neat lines—now weathered into oddly organic forms by the slow assault of mineral-rich waters. Pipes drip and hiss; ancient brick flaking away in the humidity; walls bristling with valves, vents, and metal stairs, so encrusted with sediment they seem almost part of the land.

Yet the place is neither abandoned nor truly decaying—a delicate equilibrium holds. The industrial city is alive, intensely alive, but not with human life. The emptiness is profound; between one building and the next, there may be whole blocks with nothing save worn stone and dripping water. But what fills it is movement, noise at the edges of hearing: fauna, in varieties overwhelming, inhabit every crevice, every channel, every patch of warm mist. 

Some slither, leaving shimmering mucus trails on the mineral tiles; some burrow through soft, steaming sediment, emerging to snap at unseen prey. Along the edges of pools, glassy-eyed things the color of asphalt scuttle, legs ticking. Above, tiny winged creatures—gnatlike, flickering—swarm in humid currents, darting through beams of dim light refracted by condensate. Atop crusted railings or clinging to corroded walls, finger-length creatures grasp and release, social or solitary, some emitting faint clicks or rattles, but mostly settling back into the hush that dominates this space. 

The only constant sound is the slow, quiet labor of water—dripping, sliding; the subtle breath of the settling mist; distant, rhythmic thumps or hissing as factory machines, still functional and necessary, labor on. Out of view but always present, engines rumble, compressors wheeze, and the ground vibrates softly, a lullaby of ongoing work. At times, a metallic clang or the whistle of escaping steam echoes, but these are rare; more often, quiet reigns, broken only by the life that thrives in shadow.

**Atmosphere and Rules**

Nothing unfamiliar or impossible occupies this world: its oddness is simply the effect of balance between water, stone, heat, and hidden creatures. Plants and traditional biospheres have never had a chance here; animal life fills every available niche, some perhaps reliant on chemical, not photosynthetic, energy. The city is no longer—if it ever was—a human dwelling, but its machinery continues in service to needs of ecosystem and industry alike, perhaps run by autonomous systems or maintained by some unseen intelligence.

Everything is layered—old stone beneath new pipes, sediment overlaying the buried skeletons of structures long since corroded away. The only true constants are heat, water, and life: all swirl in cycles, never truly resting. 

Scale is present but tempered—the city is wide, sprawling, but not endless; density is paradoxically high in activity, low in matter. What fills the space is not bulk, but motion and encounter: the overwhelming presence of life against haunting emptiness of organized, mineral structures. It is a world of open, steamed stone, glistening machines, almost-quiet, and too many small, lively eyes watching from dim, slow-dripping niches.
