You stand on the edge of a region that seems at first a riot of green, every variation of hue and shape you might call “plant,” but quickly you realize that none of your categories fit, and your own scale seems small. The horizon is lost behind cliffs and shelves of living architecture: trunks, stalks, stems, and fronded masses spiral upwards and outwards, cliffs veined with pulsating capillaries and mossed-over crags towering higher than any tree you know, but woven through and through with a cellular regularity like a leaf held tight between fingers.

There is no earth beneath you, not really; you step on knotted roots as broad as riverbanks, some slick and ever-moist, others springing upward in sudden, unexpected sweeps, sometimes flexing as if the whole substrate might be restless. Pockets of gleaming wetness shine in shadowed crevices—smooth bowls and runnels where condensation has slicked every surface, dripping with a constant, irregular metronome of droplets, often swept aside by the hurried rush of sap-like fluids through exposed arteries in the plant-flesh.

The place pulses with wetness and closeness, but wide, too—a series of abruptly opening halls, canopies, gnarly whorls, and caverns where plantness unfolds into sudden open spaces, atriums, climbing into the thickly layered light that filters through an endless scatter of interlaced boughs. Here, everything is overwhelmed by life, but “life” is insufficient: the notion of single organisms falters. The “city” is a mass of organized vegetative existence: chimney-like tubes extrude and retract; domes of transparent cellulose bloom overhead and then collapse into tangled archways; bridges and buttresses of cellulose and petal-like leaf, too fat and jewel-colored to come from any tree or flower you remember, span liquid-slicked chasms. Some passages close behind you as you pass, opening only a few moments later in swirling eddies of fibrous tentacles.

There is warmth everywhere—sweaty, living, but not heat like a fire, more persistent, like the exhalation of a vast, buried something. Sometimes clouds of steam bleed upward from sudden ruptures where two living bulwarks grind against each other, briefly exposing the bioluminescence tamed inside.

Here, erosion is young—surfaces are sharp, angular, the “newness” of the place visible in the way edges curl and unfurl, petal upon petal, never quite settling into the smoothness of age. Yet nothing is still: every surface trembles, grows, retracts, unfolds; the landscape itself can seem to crawl, shift, arch, digest itself. Here, a wall splits and grows a new “street,” pushing passageways wide before closing tight as a wound; there, a ramp of woven vines uncoils, layering itself into a new level as tendrils grapple to hungry outcroppings, building in seconds what mountains take decades to splay open.

This is a settlement with no hand in its making. It is organic, organized by a logic above the individual—an all-consuming garden-city, always growing/building/destroying anew. There are bluffs and thoroughfares, marketplaces erupted from bud to full bloom in minutes before being devoured by surges of folding root. There is density everywhere—every gap between these giant stems and fronds stuffed with cambium-layers, lattices of lichen-bright meshes, curtains of veined matter that shimmer where the sun or sky-fire pours through.

There are no animals; nothing with eyes or bones or silent minds. But the world is crowded—pressing in, alive with movement, every inch colonized, every cubic foot bristling with some growth, some extension of the total, communal organism. Roots and shoots clash and twine in struggles for space and reach—sometimes merging, other times repelling, ever reconfigured, never at equilibrium.

The air is so thick with noise that you cannot conceive silence. The friction of growth is constant: snapping, tearing, the shudder of plant-flesh wrenched open by cells dividing at violent pace, the sharp crackle of fronds slapping one another as a new shaft erupts upward, crushing aside its neighbors. Rivulettes of sap patter and roar in miniature falls. Leaves grind against bark, fibrous cords twang as they pull, slither, coil. There are periodic, city-wide rushes of sound, surges like organs played on a scale too large for anything separate from this plant-mind to grasp. Even the light is a torrent—shafts spill and refract through thin tissues, projected into riplic shadows and wild caustics, a dance of greens and dull golds swirling with the constant shift and pull of the living material.

The world’s rules are unstable, matter’s allegiance uncertain: a wall may become a floor in the time it takes to fall; a bright-veined “window” yawns into a hungry abyss, then closes again. Rigid matter seems to liquefy into living tissue at odd moments—no single geometry lasts. Perhaps even time is made of wood and vine here, splitting and braiding, rushing and stalling. You wander through not a place but a process, a churning biological algorithm, a near-infinite, collaborative improvisation. The city’s every fiber strains for more—more light, more wetness, more space—and in this straining, the world tears, reforms, never even momentarily at rest.

To live here—for the thinking part of this vegetal mass, if such a mind exists—is to be one with this orchestral cacophony: deafened and drowned, yet gloriously, terrifyingly alive, filled and remade, hour after hour, in a storm that never ends.
