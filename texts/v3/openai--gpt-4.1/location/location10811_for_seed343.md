You come upon the slopes of a mountain so ancient the stone no longer juts bold and sharp, but has slumped, worn and abraded by uncounted eras of wind, water, and the crushing weight of its own past. Here, where peaks once thrust above the clouds, the land sprawls in great, hollowed bowls and shallow amphitheaters, each one banded with terraces. These terraces are not lifeless ledges of scree or shale—they are built entirely from living matter. An endless, lush tapestry of plant-flesh and fiber has spread over the bones of the mountain.

The world is made of plant. The very surface underfoot squishes softly, like damp, flexible bark or the carpet of a forest’s oldest heart. Roots web out in dense braids, entire ridges packed solid with living tissue, cells swollen with the remembered moisture of brief seasonal rains and persistent, slow condensation that rolls down from cloud banks far above. Water is here, but only in rare pockets—hidden basins where reservoirs of sap and rainwater collect, and in the perpetual beads of dew that pearl on leaf and tendril in the endless, humid warmth.

You are surrounded by towering pillars—giant stalks, sometimes as thick as houses—rising from the terraces like the clustered bones of a colossus. These pillars fork and arch, interlocking overhead into cathedral vaults of green, yellow, and purple, blotched with the scars and lichen-pale memories of ages gone by. From these structures, sheets and veils of plant matter hang, gossamer yet tough, trailing downward sometimes all the way to the substrate. These canopies thrum with life, but there is a kind of emptiness within the space itself: for all the living matter, air and structure is hollow, chambers vast as valleys, with great gaps between the supports.

The world feels open—impossibly large, full of air that echoes with cacophony. For here is the source of overwhelming sound: it is not wind, nor solely the cascading rainfall that sometimes erupts in short, torrential bursts. It is life itself. The fauna are everywhere, audible long before they are seen. A riotous orchestra of call and response: thundering percussion of some vast, slow-moving creature beating against the hollowed trunks; chittering swarms hidden in crevices where damp meets light; booming, subsonic groans that travel through root and vine for miles around; the synchronous clicking of countless smaller mouths and limbs at feeding time. In the brief intervals between, liquid notes of song—complex, near-intelligent, in a hundred-thousand voices—thread the gaps.

The animals are not easy to spot at first; the scale and emptiness devour them. Yet every vantage reveals movement: enormous grazers with translucent carapace or moss-laden hides, trailing tangled palanquins of symbiotic things; swarms of ribbon-limbed gliders, seething in every upper airway, making clouds that shift the very light; eyeless tunneling things that part the living substrate, opening air-wells and venting humid steam. There are predators here as well, swift and patient—long-bodied, soft-boned, almost liquid in their motion, draping from branch and vine in sudden confusion of limbs.

Amid all this, the marks of thinking hands are scattered and rare. You find remnants: a half-spiral cut into the tissue of a vine, a low mound of stalks lashed with cord made from internal fibers. Huts, one or two, teeter precariously on ancient terraces, simple domes wounded from the living plant itself. There are fire-blackened patches, now re-grown at the edges, and simple tools—stone blades, crude baskets woven from toughened roots—left by those who passed through or dwelled for a time.

The rules here are old and inescapable. All is ancient, yet relentlessly alive—endlessly recycled, nearly nothing ever truly dead. Water moves in pulses, and life hoards every drop. Death is rare: what dies here is devoured instantly by teeming hosts, or subsumed into the root-web, memory only in pattern. The world thinks itself full, but to the eye, it is nearly empty: for all the riot of being, the volume is vast, the presence thin, a scattered brilliance suspended in the echoes of its own song. Time here is slow and immense; only the moment is loud.

The dominant note is awe mixed with a subtle sense of dissolution: a place where the living mass is both substrate and atmosphere, and all other things are passing through a cathedral built endlessly from itself.
