The high plateau sits exposed to the sun, warmth humming steadily from the pale stone that flattens the earth for endless kilometers. The air is crystal clear, thinner than lowland lungs remember—breeze perhaps, persistent but not forceful, subtle in its play along the eroded bones of an ancient world. Here, heights have been worn down across epochs, mountain teeth ground flat, flanks settled into broad, imperceptibly undulating tablelands and shallow hollows. Erosion has lingered the longest—what weather once raged is now exhausted, having sculpted away everything that would yield. The bones that remain are the patient survivors: deeply fissured pavements of ochre and faded red rock, surfaces crazed with a countless web of cracks, crevices, and sunken pockets.

Despite the sparseness of green, despite even scarcity of open water, the earth teems. Pockets of rain linger after infrequent storms, gathering darkly at the low points—shallow pools like mercury in the rock’s cups, quickly shrinking to beads, then glistening films, sometimes vanishing altogether, sometimes persisting as damp stains under a broken crust. Lichen and bleak, stubborn mosses rim these hollows and spread, almost invisible, in thin films up along the sheltered cracks. Here and there, a shrub, ancient and miniature, ekes out existence atop a little mound of alluvium, roots gripping stone. You spot thin, sapless trees—so stretched and wind-pruned they seem abstractions of wood and leaf, pale and spare, nothing like forests.

Yet life here is not shown in sap, but in movement. This is a place where animals, everywhere, touch the world in numbers that defy reason. Under the broad light, every inch between the stones is alive: small bodies—too many to identify—cluster, crawl, swarm, and slink. Insects of impossible morphology: jointed segments glassy with condensation, chitin etched so intricately it seems written upon, appendages shifting restlessly even in moments of rest. Pale, worm-like things quilt the undersides of loose stones, and in brief after-rain moments, amphibian shapes pulse out from hiding, glistening briefly before vanishing below. Birds—if they are birds—move in dense, silent waves across open ground, too thick for comfort, too organized for randomness; their feathers are dull, blending with the rocks, eyes filmed and strangely luminosity-lacking.

There's a law, here, imposed not by intelligence but by the weight of ancient habit: the strict rationing of energy, of water, of risk. No creature raises its voice needlessly; sound is absorbed by the huge, open air. The result is a world dense with presence, but quiet. Footfalls pad in muffled succession. Occasionally, you hear the odd clatter—a stone dislodged, a shell cracking, the short, humidity-thick fizzing of something moving rapidly by. But mostly, there is only the subtle, collective scuff and whisper of movement: the slide of thousands of organisms, almost touching.

Ruins interrupt the steadiness. Here and there, the stone breaks differently—a low wall, collapsed roof, lone upright post shaped from some tougher mineral, not yet reclaimed by wind. Whatever marked this land was never grand—a scattering of shelters, maybe a waystation for species since vanished, maybe not even for people at all but some other, now-gone intelligence. The artifacts blend almost too well with the rest, softened by time and dust.

Above, the sky blares a familiar blue, sun unencumbered, light reliable but never harsh. Shadows are crisp against rock, shifting only slowly as the day drifts. Now and then, a bank of cloud will form from the haze, breaking long droughts with brief, patient rain.

What makes this place strange is its silent density: an overwhelming, near-mathematical abundance of life, all compressed against the limits of deep time and sparse moisture. Movements resolve into patterns larger than individuals, like vast living mosaics pressed into the terrain. Here and there, you sense the flow of some deeper logic—not randomness, not chaos, but the last, settled equilibrium of a landscape evolved to hold as much as possible, with nothing wasted. Even the air feels inhabited, each breath dense with the exhalations of countless, near-silent creatures.

It is a place where things have not so much survived as become the persistence of survival itself—living relics enmeshed in an ancient, slow arrangement, every presence pressing close but polite, each niche filled and refilled in patient cycles, all beneath a sky as unmoved by time as the stone and the endless, teeming quiet.
