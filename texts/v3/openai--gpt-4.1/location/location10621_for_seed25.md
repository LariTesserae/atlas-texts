What you first notice is the sound: a limitless, rolling cacophony pushed against you from every direction. It is not a roar so much as a writhing of noise — endless, layered, like the song of thousands upon thousands of creatures amplified and tangled through tunnels, baffles, shells and membranes. It is pressure and presence, a force to brace against.

And then the darkness. The air is thick here — saturated with shifting humidity, a perpetual condensation limned with occasional spatters of rain or fine, gleaming mists. Dampness beads on every exposed surface, runs in gullies between bulges and hollows, pools and seeps in shallow basins, but no river ever carves its channel for long. There are watery films webbing every crevice, a sense of moisture held at the edge of bursting into true flow, but never quite overcoming the grasp of stone and the mass of life.

The landscape is open, broad, and low. Imagine a valley — but no gentle slopes nor meandering river. The ground is pitted, scored, and scalloped, worn down and settled over inconceivably long epochs. Bedrock is exposed in many places, smoothed and made slick by the passage of countless small bodies, never cut or sharpened, always abraded and made strange by organic touch. Everywhere are overhangs, caves, shallow pits and chambers where water pools, only to be quickly lapped dry or evaporated into the misty air.

There is, at first glance, no sign of life’s green — no leaf, no root. The world is built from harder things: stones veined with iridescence, slick with dark patina; sintered silica domes and terraces; slow, blue mineral blooms where the water stands longest. The only growth, if it can be called that, are faint glazes of glimmering film: perhaps the nearest thing this world has to a lichen or fungus. These mats pulse with a faint internal glow, dim as to be less than moonlight but enough to shadow the bulk of what crowds around.

For the region is suffocatingly dense, packed with living entities, overwhelming in their multiplicity. Nothing familiar stalks here — no legs, no scales nor feather nor fur. Instead, you catch the shifting impression of carapaces — layer upon layer, domes and ridges brushing in the dark. Swarms upon swarms of bulbous, wet-shelled forms, moving in synchronous herds and counter-herds, navigating by sound and by the trembling beneath their sprawling, segmented bodies. Some writhe up from the pooled condensation, slick and heavy, mouth-parts rasping at the mineral films. Others perch on overhanging shelves, bodies drooping in the perpetual damp, feeding on the strange glows.

Every surface is alive, or if not, pressed upon by the living. The fauna fill every gap — from hand-sized, glistening bundles that crawl in undulating sheets to monstrous, pod-armored leviathans moving at the valley’s pace, their bulk parting the clamoring mass for moments before being subsumed again. They speak in vibration and in collision — hundreds of distinct voices, pulses of their shell-harps, their vent membranes, their pulpy throats. The interplay of their calls and clangs shapes the darkness as much as the moist, packed terrain.

There is no trace of the deliberate — nothing built, no mark of intention beyond the blind colony-and-prey circle, the migration of wakes and straggling shadow forms in the dim phosphor of mineral and body-glow. It is a world before tools or plans, but tangled with intention: predator and prey, mating swarms, the churn and recycle of carcass and shell and mineral matter.

This is a place alive with ongoing processes — cycles of damp settling and being inhaled, wriggled upon, consumed, shed, replaced. Weather comes in flickers — mists accelerate to drizzle, fading to fine, cold specters of rain, then again to vapor, all held at the threshold of pure liquid and pure air. Here, the darkness is not simple emptiness but thick with forms; the cold not emptiness, but the friction of countless bodies jostling for warmth or, at least, for less-cold.

Rules here are ancient and unchanging: You do not outlast the swarm. What persists is what disintegrates well — what can be consumed, pulped, absorbed, left gleaming on the rocks in a film for the next tide of feeders. Life is a density contest, a war of sounds and glows to claim the little energy that filters in, or is made in the faint electric charge between stones and filmy mats. To survive is to layer, to huddle, to echo yourself in countless forms across the open, crowded dark.

A world before plants, before open sky — an overwhelming menagerie of wet chitin and faint mineral shine, loud and cold and endless, shaped by the long patience of erosion and the relentless pulse of life that fills every possible space.
