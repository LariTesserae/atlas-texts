It takes a while for your sense of the place to settle, because at first so much seems in contradiction: There is the unmistakable distant roar of wet machinery, pumps cycling, and animal calls echoing in tenors alien to any forest, yet there is not a single sprig or leaf.

You stand high—almost dizzyingly high: raised plateaus, broad as the hollows of a continent, rimmed by cliffs that run off into mist, worn to bones under the weight of epochs. The land itself is old stone, sedimentary, reshaped by millions of patient drips and flows. The color is the wet, intricate gray of weathered granite, marbled with what looks like veins of rust and shining quartz pulled to the surface by endless water.

It is a place where water never lets go. Not in rivers or open lakes, but in the ceaseless presence of moisture: slick rock, puddles collecting in hollows, shallow runnels switching direction around the bases of pillars and structures, droplets condensing on every underside, flowing as slow sheets down cold faces. The air has a plumpness—a humid density—carrying breath and sound far, and lending a slight shimmer to the vistas.

Within this density is a city, or perhaps a city-scale mechanism. Its forms are not those of plants or even human craft. Towers, sturdy and lichen-colored, rise from the bedrock, faceted and hexagonal in pattern, their surfaces slick with film of water and skimmed by mineral-crystal filigree. But these are not built in the image of trees or plants; they are schemes of function—cooling towers, habitation stacks, processing wells, and winding arcades, every tier cradling its own basin or canal. A constant haze seeps through the levels: not smoke, but fine water vapor, lit by the regular daylight. Glossy bridges cross gaps in the stone, their undersides hung with pipes and bundles of cable, and everywhere there exudes a faint ozone tang, like old machinery kept almost too clean.

You notice the overwhelming presence of creatures almost before you see them. Where vegetation would be, there is only fauna, filling every niche. They flow over the mineral city and the land, pushing against the saturation point: slick molluscan herds with opal shells, tufts of damp fur just visible in alcoves, flocks of spiderlike fliers darting between pressure vents. Some are smart, tracking you with wet, curious eyes, others simply monolithic—glassy, slow, enormous, crawling or barely shifting their pebbled mass by millimeters an hour. Layers of life stack thick; even the run-off channels are teeming with wriggling filamentous larvae, feeding on mineral-rich water and trace detritus rinsed from the rooftops above.

Sound is its own weather, woven into everything, making the place feel close and alive. Animal choruses drift through corridors, mechanical rumbling underfoot, the slosh of pumps, the hush of slow rain on stone. But though it is loud, the pace is unhurried—almost glaciated. This whole city, this ecumene of creatures and constructed habitat, shifts only by degrees. Growth is slow, even as the density never thins; change comes over seasons, lifetimes, geological time, as stone and animal and machine find their new balances.

There is no greenery, no trace of chlorophyll. Yet the city lives. Energy comes from engines and perhaps from the bowels of the plateau, distilled from the rock itself. Life derives not from sun and leaf, but from chemical niches, electrical flows, and perpetual wetness. If there are rules here, they are measured and accumulative—a civilization and ecology calibrated not to abundance, but to endurance. It is an elegant, saturated world: city as mineral reef, animal as process, water as both memory and motion, all carried by the warm pulse of a sky that never quite dries out.
