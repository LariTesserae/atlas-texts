The city is *vertical.*

Even before your senses fully adjust to the strange gravity and the jittering press of heat, you recognize the sheer, raw height: spires and ridges of mineral stacked and woven, not carved, slowly accreting from the convulsing crust. The whole landscape is built on layers—new crystalline rock slick from recent precipitation rising abruptly out of violent tectonics, reaching toward the thin haze of a high, sun-bleached sky. Even the ground underfoot is a suggestion, a shoulder of clotted mineral barely finished cooling, still bleeding wisps of vapor from hidden rilles and vents.  

There are no plants. Where one might expect moss or even the simplest lichen clutching at cracks, there is only a centipede-like array of vent-tubes and filamentary pipes, crude veins that shimmer faintly with iridescent, synthetic fluids—biotech grown from glass, perhaps, or industry masquerading as life. The “vegetation” is mineral: forests of porphyritic pylons bristle along slopes, not rooted, but crystalized in situ, born from chemical impulses and the slow, patient seep of mineral-laden steam.

**Water** is rare, never standing, always moving or suspended. There might be rain, but only in weeping bursts—a heated squall conjured by a roiling, unnatural sky, the droplets hissing as they hit the ground, vanishing into cracks or collecting in temporary mirrored pools on concave plates of glassy obsidian. Condensation is the norm: beads of vapor collect on exposed wires and industrial ribs, trickling down to hidden reservoirs deep within engineered cisterns. The air carries a bright, mineral tang, sharp as ozone after lightning; the moisture is precious, jealously gathered.

**The city** is both embryo and ruin—architecture emerging from, and inseparable with, terrain. You see grand, slow arcs of metal scaffolding and extruded basalt, sometimes burdened with the beginnings of what might become bridges or inhabited terraces, other times seemingly abandoned mid-formation, left as skeletons or buttresses against landslips. There’s a sense of ongoing construction—veins of half-fused ceramic, neurons of copper cabling snaking through clefts, the imprint of design in the ongoing birth of stone. Towers lean inward, almost touching, sometimes so close their metallic outgrowths scrape together in the thermal wind, emitting faint chimes lost in long pauses between.

The **atmosphere** is hot—not tropical, but searing, as though the sun lingers too close, drawn by the elevation. Ultraviolet glints on every angled surface. The city is constantly exhaling heat through its vents and the shimmer-heat radiates from the stone itself, spirited away only by the rare, cool sigh of rain or the gradual breath of nightfall.

There is an **openness**—the city ranges across a jagged massif, descending in switchbacks and abrupt drops from lofted ridge to broad, fractured terraces. From above, the view is wide: the city never ends, simply dissipates into lesser constructions and loosely organized strata, descending into heat-shimmered distance or evaporating into low, mineral-barren fog. But within the city, everything is *close*: enormous structures pressed together, layers and canyons of habitation, all within narrow clearances, threaded with chutes, cables, and the periodic gleam of condensate falls.

**Fauna** are seen only as hints: the ghost of a shelled creature receding behind a vent; the glimmer of multi-limbed, silicone-bodied things retreating along cables into the city’s depths. These are rare, adapted to the heat and dryness, utterly alien—perhaps feeding on trace elements or electrical discharges, their habits slow, measured across cycles imperceptible to human eyes.

**Sound** is muted. There are no wild cries or rustlings. Mostly, there is the hush of wind filtering through mineral grids, the distant thunk of shifting stone, the very occasional echoing call of some engineered alarm or the whisper of a powered conveyor. Even the rare rainfall is a brief percussion, vanishing rather than lingering.

**Ecology** is a question: the world seems pre-life, or post-life, or reinterpreted, as if some other logic dictates survival. Instead of growth, accretion; instead of photosynthesis, energy captured through slow chemical gradients and catalytic engines built into the stone. Small machines or semi-aware constructs may drift past—never in flocks or swarms, but singly, patient as glaciers.

**Rules** are emergent and slow. The city is not ruled by haste. Shifts happen over months, as ridges grow, spires bud and thicken, bridges quietly extrude their lengths. The people (if there are people) must be as patient as the landscape, living in the intervals between construction and collapse, making use of the mineral bounty gathered with each brief rain. Nothing moves quickly; even heat disperses only fitfully. The sense is of a world always becoming, barely beyond the threshold of being born, yet marked with the technology of a civilization that harnesses and shapes raw beginnings—a place where the city itself is the elemental organism, and all within it are but ephemeral motions in its slow, upward pulse.

**To stand here is to feel on the rim of creation**: the edge between living future and mineral past, a place where civilization rises in concert with the mountain's bones, and meaning condenses slowly, like rare water on the gleaming skin of stone.
