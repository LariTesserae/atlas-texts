It is easy to make the mistake of thinking there's nothing here — just emptiness, swelter, and a sense of something being unfinished. But the place is enormous, a world still writhing under the weight of its own improbable birth. Nothing that occurs here is purely natural, yet nothing is finished, either — the place is all built, but it doesn't know what it is yet.

The horizon is difficult to pick out. There is very little light, a coppery sepia, borne from clouds dense with vapor. The sky pulses copper-tarnished, as if it is always just after a thunderstorm or before a sunrise that cannot quite manifest. Air hangs humid, carrying condensation that pearls across every surface in beads that do not fall straight but wind along grooves and seams. 

But there is no plant life, nothing green, no softness. The shapes that rise from the ground are not trees, nor stone, nor architecture in any sense you would know — they are artifacts, arranged in uncertain harmony, emerging from the broad, hot, soaked surface of the world. Imagine the bones of titanic machines, the exoskeleton of a planet-scale city that was never alive, slowly assembling itself out of hot, weeping, black-and-copper panels that writhe and shift, ejecting plumes of steam.

The ground is low, like a vast plain; but it's not earth or sand you stand on, rather a surface composed of immense tessellated plates. Each panel seems recently set, edges steaming, heat rising off their fractal seams. There is space between each segment — broad fissures that drop into glimpsed, glowing machinery or into darkness that suggests there is far more below than above. Sometimes, vapor pushes up through these gaps, forming ragged drifting curtains of hot mist that move without wind, uncertain boundaries for what counts as “above” or “below.”

Water is everywhere near the surface—never pooling, never flowing freely, but always thinly layering everything, condensing, forming droplets, only to be drawn away by the next tremor of heat. Moisture collects in the joints of strange devices and along the edges of the plates. Sometimes it shudders as if startled, gathering and rolling away before vanishing into crevices, or suddenly pulled into delicate, latticework troughs cut into the surface. If there are rivers, they are only rumors, arms of vapor scooped up and hurried away by unseen processes.

The atmosphere is thick, but it’s not entirely air; sometimes you almost smell copper, ozone, or something sharper, like heated stone or volcanic glass. Sometimes the air crackles with a sense of impending formation, the smell of rain with nowhere green to fall.

Everywhere is under construction, but there are no builders. Or perhaps the world builds itself, one trembling, glistening slab at a time. Form and unform — proto-columns, unfinished arches, segments that extrude up, hover, and retract. Sometimes, a wall begins, growing algorithmically along a fault until it dissolves, seething, into vapor and is replaced by another configuration entirely. Once in a while, vast mineral sheets tilt up and click into position, echoing with a sound like thunder muffled by wool; otherwise, the world is quiet except for the subtle hiss of steam and the rare clatter of falling debris.

Temperature is oppressive, but not lethal, if only for lack of anything to wither or burn. Heat radiates from below, occasionally shimmering through the haze and making straight lines look warped, horizons seem to bend. It is as if the rules of perspective themselves fracture, bending toward and away from impossible vanishing points.

There are no animals, nothing scurries or flitters. No birds, no insects, not even the memory of their sounds. The only signs of energy, of motion, come from the land and structures themselves: pulses of liquid light darting through crystal conduits, the shivering rearrangement of panels and beams, the unpredictable emergence of complex, branching latticework, which extrudes remotely under some inscrutable imperative. If there is biology here, it is unrecognizable: webs of pattern drawn with condensation or self-fusing mineral intelligence, not cells.

Here, weirdness is the law. Reality hiccups and stutters. A wall that wasn’t there is now, and might not be again. Sometimes your shadow forms a second, perpendicular to your feet. Sometimes you hear a sound but cannot say if it was near or distant — a deep metallic groan, echoing, that sounds like it belongs to the world’s machinery, or its dreaming. Once in a while, cold color flickers in the mist — turquoise, magenta, green — though nothing casts light and you can never see the source. Sometimes, where steam and condensation gather, images appear for moments and vanish: a suggestion of eyes, or something like writing, then only droplets again.

Nothing is settled. Everything is becoming, and un-becoming, on a scale that defies memory or mapping. You sense that there are rules here, but they are not human rules: geometry is plastic, time is uneven, forms and functions are chosen by some world-sized hand or mind you cannot comprehend, an artisan at the heart of a planet, improvising in hot, wet, mineral dark.

The silence is vast and damp, but never complete. The place speaks its own language, in the slow grind of plate against plate, the click and hiss of structures birthing themselves, the pulse and rush of condensation forming and fleeing, the endless unstitching and stitching of the world’s own body.

If you wandered, you would find more of the same — never identical, never stable. A manufactured sprawl of soon-to-be, always-being, skeletal and sodden, trembling at the edge of existence, uncertain if what it is building is itself, or the possibility of anything at all.
