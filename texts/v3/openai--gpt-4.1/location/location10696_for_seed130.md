Darkness, first—dense and absolute, punctured only by muted, quivering auras: sodium flares behind grimed glass, the lopsided pulse of warning strobes, the soft blue exhalations of distant arc welders. Spread before you, a made world stretches outward—low, ancient, and strange—a manufactured land eroded not by wind and water, but by time, exhaustion, and the gradual collapse of purpose.

This is not nature’s work. The ground never was soil or stone, but plates and poured slag, every surface cast, laid, and fused together by hands or things made by hands—now all absent. The terrain is broad, open, a region of sprawling scale: what might once have been wetland or mudflat utterly subsumed by architecture, now in its most exhausted, afterimage state. Distances here are measured in the long, gray causeways that thread between districts of machinery. There are pits the size of lakes—manmade basins, their rims eaten smooth, centuries-old. Pools of oily water shimmer green-black in recesses where the flash of the lights barely reaches. Drainage gutters gurgle and overflow, giving everything a wetted gleam.

The air is cold, humid, and wracked with noise. You feel it in your ribcage: the shudder and moan of buried turbines, unidentifiable stampings, steam hissing from ruptured pipes overhead. Sometimes the whole megastructure seems to tremble, as if the landscape itself inhales. You cannot hear yourself speak here; the ambient roar drowns everything.

Vegetation exists only as relic: frail stands of wire-brush moss clinging to rusted vents, the rare tuft of chemical-tolerant lichen paled almost gray. In one forgotten sluice a barely-flickering patch of bioluminescence, engineered and left behind. All is sparse, clinging, half-alive, but unyielding—life that persists on surrender.

No animals run, fly, or skitter; not a shadow darts but your own, distorted by overhead light and rolling fog. You feel the absence as a pressure. The only motion is mechanical: a ceaseless industry, its purpose lost in the endless churning. Gantries advance on grooved rails, heavy doors cycle open with seismic finality, cranes extend and retract their arms in clockwork rituals. Conveyors snake through open spaces and vanish into depths; at intervals, rivers of coolant surge through wide troughs and overflow, pooling in shallow lagoons that steam slowly upward.

Everything is active here, but not alive—systems pulse, alarms cycle, some machinic protocol adhered to by ghosts, enforced by nothing but momentum. And always, beneath the industrial clamor, you sense a deeper, quieter process: things dissolving, settling, water working at the seams. There is a deep weariness to the materials; ancient steel and stonecrete now laced in crystalline patterns of old corrosion, everything rounded, edges eaten away, landscape worn to archetype—last stage, final form.

Rules of the place are simple, but absolute: there is no ground untouched, no sky unchoked with engineered haze. Light never truly arrives; it leaks, sulks, flickers, but never welcomes. Wet never leaves—dew forms on everything, pools where it can, flows as it must. Sounds dominate, fill, and shatter quiet: a place where silence, if it comes, is a harbinger of catastrophe.

If you look far, the same patterns repeat. Vast spans of fabricated emptiness and clustered machinery, scattered open voids hinting at vanished function: somewhere a rain of sparks from automated welders; elsewhere a spiral of mist above a heat sink field; everywhere the black mirror of steel glimpsed beneath a sheet of constant, silent drizzle.

And, if you stand still, you begin to sense an unearthly rhythm—this is a world not abandoned, but entranced: the end-state of labor, an eden of machinery that has worn itself old and strange, busy always, but with nothing left to build.
