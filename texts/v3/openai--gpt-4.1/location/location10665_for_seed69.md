**Blackwater High**

Somewhere in the long night of this world extends the sopping vastness of Blackwater High, a plateau so wide it folds the horizon, dense with life and leaching warmth, but cast in absolute darkness. Here, earth and sky are close–hemmed with churning cloud, shot with lightning you only feel, flickering in your bones rather than lighting your eyes. Above, the atmosphere drips, weeps, compresses, sweats, rains, all but drowns, though seldom opens enough to reveal any true star or moon. The dark is humid and living, total and implacable.

Land isn’t what it claims to be here. The ground is high—lifted above deeper, older valleys—but sponges underfoot, black with the memory of rain that lingers in thick, root-bound soils and ever-exposed seeps. Marsh-water snakes in lines, collecting in hollow coombs, flooding shallow basins, vanishing into belowground rivers with thunderous, unseen noise. The terrain is weathered to wholeness: boulders softened, cliffs weeping, stone grown slippery with mats of biofilm, centuries of erosion molding upland shoulders and terraces that spill into one another as if gently melting.

Vegetation is everywhere—braided, festooned, clumped, tangled, grasping upward. Giant-leaved plants rustle and spill dew even in the absence of dawn. Ferns the height of a person surge around rock outcrops. Masses of mosses pad every horizontal, trailing liana curtains, while somewhere in the upper tiers, shaftless tree-corpses bristle with fungus antlers. All green and cyan beneath a darkness so thick the air can almost be bitten–photosynthesis here draws on the slivers of geomagnetic light, bioluminescence, and low chemistries, rarely on any celestial gift.

Not quiet, never quiet: sounds abound. Water chortles, trickles, pounds; the soil shudders as some titanic root slithers deeper below. Each creature has found a way to make itself heard—some croak or thunder, others click or whirr with electric urgency. There is a constant crescendo of whines, bellows, and industrial grind. Overhead, the sky echoes with the steady roar and thump of heavy engines: cargo trams or process towers marching in the dark, their spinning turbines trembling the air. Sometimes, far off, the pulse of distant machinery—being built, breaking down, being rebuilt—rattles in the respiratory hush of the rain.

Structures lie scattered, never claimed by the dark; relics of steel and glass and glaze embedded among the root-mass. Some are whole and humming, boxy and vaultlike, humming with generators, roofed with arcane machinery or belching chemical steam. Others are empty shells, pitted with climbing plants and long since adapted as den or nest by things with slick, mobile pads. Occasionally you glimpse motionless towers, arrays of spinning arms, broken bridges lashed with vines, scattered as if a city exploded, cast seeds, and then was almost erased.

Industrial presence stains the land as surely as any climate: above the marsh, pipes protrude, leaching vapor, shuddering with fluid transport. Platforms drift, carried on slow belts, ferrying strange goods made from the living matter around them. Light rarely leaves these factories. Instead, the dark is broken by electric flashes, arcs and emergency beacons—so sporadic and strange you cannot map them to a human intent.

Fauna is nearly overwhelming. Heavily built grazers wallow in the root-choked pools, feeding on churning mats of microflora, muzzles glowing faintly as they sift luminous detritus. Predators move in pods, membranous wings shuffling the muggy air, tracking the static of their quarry. Smaller things—hundreds of species—burrow, leap, surf on waterflows, or grip to slick bark and relay coded light-signals to kin. Parasites and symbionts swarm; nothing is solitary long.

Almost nothing here is as straightforward as it seems. Life maintains uneasy bargains with machine and minerality. Some creatures have integrated remnants of industry: glowing pipes gnawed clean and pressed into shells, or metallic weave braided through featherless wing-fur. Factories grow moss-skin and root-anchors; old generator cubes now encircle the nests of great amphibians with their own sluices and drains.

Rules are different in unending dark. Everything competes or cooperates for heat, for electricity, for chemical gradients as much as for food. Some plants run on capillary batteries, coordinated by the whine of distant engines. Even the rain is not quite water—sometimes it tastes of oil, or metal, or bitter ozone.

It is a loud world—loud with living, breathing, digesting life. Loud with the warp and resonance of machines still awake. Loud with the sheer scale of water, wind, stone, and process working without witness in the dark.

Yet, for all its wet, sprawling, layered enormity, Blackwater High is in balance. Erosion no longer undoes the plateau: it, the life, and the built have all grown into a single system, humming and bustling in the thick black, a world whose physics are writ by the friction of root and turbine and the hush of rain falling on riotous, unseen green.
