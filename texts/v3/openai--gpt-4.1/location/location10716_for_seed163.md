The landscape opens dazzling and relentless under the high, white sun—a brightness that eats shadows and fills even the blueest air with a kind of glare. You stand at the lip of a broad, high plateau: the terrain feels unsettled and freshly made, as if geology itself has only recently grown confident enough to extend its handiwork into the sky.

Everywhere, the ground oozes with wetness. Underfoot, the path is spongy, not of earth or stone, but of intertwined roots and sodden mats of foliage. When you look closer, there’s no real soil at all—just the dense, fibrous tangle of living plant, layer over layer: spongy sphagnum, broadleaf creepers, and thick, silver-threaded moss, all fed by ceaseless moisture. Water is everywhere, pooling in shallow, irregular basins, beading on leaves, condensing in the open air before dripping in fat, cold droplets to the forest of plants below. Great columns of living green rise here and there like the supports of some titanic structure, grown rather than built, each vanishing into a fog-bright height well above sightline.

Despite the completeness of plant life, the scene feels sparse. The plateau is vast—open spaces stretch between tangles of swelling root-ridges and living buttresses. Where the vegetation huddles together in knots or archways there are pockets of greenhouse humidity, but much of the air is cool, brushed repeatedly by moving drafts that slide across the sky-cleaned ridges. New rivulets cut down through the living mat, lazy and slow, hinting at a landscape only lately emergent from rock and sky—erosion is in its infancy; there is no hint yet of valleys or canyons, only the probing work of trickles and infant streams.

The elevation is commanding, high enough that clouds roll through, trailing mist over the long, green expanse. Here and there, the plant-mass forms entire bridges or canopies, suspended between the higher promontories—always alive, always dripping, thick with veined leaves the size of banners. The terrain is never steep, never precipitous, but its slight undulations, its aired ridges, and sudden lacustrine hollows give a sense of consequence—as though you walk in the exposed ribcage of something monumental still assembling itself.

Faint evidence of industry is scattered across this region. Here: an ancient boiler, slumped half-swallowed by root and moss, its pipes reaching nowhere. There: the faint geometry of a rusted tram-line, rising one last time above the hummocky sea of green before vanishing into the living mass. Powerlines, occasionally strung, have become trellises, with metallic cable wrapped in climbing vines and bracketed by the thick, enveloping foliage, now more plant than infrastructure. No true roads, only the rare exposed structure or a solitary machine abandoned before the last great surge of the living substrate. These glimpses are tiny in the regional sweep—a suggestion of what once was intended here, becoming increasingly irrelevant except as supports for the plant’s upward surge.

Fauna is a rumor—scarcely seen, almost imagined. Maybe a single sharp-eyed shape gliding above the mist, or a shiver that slinks low to the mat before silence returns. In places where water pools glass-clear, the faintest disturbance hints at life below: insects of enormous size, perhaps, some amphibian rumbling in the reeds, but never more than possibility, their presence utterly dwarfed by the mass and intent of the flora.

Deafening sound saturates the air. The white roar of water—endless drip, cascade, miniature falls, and the guttural murmur of hidden springs bursting up from under the roots—mingles with the whip and rattle of leaves in strong upper winds. Sometimes, the booming groan of settling roots, vast panels of moss shifting as they tighten their grip around half-buried machines, makes you think the land itself is alive. Echoes ricochet far, small sounds lost entirely beneath the drone.

Yet, despite the immensity and the unceasing sound, nothing here is hurried. All is slow. The change is measured by the slow thickening of the plant-mass, the slow submergence of industrial remnants, the slow deepening of rivulets and emergence of boggy pools. Light shifts with the hours but rarely with the weather, filtered as it is through veils of cloud and the strands of humid mist. The world turns in ages-long cycles: growth, swallowing, reclaiming, without drama or conclusion. It is a domain marked more by process than by event—a bright high wetland smeared with relics, where plants rule, water rules, and everything else must find its cracks and its pace in the endless, living sprawl.
