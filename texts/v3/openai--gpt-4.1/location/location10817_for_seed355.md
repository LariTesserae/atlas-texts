You step, or perhaps float, into a landscape heavy with water and emptiness and darkness. At first, you are not sure where ground ends and air begins. The air itself is thick with vapor, and its movement is a slow convecting tide; you can almost hear, or imagine hearing, the gentle rustling swell of condensation against stone—a sound that is not quite sound, more like a tactile brushing at the soles of your feet, or the base of your skull.

The heat saturates the space, enveloping everything with an oppressive, close warmth, as if the sun presses endlessly through vast humidity. But there is no sun here, nor true night, nor day: only a dimness, pervasive and stubborn as if the world is cast under the shadow of the heaviest storm that never truly comes or leaves.

You find yourself in a broad, sunken basin, unbroken by hills, its distant horizon only sometimes visible as shadowed suggestions beyond the fogged air. The terrain settles in shallow depressions and gentle folds, a palimpsest of ancient erosion—rock, silt, and half-clay molded into old webs of runnels and shallow hollows; everywhere, the land holds water close, brimming in pools and quietly seeps.

You notice the paleness of stone—some dull geological matter worn smooth, some slick and glassy, almost translucent in patches. The surface is pocked and laced with odd mineral blooms: rusty-red, mustard yellow, motes of green-blue that catch dim light like distant stars. Every so often, water wells up in slow, deliberate pulses from cracks, trickling over rounded forms that might once have been walls, arches, or even statues, though time and water have erased their intention.

Sparse vegetation—hard, twisted, and rare—clings to edges and higher piles of detritus. Some are black-limbed, leafless things, rising in lonely silhouettes: perhaps roots exposed by time, or calcified remnants of plants that needed no sun. Strange carpets of slime or pale lichen drift in pools, trembling softly at unseen currents. No animals stir; no insects hum. The place is close to lifeless, yet active, disturbed by the persistent labor of water and stone and chemical metamorphosis—like a city built by the hand of geology rather than any creature.

All around are signs of intricate, persistent structure: vast terraces or foundations, spindly pathways, and drops where water channels cut through deep underlayers—a half-remembered urban order, at once organized and ruined. The civilization here was never built by hands or minds—it was summoned, sorted, and assembled by the will of the terrain, by the logic of runoff and mineral accretion. It resembles a city in outline only: empty plazas with repeating patterns, endlessly mirrored in the puddles; rows of pillars that are only hollow tubes extruded by evaporation; vaults where the ceiling drips in ceaseless rhythms.

The darkness is vast but not absolute. Here and there, in the deeper pools, there pulses a light—soft, colorless as if leaking from the cracks in reality itself, outlining surfaces in forgetful flickers. Sometimes, these lights seem to move, as if responding to some unseen process or cycle deeper underground. The shadows do not behave entirely as they should—some stretch and fold in slow, impossible ways, as if the fabric of space is subtly warped. Out of the corner of your eye, surfaces ripple in an impossible third direction: walls fold into themselves; ground dips, yet doesn’t descend.

The quiet is profound, but not pure. Water hisses as it finds new paths beneath stone; gases bubble up with the groan of shifting pressure. In some pockets, the air smells sweet and alien, in others, acrid with the promise of dissolution. Sometimes, in the intervals between inhalations, you can almost—but not quite—catch an echo of language: a murmur in the act of becoming speech, though it is only the arrangement of wind and wet stone pretending to converse.

Nothing moves except the water, the feeling of heat, the slow rearrangement of this city that was never built but always exists, shape-shifting at the pace of chemistry and geologic time—which, here, you suspect, is not quite the same as elsewhere. Occasionally, the landscape inexplicably shifts: a plaza you are looking at lengthens, corridors bend outward, and the whole ground flexes as if breathing or waking up. The skeletal trees unfold new limbs, or shed them, in moments unbound by seasons.

There is no history here, or perhaps there is only history—a record written and rewritten, randomized, erased, yet persistent in the outlines of order among chaos. Water always finds its level; heat always rises; darkness settles but never conquers. The rules are not physics or biology, but a hidden architecture, more abstract and ancient than life itself.

You move, and the space remembers the places you do not go.
