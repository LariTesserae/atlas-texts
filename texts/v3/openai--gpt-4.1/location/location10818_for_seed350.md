The sky is water.

Everywhere, above and beside and through, is a body of liquid—no, not exactly water, or only water: a viscosity like warm oil, a shimmer as if metal rain is compressed into a lens, every view refracted and doubled by interleaved sheets. There is no sky, no open air, only the presence and pressure of overwhelming submersion. Walk, and you do not walk, but wade. Swim and you push through a medium thick with warmth, always buoyed, never quite touching the ground—but there is ground, newly lifted, razor-sharp, young and unsoftened by time.

The soil is clay and dark basalt, ridges and new shelves razor-edged but already lush with emergent, alien life: plants like glass-reef fronds, bursting with chlorophyll that flashes in palettes made for darkness—electric-blue, ultraviolet, a spectrum the dim light can barely excite but which blazes in flashes, like underwater lightning from the constant pressure and friction. Vines climb skyward, but the sky is so near, sometimes mere inches above the broken stone, elsewhere rolling away in tidal, roiling flows. There are moments of inexplicable turbulence: the water above shifting sideways, then falling in curtains, then reversing to pour upward in defiance of sense. Gravity feels like a loose suggestion, and sediment clouds swirl in the odd-light: ochre, jade, pink, bismuth.

The region is vast—the landscape never far, dense and insistent, thicker than dreams—with the terrain arranged in shelves and tiered mesas, sudden holes into shadowy floods, upright pillars budding with overgrown machinery. It is a place where many things, natural and unnatural, have begun to grow or been built, but only half-finished, projects started and abandoned or consumed, scattered marks of intent: spindly towers webbed by pipes, turbine-flowers spinning weakly in thick liquid, rusted fragments of conveyors petrified into living lichen.

No animal life—no fins, no wings, no insect whir. Instead, the pulse of industry is the heartbeat: deafening, all-consuming. The world hums, groans, and shrieks—a million engines run beneath the slime-lined stone, the working of hidden manufactories echoed above by plant-life that mimics the sound of manufacture: bashings and clonks as seedpods burst metallically, high pings as vesicles snap in rhythmic pulses, the constant whiteout of rushing, boiling current. The air—if you can call it air, in a place where to breathe is to taste machine-oil and loam and storms—vibrates with the effort of change. Nothing is still. The landscape threatens always to mutate, as new mechanical growths erupt from hillside calderas, as entire sections of ground shear sideways, as fluid rushes and retreats, swallowing and exposing whole valleys in minutes.

Light is thin, a minor chord barely present in the endless, pressurized dusk. It seems to crawl from fissures in the rocks, linger behind polished leaves, flicker fitfully in the storm-dim, smudged and occluded by constant suspended matter. Luminous algae—if “algae” is even the right word—form glowing filaments that trail from one structure to the next like living wiring, fragile but everywhere.

Weirdness is the currency here, reality a palimpsest written in a fever: time tunnels in the water’s folds, gravity sails sideways, and even identity—the difference between plant and machine, liquid and land—bleeds and runs. Sound sometimes fractures into visual motes, roughening the edge of your vision, physical and aggressive, a sensation as much as a noise.

The rules of this place are turbulence and hypergrowth. Everything is new, everything is entropic, everything is in the process of forming or dissolving and noise is not the byproduct but the cause. The land hums itself into being, a young world in a slow riot of industrial and vegetal birth, with no final shape, no animals, no safe border between the organic and the made—only overwhelming, submerged creation, deafening and miraculous, half-night and all-energy, reality writhing, thick with potential, at the bottom of a warm storm ocean.
