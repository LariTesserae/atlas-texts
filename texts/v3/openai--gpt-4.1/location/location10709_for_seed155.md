**There is no dirt here.** Earth—the concept, the layer beneath—is a myth. The high plateau is forged of stems and woven canopies: all the world is plant, coiled and stacked and mortared by its own fallen bodies, pressed for eons by fog, rain, and gravitational time. The only ground is a composite of bark and fiber, cross-hatched in ancient tessellations, highways of root and spongy rhizome latticed into stable flatness over centuries. Even the “horizon” is ambiguous: it is the undulating rim of the plant-mass, not true stone or sediment, but a living fossil in slow, continual transformation.

### Terrain and Materials

Picture an open region, miles of plateau shaped not by stone or tectonics but by the bulk and death of tree upon tree, vine upon vine—layered to a breadth, then weathered and settled by the oldest rains. The plateau’s slopes spill downward in vast, gentle terraces. Each is a shelf of plant, grown thick where ageless water once pooled and dried, frond-bone as sturdy as concrete, giving way at the edges to vaporous drops—the “cliffs” are tangles of decayed stem, trailing long pale lichen streamers.

Here and there, shallow basins have held water longer—left behind cups of condensation, pools rimmed by dense moss or open to the sky in the gaps of the layered “floor.” Fog creeps nightly from crevices where temperature gradients spark dew in sudden veils, and the air itself smells of leaf-oil, rotting growth, something ancient and sweet.

### Architecture and Urbanity

An entire city is here, though little of it is recognizable by human terms. The built world has grown from this substrate—layered towers of lignified flowering columns, “rooms” hollowed through the living tangle, walkways balanced high and open to every shifting wind. Bridges stretch between broad platforms, some sprouting mechanical lattices half-merged with the trunks—rusted gantries, aerial pipelines, platforms hung from swollen boughs. Light fixtures are embedded directly into plant matter, trailing power cables through taproot tunnels.

All construction is scavenged and grown in tandem. Factories, their engines dull-throated, are nestled where the plant-substrate thickens to bear heavy weight. Pistons and gears are loud, often steam-driven, their exhausts venting into the thickening air or channeling into root-warmed condensation cisterns—a crucial recycling of water in a thirsty biome.

Urban life is a product of adapted necessity—streets drape across living stilts, plazas are broad mats of leaf-fiber left bare, and homes carve foam from the pith of six-hundred-year-old stalks. The city feels airy, even with the closeness of construction, because all is suspended above fading voids, filled with open rooms and the echo of wind.

### Climate and Atmosphere

The temperature is ever cool. This is an elevation higher than clouds, a plateau so ancient that every day feels twice-lived. Winds rush through open canopies, sharp with moisture stolen from the rare, intermittent rain. Water comes most often as mist or sudden downpours, cold enough to sting, but quickly siphoned off to interior gardens and cisterns—survival is built on retaining every precious drop.

Mist wraps the city at dawn and dusk, parting where heat from industrial cores rises in wavering pillars. The plateau is loud: engines throb, wind rattles hollow trunks, above all a near-constant chorus of creaking wood. Somewhere deep, the endless crackle of plant cells reforming themselves sounds almost like static.

### Ecology and Fauna

Fauna are rare, almost mythic. Occasionally, a dull moth with translucent wings or a long-legged lizard will flit through the latticework, but most animal motion here is unwelcome or ghostlike. Instead, life is almost all green: predatory pitcher-plants grown fat as barrels dangle above walkways, hundreds of species of fungal bloom pop open in cold light, lichens shear off dead wood and send spores on the cool wind.

The city is alive with roots, stalks, and evolving forms. Some structures are built to harvest the plant mass itself—a local industry where spongy root-threads are grown under lamps from prized starter stock. Pollinating insects are iron-rare, their presence the cause for quiet celebration. More often, pollination is forced by custom-tuned fans, with hands brushing along flowers clustered in the city’s vertical orchards.

### Activity and Sound

It is never truly quiet. Air vibrates with the constant churn of machines: mills slicing fibrous lumber, pumps drawing condensation, hammers echoing against woody frames. The urban presence is not overwhelming—buildings and activity are scattered, spaced by the vast emptiness of slanted decks, aerial gardens, communal plazas open to every direction. In between, wind roars and plant-mass shifts with deep, resonant groans.

Every so often, a section of the very ground seems to sigh and reform—a patch of old dead wood collapsing into itself, then quickly reclaimed by climbing vines and sunhungry moss. Cracks echo for minutes before quiet returns.

### Rules, Logic, and Reality Breakdown

This place holds itself together, but only barely. Ancient gravity and biomass maintain an uneasy alliance. Sometimes, the plant-mass resists logic—trails or bridges will lead in loops, only to return you to an unknown platform miles from your start. Time seems altered: old structures never rot away, but also never entirely mend, held together by intent as much as by cellulose.

People here measure reality by what remains stable: a walkway that stays whole through a winter is trusted, an elevator shaft that shifts its height from day to day is skirted. Journeys are mapped by living memory as much as by signage, which itself grows and warps, runes turning and twisting into unreadable scripts as bark swells beneath paint. Gravity sometimes flows odd: a dropped object may spiral in slow-descending arcs, never quite landing where gravity suggests.

### What Emerges

This world is a paradox: open in scope but suspended in emptiness, ancient yet humming with relentless, layered activity. Life here is precarious and communal; the city is a living organism, both scaffold and resource, both home and hazard. Survival means tending the roots below, harvesting moisture where it can be coaxed, listening to the thrum of engines through wood and mist, and always, always navigating the silent, awkward logic that governs this endlessly morphing, high green plateau.
