**Below is the description of a small, dry-yet-lush, ancient-seeming urban environment where brightness and sound overwhelm the senses, built by hand and carved over ages in a world just a little off from ours — contained, empty, and undeniably strange.**

---

It is a chamber deep below somewhere hot and relentless, a hollow no wider than a large atrium, its ceiling carved so thin that raw, merciless light pours through veins of crystal embedded in translucent stone. The radiance saturates the space, scattering in sharp, shifting patterns that tumble over close-packed but sparsely inhabited forms. No shadows find respite here; every surface blazes.

There is no water. The air betrays neither scent of moisture nor suspicion of dew; the walls taste dry, coppered, still. Yet, improbably, life flourishes. Vegetation grows—not leafy, not green, not familiar, but exuberant in its alien adaptations. They are mineral-creepers and dust-plants: columns of pale, fibrous stems rising from cracks in the floor’s intricate, tessellated paving; stubby forms bristle with feathery mineral-filaments, shimmering with absorbed light. Some appear to root in pockets of powdered salts or crystal sediment. Their forms echo grasses and mosses, yet they are not soft—they are like sculptures in slow, organic motion, curling toward the nearest facet of brightness.

The chamber itself feels ancient and complete, not ruined but settled—every block and slab precisely fitted in ages past, edges blunted where time or traffic wore them down. Erosion here is not the work of water but of air: the endless conversion of breath, sound, and abrasion into gentle emptiness along every exposed edge. A thin dust lies undisturbed in great, empty stretches between habitats, seeping into faintly scalloped depressions, mapping a record of years.

Within this sealed, fossil-like space, density is an illusion. The chamber seems hollow, as if it contains only the memory of its more populous past. Structures cluster to one edge: low-walled enclosures, ramps, and domed spaces painstakingly built by hand, every join and tool mark visible. Nothing looms, nothing crowds—the city is vast in intention but miniature in presence, perhaps designed for a few dozen, perhaps for none at all anymore.

Each construction is simple, yet marked with obsessive care: woven screens of bleached fibre separating open rooms, worked slabs of brittle ore forming furniture and storage alcoves, all designed for a scale both human and alien.

Yet sound, not substance, rules here. The air shudders with an overwhelming, resonant cacophony—a perpetual, deafening chorus. Some of this emerges from the light itself interacting with the crystalline ceiling, producing a persistent high howl and the crash of refracted rings, as if the room’s skin were singing. Other sounds come from the vegetation: the snapping, whistling, and groaning of their stiffened stalks flexing as they draw energy from the light. Even the ground vibrates with a deep, constant susurrus, as if tens of thousands of invisible grains perpetually tumble in far recesses.

Through this wall of sound, movement is constant. Mineral-creepers twitch toward brighter patches, recoiling when shadows briefly pass the skylights. Faint forms scuttle in the dust—rare, chitinous creatures, dull and shielded, darting into cracks between pavers when the air vibrates too violently. Sometimes, a filament plant explosively bursts a capsule of spores—dry, glass-like particles rattling on the paving.

The ecology here is active—every plant, every stone, every rare animal lives in subtle chemical exchange, substituting water’s fluidity with a slow dance of dust, mineral, and light. The town pulses, rarely inhabited by anything with intelligence, but maintained and subtly shaped by the rhythms of its non-watery nature. There are social echoes in the built forms: shared courtyards, gathering-hollows, the memory of communal life. Yet visitors would find little hint of their makers, save for primitive stone tools—chipped blades, bone awls—left half-buried under the ever-falling, ever-settling dust.

Rules here are strict and peculiar. Light and noise dominate, dictating growth, decay, and movement. Dryness is law—what flourishes must do so without water’s blessing. And the emptiness is the most solid thing of all: a population relieved, or exiled, or extinguished, leaving only their city, alive with the unwatered, sun-struck chorus, forever concealed from the world outside, forever strange.
