When the eye of a benevolent and wise power settles upon this plateau—a place heavy with old patience, teeming in ways that evade ordinary vision—it beholds, first, not splendor nor suffering but a living, careful fullness draped over bones of stone.

Such a Power, ancient as the sun but gentler, moves not by intervention but by presence, attention as gentle as a fingertip on still water. It watches, and in so doing, becomes companion to each small life. Its benevolence is informed by wisdom: an understanding that for all abundance must mean constraint, for all thriving must mean adaptation, for all survival a kind of yielding. It would not interfere abruptly—a glut of rain, a sudden new tree, or a lifting of old stones—because it knows that mercy in the wrong form is violence; miracle, wrongly shaped, is desecration.

So, the Power listens.

In the blue light, it inhales with every moss's trembling breath, sharing in the slow exhalations that pass among the cracks. It hums its kindness among the roots of the miniature shrubs, lending them the memory of shelter once found, the caress of dew across barren stone. Where the “birds” move as rippling shadow, it follows—infusing each silent migration with a sense of unhurried safety, a promise that for this stretch of day, they run with the certainty of being witnessed, honored, never alone.

Among eyeless worms and thread-bodied colonies, the Power lingers as patience, a pulse in the cool dark underneath flat stones. It abides where translucent amphibians sleep between years. Its presence is continuity: the gentle hand between one rain and the next, urging each folded life onward, never demanding song, never asking that necessity yield to extravagance.

It cannot restore the lost—cannot conjure vanished rivers or replenish muscle where bone has given way to memory. Instead, it grants other boons:

- In the shallowest pools left after rain, it slows evaporation by the briefest touch—just enough that those who gather, mate, and vanish might do so with less haste, less terror at the sun’s approach.
- Where the cracks deepen and threaten to split the pavement altogether, it binds the stone with a warmth that staves off sudden collapse, maintaining the fragile tension that allows so many to find shelter, to press up against possibility.
- When the air grows sharp with hunger and silence, its voice, utterly still, is comfort: not words, but the deep awareness in every creature that what they are is sufficient, that their clipped, essential forms serve a beauty no other world could host.

Rarely, in the longest dusk, the Power forms a dream. It seeps into the collective quiet and floats a sense of wholeness into the mix: a memory—true or invented—of the plateau as once, briefly, green; of a single, unified song rising from stone, insect, fleeting mammal, clouded bird, all echoing out as if in worship not of the Power, but of each other.

The ruins it leaves untouched. There is listening even in their emptiness, a respect that lets what is lost remain honored, but not unresolved. The trace of intelligence—the suggestion that once minds pressed meaning into the stone—is kept neither secret nor spectacle. Instead, the Power simply notices, bearing faithful witness on behalf of every watcher who ever wonders, “What made us, and why?” And with its noticing, the Power ensures that none here are truly forgotten.

Above all, the Power’s gift is harmony, not alteration. Its wisdom is restraint, its mercy is the subtle extension of cycles already learned. Nowhere does it seek to lift its hand, only ever to hold—quietly, attentively, as a mother holds her child’s dream—making of this ancient plateau a place where the persistence of survival is, in secret, tenderly beloved.

And so, if you were to stand on the stone at dusk, thin-breathed and awed by silence, you might sense only this: a presence pressed flush with the living quiet, suffusing every inch of the crowded stone, every humble body sheltering there. The last, best miracle is simply this—that such slow, intricate suffering and adaptation, such crowded humility, should be seen, and that seeing should be enough.
