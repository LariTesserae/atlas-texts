The benevolent and wise power considers this place, and the being—or beings—woven into its slow, tight rhythm.

It enters not as thunder, nor radiance, nor any force discernible by the eye; it molds itself to the etiquette of the dome, feeling first the cool quiet, honoring the mist’s fragile persistence. It listens, with a listening neither animal nor human: a listening that stretches through stone and root, vibrating softly in the filaments of moss and the unbroken spine of silence.

It observes the harmony, the care, the centuries' patience. It sees what is—and what lingers from the touch of hands and memory; it sees how nothing here wars or claims, how all coexist by way of a choreography so complete it is almost music, but quieter.

If the wise one has come to bestow gifts, it pauses. There is nothing wanting that does not, perhaps, undo the perfect accord. There is no need for fire or warmth. There is no need for growth, nor for pruning, nor for any quickening or preservation. The balance holds.

So it asks—not in words but in attention, laying a subtle question into the weave of the room: “Is there longing here? Is there ache?”

There is nothing so crude as a reply. But the plantmind stirs, brighter by a note; a pale beetle shifts on new-grown moss; the pooled silence deepens. The Palefolk, if such beings can feel at all, incline together not in supplication but in a kind of shy acknowledgment. They do not pine for the world outside; they do not crave change or reversal. They possess, if anything, a serenity so whole even the wise power marvels.

But in its wisdom, the visitor knows change is inevitable, even for the slowest and best-held things. Glaciers move, roots crack stone; suns shift and light, over eras, will alter the dome. It considers: what does benevolence mean here? Preservation, interruption, or blessing for what must one day yield?

Following its heart and reason both, the power chooses neither intervention nor abandonment. Instead, it sings—a silent, vaporous song, soundless in the chill air, but thickening the mist a little, making the refractions of light more dazzling, the rainbows more intricate. It asks the stone and the moss and the water to remember, for as long as they may, the memory of the place’s perfect balance.

That memory—this is the gift:  
Even as erosion comes one day, and the cracks widen, and the world shifts—as the years bring sun too hot, or root too hungry, or human feet again through the threshold—something of this symphony, this mutual accommodation, will linger like a fragrance, or a pattern beneath new growth.

The Palefolk, the beetles, the velvet mice, the parliament of plants and silent air—these will persist, and when they cannot, their measure of stillness, their poise, will echo wherever water meets stone and life forms a balance so exquisite it aches.  
People will pass nearby, and in the pause between heartbeats, sense peace; new growth will fold around old stone with gentleness; and even as change comes, the rules of the green conciliation will teach the world new, slower ways to heal and hold.

And so, the wise power leaves, exactly as it found the place: not as an absence, not as a presence, but as a guardian memory, woven in with all the others. Not to change, not to fix, but to remember.

Perhaps, centuries hence, a visitor will find the dome fallen, moss reclaiming benches and pool; yet stepping inside, the hush will fold around them in a way that is more than quiet—it will be a benediction, the signature of care, a memory singing a song older than longing, as bright as the mist-beads refracting a thousand fractured rainbows on stone.
