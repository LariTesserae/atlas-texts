It lies among the lowa hills, in a broad, shallow valley that tops a rise from the Mississippi. On the east the bluffs are steep, heavily wooded, and broken into fantastic peaks; on the west the slope is gentler and the countryside more open, with cultivated fields and occasional pasture lands. The soil is strong and black, and the fields bear heavy crops of grain. In summer the valley is warm and living, green with growing things, and noisy with the chorus of birds; in winter it is quiet and grey, its only life the reflections of the houses along the road, and the smoke from kitchen chimneys. North and south the valley stretches, indefinitely, failing at last among the dunes of the river. Before it, the Mississippi flows broad and brown, bearing traffic to and from the world outside. Behind it, the hills recede, and leave an opening into wider plains that reach uninhabited to the Mississippi bluffs. The place has been settled since the forties, and the road that traverses it has been trodden hard by generations of farm feet. The houses are old, of wood, set in orderly rows along the thoroughfare, and behind them stretch the fields. Through seedtime and harvest, through winter cold and summer heat, the valley lives and prosper.
