A high, long, treeless ridge runs transversely east and west, dividing the waters which flow north into the Irish Sea from those which flow south into the Bristol Channel. It is enclosed among hills, from which it rises steeply on the north, and more gradually on the south. The crest is broad and smooth, the sides shaggy with heather and coarse short grass, the base rocky and scarred. From it you look down on little cultivated fields and white farmsteads in sheltered hollows, on a shining plain of wet lowland, and far-off towns and spires. On clear days the sea is visible both ways, from Currab town in the north to Launceston in the south, and on favourable nights the sky is dotted with stars. The ridge itself is monotonous, but the prospect from it varies endlessly. In summer it is green, in winter grey, and in spring and autumn brown and yellow. Rooks fly over it daily to and fro, between their nests in the lower woods and the gleanings of the furrows. In summer evenings the sound of wheels and hooves comes up from the plain, and in winter mornings the pack goes down in white but silent file. The air is keen, and the waters clear; and about the roots of the hills which enclose the ridge, east and west, are little fishing ports. Such is the landscape, which has been familiar to me from childhood, and which I call Broad Plain.
