The landscape is desert in character, a plain broken by hills which run from north to south. The lowest parts are by the sea, and the highest lie inland, where they form down-bending meeting ridges. Between the sea and the interior the land falls gradually, but steeply, from a height of a thousand feet to below sea-level in a distance of sixty miles. The plain is diversified by shallow valleys and dry water-courses, but close to the shore there is little vegetation except among the gullies and ravines of the hills. The shore itself is bordered by a broad belt of sand, beyond which the water deepens suddenly. Far out are scattered rocks and islets, and the sea has a gloomily impressive aspect. The climate is hot and dry in summer, and in winter the plain is subject to white frosts. The waters of the coast are little fished, and the shores are silent, save when a storm arises. The hills are bare, of a dark, red earth, scored and scarred, and in places dotted with stones. From their summits a wide prospect is afforded — over a blank expanse of waste, broken only by a few iso lated bushes, to where the sky and the sea meet in the south. In the midst of this solitude stands a single building.
