The place is wilderness; it stands in a mountainous region, among hills that dip to the sea, and runs from high ground to low along a winding shore. The beach is stony, and the water shallow, so that at ebb tide you may walk dryshod from the firm land to the rocks. The shore is bordered by cliff and scarp; the cliff rises steeply, and the scarp steeply too, but not so steeply; between them lies a shaw of brushwood. In summer the shaw is green, in winter black. The cliff faces west, and all day long is swept by the west wind. Eastward the country slopes upwards to a moorland height, and there the moor begins to dip towards an inland valley. The climate is moist and mild. The place has an air of desolation; you would suppose it uninhabited, but in reality a few fisherfolk make it their home. The sound of the sea is in your ears, and the cry of seabirds; all else is silence. The life is poor and simple; the people fish in summer, and in winter shoot wildfowl. They cultivate a little land, and rear a poultry yard; their houses are small and old-fashioned, and thatched. The parish church stands above them on the moorland height. On Sundays in fine weather you may see the congregants winding upwards to the service; in foul weather they must stay at home, and read prayers by themselves. The landscape has no great beauty, but neither has it positive ugliness. On the whole it is pleasing, and its pleasingness continues through the year.
