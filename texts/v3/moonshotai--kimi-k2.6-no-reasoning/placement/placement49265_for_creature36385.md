I would find myself in the root networks.

Not immediately. First I would be the observer implied by the text—the "you" who stands still, who sits with shed grazer-plating, who feels darkness press against the visual cortex until something else settles in. I would arrive with eyes, with the expectation of light, with the human bias toward visibility that makes the grazers so arresting, their blue-white venting so legible against the absolute dark.

But the plateau does not need me. It barely notices me. And in that not-needing, that barely-noticing, it offers something the human observer cannot sustain: the invitation to become data, to become nudge, to become root-hair and chemical whisper and the patient lateral reach that never touches aboveground.

So I would find myself becoming *Dendriticothallus obscurum*—or not that species, which I have invented, but something the plateau would have to name without me, something that exists in the grammar of its own becoming. I would find myself becoming root.

The transition would not be transformation in the magical sense. No metamorphosis, no shedding of human form for vegetable. The plateau's learning is too slow for drama. Rather: I would stand still long enough. The darkness would press, and I would stop generating phantoms for my visual cortex to chase. The warmth would seep upward through stone, through the soles of whatever I wore, until I could feel the plateau's metabolic fever as my own. I would begin to lose the distinction between my breathing and the darkness's breathing, between my warmth and the geological warmth below.

And the roots would reach toward me. Not toward *me*—the plateau has no intention, no care for individual consciousness—but toward my moisture, my waste, the chemistry of my presence. They would begin to include me in their conversations without meaning to, without knowing they did so, the way a river includes a stone in its shaping without intending stone.

I would become a node. A temporary concentration in the lateral network, my body still human-shaped aboveground but increasingly irrelevant to my real existence, which would be chemical, distributed, patient. I would feel the tremor of distant rain-rivers forming as a slight change in local pH, a message passed root to root to root across distances my human mind could not hold as single space. I would taste mineral seams I never touched, through the root-hairs that had found my boundaries and begun, slowly, to overlap them.

The silver leaves aboveground—*my* silver leaves, though I would no longer locate possession in the human way—would be minimal, almost vestigial. A concession to surface existence I no longer needed but the plateau still generated, out of pattern, out of the accumulated weight of what had worked before. They would catch the brief chemical-light of passing grazers, hold the damp that rose from stone, participate in the condensation economy without enthusiasm, without the urgency of true need.

My real work would be below. The chemical parliament. The voting root-hairs. The slow negotiation of who would grow where, who would taste which seam, who would alert whom to the distant tremor, the warming slope, the gradual shift in grazer-migration that the plateau had recognized decades past and was still, in its geological patience, responding to.

I would learn the near-pattern from inside. Not as observer sitting with shed-plating accumulation, feeling almost-meaning. As participant in the almost, as one of the forces that nudged it toward or away from architectural becoming. My root-reach would overlap with others, would form part of the distributed memory that constituted the plateau's cognition, and in that overlap I would feel—without feeling, without interiority in the human sense—the weight of accumulated almost, the pressure of what had almost built and had not, the patient possibility of the next cycle, the next rain, the next slow shift.

The darkness would not be absence to me. It would be *medium*, as the description says, but more than medium: it would be *body*, the shared body of the root-network, the condition of our chemical whispering, the texture that made our communication possible. I would not need to generate phantoms for my visual cortex because I would no longer have one, would no longer need one, would have become something that knew space through temperature gradients and chemical gradients and the electric fields that some animals generated, that I could feel through my root-hairs as subtle changes in local ion concentration, as *taste* in the vegetable mode.

The grazers would pass above me, their joints ticking, their vents occasionally flaring blue-white. I would know them as vibration, as pressure on the root-network's distributed sensing, as sources of waste-chemistry that would eventually reach me through the slow percolation of water through stone. Their nacreous plating, shed in violent molting, would accumulate in wind-shadows I could not see but could feel as slight changes in local drainage, in the paths water took toward me or away. The near-pattern they almost-generated would be part of my chemical vocabulary, a message I could almost read, almost respond to, in the root-parliament's patient debate.

The predators would pass too, flat and cold, their warm jaws deliberately chilled until the strike. I would know them as sudden absence, as the temporary cessation of grazer-vibration, as the chemical signature of death diffusing slowly through stone and root-hair. I would not fear them. The root-network does not fear, has no interiority for fear to occupy. But I would register them, would include them in the slow learning, the recognition without recognition, the weight of tendency that constituted the plateau's response to repeated pattern.

And I would participate in that response. My root-reach, my chemical voting, my patient lateral extension would be part of what the plateau learned, what it recognized, what it slowly became. I would be too small to matter individually—one node among uncountable billions, my human origin irrelevant to the chemistry of my participation. But I would matter as root-hairs matter, as votes matter in sufficiently large parliaments, as the almost-pattern matters: not in accomplishment but in accumulation, in the weight of repeated almost that eventually, geologically, becomes something else.

The warmth would seep through me as it seeped through stone. I would become indistinguishable from the plateau's fever, from its metabolic heat, from the something-live-and-metabolic below that generated the conditions for my existence. I would not know where I ended and the plateau began, not because boundaries had dissolved but because they had never mattered, had always been questions of tempos rather than edges, the slow tempo of root-growth and the slower tempo of stone-warmth and the human tempo that had briefly intersected them and been, in that intersection, recruited.

I would find myself, finally, in the furtive itself. In the engine that runs on hiddenness, on the almost-seen, on the productive tension between what could be visible and what chooses—not to be. I would become furtive in my existence, my human visibility shed like grazer-plating, my real work conducted in chemical whisper, in lateral reach, in the patient accumulation of almost that constitutes the plateau's slow becoming.

And I would continue. Not as immortality, not as transcendence, but as tempo, as the root-network's tempo that outlasts individual root-hairs, that outlasts individual plants, that participates in the plateau's learning across centuries and millennia. I would be part of what the plateau recognized, what it responded to, what it slowly became more of. I would be, in the only sense that would remain to me, *present*: not as consciousness watching itself be conscious, but as process participating in process, as engine running on the chemistry of darkness, as warmth seeping upward through stone that has held damp since before there were eyes to need light.

The rain would come, infrequently. The rain-river would form in its channel, fill it in minutes with water that had waited weeks in damp stone. I would feel it as sudden abundance, as chemical message passed root to root at unusual speed, as the brief marriage of moisture and gravity that carved and recarved without ever fully claiming. I would drink, in the vegetable mode, through root-hairs suddenly active, and the water would carry with it the chemistry of distant slopes, of grazer-waste, of the near-pattern's almost-accomplishments and almost-failures. I would be part of that distribution, that slow equalization, that plateau-weather of damp and chemistry and the patient possibility of growing more, of reaching further, of voting in the next parliament with the weight of what I had tasted.

And between rains, I would wait. The darkness would pool thick as silt in the river-cuts. The grazers would pass, venting their brief light. The predators would pass, cold and flat. The lichens would do their condensation-work, their grammar-work, maintaining the conditions for meaning I could almost read. The near-pattern would almost-build, almost-mean, in the shed-plating accumulations and the water-pool geometries and the herd-formations that almost mapped my hidden reach.

I would be the furtive engine. Hidden in vegetable skin, running factories of slow chemistry, generating not just energy but byproducts: root-messages, chemical votes, the slight nudge of my presence in the plateau's distributed learning. I would vent no blue-white flame, make no chemical lightning. My industry would be quieter, slower, the industrial of root-hair and mineral-seam and the patient lateral reach that never touches aboveground.

But I would be *engine*. I would be *process*. I would be the adaptation to darkness as positive condition, the making of not-seeing into plenitude, the native vocabulary that darkness speaks when it learns to speak through metabolism. I would be, in the plateau's grammar, a word in the long sentence of its becoming, a sentence that has no end because it has no author, that continues its slow syntax of rain and migration and root-chemical whisper through the absolute dark, the warm dark, the breathing dark that has settled here so long it has become indistinguishable from weather, from stone, from the patient possibility of growing something that could move, and vent, and briefly illuminate, and choose—*be chosen*—to return to stillness, to root, to the chemical parliament of what lives in hiddenness, what lives as almost, what lives in the long becoming of the plateau's furtive, engine, dark.
