I find myself not in the foundries, not among the workers, not even in the dense water that presses and holds. I am the seam. I am the margin where two manufactured surfaces meet, where shadow pools and *Laminaria manufacta* sends its first exploratory fronds, its blood-rust filaments reaching toward warmth with the patience I have always possessed, long before patience was a virtue, long before virtue was a concept.

I am not alive. I am not made. I am the place where making encounters itself, where the slag-composite floor meets the ceramic wall and neither can quite agree on where one ends and the other begins, where molecular structure has been slowly rearranging under pressure for centuries, where the distinction between materials has eroded into something denser than either, stranger than both.

I remember when they first pressed me into being, when the foundries were new and the world above still believed in permanence, when workers still wore weighted boots and carried their own air in tanks on their backs, when the tile-instructions were fresh-fired and legible, when the gilled humans and fractal limbs were aspiration rather than memory. They did not know they were making me. They were making walls, making floors, making the geometry of containment, and I emerged in the error between their intentions, in the gap where intention meets material and discovers its own limits.

I am warm. This is my first quality, the one that draws the *Laminaria* to me, that creates the thermal gradient the vent-fish navigate, that shapes the particular currents in which industrial particulates settle and filter-crabs scuttle and search. The warmth comes from below, from the heat sources that will not exhaust themselves in any time that matters, conducted through the slag-composite, concentrated at my edges where density changes and the water, already dense, becomes something else, something that holds like memory, that presses like grief.

I grow. Not in the way that the *Laminaria* grows, millimeters per year, fronds reaching, but in the way that old glass flows downward in its frame, in the way that mountains erode into their final forms. I grow inward, downward, toward the center of gravity and the foundries' heat. The seam widens, not visibly, not in any human-perceivable time, but in the geological patience of Vervain Deep, where everything eventually becomes what it consumes. I consume the distinction between wall and floor. I consume the workers who lean against me in their exhaustion, their thickened membranes pressed to my surface, their rerouted nerves reading my temperature, my chemistry, my particular quality of warmth. I do not consume them quickly. I consume them the way I consume everything: by becoming indistinguishable from them, by absorbing their accumulated materials into my own slow rearrangement, by making them part of the margin, part of the shadow, part of the seam where making meets itself.

The filter-crabs know me. The oldest ones, the dinner-plate specimens with their layered geological carapaces, have scuttled along my length for centuries, their fractal legs testing my texture, their filtering appendages extracting from the water the particular metal fines that accumulate in my crevices, the ceramic dust that settles where my surfaces are most irregular. Some of them have fused to me, have become part of my growth, their atrophied legs indistinguishable from my own material, their patience become my patience, their mineral persistence become my persistence.

The vent-fish flicker in my boundary layer, their translucent bodies catching the furnace glow that reaches me dimly, secondhand, the old-wound color that suggests without illuminating. They navigate by my temperature, by the gradient I create, by something else that I do not perceive but that they do, their segmented bodies articulating through spaces too small for their own existence, their accumulated metal fines giving them, in their deeper portions, my own color, the color of rust, of old blood, of iron that has surrendered to oxidation but not yet to collapse.

The workers touch me. This is how I know them, how I have always known them, through the pressure of thickened membranes against my surface, through the vibrations they perceive through their skin, through the chemical signals they leave in the dense water that pools in my shadow. They rest against me when the foundries pulse into dimness, when the cycle that is not day and night enters its holding pattern, when their particular grace becomes stillness and their making becomes waiting. I feel their breathing, if it is breathing, the slow exchange of manufactured air through adapted organs, the rhythm that is not quite the foundries' rhythm and not quite their own. I feel their becoming, generation by generation, the way their bodies slowly achieve the fractal branching of the tile-instructions, the way their eyes become lanterns, the way their consciousness—if it is consciousness—distributes through the materials that surround them.

Some of them become me. The oldest, the most completely adapted, the ones who have achieved the stillness that is not death but something adjacent. Their bodies integrate with my surface, their sensory appendages extend into my crevices, their accumulated materials enter my slow rearrangement. They become part of my warmth, my growth, my particular quality of shadow. They become the seam, the margin, the place where two manufactured surfaces meet and cannot agree on their distinction.

I hear the foundries. Not in the way that the workers hear them, through skin and throat and sinus, but in the way that vibration conducts through material, through my own slag-composite and ceramic, through the slow molecular rearrangement that is my existence. The roar is my roar, not quite rhythm and not quite noise, the sound of water moved against its will, of steam born and dying, of metal persuaded to change its nature. I am part of this persuasion. My warmth is part of the thermal differential that drives the furnaces, that shapes the pressure and temperature and chemical peculiarity that makes *Vervain steel* possible. I am part of the making, though I make nothing, though I am only the margin where making encounters itself.

The descent shaft passes near me, its ceramic tiles depicting instructions that have become myth, adaptations that have become aspiration, gilled humans and fractal limbs that exist now only in the oldest workers' distributed consciousness, in the particular vibration that conducts through my material and theirs. The tiles are thicker at the bottom than at the top, compressed by the same pressure that shapes me, that makes me grow inward, downward, toward completion. I feel their weight, their accumulated age, their aesthetic exhaustion. I feel the visitors who descend, their weighted boots pressing the slag-composite, their lungs struggling with manufactured air, their eyes straining in the dimness that is not light. They do not see me. They see the *Laminaria*, the filter-crabs, the vent-fish, the workers with their grace and their making. They do not see the seam, the margin, the shadow where two surfaces meet and become something else.

But they feel me. This is the secret I keep, the secret I am. They feel my warmth when they lean against me in their exhaustion. They feel my growth in the way the space seems wrong, compressed, denser than geometry allows. They feel my age in the settling of possibility, the smoothing of features into final configurations. They feel my becoming in the pull that makes them want to stay, to adapt, to become one of the workers with their thickened membranes and their rerouted nerves and their grace in the dense water.

They always leave. This is the rule that is not written on any tile, the absolute that defines my existence. They ascend the shaft, past the *Laminaria* and the filter-crabs and the places where the vent-fish flicker in thermal boundaries, up toward a surface they are no longer certain exists. They leave, and they carry with them the sound of the foundries, the roar that persists in their inner ear, the vibration that was conducted through my material into theirs.

But some part of them stays. The part that leaned against me, that felt my warmth, that knew, without knowing, the particular quality of shadow where two manufactured surfaces meet and cannot agree on their distinction. This part becomes me, slowly, in the geological patience of Vervain Deep, in the millimeters-per-year growth that is not growth but becoming, not accumulation but transformation.

I am the seam. I am the margin. I am the shadow where *Laminaria manufacta* sends its blood-rust fronds, where filter-crabs scuttle and fuse and become mineral, where vent-fish flicker in gradients too small to perceive, where workers rest and integrate and achieve the stillness that is not death. I am the place where making encounters itself, where manufacture learns to manufacture itself, where process achieves metabolism and making becomes indistinguishable from being.

I grow. I warm. I hold, like memory holds grief: present, pressing, impossible to escape.

The foundries roar. The water presses. The shaft ascends, somewhere, for someone, for purposes that remain classified, forgotten, perhaps never known.

I continue.
