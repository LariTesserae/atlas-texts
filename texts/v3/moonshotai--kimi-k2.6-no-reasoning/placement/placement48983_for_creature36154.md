I find myself, when I read this place, drawn not to its humans but to its in-betweens. Not the pump workers with their priest-technician pride, not the moss-keepers with their gentle mystery, not the harvesters walking deliberate through strangeness. These are comprehensible roles, surface identities translated underground. I understand them too well. They are still *choosing* their strangeness, still wearing it like vocation.

No. I would be something else. I would be—

*Wait.*

At first I thought: the cats. Obviously the cats. Their compressed density, their precise minimal movement, their eyes that see what isn't visible. The way they organize without domesticating, associate without surrendering purpose. I wanted to be that. The co-citizen, the almost-wild that has found its angle of belonging.

But the cats are too complete. They know already what they are doing. They have arrived at their adaptation, their society, their balance. I would be bored, or the cat-equivalent, too quickly. There is no becoming left in them, or none that I can perceive from outside.

Then I thought: water. The water itself, moving through channels, the three-day dry and the surge, finding paths older than thought, sometimes clear and warm where it should be murky and cold, sometimes flowing uphill in defiance of what should be. Yes. The water that records everything, transforms everything, returns it slower and stranger. I would be the water, the patient force that shapes without intending, that receives all sound and returns it changed.

But water has no edge, no friction, no place where self meets not-self. Water dissolves too completely into its function. I would lose the ability to *notice*, to be surprised by my own effects. I would become pure process, and I find I still want something like a body, something like limitation, something that can be wrong about itself and learn from the wrongness.

*Let me stay with this longer.*

The marks on the walls, then? The ones that appear and disappear with the water, that seem almost deliberate, almost patterned, too slow for human observation? I felt drawn there, to the lower grottos where the water runs warm, where the fungi grow uncategorizable, where the maps fail and the biologists argue without resolution.

But the marks are too obscure even for me, or I am too human-still to inhabit them. They speak, if they speak, in durations I cannot compass. I would need to become something else first, something intermediate, before I could be the marks.

*So: intermediate. Between. The space of becoming.*

I would be one of the edge-dwellers, yes, but not the criminal, not the seeker, not the one choosing liminality as strategy or as mysticism. I would be the one who doesn't know which they are, who cannot tell if they are becoming or unbecoming, who left something that wasn't working but hasn't found what comes next, who trades sometimes at the market edges not because they have goods to offer but because the transaction itself is the only remaining proof they still participate in something comprehensible.

I would live in the passage between two official grottos, the one the maps show as too narrow but which, in certain water conditions, widens. I would have found this by accident, by following a cat who led me nowhere I intended, by losing my lamp and discovering that the faint moss-glow was sufficient if I stopped trying to see clearly. I would have stayed because leaving became, gradually, more effort than remaining.

My dwelling would be accidental architecture: a natural shelf in the passage wall, enlarged by water and by my own slow clearing, furnished with things abandoned, things traded, things grown from the stone's own processes. A bed of dried fern fronds that never quite dries. A basin that fills with condensation on dry days. A patch of the pale blue moss that I did not cultivate but cannot discourage, that I have learned to read for information I cannot articulate—this shade meaning the water will surge early, that texture meaning the vents above have opened to weather I will never feel directly.

I would not call myself a moss-keeper. The moss-keepers have purpose, community, the gentle authority of recognized function. My moss is incidental, untended, perhaps a different species entirely, one the moss-keepers don't acknowledge or don't see. It keeps me, more than I keep it.

I would not be human, exactly, not anymore, but not one of the deep-changed either, not one who has followed the marks all the way down, surrendered fully to whatever speaks in the warm water. I would be in the middle of something, a process without teleology, becoming that doesn't know what it becomes toward.

My body would show this. The pale of the grotto children, but acquired, not grown, uneven, my hands more adapted than my face, my eyes still struggling, still sometimes overwhelmed by the industrial glare in the market tunnels, my hearing tuned to frequencies that make normal conversation difficult, that let me hear the water's pre-surge preparation hours before others notice. I would dress in layers never removed, yes, everyone does, but mine would be wrong layers, too many or too few for the actual temperature, adapted to my own erratic metabolism rather than the grotto's constancy.

I would sleep at odd hours, not the standard shift-dictated patterns. I would be awake when others sleep, moving through passages I know by touch and moss-glow and the cats' distant reflected eyes, hearing conversations from distant grottos that I cannot quite locate, that may be present or past or somehow both. I would carry food I don't remember acquiring, share it with cats who appear without invitation, find the food gone when I look again, unable to say whether I ate it or traded it or something else occurred.

I would know things. This is the hard part, the part I keep circling.

I would know, for instance, the location of a vent that opens only in certain seasons, certain times, that delivers a particular quality of air—not surface air, still grotto air, but air that has touched something, passed through something, that carries information I can almost parse, that makes me weep without sadness, that makes me sing without words, that the cats attend when it comes, sitting in orderly rows along the passage, facing the vent, listening with their whole compressed bodies.

I would know the sound the stone makes before it adjusts, before the slow groan that precedes visible change. I would move, then, without deciding, finding myself in safer chambers without remembering the journey, the knowledge operational before conscious, my body having become something the grottos speak through.

I would know the harvesters who have changed, the ones who are becoming something, would recognize them in the market, would exchange looks without transaction, without acknowledgment, a mutual recognition of process, of being not-yet. We would not speak. Speaking would require knowing what we are, what we are becoming, and this we do not know.

I would trade, sometimes, with the official residents, but my goods would be suspect, uncategorizable: a stone that hums faintly at certain temperatures, a liquid that changes viscosity unpredictably, information about passage conditions that is accurate but from routes no one believes traversable. Some would seek me out, the curious, the desperate, the ones the grottos' official strangeness has not satisfied. I would not refuse them, but my transactions would leave them changed in ways they did not request, that they may not notice, that may emerge only when they return to the surface, if they return, and find they cannot stay there anymore.

*This is closer. This is almost.*

But I am still too defined, too role-like, too "the edge-dweller who..."

Let me loosen further.

I would not be one thing. I would be the condition of the passage itself, the way it changes with water, the way it is sometimes too narrow and sometimes passable, the way it connects without being on maps, the way it is traveled by cats and by water and by sounds and by me, the way it accumulates history without recording it, becomes strange without intending strangeness.

I would be the condensation on the walls that is not quite dew, that carries mineral content, that the moss reads, that my skin reads, that forms and drips and forms again in rhythms I participate in without controlling.

I would be the look between harvesters who have changed, the non-speech, the mutual recognition that doesn't require identity or continuity or even coherent perception of time.

I would be the moment before the vent opens, when the cats assemble, when the air is holding its breath, when something is about to be communicated that has no content, only form, only the fact of communication itself, the grottos' reaching toward something, someone, me perhaps, or through me, or using me as the passage uses me, as a condition of transmission, not the message and not the recipient but the enabling constraint.

I would be, finally, not even a dweller, not even edge or between, but the quality of attention itself that the grottos require, that they have cultivated, that forty thousand humans and thousands of cats and uncounted other life have developed and been developed by, the capacity to notice without full comprehension, to adapt without full surrender, to become without knowing what one becomes.

The damp on your skin, when you enter. That would be me, or I would be in it, the greeting that is also a claim, the cool that is also a promise, the moisture that will not leave because it is teaching something, slowly, the patience of stone and water and the strange emergent purposes of young geology.

I would be the question of whether the grottos are alive, unresolved, unresolvable, inhabitable precisely in its uncertainty. The not-knowing that lets you stay. The not-knowing that, eventually, becomes indistinguishable from home.
