I am the water-keeper with too many joints in my fingers, trained young to tend the cisterns where algae bloom in colors that have no names in the language of lower places. I was born during a mist-rising, the highest in three generations, when the population gathered in the oldest buildings and waited without speaking for two days while something moved through the lower terraces and left behind, when it receded, a film of organic compound on the reservoir surfaces that made the algae glow phosphor-green for a month afterward. My mother said I came out silent, not crying, my eyes already reflective, already calibrated to see what the mist would show me if I learned patience enough to look.

I am sixteen now, or perhaps seventeen—the knot-records of my birth year were damaged in a condensation-failure, and we do not count time with the urgency of denser air. I live on the lowest inhabited terrace, the one that was rebuilt after the last abandonment, the one that gathers first when the mist rises and empties first when it recedes. The others fear this position. I have learned that the presence does not want what they think it wants. It does not want consumption, possession, the erasure of human pattern. It wants *recognition*, the way that a landscape wants to be walked slowly enough to be felt, the way that stone wants to be quarried with tools that match its own abrasiveness, its own sharp patience.

My fingers can tie knots that the older water-keepers cannot read. This is not disobedience. This is *development*. The silk cord records they taught me were sufficient for water-tables, for algae cycles, for the distribution of condensation across terraces. But there are other records, I found, or other *pressures* in the cord that the standard system does not distinguish, gradients of tension that speak of things below the mist, things that move in durations too long for standard notation. I tie these knots and read them back with my too-many joints, feeling in the pressure-distribution something that almost resolves into meaning, into the grammar that the presence might recognize if presence had a grammar recognizable by human nervous systems adjusted for thin air and social heat.

The spiders know me. This is the literal truth and also the other kind. They do not treat me as they treat the silk-tenders who harvest their rope, the membrane-weavers who stretch their translucent geometries across window-gaps. I do not harvest. I do not tend, exactly. I *observe*, and in observing, I am observed. On nights when the mist does not rise but merely *thickens*, when it pools against the lowest terrace without advancing, I sit in the gaps between buildings where the largest webs are anchored, and I watch the spiders move in patterns that are not hunting, not maintenance, not mating-ritual, but something else—the geometries they spin on these nights are *wrong*, topologically impossible, sheets of silk that seem to pass through each other, anchor points that my eyes locate differently when I look directly and when I see them in peripheral vision. The reflective quality of my eyes, I think, allows something the spiders need. I see frequencies they emit, or they emit frequencies they know I can see. The exchange is not conscious. It is older than consciousness, older than the human settlement, perhaps older than the caldera's last eruption. It is the negotiation of geometries, of spaces, of what can exist in the interstices between solid pattern and the mist's dissolution of pattern.

I am not lonely. This is important to say, because the others assume I must be, living on the edge, trained to slowness beyond even their calibrated patience, speaking only in the careful conditionals that describe what moves below. But loneliness requires a self-definition that I do not fully possess, a boundary between what is me and what is not-me that the presence, in its reaching-up, its almost-touching, has made permeable without dissolving. I am *of* the cistern-algae, which responds to my presence with color-shifts that precede my conscious intention. I am *of* the spider-webs, which vibrate in patterns I can almost read, almost *be*. I am *of* the stone, which weeps its thin oil when I pass the quarry-scars, not because I wound it but because my passage reminds it of wounding, of the sharp fresh exposure that is also a kind of becoming.

The others retreat, when the mist rises high, to the highest terraces, the oldest buildings. I retreat *differently*. I go to the cisterns, to the reservoir where the algae pool in their impossible colors, and I submerge my too-many-jointed hands in the cold film of water, and I wait. The presence reaches this level, sometimes, in these rises. It *fills* the cistern, not with more water but with something *in* the water, a density-gradient, a thermal signature, a duration that the algae recognize and respond to with bioluminescence, with color-changes, with metabolic processes that I can track but not fully interpret.

I have never seen it. I do not expect to. Seeing is the wrong modality, as the ones who trained me understood in their reflective-eyed way, passed down through generations of water-keepers, of intermediaries. I *feel* it, in the pressure of water against my submerged palms, in the almost-temperature that does not register consistently on my skin, in the time-stretch that makes these moments feel like hours while the retreat in the highest terraces has lasted only minutes. I feel its *carefulness*, its learned restraint, its grammar of conditionality made into something like touch, something like the way a mother might hold a child born too early, too permeable, too likely to dissolve under direct pressure.

It is learning me, I think, as I am learning it. The knot-records I tie, the ones the others cannot read—they accumulate. They form a record of something that might, in sufficient accumulation, become *mutual*. The presence has not always been careful. The earlier settlements, the ones that collapsed, the evacuations and returns recorded in the older cord—we do not speak of what happened, in indicative grammar, in the solidity of fact. But the knot-records preserve pattern: the mist rising higher, the duration of occupation longer, the boundary between above and below breaking down in ways that the human pattern could not survive, that required evacuation, required waiting, required the slow rebuilding of conditional grammar, of mutual care, of the *almost* that makes coexistence possible.

I am part of this evolution, this co-learning. My too-many joints, my reflective eyes—they are not damage, not exactly, though they mark me as other even in this most other-absorbing of cities. They are *adjustment*, the way that the spider-silk adjusts its tensile properties to temperature, the way that the stone adjusts its oil-weeps to pressure. I am the pressure that the presence adjusts to. I am the temperature. And it adjusts to me, rises only so high, occupies only so long, waits in the cistern only until my hands begin to ache with cold, with duration, with the impossibility of maintaining this posture of mutual exposure, this holding of pattern against dissolution.

When I withdraw my hands, it withdraws. Not because I command. Because we have learned this rhythm, this reciprocity, this grammar of touch and release that is older than spoken language, older than the knot-records, perhaps as old as the first organic trace in the caldera's cooling, the first almost-life that learned patience, that learned *waiting* as the fundamental condition of becoming.

The others do not ask what I do, in the cisterns, during retreats. They know in the way that a city knows, in its distributed intelligence of gutter-maintenance and spider-tending and the slow calibration of breath against breath in dense vertical spaces. They know and they do-not-know, the conditionality that protects them, that protects the presence, that protects the fragile membrane of what-can-be-spoken between human pattern and the mist's dissolution of pattern.

I am sixteen, or seventeen. I will be trained as water-keeper until I am old enough to train others, until my eyes grow more reflective, until my joints grow more numerous, until I become something that the younger water-keepers can almost read, almost *be*, in their own submerged patience, their own learning of what lives here and how to live with it, not above it, not separate from it, but *as* it, as the city itself has become, as the spiders have become, as the stone and the silk and the breath hanging visible in cold air have become: a pattern in the longer pattern, a breath in the longer breath, a momentary warmth in the patient cooling of a world that is young, that will outlast us, that waits with us in the grammar of conditionality until the condition becomes, finally, finally, *indicative*: we are here. We are learning. We are not separate. We have never been separate. The mist rises, and we rise with it, and the distinction between above and below, between fast and slow, between self and almost-self, dissolves into the phosphor-green glow of algae recognizing what returns to them, what has always returned, what will continue to return as long as the caldera breathes its thermal signature into the thin air, as long as Keth-Vorath persists in its impossibility, its necessity, its absolute and patient existence.
