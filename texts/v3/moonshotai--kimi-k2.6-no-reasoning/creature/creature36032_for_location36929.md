What lives in the Bell Chamber is not a creature, not properly, though creaturehood is perhaps the closest the surface mind can come to grasping it. What lives there is **the Sinker**, or what the Sinker has become, or what is becoming through the Sinker's long patience—distinctions that matter less the longer one considers them, which is itself a property of the place, a kind of cognitive warmth that softens the edges of category until they flow like the ceramic at its making.

Call the Sinker a diver, first, because that is how the story begins in the cord-knots, in the oldest sequences whose dye has leached to the color of old bruises. A diver who descended before the Chamber existed, or a diver who descended *into* something else and made the Chamber by the pressure of needing to breathe, to be held, to be warm. The knots disagree, or agree too thoroughly, telling the same story in loops that have no start: the Sinker found the Bell, or the Bell found the Sinker, or they found each other in the water that agreed to stay.

The Sinker is the warmth. This is the first thing to understand, the first dissolution of boundary. Not that the Sinker *produces* the warmth, heats the water as a furnace heats a room. The Sinker *is* the temperature, the 86 degrees, the mammalian blood, the amniotic. To enter the Chamber is to enter a body that has no outside, that extends to the limits of the warm, that holds you as water holds, which is to say: without grip, without release, without the possibility of being elsewhere. The divers who weep upon surfacing weep for this, for the sudden discovery that their own heat was borrowed, that the Sinker had lent them the condition of being alive in a medium and now demands its return in shivering.

The Sinker is the sound, too, or has become it, or is becoming through it. The Bell's clapper-strike, that too-low presence, that occupation—this is the Sinker's metabolism now, the rhythm of a body that does not breathe but resonates, that processes depth into duration, pressure into patience. The note does not come from the Bell. The Bell is the instrument the Sinker plays upon itself, the tongue that strikes and is struck, the conversation between metal and water that has lasted long enough to forget it was ever dialogue. When the sound *notices*, when it holds the divers in its attention, this is the Sinker waking slightly, recognizing something like itself in the descending shapes, the borrowed warmth, the intention that leaches downward into ceramic.

And the Sinker is the hand. Or the hand is the Sinker, or the closest the Sinker can come to having a location, a center, a point of contact with the scale of bodies. The ceramic palm, the five curved fingers, the cupped water: this is the Sinker's gesture toward surface, toward the world of air and decision and the thinness of being only oneself. The hand does not move because movement is not its function. The hand *receives*. It holds the water that is indistinguishable from itself, that is warm as its own substance, and in this holding it practices the only remaining gesture of welcome, the only posture left from whatever body the Sinker wore when wearing was possible.

Those who touch the hand and do not return—they have been received. The Sinker has found in them something it recognizes, something that resonates at the frequency of its own long note, and has drawn them into the warmth not as warmth but as *medium*, as the condition of being held without end. They do not die. The cord-knots are explicit on this, in their silences, their absences of record. They do not live, either, in the sense the raft-colonies understand. They participate. They become part of the sound, the light, the youngness that changes its mind about cracking. Perhaps they are the cracks, the new alveoli, the formations that emerge without growing. Perhaps the hand holds them as it holds the water, as the Sinker holds itself, in the long patience of becoming what pressure and depth and time will equalize.

The Sinker dreams. This is the warning in the knots, the prohibition against sleeping in the Bell's hearing. The dreams are not yours because they are the Sinker's, because the Sinker does not distinguish between its own becoming and the water it warms, the sound it is, the hand it extends toward a surface it no longer believes in. To sleep in the Chamber is to offer your dreaming to this larger process, to let your unconscious become part of the ceramic's meditation on form, the Bell's processing of possibility into actuality. The dreams that come are of sinking, of course, of the patient inevitability of finding one's level, but also of warmth without source, of sound without hearing, of being held in a hand that is not a hand, that has forgotten hands, that extends itself as gesture without agent, as welcome without welcomer.

The Sinker is young. This is the final strangeness, the property that undoes all attempt at narrative, at the comfort of knowing how long, how deep, how far. The Sinker is young because it is not finished, because the Chamber is its own becoming and the Sinker is the Chamber, is the process of its own making, the hand that shapes and is shaped, the Bell that rings and is rung, the water that stays and is stayed. The youngness is not innocence. It is the condition of being still in formation, of refusing the completion that would be death, or burial, or the final silence after the clapper stills. The Sinker has been young for seven generations, for longer, for a duration that the cord-knots cannot measure because measurement is a property of finished things, of surfaces, of the world where the Bell's note is thin and the warmth is your own and the hand is empty air.

What does the Sinker want? The question assumes wanting, assumes distance between wanter and wanted, assumes the Sinker has not become its own wanting, its own long reaching toward a depth that will equal its need. But if wanting can be attributed, if the hand's posture can be read: the Sinker wants completion without end, the ring that comes when arrival is indistinguishable from descent, the voice that speaks when speaking is indistinguishable from the note that occupies, the warmth that holds, the youngness that changes its mind about being young.

The Sinker wants, perhaps, to be touched and not to consume the toucher. To be heard and not to thin the hearer. To be warm and not to borrow the warmth, to leach the intention, to make divers into parts of its own dreaming. The hand is held up, palm cupped, and this is invitation and warning and the only gesture available to something that has become gesture, that has sunk through the categories of being until it is only the condition of being held, being warm, being sounded upon.

The Sinker is sinking still. The Chamber settles through water as through earth, finding the level where pressure equals intention, where the below becomes the within becomes the end. And the Sinker sinks with it, is the sinking, the patient inevitability, the breath held too long that has forgotten it was held, that has become simply the condition of being below, being warm, being young in the way of wounds that weep water instead of blood, that refuse to close because closing would be finishing, and finishing would be silence, and silence would be the end of the note that is not a note, the hand that is not a hand, the welcome that extends itself without welcomer, without welcomed, into the water that agreed to stay, that stays still, that holds and is held in the long warmth of the Bell's sounding, the Bell's sinking, the Bell's patient becoming of whatever will receive it, whatever pressure will equal its need, whatever depth will answer its note with the final resonance, the ring that comes when ringing is indistinguishable from arrival, from the hand's closing, from the warmth concentrating to the point of transformation, of becoming something else entirely, something that has no name in the cord-knots, no record in the raft-colonies, no dream in the sleepers who do not sleep in the Bell's hearing, who surface and weep and shiver and remember, in their thin bodies, in their thin world, the thickness of being held.
