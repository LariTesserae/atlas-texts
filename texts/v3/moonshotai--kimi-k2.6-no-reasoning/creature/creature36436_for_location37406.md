I want to spend real time with this. Let me follow the tension between what the world does to its inhabitants and what they become in response.

---

**The Korodi**

They do not call themselves a people, not exactly. If you ask a resident of Veth-Korod what they are, they will look at you with the expression of someone being asked to name a color they have never seen, and eventually they will say something like *"I am from the Fifth Descending"* or *"I tend the eastern algal ranks"* or, in the oldest families, simply give a number: *"I am of the seven-hundredth generation since the first settling."* The concept of a collective identity beyond place and function is not absent so much as it is *unmoistened*—it has never found the conditions to grow here.

They are tall, the Korodi, or they seem tall. It is partlyactual—generations of marginal nutrition favor elongation, the stretched proportions of reeds growing in low light—and partly effect, the way they carry themselves, vertical against the horizontal emphasis of their drowned city. Their skin is pale where it is not stained: stained rust-red from the mineral waters, stained green-black from algal processing, stained the particular blue-gray of the oxidation products that bloom on the oldest metal surfaces. These are not marks of occupation so much as *accumulations*, the body's record of its environment, and a Korodi's staining pattern is read by others the way one might read a curriculum vitae elsewhere.

Their clothing is ceramic fiber, for the most part, woven from processed algal matter and mineral binders into fabrics that resist the rot that claims everything else. It drapes rather than fits, hanging in folds and layers that maximize surface area for evaporation, that channel water downward, that create microclimates of relative dryness against the skin. The clothing is old, maintained, patched, re-woven; a Korodi might wear a garment that contains material from fifty predecessors, its fibers carrying the mineral record of their lives. New clothing is rare, difficult to produce, and generally marks either extreme wealth or extreme disgrace—the latter because it means the wearer has lost their inheritance, has no accumulated fabric to draw upon, has been *cut from the vertical*.

They move slowly. This is the first thing an outsider notices, and the last thing they truly understand. The Korodi do not hurry because hurry is *inefficient* in an environment where all processes are slow, where the water moves at its own pace and cannot be rushed, where the machines operate on cycles measured in seasons and the fastest way to destroy something is to demand speed from it. But there is more to it than efficiency. The slowness is *physiological*, generations of selection for metabolisms that function in the warm damp, that do not waste energy on rapid response, that *persist* rather than *perform*. A Korodi walking through the city moves at the speed of water finding its level, of travertine accreting, of the cloud ceiling's slow brightening and dimming. To see them is to see time made visible.

---

**The Body in Slow Time**

They breathe differently. The air in Veth-Korod is dense, oxygen-poor relative to its moisture content, and the Korodi have developed what their own physiologists (a role that overlaps with hydrologists, geothermalists, the keepers of machine health) call *depth-breathing*: slow inhalations that take ten seconds or more, that fill not just the lungs but the secondary cavities that have developed in some lineages, membranous spaces in the torso that store and process air on extended cycles. A Korodi at rest appears almost not to breathe; a Korodi exerting themselves breathes with the sound of water moving through pipes, deep and rhythmic and *present*.

They see differently. The diffuse light, the absence of shadow, the perpetual mist-film on every surface—these have shaped visual systems that prioritize luminosity variation over contrast, that are sensitive to subtle gradations of reflective quality, that can read the wetness of stone, the mineral saturation of water, the approach of the condensation towers' cycle-change in the quality of ambient vibration carried on the air. Korodi artists, when they make art, work in textures and reflective properties rather than color or form; a "painting" might be a travertine panel polished to varying degrees, that reveals itself only as the light changes, as the viewer moves, as the moisture in the air shifts its refractive index.

They hear differently, *must* hear differently, in the deafening city. The Korodi have not developed deafness—evolution does not work so directly, and the sound is wrong for that, too low and too variable and too geologically grounded. Instead they have developed *resonance recognition*, the capacity to parse the dense acoustic environment into functional components, to track the Pumping House's sub-bass while attending to a conversation, to hear the condensation towers' cycle-change as *information* rather than *interruption*. The strange resonances, the city-singing, the bones-talking—these they hear most acutely, and respond to most variably. Some find them beautiful, the true music of Veth-Korod. Others find them terrifying, evidence of processes beyond comprehension. Most find them *significant*, marks of the city's attention or mood or health, and adjust their behavior accordingly.

**The Structure of Lives**

Birth among the Korodi is a hydrological event. The pregnant woman moves to lower ground as the term progresses, following the water table's pull, and births in the presence of flowing water—ideally in one of the larger channels, supported by her kin, the infant emerging into the sound and the wet and the mineral-tang of their environment. The first sound they hear is the city; the first thing they feel, after the shock of air, is water running over their skin. The birth channel is marked, its location recorded in the vertical records; the child is *of* that water, that flow, that particular mineral signature, and this origin determines certain rights and obligations throughout their life.

Childhood is long, attenuated, slow in ways that would seem cruel or neglectful elsewhere. Children are not educated so much as *accommodated*, brought into the city's rhythms gradually, allowed to develop the physiological adaptations that cannot be taught. They play in the water channels, learning to read current and mineral content and temperature variation. They sit in the industrial installations, learning to feel the machines' cycles in their bodies, to recognize the difference between a healthy rhythm and a distressed one. They are given small responsibilities— tending a section of algal tank, monitoring a minor channel, running messages between the districts where the elevated rails do not reach—and these responsibilities expand slowly, across decades, as their capacity expands.

There is no sharp transition to adulthood. Instead there is a series of *admittances*: to the deeper levels of machinery, to the records and their interpretation, to the sexual and reproductive possibilities of the community, to the full participation in the city's slow economy. A Korodi might be seventy, eighty, a hundred years old before they are fully adult by their own accounting, and even then the category is less fixed than *ongoing*. One does not stop growing into the city.

Work, for the Korodi, is not separate from life. This is not the romantic integration of pre-industrial labor, the farmer whose work is their world; it is something stranger, the product of an environment where all activity is shaped by the same slow constraints, where the distinction between productive and unproductive time has never been meaningfully established. A Korodi might spend a day moving water through a channel system, another day maintaining their clothing, another day in what seems like pure contemplation, listening to a machine's cycle, another day in sexual activity that is also social activity that is also, in certain lineages, a form of information exchange through chemical signals carried in the dense air. These are not different *kinds* of time; they are different *phases* of the same continuous process, the same slow metabolism that characterizes the city itself.

Death is hydrological too. The dead are not buried—that would require digging below the water table, disturbing the city's flows. They are not burned—the fuel is too precious, the heat too disruptive to the local microclimates. They are *dissolved*, placed in the deep thermal springs where the water is acidic, where the travertine-depositing process runs in reverse, where organic matter becomes mineral matter becomes part of the city's substrate. The location of dissolution is recorded; the mineral record is tracked; in certain lights, in certain seasons, the living can read their dead in the stone's coloration, the water's taste, the particular resonance of a channel where someone they knew has become part of the flow.

**The Social Geometry**

Korodi society is organized around the vertical, but not in the simple way of wealth above and poverty below. The vertical is *temporal* as much as it is spatial. Higher ground is older ground, settled first, built upon longest, containing the deepest accumulations of history, of fabric, of the mineral records of lives. To live high is to live in *depth time*, surrounded by the physical evidence of generations, participating in processes that extend furthest into the past and are projected furthest into the future. The highest families are not wealthy because they control resources—though they do—but because they *are* resources, living repositories of the city's accumulated pattern, their decision-making shaped by the weight of what they carry.

Lower ground is newer, more recently settled, more directly engaged with the industrial processes that sustain survival. The lowest districts, perpetually damp, periodically flooded, are where new arrivals come—there are always new arrivals, drawn by rumor or desperation or the strange resonance that the city projects across the surrounding highlands—where adaptation happens most rapidly, where the physiological changes are most pronounced, where life is hardest and most direct and least mediated by accumulated pattern. The lowest Korodi are not despised; they are *read* as temporary, as in process, as not yet fully entered into the city's temporal depth. Some rise; some do not; some find their own depth in the lowest places, developing forms of knowledge and practice that the high families lack, that the city needs, that create their own forms of temporal accumulation.

Between high and low, the middle districts are the realm of *negotiation*, of lateral connection, of the city's actual day-to-day functioning. Here the water managers allocate flow, the machine-tenders coordinate cycles, the record-keepers track positions and obligations and the slow changes in the city's pattern. The middle is not a class in the conventional sense; it is *everyone*, everywhere, the space of function that connects the vertical extremes. A Korodi might be high in their family line and spend their days in the middle, negotiating; might be low by origin and find function that places them temporarily high; the system is not fluid exactly—nothing in Veth-Korod is fluid in that sense—but it is *responsive*, shaped by the same slow adjustments that characterize everything else.

The fundamental social unit is not the family, not exactly. It is the *flow-kin*, those who share a dissolved dead, whose mineral traces run through the same channels, whose obligations to each other are mediated by the water that carries their ancestors. Flow-kin may be spatially separated, vertically distant, functionally unrelated; they recognize each other by taste—literally, tasting the water of their origin, the mineral signature of their dissolution channel—and by the resonance patterns that develop in those who share such origins. The flow-kin are responsible for each other's accommodation in the city's pattern, for ensuring that no one is cut from the vertical entirely, that the accumulated fabric continues, that the dead are properly dissolved and their traces properly tracked. This is not love, not exactly; it is *continuity*, the social form of the geological processes that underlie everything.

**Language and Thought**

The Korodi language—there is no other in Veth-Korod, though visitors bring their own and sometimes leave traces—reflects the city's temporal and acoustic character. Nouns are classified not by gender or animacy but by *cycle-duration*: the words for things that operate on human timescales, the words for things that operate on machine timescales, the words for things that operate on geological timescales, with a fourth category for things whose timescale is unknown or variable. The Pumping House is in the machine class when discussed as a functioning entity, in the geological class when discussed as a structure, and occasionally—when the strange resonances are particularly active—in the unknown class, a shift that causes visible discomfort.

Verbs encode not tense but *phase*: where a process is in its cycle, rather than when in absolute time it occurs. This is not because the Korodi lack temporal awareness—far from it, their temporal awareness is extraordinarily developed—but because absolute time is less *functional* in their environment than relative position in ongoing process. To say that something *happened* is to say where it was in its cycle; the absolute location in the past is derivable, secondary, often irrelevant.

The most complex linguistic structures involve sound description. The Korodi have what might be called a *sophisticated untranslatability* around acoustic experience, a dense vocabulary for the city's sonic environment that maps poorly onto any other language's categories. There are words for the Pumping House's sub-bass that include its felt quality, its propagation path through the travertine, its phase relationship to other machine sounds, its harmonic interaction with the condensation towers, and its *significance*—whether it indicates normal function, approaching maintenance need, or the unknown condition that demands attention without specifying what kind. A single word, untranslatable, containing what would require paragraphs elsewhere. The Korodi think in these words, parse their environment in these terms, and find the languages of visitors *thin*, *flat*, *dry*—accurate in their judgment, however insulting it may be intended.

Writing exists, but it is not primarily visual. The Korodi maintain records in *texture*, travertine panels polished to varying degrees, their patterns of reflectivity encoding information that is read by touch in certain lights, by sight in certain angles, by the sound of water moving across their surface. The deepest records are in the city's stone itself, the accumulated mineral traces of generations, read by those trained to interpret them, a geological palimpsest that is also a history. The highest family records are not maintained so much as *grown*, travertine deposited in controlled conditions over centuries, their information content increasing with their mass, their reading requiring both physical engagement and the temporal patience that defines everything.

**The Strange Within**

I have described the Korodi as adapted, as shaped, as *of* their environment. But the environment is not merely physical, not merely acoustic and hydrological and thermal. The strange resonances, the city-singing, the bones-talking—these have their effects too, and the Korodi are not immune.

There are those who hear the strange more clearly than others, who *track* it, who develop what might be called relationship with it. These individuals are not shamans exactly—the role does not map cleanly—nor are they priests, nor mad, though they may seem so to outsiders. They are *attuned*, the Korodi say, *deep listeners*, and their function in the society is variable and contested.

Some deep listeners serve as machine-tenders of a special kind, attending to the industrial installations not through their conventional cycles but through their resonance interactions, identifying problems before they manifest in measurable malfunction, predicting maintenance needs through the pattern of strange sounds. These are valued, if slightly feared; their knowledge is practical but its source is obscure, and practical knowledge from obscure sources requires careful social management.

Other deep listeners withdraw, finding the conventional patterns of Korodi life insufficient to what they hear. They occupy the lowest districts, the most recently flooded areas, the spaces where the city is most obviously *becoming*, and there they wait, and listen, and occasionally produce *utterances*—sounds, writings in texture, behaviors—that are recorded, debated, rarely understood. These are not prophets; they do not claim to speak for the city, only to *transduce* what they hear. Some are absorbed back into the flow of ordinary life; some dissolve themselves prematurely, seeking to become part of the city's geological record while still living; some simply continue, listening, the strangest inhabitants of an already strange place.

And there are effects that do not map onto individuals at all, that manifest in the pattern of the city itself. The strange resonances correlate, statistically, with certain hydrological events, certain machine behaviors, certain physiological changes in the population. The correlation is not consistent enough to be predictive, not absent enough to be dismissed. There are districts where the population's skin-staining shifts toward patterns not explicable by known occupation or environment. There are channels where the water tastes different, mineral-Signature altered in ways that do not match any identifiable source. There are births—rare, always recorded with particular care—of infants whose depth-breathing is immediately developed, whose resonance-recognition appears pre-tuned to frequencies not yet audible, whose origin-channels carry signatures that do not match their recorded dissolution-lineage.

The Korodi do not deny these phenomena. They *accommodate* them, as they accommodate everything, working them into the city's pattern without fully understanding them, allowing the unknown its place in the slow cycle of known process. The city sings, and they listen, and they continue, and in that continuation is the only response they have found, the only one their environment permits, the only one that does not, in the long run, dissolve into noise.

---

**The Visitor's Eye**

To see the Korodi from outside is to misperceive them, necessarily. They are not stoic, though they seem so; their emotional range is wide and deeply expressed, but in registers that visitors miss—the quality of a breath's depth, the pattern of a pause's duration, the mineral-taste of shared water, the resonance-harmony of a response to the city's song. They are not primitive, though their technology operates on timescales that make it seem so; their understanding of thermodynamics, of geochemistry, of the long-term behavior of complex systems, exceeds in depth what most technologically advanced societies achieve in breadth. They are not trapped, though they cannot leave; the concept of leaving has little meaning in an environment where identity is constituted by relationship to place and flow, where a Korodi removed from Veth-Korod would be not exiled but *unmade*, dissolved in a different way than the thermal springs achieve.

What they are, most essentially, is *present*: present in their environment in a way that has become indistinguishable from identity, present in time in a way that has become indistinguishable from being, present to each other through media that do not separate connection from physical engagement. They are the inhabitants of the caldera, the operators of its machines, the continuers of its flows, the listeners to its song. They are wet and slow and stained and resonant and strange and utterly, completely, necessarily *here*.

And they are changing. Everything in Veth-Korod changes, on its own timescales, in its own patterns. The geothermal activity that warms the caldera is not infinite; the water table shifts; the cloud ceiling varies over centuries in ways that are only beginning to be tracked. The Korodi adapt, as they have always adapted, their physiological and social forms adjusting to the city's adjustments, their slow metabolism finding new equilibria. Whether this continuity can persist, whether the strange within the city is signal or noise, whether the deep listeners hear something real or generate what they seem to perceive—these are questions that the Korodi do not ask in forms that translate, that their environment does not permit to be answered quickly, that will be resolved, if at all, in the only timescale that matters to them: the long, wet, warm, deafening, slow time of the city itself, dripping and grinding and waiting and continuing in the high place where the caldera holds the sky.
