At first you might think the workers are human, or were, or remember being. You find the boot, the hand, and you project onto them—the loneliness of abandoned labor, the tragedy of industry consuming its own. This is incorrect. The boot is not evidence of departure. It is a stage. The hand holding the tool that is also a larval stage: this is not a worker interrupted mid-task but a worker *continuing*, transforming into the next necessary form, the way a caterpillar does not abandon its body but reconfigures it entirely while remaining, in some sense, itself.

The workers are the moths are the factories are the workers.

Consider: a moth emerges from ore-silk, its wings wet with mineral liquor, and the patterns there are not merely schematics for machines. They are schematics for *becoming*. The moth does not read its own wings—blind, mostly, navigating by humidity-gradient and the electric breath of deep engines—but something reads. The condensation reads. The young basalt, still screaming with its birth-heat, receives the pattern in its glassy surface and remembers a future where it is weathered, where it has become something else. The stone learns its own aging from the moth-wing, and in learning it, begins to perform it.

This is the labor. This is the work that continues.

The scattered buildings that are also occasional artifacts: they are not abandoned. They are *inhabited by their own potential*.
