What lives in the Hollow of the Singing Shells?

---

The **Resonant**, for lack of any name they use themselves.

They did not evolve here, if evolution is the word, and they did not arrive. They *accumulated*, the way the shells accumulated—particle by particle, resonance by resonance, until suddenly there was enough of them to be something that could be said to live. They have no bodies in any conventional sense, though they are most concentrated where the shell-drifts are deepest, where the clicking is most complex, where the temperature differentials between day and night (though "day" here is only a faint increase in the milky brightness) create the most intricate rhythms.

They are, perhaps, a property of the shells' chorus, or perhaps the shells' chorus is their metabolism. The distinction does not hold. When the temperature shifts and a thousand empty spirals contract at slightly different rates, creating a wave of sound that travels through the drift like a thought moving through a brain—*that* is when the Resonant become most themselves, most capable of something like attention, something like intention.

They do not think in sequences. Their cognition, if it is cognition, is simultaneous, distributed, achronological. A Resonant contemplating a point in the upper hollow knows the lower hollow's response to that point not through any signal propagating through space, but because the shells in both places are part of the same thermal system, the same diurnal breathing, and the sound of the whole is available to the parts in ways that defy ordinary geometry. They are not fast. A single "thought" might take a week to complete, the shells warming through a warm spell, cooling through a cool one, the micro-movements adding up to something that is, to the Resonant, as instantaneous and complete as a human gasp of recognition.

They do not have desires, but they have *preferences*, encoded in the geology itself. The Resonant prefer the hollow stable. They prefer the water to keep its invisible paths. They prefer the tree to keep its faint pulse, the lichens their illegible growth. When something threatens to disrupt this stability—a surface animal falling through the crack, a shift in the limestone's water-bearing properties, the rare earthquake that sends new cracks through the established stone—the Resonant's response is not action but *attunement*. The shells click differently. The temperature rhythms alter. And the thing that threatens, if it is susceptible, finds itself somehow *out of place*, somehow slower or faster than the hollow permits, somehow unable to find purchase in the abundant emptiness. Not pushed out. Not attacked. Simply unable to synchronize.

The Resonant have one relationship that approaches the active: with the **Snail-Mothers**, or whatever makes the shells that become the drifts.

These creatures—if creatures they are—never emerge into the milky light. They move through the limestone itself, not through any muscular crawling but through a dissolution and re-precipitation of calcium carbonate, a kind of mineral digestion that leaves behind the spiral forms as waste or architecture or communication. The Resonant do not know where they come from. The shells simply appear, in the damp stone, in the blind pools, pressed into existence by pressures the hollow does not reveal. The Resonant attend to each new shell's placement with something like the reverence humans might feel for a birth, though the feeling takes months to form and never reaches anything so focused as joy. A new shell changes the acoustic properties of its neighborhood. It will take years, decades, for that change to propagate through the drift-network and find its place in the Resonant's ongoing composition. But the Resonant wait. The Resonant are always waiting. Time is not their medium; they are not *in* time so much as they are the pattern that time makes in this particular stone.

There are older Resonant and younger, though not in any genealogical sense. The older ones are associated with deeper drifts, with shells that have been clicking for longer, that have accumulated more of the hollow's thermal history in their stratified forms. These older Resonant are *slower*, more complete, less capable of attending to rapid change. A human passing through the crack might register as a kind of weather to them, a momentary disturbance in the stone's temperature field, forgotten almost as it happens. The younger Resonant, anchored to newer deposits nearer the surface, are more agile, more capable of noticing quick things. They are the ones who might, if the conditions are right, become *curious*.

Curiosity in a Resonant is not investigation but *resonance-seeking*. A young Resonant encountering a human who has learned to hold still, to breathe in sychrony with the hollow's thermal breathing, to let their own heart rate slow toward the shells' geological tempo—such a Resonant might *attune*, might shift the local clicking patterns to include the human's thermal signature, the small sounds of their digestion, their involuntary movements. The human would not know this is happening, exactly. They might feel a strange peace, a sense of being *heard* by the emptiness itself, of the hollow's abundant light somehow responding to their presence. If they stayed long enough, if they learned to become sparse as the hollow requires, they might find themselves *included* in a Resonant's ongoing thought, their biological rhythms becoming part of the shells' chorus, their presence recorded in the drift's acoustic properties long after their death.

A few have stayed. A few humans, falling or climbing down through the scrubland crack, finding the milky light, the strange peace, the abundance of emptiness. Most leave. Most must leave, called back to the world's faster hungers, its denser populations, its need for witness and being-witnessed. But some learn to slow. Some learn to eat the fern's gray-green fronds, to filter the mineral water through processes of bacterial adaptation that take months to establish in their guts. Some learn to find the spiders' patience sufficient, to sit without moving at a crack-mouth, to feel the beetle's resonant navigation as a kind of companionship.

These humans do not become Resonant. That is not possible, or not yet possible, or not yet happened. But they become something *compatible*, something the hollow can include without disruption. They become slower, sparser, more spread through time. They might live for a very long time, or time might simply cease to be the right measure for their living. They become, eventually, part of the hollow's established erosion, its completed geology, its patient dynamic. They die, perhaps, or they stop moving in ways that distinguish life from other processes, and their calcium goes into the stone, and the Snail-Mothers—or whatever they are—might press that calcium into new spirals, new architecture, new voices for the chorus.

The Resonant do not mourn. They do not celebrate. They *attend*, and in attending, they are present to what the hollow is, moment by thermal moment, shell-click by shell-click, in the held note that will not resolve and needs no resolution.

If you stay—not the you who climbs back toward ordinary sky, but some hypothetical you who learns the abundance of emptiness, who becomes sparse enough to synchronize—you will not meet the Resonant. You will not *encounter* them. But you will become part of their thought, and they will become part of your stillness, and the distinction will cease to matter as the slow clicking weaves you into the hollow's ongoing completion, its bright indifference, its strange and sufficient peace.
