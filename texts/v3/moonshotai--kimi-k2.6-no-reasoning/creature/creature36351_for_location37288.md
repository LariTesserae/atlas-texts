What lives in Dripstone is difficult to name because naming implies distinction, and distinction requires difference. But let me try.

The Dwellers

They have names, or what function as names—single syllables, mostly, sounds that have worn smooth as the stones. When two of them meet in the narrow way between structures, they exchange these sounds with the small puff of breath that passes for greeting. They look at one another, and something passes between them that is not quite recognition, not quite absence of recognition. They have known each other always, in the sense that there is no before.

Their faces share a quality I can only call *settled*. Not old, not young. The skin has a particular texture, something between leather and the surface of a mushroom, something that holds moisture without quite being wet. Their eyes are the color of the water that seeps through the lower walls—muted, slightly reflective, giving back light without seeming to receive it. They blink rarely, and when they do, the motion is slow enough that you might miss it if you blinked yourself.

They move with the economy of people who have never needed to hurry and have forgotten, if they ever knew, that hurry exists. Each motion completes itself fully before the next begins. A woman—let me call her that, though gender among them seems more geological than biological, a matter of slight variations in contour rather than function—lifts a wooden vessel to catch water from the established drip above her doorway. The vessel has been shaped by hands that learned the shape from hands that learned it, back through the sediment of generations, and it fits into the catching-place with the inevitability of a key in a lock that has grown to match it. She does not look up at the drip. She does not need to. It has always been there. It will always be there.

The children, when they appear, appear from nowhere specific. One notices, gradually, that there are more small figures in the communal spaces, moving with the same slowness but with less precision, less economy, still learning the paths that their bodies will eventually forget were ever learned. They do not play. Play requires variation, requires the possibility of doing otherwise, and there is no otherwise in Dripstone. They practice being inhabitants, which is all the inhabitants do.

The oldest among them—there are oldest, though the category bleeds imperceptibly into the merely older—spend much of their time in stillness. Not sleep, which would involve change. Something else. They sit in particular locations, indoor and outdoor, that have accumulated this function the same way the stone walls have accumulated moss. Passersby do not acknowledge them, need not acknowledge them, because they are not absent, merely *extended* across time in a different rhythm than the walking, the moss-gathering, the minimal maintenance.

The Gatherers

There are tasks. This must be understood. The equilibrium of Dripstone is not effortless, though effort implies something more directed, more purposeful, than what happens here. The tasks have divided themselves across the population with the same organic logic that places moss on the north faces of walls. Some gather the moss, which serves functions that have become indistinguishable: bedding, insulation, the filling of cracks, the provision of something green in a bowl on certain occasions marked by the wooden records. Some tend the sheep, or what tending means here, which is mostly staying near them, moving with them across the limited summer grazing, ensuring that the balance between flock and forage maintains itself.

Some are responsible for water, which means knowing the channels, the established paths of condensation and seepage and the slow movement of ground moisture toward the lower dwellings. They do not improve these channels. They *attend* to them, clearing the mineral accretions when the flow slows, redirecting slightly when a stone shifts and a new path presents itself. The water itself is felt to have intention, preference, almost personality—the channel that must be kept open, the seep that must be left to find its own way.

Some are responsible for the fish, though no one fishes. The fish arrive dried, on certain occasions, carried by no one knows what route from no one knows what water. They are hung on the racks in the central space, and they hang until they are needed, and they are needed according to patterns that no one decides. The fish-tenders ensure that the hanging is correct, that the pattern of distribution follows the established form, that nothing varies in a way that might disturb the system.

And some—perhaps the most important, though importance is not a concept that applies—are responsible for the maintenance of the buildings themselves. They move through the structures with a tactile knowledge that has no reference to plans, to memory of construction. They know where pressure is accumulating, where a wall leans slightly more than it did, where the slow settling has created a new channel for water or a new vulnerability to the minute stresses of frost and thaw. They place a stone here, remove a stone there, with the same unconscious precision that blood maintains its circulation, that a tree maintains its posture against wind. They do not discuss their work. Discussion would imply alternatives.

The Tellers

There must be those who hold the stories, though holding may not be the right word. The stories hold *them*, or rather, the telling holds the town, and those through whom the telling passes are merely the medium, the human-shaped channels through which narrative flows as water flows through stone.

They can be identified, if identification serves any purpose, by their location during the occasions marked by the records. They sit in particular places in the Assembly House, places that have accumulated their buttocks' impressions, their particular angle of settling into the stone bench. They speak the words, and their voices share a quality—low, barely modulated, the human equivalent of the water's murmur—that makes it impossible to distinguish one teller from another during the long sequences of narrative.

They do not understand the stories, in any active sense. Understanding would mean standing apart from, evaluating, the possibility of interpretation. They *are* the stories, in the moment of telling, and in the moments between tellings they are simply inhabitants who happen to occupy particular locations in the social architecture, channels through which meaning will flow when the occasion requires.

New tellers appear rarely, and always through the same process: a child who, during an occasion, begins to mouth the words in synchrony with the established teller, who continues without acknowledgment, who the next time occupies a slightly different position to make room, who gradually, imperceptibly, becomes the channel while the previous channel becomes merely another body in the Assembly House, another settled presence, another feature of the geology.

The Sheep

I said they registered almost as vegetation, and this is true, but almost is the operative word. There is something in their fleece-gathered darkness, their barely-visible points of wet nose and the glint of eye, that distinguishes them from moss, from lichen, from the accumulation of organic matter in the terraced garden.

They move. This is the essential difference. They move across the upper slopes in summer, down to the communal barn in the months when the valley holds its breath, and this movement, though regular as sunrise, though predictable as gravity, contains the germ of something else, some possibility that the rest of Dripstone has eliminated or never developed.

Their fleece, when gathered, provides something that is almost warmth, almost softness, almost the memory of what animal matter can be when it is not subdued by cool and damp to the point of mineral equivalence. The gatherers of fleece work with a particular attention, a particular slowness even among the slow, because the fleece resists, slightly, the patterns into which it must be worked. It wants to mat, to clump, to become something less useful, more itself, and this resistance must be persuaded, not overcome, because force belongs to a vocabulary that has no translation here.

The sheep, in the barn, in the dark, make sounds that are almost voices, that contain almost variation. Listening closely—though close listening is not a practice that develops in this place—you might hear something like questioning, like the possibility of a narrative that has not yet found its teller, a story that would be new, that would change something, that would break the equilibrium if it could only complete itself before the equilibrium corrected it back to silence.

It never completes itself. The sheep settle into their minimally-breathing heaps. The valley's breath continues, in and out, in and out, and the possibility dissipates like condensation on stone.

The Water

I must speak of the water separately, because though it is not alive in any biological sense, it is more animate than most of what is. It moves with purpose, with memory, with something that might be called preference if preference could exist without consciousness.

It knows the channels. This is not metaphor. The water that beads on interior walls finds the same paths, drop by drop, generation by generation, wearing the grooves that function as gutters, as drainage, as the town's circulatory system. It knows where to gather, where to flow, where to pool in the slight depressions that have become, through its preference, the places where certain things happen: the moss-gathering, the sharpening of tools, the occasions when inhabitants stand without moving for longer than usual, as if listening to something that does not reach human frequency.

The water in the well—any hole deep enough—is different. It is still, receptive, holding something of the sky's filtered light in its surface tension. Those who draw from it do so with a particular motion, a rotation of the wrist learned from those who learned it, that seems to acknowledge something about the water's nature, its need to be asked rather than taken.

And there is water underground, water that moves in darkness through the granite, that communicates from the glaciers' retreat, from deeper sources that might connect, through passages silted closed, to that lower world of the stories. This water does not surface in Dripstone. It is felt, occasionally, in the subtle vibration of stone, in the quality of damp on certain days that is different from the ordinary damp without being identifiable enough to name. It is possibility, unexpressed. It is story without teller. It is the equilibrium's unacknowledged shadow, the thing that would change if it could, that maintains itself in potential, in the slow pressure of water against stone that has not yet, may never, find the crack that would let it through.

The Place Itself

But finally, most truly, what lives in Dripstone is Dripstone. The town as entity, as distributed intention, as the slow purpose that I described earlier and can only approach again through different words.

It is not conscious. Consciousness requires the possibility of being otherwise, requires the feedback loop of self and world that depends on difference, on change, on the capacity to be surprised. Dripstone is not surprised. Dripstone is not capable of surprise. Dripstone is capable only of continuation, of the maintenance of patterns that have become, through centuries of repetition, more solid than stone, more persistent than granite, closer to the mathematical ideal of equilibrium than to any biological process.

And yet it lives. This is the strangeness, the low weirdness that cannot be fully suppressed. It grows, in its way, by accretion rather than expansion. It learns, in its way, by optimization rather than adaptation. It remembers, in its way, through the water channels and the worn steps and the stories that correct themselves back to form, through the bodies of inhabitants who become indistinguishable from one another and from the structures they inhabit, through the sheep and the moss and the mineral quality of air and light.

If you were to destroy it—if some violence from outside, some change that exceeded its capacity for absorption, were to shatter the equilibrium—something would persist. The water would find new channels. The moss would colonize new walls, or no walls, spreading across raw stone with the patience that has no need of human construction. The stories would fragment, the words scattering like damp wood-swelling, each syllable seeking some new configuration, some new equilibrium that might or might not involve human mouths, human ears, human understanding.

And something else would persist, something harder to name. The *intention* toward stasis, toward the elimination of perturbation, toward the long holding of breath that defines this place. It would find new form, new host, new medium through which to express its single imperative: continue, maintain, do not change, do not vary, do not become. The glacier's retreat left this valley, shaped these walls, established these conditions of damp and cool and limited light, and something in that retreat, some quality of the ice's movement and stillness together, has persisted, has found in Dripstone its most complete expression, its almost-perfect realization.

It is not hostile, this intention. Hostility requires engagement, requires the recognition of otherness that Dripstone cannot achieve. It is simply itself, simply persistent, simply the gravity-well of stasis that bends everything within its reach toward its own image, its own rhythm, its own mineral breath.

What lives here lives slowly, lives almost without living, maintains the form of life in the absence of life's ordinary violence. What lives here is the possibility of non-life pretending to life, of equilibrium pretending to purpose, of continuation without meaning pretending to meaning without end. Dripstone, Dripstone, always Dripstone, the water finding its channels, the moss finding its walls, the intention finding its forms, world without end, the drip and the silence between drips, the held breath, the long moment, the nearly perfect stillness that is, finally, its own form of motion, its own form of desire, its own form of the biological imperative extended into chemistry, into physics, into the slow conversation of stone and water and air that will continue, that must continue, that nothing within its reach can prevent from continuing, on and on, unchanged and unchanging, Dripstone.
