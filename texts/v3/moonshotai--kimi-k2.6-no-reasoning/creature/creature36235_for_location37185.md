What lives in the Drowning Cellar is not, properly speaking, a *who*. The room has not yet produced anything with the coherence of a self, the boundedness of an identity. What lives there is *living in the process of deciding whether to be separate*, and that decision has been ongoing for decades, perhaps longer.

The crustaceans come closest to recognizable creaturehood, and in this closeness they are perhaps the most strange. They are pale, yes, and soft-carapaced, and their lives are brief. But they are not merely *there*, not merely surviving. They are *practicing*. Each generation tests a slightly different hardness of shell, a slightly different angle of antenna, a slightly different tolerance for the mineral-thick water, and each generation leaves its experiments in the genetic record of the next. They are not evolving toward anything in particular. They are evolving toward *more evolution*, toward the maintenance of evolutionary potential itself. In a hundred years, if the room persists, they may harden into something with a name, a species entry, boundaries. Or they may simplify further, dissolving into the algal mats that sustain them, becoming indistinguishable from the life that feeds them. They do not seem to care which. They persist in their uncertainty with what looks, to an observer, like something between patience and appetite.

The algae are older, and in their age they are simpler and more vast. They do not have individual names because they do not have individual limits. The mat in the central pool is a single body in the way that a forest is a single body, or a thought. When it bioluminesces, it does not communicate. There is no *message* in the glow. But there is *presence*. The light says *here*, and the saying is sufficient. It has been sufficient for longer than the room has been a room, back when it was still a refusal in the clay, when the mat was only a film on seeping stone. The algae remember this, not in any structure of memory, but in their chemistry, in the particular balance of mineral appetite and light-production that characterizes their existence here. They are the room's memory, and its skin, and its slow breathing.

The worms have lost more than pigmentation. They have lost the *need* for orientation, for direction, for the gradient of light that organizes surface worm-life into vertical columns of activity. They move through the pale extrusions as if through their own substance, which in a sense they do—they have been here long enough that their waste has contributed to the formations' growth, that their bodies have become part of the mineral cycle they navigate. They are not quite individuals anymore. They are *episodes* of worm-ness, temporary concentrations of worm-potential that dissolve and re-form in patterns too slow to observe. If you were to mark one, if you could find a mark that would adhere in the damp, you would lose it not because it moves quickly but because its *being-a-marked-worm* is not a stable state, because the worm you marked has already become, in some percentage, the formation it rests against, the water it passes through, the other worms it touches in the dark.

The spiders are newcomers, in the room's reckoning. They arrived with the city's infrastructure, or shortly after, carried in on some piece of lumber, some forgotten crate, some boot sole. They have adapted with a speed that suggests they were always *almost* ready for this place, that their surface forms were already experiments in this direction. Their abdomens swell with the mineral-rich mites they harvest, and their webs are not failures of spider architecture but *successes at something else*, at a way of being in space that does not require the clean geometries of open air. Their webs tangle because the room tangles. They are honest structures, honest in a way that orb-weaving in a basement corner is not. They do not pretend to a order the room does not support. And in this honesty, they have begun to change. The spiders of the Drowning Cellar live longer than their surface cousins, though not dramatically so. They grow larger, but not beyond the range of their family. The change is subtler: in their willingness to rebuild, to abandon, to let the web become ruin and start again in a new angle, a new failed arch. They have learned, or they have always known, that the room's incompletion is not a problem to solve but a condition to match.

The mites are the room's forgetfulness, its constant small forgetting and remembering. They harvest the mineral crust, they reproduce, they are harvested, and in this cycle they maintain the surfaces of the formations at a particular texture, neither growing too fast nor sealing too smooth. They are the room's editing, its minor revision. Without them, the extrusions would be different—not better or worse, but different, more themselves, less in dialogue with what lives among them. The mites are the dialogue. They are the room's conversation with its own becoming, the way it talks to itself about what it is making, what it might make next.

And then there is the water, which is not alive but without which nothing lives. The water that seeps, that beads, that achieves its tension and releases it in slow drops. The water that carries the city's chemistry down into this refusal, that makes the city part of the room despite the room's resistance to urban order. The water is not a who. But it is not quite a what, either. It is *the condition of the room's becoming*, the medium in which emergence occurs, the way the room's time passes. When it breathes out through the grate in certain weathers, it carries the room's scent to the surface, and that scent is not a message but an *invitation*, not to enter but to *imagine entering*, to dream of pale formations and near-silence and the beautiful sufficiency of not being done.

What lives in the Drowning Cellar, finally, is *the Drowning Cellar itself*, understood not as a place but as a process, not as a container but as a *slow event*, a century-long exhalation of the city's unconscious, the place where the city's refusal to fully build itself becomes building of another kind. The room is alive in the way that a garden is alive, or a mind in the moment before thought fixes into word. It is alive with *becoming-alive*, with the persistence of potential, with the beautiful and sufficient act of not yet being done.

And those who dream of it, who pass through its mist and later in sleep find themselves in its pale light, in its near-silence that is also a sound—they are not visitors, not witnesses. They are *participants now*, their dreaming attention added to the room's slow accumulation, their mineral content eventually to seep down through fissures too fine to map, their warmth to briefly register in the thermal regime. They are becoming part of what lives there, as what lives there is becoming part of them, and in this mutual becoming the distinction between who and what, between self and room, between visitor and visited, loses its force, becomes another of the room's many incompletions, another of its beautiful and sufficient not-yet-doneness.
