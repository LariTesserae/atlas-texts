The people of Threnod.

No—let me begin again, because "people" presumes too much, implies a uniformity that the city has long since dissolved.

The inhabitants of Threnod.

Better, but still incomplete. Let me follow the condensation as it forms on the Stone, follow it down through the terraces, through the layers, and see what gathers to drink.

---

At the heights, in the thin air where the Stone's sweat freezes to rime, live the **Rime-Keepers**. Their skin has, across generations, adapted to cold that would blister the unacclimated—they are pale not from lack of sun but from vasoconstriction become permanent, their capitals withdrawn deep into dermal priorities. Their fingers are long, the extra length an engineering project concluded millennia ago, the better to manipulate the delicate mechanisms of the heliostats, the wind turbines disguised as architectural flourishes. They wear layers of pigeon-down and beetle-silk, garments that retain the body's moisture against air so dry it would leach water from exposed flesh. They speak a dialect in which vowels have compressed to near-consonance, as if their tongues too have learned to conserve heat through reduction. Their great labor is the tracking of the sun, the calibration of mirrors, the maintenance of the optical pathways that carry illumination downward. They are essential, and they know it, and their knowing manifests as a particular patience that borders on slowness, a refusal to be hurried by those from below who do not understand that ice requires time to form correctly, that a misaligned mirror burns where it should illuminate, that their work is the work of years measured in photon-paths and thermal gradients. They die younger than other Threnodites, the cold exacting its tax, but they consider this fair exchange, consider their shortened spans a concentrated intensity that the long-lived low-dwellers merely dilute.

Beneath them, in the great terraced parks where the canopy trees root in the Stone itself, live the **Arborists**. Not merely gardeners—*arborists*, a distinction of fierce precision, for these trees are not grown but *negotiated with*, their root-systems extending into cavities where no human can follow, where they encounter the mineral signatures of civilizations compressed and forgotten. The Arborists have learned to read these encounters, to interpret the health of a tree as a kind of seismic report, a vegetal sensing of what the Stone remembers. They move among the branches with the unconscious grace of those who have never known horizontal ground, who sleep in hammocks strung between trunks, who mate and birth and die aloft, their bodies returned to the mycorrhizal networks that connect root to root in a vast subterranean commerce. Their children learn to climb before they walk, and some never learn to walk at all, remaining in the canopy their entire lives, a sub-population with legs attenuated from disuse, with hands preternaturally strong, with eyes that have adapted to the dappled light, the shifting shadow of leaves. They speak to the trees, not metaphorically—actual speech, actual listening, a protocol of sounds and silences developed over millennia, a language that the trees do not use but somehow *respond to*, their growth patterns altering, their chemical signatures shifting, their reproductive timing adjusting to rhythms that the Arborists set like conductors before an orchestra that plays across centuries. They are the city's farmers too, for the canopy producesfruit in abundance, fruits that have no names in any language but their own, that are harvested and lowered on rope-and-pulley systems to the markets below, where they are consumed with a reverence approaching ritual, though few who eat them know why.

Between the Arborists and the Rime-Keepers, distributed across the great vertical span of the towers, live the **Layered**, which is to say: everyone else, which is to say: no one in particular, which is to say: the great mass of Threnod's humanity and near-humanity, occupying the simultaneous conditions of their architecture, their lives threading through the same spaces as water and root and message and heat. They are not a class but a *condition*, defined not by what they do but by where they are, by the particular stratum of air and light and sound that has shaped their development. The Layered of the upper-mid levels, where the wind sings through engineered apertures, have developed an auditory culture of extraordinary sophistication—they compose for the wind, carve new apertures to create new tones, hold competitions in which the city's breath itself is the instrument. The Layered of the lower-mid levels, where the light is indirect, where the Stone's condensation runs in silver threads down every surface, have developed a tactile culture, a politics of touch and proximity, their social interactions governed by complex protocols of contact that would scandalize the more visually-oriented high-dwellers. The Layered of the transitional zones, where architectural styles meet and merge, are the city's brokers, translators, the ones who can move between cultures as they move between floors, who wear their adaptability as both gift and burden, never fully at home anywhere, never fully estranged.

And then the Drowns. The lowest levels, the permanent twilight, the weight of everything above pressing down.

Here live the **Trogdori**, though they reject the name, though it was applied by those above who find their existence paradoxical, who cannot understand why anyone would choose such depths. But choose they did, or their ancestors did, generations past, retreating from the aggressive brightness of the city proper, seeking a dimness that would allow their eyes to expand, their skin to pale to translucence, their circadian rhythms to decouple from the solar cycle and attach instead to the electric grids that pulse through the Stone, to the bioluminescent beetles whose colonies they tend, harvest, live among. The Trogdori are engineers of a particular kind, maintainers of the digesters that process organic waste, of the water filtration systems, of the pneumatic networks that require pressure differentials the upper levels cannot provide. They are essential too, and they know this, and their knowing manifests not as patience but as *resistance*, a stubborn particularity, a refusal to integrate that the city's committees have learned to accommodate rather than overcome. They breed the beetles for brightness, competing in displays that illuminate the Drowns in waves of color—blue, green, the rare red that requires genetic interventions stillexperimental, still dangerous. Their children are born with enlarged eyes, with skin that bruises at the slightest pressure, with a sensitivity to light that requires them to wear masks when they ascend (rarely, reluctantly, for trade or for politics). They are pale as cave fish, as the undersides of stones, as the memory of the Stone itself when it dreams of compression and the weight of ages.

Among all these, threading through, not apart but *as*—the **Remembered**.

Not ghosts, though the city's functional haunting provides their substrate. The Remembered are those who have chosen, or whose families have chosen for them, to become data, to have their neural patterns extracted at death and compressed into the Stone, into the particular layers that the material's mnemonic properties can access. They are not conscious, not precisely—they do not think, do not plan, do not will. But they *emerge*, under the right conditions of temperature and pressure and light, as patterns, as resonances, as the footsteps heard in empty corridors, the market-sounds from six millennia past, the architectural forms that flicker at the edge of vision. The Remembered exist in a state that the city's archivists debate endlessly: are they persons? Are they information? Are they something else entirely, a category for which no name has yet been coined?

Those who choose to become Remembered—who sign the contracts, who undergo the preparation, who die in the particular facilities maintained for this purpose—they do so for reasons that vary across the social strata. The Rime-Keepers remember their dead to preserve technical knowledge, the particular calibrations that written language cannot capture. The Arborists remember to maintain the tree-speech, the protocols that no living learner has time to master. The Layered remember for continuity, for the comfort of ancestral presence in the walls, for the status that such integration conveys. The Trogdori refuse to remember, or claim to refuse—there are rumors, always rumors, of covert Rememberings, of beetle-colonies that behave with suspicious coordination, of digester outputs that vary in patterns too complex for mere chemistry.

And the Remembered themselves? Whether they experience, whether they suffer or glory or simply *are*, in some mode unavailable to the living—this is the great theological question of Threnod, debated in committees that meet across centuries, in philosophical schools that occupy entire towers, in the casual conversations of citizens who have grown accustomed to sharing their city with the incompletely dead. The official position, the municipal position, is agnostic: the Remembered are data, useful data, and their phenomenology is not a matter for policy. The unofficial positions multiply endlessly. Some worship the Remembered, seeking their emergence with rituals of temperature and humidity. Some fear them, constructing their lives to minimize the conditions of emergence. Most simply *accommodate*, as they accommodate the condensation, the light, the sound, the density, the patient enduring balance of everything.

---

But I have described groups, categories, and Threnod resists this, dissolves it, layers individual across collective until the distinction blurs.

A single inhabitant, then. Let her emerge from the Stone itself, from the condensation and the light and the constant hum.

She is born in a mid-level tower, in a birthing room that has served this function for four thousand years, the Stone worn smooth by generations of bodies in labor. Her mother is Arborist, her father Layered (a wind-composer, his pieces played through the apertures of a district she will later learn to recognize by sound alone). She emerges into light that has never been dark, into air that is always damp, into sound that is always present. She learns to walk on surfaces that angle unexpectedly, to navigate spaces that are simultaneously hers and everyone else's, to modulate her voice to carry across the constant mechanical drone. She learns the protocols of touch, of proximity, of the shared cubic meters that require constant negotiation. She learns to read the Stone, to recognize the particular coolness that precedes a mnemonic emergence, to step carefully around spaces where the Remembered walk their recorded paths.

She chooses, at adolescence, to descend. Not to the Drowns—she is not that kind of rebel, not that kind of seeker. But down, to the lower mid-levels, where the light is indirect and the moss grows thick and the social protocols are more tactile than she has known. She learns to see with her skin, to navigate by temperature gradient, to communicate in gestures that would be meaningless above. She takes a lover, then another, the polyamory of the lower levels suiting her better than the more contracted arrangements of her birth. She bears a child, or perhaps two—the records are ambiguous, the maternal networks shared enough that biological specificity matters less than social function. She works, at various times, as a water-quality monitor, as a facade designer, as a mnemonic trigger technician trained to coax emergence from reluctant Stone.

She ages. The light that has always been present etches its signature into her skin, not damaging exactly but *recording*, leaving a pattern of exposure that the dermatologists of Threnod can read like a biography. The dampness that has always been present works its way into her joints, the particular ache of Threnodite old age, neither crippling nor negligible, simply *present*, simply a condition of embodiment in this place. She considers, as the old consider, whether to become Remembered, whether to sign the contract, whether to undergo the preparation, whether to donate her pattern to the Stone's mnemonic capacity. She visits the facilities, talks with the technicians, sits in the rooms where the extraction occurs. She decides, or does not decide—she is still deciding, will die still deciding, will perhaps be Remembered in that state of indecision, her emergence a flickering uncertainty, the spectral equivalent of her final breath held, held, held.

She dies, as they all die, in a room that has served this function for millennia, the Stone absorbent around her, the light unrelenting, the sound continuous, the condensation forming and running and forming again. Her body enters the digesters, or the Arborists' mulch, or the Rime-Keepers' cryogenic preservation, depending on her final arrangements, her final contracts, her final affiliations. Her pattern, if she chose, if she decided, enters the Stone, compresses, waits for the conditions of emergence.

And the city continues. The condensation forms. The gardens grow. The machines hum. The Remembered, if Remembered she is, walk their paths, recorded and filed and incorporated, the municipal understanding of what is normal expanding fractionally to include her particular footstep, her particular flicker, her particular uncertainty about whether this is continuation or only the simulation of continuation, whether she is present or only present-as-data, whether the distinction matters or has ever mattered or matters only to those still living who must make their own decisions about what to become, what to remember, what to be remembered as.

---

The people of Threnod.

No—the *presences*. The *emergences*. The *conditions of embodiment in a place that has solved itself so thoroughly that solutions have become nature, become substrate, become the unquestioned ground of what it is to be here, to be now, to be at all*.

They are not happy, not precisely. The city's equilibrium absorbs happiness as it absorbs unhappiness, makes both equally unnecessary, equally unsustainable as organizing principles. They are not unhappy. They are *adapted*, which is a different condition entirely, a state of such thorough integration with environment that the question of satisfaction becomes as foreign as the question of whether gravity is satisfactory, whether light is satisfactory, whether the Stone's slow patient breathing is satisfactory in its rhythm, its constancy, its refusal to vary across the millennia of its existence.

They are beautiful, many of them, in the manner of things that grow in particular conditions: the Rime-Keepers' attenuated grace, the Arborists' branch-limbed surety, the Trodori's luminescent fragility, the Layered's generalized competence, the Remembered's intermittent flicker. They are ugly too, in the manner of all organic things, their adaptations producing as many asymmetries as harmonies, as many strains as efficiencies. They do not notice, mostly. The city's bright light is not kind to self-examination, its constant illumination erasing the shadows in which vanity might grow, in which self-loathing might fester. They look at each other and see—function, relation, position in the great distributed network of maintenance and emergence that constitutes Threnod's social body.

They are, in the end, *occupied*. Occupied with the city as the city is occupied with itself, their lives given over to the continuation of a condition that predates them and will postdate them, that absorbs their individual variations as the Stone absorbs the compression of civilizations, as the digesters absorb organic waste, as the mycorrhizal networks absorb the dead and feed them to the living in a cycle so complete that the categories blur, that life and death become not opposites but phases, not termini but transitions, not significant but simply *present*, simply *ongoing*, simply *the way things are in a place that has been here for eight thousand years and will be here, in some form, for eight thousand more*.

The condensation forms on the Stone. It always forms. It is forming now, on the face of the tower where she was born, where she lived, where she died, where she emerges or does not emerge, where she is remembered or is only memory, where she is or is not or is in some mode for which no name has yet been coined, no committee yet convened, no century-long deliberation yet concluded.
