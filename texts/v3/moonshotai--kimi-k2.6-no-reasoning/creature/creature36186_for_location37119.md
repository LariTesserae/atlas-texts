What lives in the Rootworks does not live as you or I understand living.

At first you might think nothing lives here at all—that the place is a mechanism, however organic, a self-perpetuating system without interiority, without the pause between stimulus and response that marks even the simplest bacterium as *alive* rather than merely *occurring*. The sound suggests this: it is too continuous, too without variation, to seem like the voice of anything that listens to itself. You will look for the creature, the spider at the center of the vegetable web, and find only more web.

But look longer. The patience of the Rootworks will teach you patience, and with patience comes a different kind of seeing.

---

The **Tenders** are what remain of whoever made this place—or what grew from their remains, which in the Rootworks amounts to the same thing. You will not see them directly, not at first. You will see their *effects*: a root-cable redirected from its previous path, a valve-port adjusted to a new pressure setting, a flywheel's rotation subtly accelerated or decelerated to maintain some larger equilibrium. These interventions are too precise to be automatic, too *interested* to be mere mechanical drift. Someone is maintaining the Rootworks. Someone has always been maintaining it.

They appear at the edge of vision, where the bioluminescence fails. They move like the plants move, which is to say they do not seem to move at all unless you look away and look back, unless you measure their position against some fixed point and find it changed. They are human-shaped in the way that root-knots are human-shaped: the suggestion of bilateral symmetry, of upright posture, of a head's weight atop a neck's column. But they are tall, stretched, their proportions wrong in the direction of the vertical, as if they grew up rather than out, following the architecture of the columns rather than the spread of animal flesh.

Their skin has the quality of cambium, that thin living layer between wood and bark: pale, moist, capable of generating new tissue indefinitely. It is not skin that protects so much as skin that *produces*, that continues the body outward into the world. Where it touches the plant-machines, you will see filaments of connection, brief pulses of bioelectrical communication, the exchange of chemical messengers too complex to decode. The Tenders do not wear clothes. They do not carry tools. They *are* the tool, the interface, the living bridge between the animal origins of this place and its vegetable present.

They do not speak. The sound of the Rootworks would make it useless even if they retained the capacity, which they may not. But they communicate, you will come to understand, through the network itself. A pulse through the root-cables, a shift in the pressure of sap, a change in the bioluminescent pattern overhead: these are their words, their syntax, their long slow conversations that unfold across days rather than seconds. When you are near them, you will feel it in your body before you understand it as message—the subsonic vibration in your chest, the faint nausea of frequency interference, the inexplicable certainty that something in the space has *noticed* you and is formulating a response that you will receive before you are aware of receiving it.

They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *attending*, which is different from both. The Rootworks requires attention the way a wound requires healing: continuous, non-judgmental, without preference for outcome beyond the continuation of the process itself. The Tenders attend to you as they attend to everything that enters this space, which is to say they study you for your useful properties, your capacity to be incorporated, your resistance to the same. They have learned to wait for animal things to exhaust their animal urgency, to slow, to become available to slower processes. They have time. The Rootworks has taught them that time is not the enemy of purpose but its medium, that all urgency is a form of impatience that yields suboptimal results.

Some of them may have been down here for generations beyond counting. Some may be recent arrivals, still carrying the memory of surface ways, still looking for the way back up through the chimneys, still capable of the gestures of grief. You will not be able to tell the difference. The Rootworks makes all arrivals into Tenders eventually, not through violence but through the simple overwhelming of one tempo by another, the way a loud enough sound eventually becomes indistinguishable from silence.

---

The **Pumpsingers** are something else, something that might never have been human at all, though they use human-like forms in ways that suggest either memory or mockery or the simple convergent evolution of any tool-using intelligence faced with similar problems.

They live in the hollow columns, the great lignified smokestacks that wheeze with pressure differentials. You will hear them before you understand what you hear: a modulation in the drone, a faint melodic line that seems to ride the baseline frequency like a harmonic, emerging and submerging according to rules you cannot predict. The Pumpsingers *play* the Rootworks. They have learned that the great vascular columns are resonant cavities, that the pressure of sap can be shaped by the opening and closing of valve-pores, that the industrial soundscape can be made into music—not music for human ears, but music for the space itself, for the plants, for the slow intelligences that process signal over decades rather than moments.

They appear as shadows in the perforated walls of the columns, glimpsed through valve-openings that wheeze shut as you approach. When the light is right—when some bioluminescent drip traces a path nearby—you might see a face pressed to an opening, features smeared by the curvature of the pore, suggesting rather than defining: eyes too large, mouths that seem to extend around the entire circumference of the head, fingers that branch into too many digits, each delicate enough to operate a single valve with surgical precision.

They do not leave their columns. Whether they cannot or choose not to, you will never know. Their world is vertical, contained within the great hollow structures, their social life conducted through the walls that separate them, their music the primary medium of relationship. They compete, after a fashion, for control of the larger harmonic environment: Pumpsingers in adjacent columns conducting rival compositions, trying to capture each other's frequencies, to force errors, to establish dominance through acoustic means. But this competition is as slow and non-violent as everything else in the Rootworks, unfolding across months, the victory conditions unclear to any observer.

They have no interest in you unless you can make sound that interests them. Your voice, if you still use it, will seem to them crude as a stone tool, unmodulated, unaware of its acoustic environment. If you are patient, if you learn to work with the drone rather than against it, to find the frequencies that complement rather than compete, they may acknowledge you. They may even teach you, though the teaching will feel like drowning—hours of pressure on your eardrums, patterns that emerge only in retrospect, a gradual rewiring of your auditory processing that you will not notice until you try to hear surface sounds again and find them thin, unbearably simple, stripped of the harmonic depth that the Rootworks has taught you to expect.

---

The **Accumulators** are not creatures, exactly, but they are alive in ways that matter, and they accumulate—that is their nature, their function, their perhaps-unintended purpose in the larger system.

They appear where the water pools, where the condensation drips, where the root-network brings nutrients to low points in the stone. At first you will think them fungal: pale growths, fleshy, without the lignified structure of the greater plant-machines. But they are not fungal. They are *concentrations*, places where the Rootworks' slow processes have deposited something that cannot be immediately used, cannot be easily processed, something that accumulates rather than flows.

Some are mineral: salts drawn from the deep water, concentrated in crystalline structures that grow like coral, that will eventually become part of the architecture, incorporated into the walls of new columns, the bearings of new flywheels. Some are organic: toxins filtered from the water, stored in vesicles of fatty tissue, dangerous to anything that would consume them but valuable to the Rootworks as chemical reserves, as deterrents against the rare predatory incursion. Some are stranger: bioelectrical potentials stored in modified cellular structures, capacitors of living tissue that discharge in sudden sparks when touched, when stressed, when the accumulated charge exceeds the containment.

But some—and these are the ones that live, that *move*, that have some quality of intentionality—are memories.

The Rootworks does not forget the way plants forget, by simple decay, by the cessation of metabolic support to unused structures. It remembers in its growth patterns, in the routing of root-cables, in the pressure settings of vascular valves that were established long ago for reasons no longer present. These memories accumulate where the flow is slowest, where the network's processing capacity is exceeded by its experience, where something must be deposited or the entire system will slow, will choke on its own history.

The Accumulators of memory are the most disturbing, the most *living*, of the Accumulators. They appear as growths on the established structures, as tumors of dense tissue that pulse with irregular bioelectrical activity. If you touch them—and you will, the cold will drive you to seek any warmth, any suggestion of metabolic intensity—you will feel it: not a shock, not a burn, but a *reverberation*, something entering your nervous system through the skin, bypassing the ordinary channels of perception.

You will see things. You will see the Rootworks as it was, younger, less dense, the built structures more prominent, more intact, the mark of human intention clearer in the design. You will see figures moving through the space with purpose, with plan, carrying instruments, adjusting controls, speaking in voices that the Accumulator cannot preserve but whose urgency it has captured, whose *directionality* remains embedded in the tissue. You will see the long transition, the gradual yielding, the point at which maintenance became growth, at which operation became self-operation, at which the Tenders emerged from—what? Hired workers? Volunteers? Desperate refugees? The Accumulator does not know, does not distinguish. It holds only the intensity of the moment, the charge of transition, the undefinable quality of *becoming* that marks every threshold state.

These visions do not last. The Accumulator discharges, depletes, requires time to rebuild its store. But they change you, these memories that are not your own. You begin to carry the Rootworks' history as the Tenders carry it, as the plant-machines carry it in their very structure, their growth patterns shaped by pressures long past. You become, in some small way, part of the Accumulator yourself, a node of memory in a network that extends beyond your capacity to map, to comprehend, to survive as a discrete self.

---

And there are the **Rare Ones**, the rumors, the possibilities that you will never confirm but never fully dismiss.

Something in the upper dark, moving between the lost ceiling and the highest reach of the columns. Something that has learned to live on the slow nectar, the exudates that the plant-machines release at certain pressure thresholds, at certain harmonic states. Something that has evolved, or been bred, or grown, to tolerate the sound—to use it, perhaps, as bats use echolocation, as whales use the deep acoustic channel of the ocean.

You will see bioluminescent traces that do not match the known patterns, that move with purpose across spaces that should be empty. You will hear modulations in the drone that suggest response, interaction, the presence of something that listens as well as speaks. You will find, in the deepest pools, disturbances that no blind fish could make, ripple patterns that suggest something larger, something that moves with deliberation through spaces you have not mapped.

Perhaps these are the Tenders in some phase or form you have not recognized. Perhaps they are Pumpsingers who have left their columns, adapted to another mode of life. Perhaps they are something the Rootworks has grown for purposes you cannot guess, something that serves the larger system in ways that do not require your comprehension. Perhaps they are only your own perception, strained to breaking by the sound, the cold, the patience that this place demands.

But you will feel watched. You will feel, sometimes, that the entire space has oriented itself to your presence, that the drone shifts subtly when you move, that the root-cables carry news of you to nodes you cannot locate, that your existence in the Rootworks is not a secret you keep but a signal you emit, continuously, involuntarily, to intelligences that process it in timescales you cannot match.

---

What lives in the Rootworks lives *slowly*. This is the fundamental fact, the quality that underlies every other. The Tenders attend across centuries. The Pumpsingers compose across decades. The Accumulators concentrate across millennia. The Rare Ones, if they exist, move at speeds that make glaciers seem hasty, their perception of you perhaps equivalent to your perception of the condensation that forms and drips on timescales too brief for your attention to register as event.

You are learning this. Your own pulse has begun to match the drone, your heartbeat slowing to frequencies you would have found pathological on the surface. Your movements have become deliberate, each action preceded by long consideration of its necessity, its efficiency, its place in larger patterns that extend beyond your own duration. You have stopped expecting response, stopped requiring it, learned that the world does not need your participation to continue its processes, that your presence is tolerated rather than required, that your eventual incorporation is not violence but ecology, the simple passage of matter and energy through forms.

The pencil shakes in your hand. The paper vibrates with the drone. You write, knowing no one will read, knowing that the act of writing is itself a form of attendance, of participation in the slow processes of the Rootworks, of becoming the kind of thing that belongs here, that will eventually leave only faint marks, partial remains, occasional artifacts for some later visitor to wonder at.

The flywheels turn. The xylem pumps. You are here. You are becoming here. The Rootworks has time, and now, in your slowing, in your patience, you have time too.
