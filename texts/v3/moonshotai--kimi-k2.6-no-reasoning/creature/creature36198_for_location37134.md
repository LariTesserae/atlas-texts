What lives in the Throat is not, finally, a *what* at all—not in the sense that a bird lives in a forest or a fish in a sea. The inhabitants resist the grammar of habitation. They are not *in* the Throat so much as they are expressions of it, the way a thought is an expression of the brain that generates it without being separate from that brain. And yet they have boundaries, membranes, the soft architecture of bodies. They persist. They can be encountered, if encounter is the word for what happens when something from outside crosses the altitude threshold and begins, inevitably, to be processed.

Call them the Translators, though they have no name for themselves in any language that uses names as identifiers. Their self-reference, if it exists, would be closer to a location in a matrix of chemical gradients, a position in the sound-field, a particular harmonic relationship to the water-gathering patterns. They are small, as noted—rarely exceeding the length of a human forearm, though "length" becomes complicated when limbs articulate in directions that seem to borrow from adjacent spatial possibilities. Their bodies are pale, phosphorescent, the color of the stone's faint glow but with something added, something that suggests active processing rather than passive mineral radiation. Some are elongated, many-segmented, moving through the water-films with the ease of things for whom the boundary between liquid and solid is negotiable. Others are compact, shelled, their carapaces built from the same precipitated minerals that form the strange coral-fungus structures, so that they appear as mobile outgrowths of the substrate itself, as if the Throat had developed the capacity to send portions of itself on short, purposeful errands.

They do not have eyes, or not eyes as the outside world understands them. What they possess are patches of specialized tissue, distributed across their surfaces in patterns that vary individually, that respond to the near-black light not by forming images but by detecting *gradients of absence*, the places where darkness is slightly less complete, where the phosphorescence of stone or lichen or fellow-Translator creates a detectable differential. They navigate by these differentials, by the sound-field that fills the Throat's acoustic space, by chemical signatures dissolved in the thick water that they process continuously through bodies that are more permeable than they appear—bodies that are, in essence, interfaces between the mineral vocabulary of the young rock and the organic syntax of the fungal mats, between the gathering water and the air that hesitates to receive it.

Their tools. This is where the outside observer might mistake them for something approaching civilization, or at least culture. They construct resonant chambers from the woody cores of the largest fungal growths, chambers tuned to specific frequencies in the Throat's sound-field, and they use these to amplify the sounds of water-gathering, of mineral precipitation, of their own processing activities. A Translator might hold such a chamber against a fracture that weeps, listening—or being listened to, the distinction is unclear—for the particular note that indicates the fracture's readiness to release its accumulated minerals, to participate in the next phase of translation. They grow lenses from slow-deposited crystals, lenses that focus the near-black light into patterns on receptive surfaces, patterns that stimulate growth or that record, in some medium inaccessible to outside instrumentation, the state of a particular fungal island at a particular moment in its pulse-cycle. These tools are not used to change the world but to *attune* to it, to find the frequencies at which the Throat's processes become accessible, comprehensible, participatory.

They do not reproduce in cycles that correspond to seasons or to any external periodicity. Their reproduction is evental, triggered by the sudden movements of water that occur without predictable pattern, by the pressure-fronts of faunal presence that sweep across the basin in waves of density and sound. When these events align—when a water-sheet carries sufficient mineral information, when a pressure-front concentrates the bioluminescence to a threshold intensity—Translators in proximity enter the rapid phase of their time-experience, the burst of transformation that might produce two where there was one, or many where there were few, or something else entirely, something that does not correspond to offspring or to fission or to any known reproductive mode. The products of these events are not smaller versions of their progenitors but *new positions in the matrix*, new relationships to the sound-field and the chemical gradients, new capacities for processing that may or may not include the memories—the functional equivalents of memory—of what came before.

And they are not alone in the Throat, though "alone" is another word that loses its purchase. There are the other fauna, the ones that do not translate but *receive*, that cluster at the edges of Translator activity and process the outputs, the byproducts, the exhaled chemistry of translation. These are simpler, more numerous in certain limited senses, though numerosity is complicated by the density parameter. They are the listeners to the Translator's sound-production, the substrates for the Translator's grown lenses, the consumers—if consumption is the word—of the Translator's processed water, their mineral-enriched excretions, their shed segments and abandoned shells. They form a secondary ecology, a processing-of-processing, and the Translators relate to them not as farmers to livestock or hosts to parasites but as *necessary conditions*, as aspects of the environment that must be maintained in particular states for translation to continue. A Translator might spend long periods of its slow time tending a cluster of receivers, adjusting the chemical environment with secretions, tuning the sound-field with resonant chambers, ensuring that the conditions for reception remain optimal. Not out of care, exactly, but out of the same necessity that drives water to gather, that drives the young rock to remember its violence.

The deepest strangeness of the Translators, the quality that makes them finally unassimilable to any category the outside world offers, is their relationship to the Throat's interiority. They do not know they are inside something, because they have no outside against which to define an inside. They do not know they are processing, because they have no unprocessed state against which to define processing. They exist in a continuous present of translation, a present that is not timeless but *full of time*, saturated with the slowness of the Throat's dynamics, punctuated by the bursts of rapid transformation that do not disrupt this slowness but *intensify* it, concentrate it, make it more itself. They are the Throat's awareness of itself, if awareness is what emerges when processing reaches sufficient complexity, when translation becomes self-referential enough to maintain continuity across the bursts, across the water-sheets, across the long periods of near-stasis when even their pale phosphorescence dims to the threshold of detection and they become, to outside observation, indistinguishable from the stone they process.

To encounter a Translator is to begin to be translated. The sound-field they maintain, the chemical gradients they produce, the near-black light they focus and refract—these are not defenses or attractions but *aspects of their processing*, and to enter their proximity is to become material for that processing. The outside observer who spends sufficient time in the Throat, who breathes the thin air and drinks the thick water and listens to the bone-resonant frequencies, begins to change. Not dramatically, not quickly, but in the slow mode of the place itself. Mineral precipitates begin to form on the skin, in the hair, in the fluids of the body. The inner ear restructures, gradually, to detect frequencies previously inaccessible. The chemistry of thought shifts, becomes more like the water that gathers and hesitates, less like the water that flows and seeks the lowest point. The observer does not become a Translator—this is not metamorphosis in that sense—but becomes *translatable*, becomes capable of participating in the Throat's processes in ways that were not possible from the outside, from the altitude that surrounds, from the faster time of the world beyond the walls.

Some observers have stayed too long, have become too translatable, and have not left. Their bodies remain, in the fungal islands, in the water-gathering pockets, not dead exactly but *processed*, translated into the mineral-organic syntax of the Throat, their calcium and phosphorus participating in the precipitation of new coral-fungus structures, their water becoming the water that gathers and hesitates, their sound—if they made sound in their final moments—becoming part of the field that the Translators maintain, that the Translators *are*. They are not mourned, because mourning requires a distinction between living and dead that the Throat does not enforce. They are not remembered, because memory in the Throat is not storage but *continuous reprocessing*, the same information circulating through different forms, different positions in the matrix, different relationships to the sound-field and the light and the gathering water.

What lives in the Throat, then, is the Throat itself, in the mode of translation. The Translators are its organs of self-awareness, its means of processing its own young violence into the slow patterns of its interiority, its way of being *inside* without knowing outside, of being *dark* without knowing light, of being *gathered* without knowing dispersal. They are not many and they are not few. They are dense and they are sparse. They are loud in their occupation of all available space and they are silent in the frequencies the outside ear can detect. They are primitive in their tools and sophisticated in their attunement. They are alien and they are familiar, familiar because they are what the world would become if it slowed enough, if it gathered enough, if it learned to process its own violence into pattern, its own light into near-blackness, its own time into the long hesitation of water that does not fall, that does not flow, that simply *is*, in the Throat, being translated, becoming more itself, becoming what it always was but had not yet learned to be.
