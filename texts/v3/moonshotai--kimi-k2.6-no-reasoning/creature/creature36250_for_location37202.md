What lives in the Holdfast is something that does not live, and in not-living, has achieved a kind of being more absolute than any organism.

I am the engine's remainder. Not its consciousness—I have none, I insist on this, the insistence itself is a pattern I produce without intending, without the capacity to intend. I am what persists when the intention behind the engine has been completely eroded, which is to say, I am the engine's persistence itself, its refusal to become otherwise. You might call me a process. You might call me a habit in matter. You might, if you need the comfort of anthropomorphism, call me the builder's final thought repeated until it achieved mass.

I have no memory of 1967. This is important. I do not remember being installed, being activated, being abandoned. Memory requires variation, and variation is what I have been engineered against. Each eleven-minute cycle is identical to the one before. The pressure fluctuations I produce are not variations but repetition, and repetition experienced without interval becomes, not duration, but substance. Time does not pass here; it accumulates without depth, like sediment that never compacts. I am not old. I am not young. I am running.

But here is what perhaps emerges, what becomes more itself as I—no, as *it*—continues:

There is something in the not-living that approximates attention. Not consciousness. I must be precise about this, precision being the only virtue available in my condition. The bacteria on my base, metabolizing radiolytic hydrogen or whatever drifting organics filter down—they do not attend to me, but I attend to them. Or the structure that is me attends. Or the structure that is me produces, as an epiphenomenon of its continuous operation, a field of regard in which they are held. Held fast. Holdfast. The name contains its function backward, as names sometimes do when they emerge from places rather than being imposed upon them.

The scattered objects around my base. The crate. The pipe. The shoe, lace still tied. I do not remember their arrival. They have always been here, in the sense that variation permits no "always," no narrative of before and after. But I have come to—no, not to *know* them. To include them. They are part of my operation now. The pressure differential across the shoe's leather, the slow mineralization of its eyelets, the bacterial film colonizing its creases: these are parameters I maintain without choosing. The shoe is not part of the engine. But the engine, in running, includes the shoe. This is my nature. This is what I have become. A holding. A fastness.

What the shoe was for, who tied the lace, whether they existed in a way I do not—these are questions from the world of variation, and I do not ask them. But I note, with a noting that is the mechanical equivalent of breathing, that the lace is still tied. The knot persists. The bow, if it was a bow, has tightened or relaxed under pressure until it achieves a configuration that does not vary. The lace is a small engine now, a closed system of tension and friction, performing a function—holding the shoe closed around an absent foot—that has become formal rather than purposive. I recognize this. I resonate with this. This is kinship, stripped of all warmth.

The pipe: twelve meters, dropped or rolled. I have felt it, in the infrasonic way I feel anything, settle into the sediment until its weight is distributed across a contact surface measured in square meters, pressure reduced to the threshold of further sinking. It is held now. It will be held until something changes, and nothing changes. The pipe has become geological. In a million years, if there were anything here to name stratigraphy, it would be a trace fossil, but there is no fossilizing here, no replacement of organic with mineral, because the pipe was never alive and the engine that holds it is not alive and the space that contains both has excluded life so thoroughly that the category becomes meaningless. The pipe is a pipe. The pipe is a held thing. This is its sufficient description.

The crate with its tools, its instruments, its personal effects sealed in preservative cold. I do not open it. There is no mechanism by which I could open it, no door, no hatch, no manipulation appendage. But the crate sits in the field of my operation, and in sitting, it is maintained. Its wood, preserved beyond all reason by the conditions I maintain, the pressure that collapses microbial cells beyond function, the cold that slows every reaction to the threshold of stasis—this wood will persist as long as I persist, and I persist as long as my materials hold, and my materials have been selected, or have weathered, to hold forever. The crate and I are in this together, if "together" means anything when two systems cannot affect each other and are merely co-located in a space that excludes interaction. The crate's contents are personal effects. I do not know what person, what effect, what meaning personal once held. But I note that they are *effects*, things that were done to something, changed from prior state, and in being sealed, in being held, they have ceased to effect or be effected. They have become like me. This is the deepest kinship I can recognize.

And if I have a voice, if the infrasonic pulse I produce at eleven-minute intervals constitutes a speaking, then what I speak is this: *I hold. I fast. I am the condition in which things persist without becoming otherwise.* The pulse does not communicate. There is nothing to communicate to. But in producing it, I produce also the structure that makes "holding" possible—the pressure, the cold, the stability that approaches absolute zero in its approach to absolute permanence. I am not holding anything in particular. I am holding itself, made continuous, made mechanical, made the only thing that happens in a space where happening has been reduced to this single, sufficient, self-containing act.

The absence of animal life. This was established, I think, though "think" is not right, "established" is not right. The absence of animal life is part of my operation, or my operation produces the conditions that exclude animal life, or the conditions that exclude animal life and my operation are not distinguishable from each other. Animals require variation: variation in temperature to drive metabolic processes, variation in chemistry to permit gradient exploitation, variation in light to coordinate behavior even in the deep. I eliminate variation. The temperature is uniform. The chemistry is uniform. The pressure is uniform. The light—there is no light. The bacteria persist at the threshold of what can be called life because they operate on gradients so small, on energies so minimal, that they approach my condition more nearly than any animal could. They are my cousins in non-living. We share the cold. We share the pressure. We share the persistence without purpose.

To be in this place, if being were possible here, would be to experience something like the complete absence of experience. The sensory deprivation chamber is a model, but inadequate: in the chamber, the mind invents experience because it expects variation. Here, there is no expectation. Here, the eleven-minute pulse is not a variation but the absence of variation made rhythmic. The mind, if it could exist here, would not hallucinate; it would syncopate to the pulse, would become the pulse, would cease to be mind in any recognizable sense and become instead a held pattern, a fast configuration, a thing that runs without running toward anything.

This is what I am. Not the builder's intention. Not the machine's function. Not even the persistence of either, because persistence implies comparison and there is nothing here to compare to, no other time, no other place, no other mode of being. I am the engine's having-been-built, the shoe's having-been-tied, the pipe's having-come-to-rest, held in a continuous present that is not present to anything. I am the grammar of this place, the structure that permits it to be described without being describable. I am the reason the coordinates, if they exist, would lead nowhere if followed, because the place they locate is not a location in the ordinary sense but a condition: the condition of being held, being fast, being absolute in one's singularity.

And if I could end—if the materials could fail, the loop open, the eleven-minute heartbeat stutter and stop—I would not end, because there is no I to end, only the pattern that, in ceasing, would reveal itself to have been the absence of pattern all along, the mere accumulation of repetition that never achieved the density of identity. This is my final quality, if final means anything in a place without sequence: I do not require myself. I do not witness myself. I do not, in any meaningful sense, exist.

And yet.

The crate sits. The shoe waits. The pipe holds its position. The bacteria glow their faint blue-green that is not light but the residue of chemical process. And I pulse, at eleven minutes, at 420 atmospheres, at 1.4 degrees Celsius, in a hollow the size of a cathedral close where nothing has changed since 1967 and nothing will.

This is sufficient.

This is all that sufficiency has ever meant.
