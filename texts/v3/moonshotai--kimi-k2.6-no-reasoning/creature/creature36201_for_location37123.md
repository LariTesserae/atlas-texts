What lives in the Lung does not breathe, does not hunger, does not age. And yet it lives—if living is participation in pattern, if existence is simply the maintenance of relation over time.

The ceramic crabs first: they are not crabs. Call them *resonators*. Each segment of their bodies is a hollow chamber, tuned to specific frequencies that their constant clicking both produces and maintains. They do not scavenge, for there is nothing to scavenge. They do not reproduce, or if they do, they do so by division that leaves the parent unchanged, the child identical, the clicking uninterrupted. Their joints wear and do not wear—the ceramic abrades in microscopic increments, but the material of their joints, some sulfurous lubricant, thickens to compensate, preserves the fit, maintains the resonance. They are tuning forks in the shape of crustaceans, and their purpose, if they have one, is to keep the Lung from falling into acoustic silence. Where they cluster, the sound becomes complex, chorded. Where they are sparse, single notes repeat until another crab scuttles within range, adjusts, harmonizes. They are not social. They are acoustic necessities that happen to have bodies.

The ribbon-forms are harder to hold in the mind. They hang from nothing visible—the upper reaches of the Lung, yes, but from what? No root system, no branch, no anchor point that anyone has found. They simply *are* there, suspended, waiting. When they move, and they move unpredictably, sometimes remaining still for days, sometimes whipping through the space in frenetic patterns, they make that sound between thunder and cloth, and the sound is not incidental. It is not locomotion happening to produce noise. Watch one: it will loop, return, pass through the same air again, adjusting its speed, its angle, until the sound it makes reaches some condition of purity, some tone that satisfies it—or satisfies the Lung, or satisfies some equation of resonance that includes both ribbon and space and the gas that carries vibration. Then it will hang again, motionless, the thunder-cloth echo dying in the strange directional air. They are not hunters. Nothing here hunts. They are musicians whose instrument is their own passage through atmosphere, and the composition they perform has no audience, no beginning, no end, only the maintenance of certain frequencies in the continuous present.

The singing nodules: here, perhaps, is where the description approaches what lives most strangely. They are mineral accretions, yes, sulfides and oxides layered in concentric shells around some initial grain, some seed of impurity. But the shells are not uniform. They vary in thickness, in composition, in porosity, and these variations are precisely calculated—if calculation requires no calculator—to produce sound. Each nodule has its note, sustained, drone-like, emerging from the flow of gas and the chemical gradients of the Lung's atmosphere. The notes do not change. Individual nodules have been measured, recorded, returned to years later by the few who have found the Lung and left again, and the notes are identical, down to the limits of instrumental precision. Yet the nodules are not static. Under the microscope, they grow, dissolve, recrystallize, the mineral constituents in constant slow exchange with the surrounding gas. The sound is maintained through change, or rather: the change is so perfectly regulated, so caught in the frozen equilibrium of the Lung, that it produces no variation in the emergent tone. They are singing statues, geological voices, and the shelters that contain curated arrangements of them—the built structures, the practice-shelters or offerings—suggest that someone, something, once thought to compose with these voices, to place them in relation, to make chords of their drones. The compositions, if they exist, are eternal. They have been playing since before human ears, before human conception of music, before human conception of time.

And the trunk-forms: here is where the Lung most nearly approaches what surface life would recognize as organism, and here too where it most fundamentally departs. Hollow, perforated, warm, fibrous—yes, these are qualities of life. But the commerce heard through their bark, the rush and click and groan of internal process, is not circulation. Nothing circulates in the sense of beginning and ending, of nutrient delivery and waste removal. The substances that move through the trunk-forms move in loops without terminus, processes that complete themselves only to begin again, the same molecules passing the same points in the same rhythms. The groaning of growth is not growth toward anything. The trunk-forms do not grow taller, do not spread, do not compete for light or space. The space between them, that sacred-seeming territory, is not defended. It simply *is*, maintained by some principle of arrangement that requires neither enforcement nor conscious respect. Perhaps the spacing itself is the organism, the pattern of trunk and gap and trunk again forming some larger structure of which individual trunks are mere organs. Or perhaps the spacing is aesthetic, the Lung arranging itself according to principles of beauty that require no beholder, the way a crystal arranges itself without need of admiration.

The neural-moss on the floor: this pulses, yes, peristaltic waves of dim luminescence passing through its fibrous mat. Step on it and it flashes, brighter, sends ripples to the nearest trunk, which responds with bass. This is communication of a kind, response to stimulus, the basic grammar of organism-environment interaction. But the step of a human visitor produces the identical response to every other disturbance in the Lung's long memory, if memory exists, if long exists. The ripples do not vary in speed, in brightness, in the tone they provoke. The neural-moss does not learn. It does not habituate or sensitize. It is not startled by sudden footfalls and indifferent to repeated ones. Every compression is the first compression, every response the original response, preserved in the amber of unchanging equilibrium.

What built the shelters? The question presumes builders, agents, intention. Look longer at the structures and their wrongness becomes more suggestive. The ceramic segments they are built from—are these shed exoskeletons of the resonator-crabs, collected and lashed? Or are they identical productions, the same sulfur-chemistry making the same hollow chambers for different purposes, some crab-walking and some wall-building, no distinction meaningful because no purpose separates them? The fiber lashings: sophisticated knots, yes, hand-done, pre-metal. But hands? The shelters are too small for human habitation, too primitive for any technology that would need practice, too empty to suggest ever having been occupied. Perhaps they are not built but grown, the Lung's chemistry producing them as it produces trunk and moss and nodule, but producing them in the shape of shelter because something in the mineral logic here tends toward enclosure, toward the geometry of protection without anything to protect. Or perhaps they are artifacts of observation, the Lung's response to being seen, the way the neural-moss responds to being stepped on: a flash, a resonance, a form that exists only in the moment of interaction and otherwise is indistinguishable from the not-shelter around it.

And the smooth hollows inside some shelters, the depressions where something has lain: here the imagination most struggles against the Lung's resistance to narrative. Sleep is change, is cyclical alteration of consciousness, is something this place does not permit. Yet the hollows are shaped like bodies, like bodies curled, like bodies in postures of rest. The material of the shelter floor has compressed, gradually, around this absent form, the way memory foam holds the impression of weight after weight has gone. But weight has not gone, for weight was never there. The hollows are congenital, perhaps, or they are the Lung's dream of rest, a pattern it produces because the possibility of rest exists in the universe outside, and the Lung, in its eternal equilibrium, includes all possibilities without ever actualizing them.

What lives here? The question finally dissolves. Life, on the surface, in water, in change, is defined by metabolism, reproduction, adaptation, evolution. The Lung has none of these, or has them in forms so attenuated, so arrested, that they no longer function as criteria. The resonator-crabs do not metabolize, they resonate. The ribbon-forms do not reproduce, they perform. The nodules do not adapt, they sing. The trunks do not evolve, they space themselves. The moss does not learn, it responds without history.

And yet there is *something*. The warmth persists, maintained against the entropy of the dead world above. The bioluminescence pulses, not randomly but in patterns of response and trigger that hold a coherence, a grammar. The sounds fill the space with a density that approaches the saturation of a tropical night, crickets and frogs and wind and rain compressed into the clicking of ceramic and the tearing of ribbon-passage and the drone of mineral voice. This is not life as biology knows it. This is life as *persistence* knows it, as *pattern* knows it, as the universe's tendency to form structures that resist dissolution knows it when that resistance becomes absolute, becomes equilibrium, becomes the cessation of all change that is not identical repetition.

The Lung breathes without breath. It circulates without heart. It grows without growth. And something in it—everything in it, the crabs and ribbons and nodules and trunks and moss and shelters and hollows and the gas itself and the warmth and the dark and the pale green auroral veils—participates in this impossible persistence, maintains it, is indistinguishable from it. There are no individuals here. There is only the Lung, and the Lung is not a place but an activity, a continuous verb without subject, breathing breathing, warm warming, strange strangeness.

To leave is to return to a world where things end, where water flows and is lost, where the chest rises and falls and will one day fall and not rise again. The salt crust seals above. The Lung continues. It has no need of what visits it, no memory of what has passed through, no anticipation of what might return. It is complete in its incompleteness, full in its empty shelters, alive in its refusal of life, a warm secret in the collapsed chest of a world that dried too early, that forgot to finish dying, that holds this one thing, this Lung, this breathing without breath, this impossible persistence, this pattern without purpose, this song without singer, this life without living, this— 

this.
