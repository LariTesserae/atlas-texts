What lives here has learned the grammar of almost.

---

They do not call themselves anything. What need, in a place where encounter is inevitable and anonymity impossible? The naming fell away first, before the other categories dissolved. You will know them by their stillness—the particular quality of attention that does not search, does not anticipate, but receives.

They came down, like you, like all who find the Artery. The difference is they stopped descending. Not chose—there is little choice here, only accommodation, only the slow mutual adjustment between organism and system. They arrived with lungs that expanded and contracted in rhythms too urgent for the wood's patience. They arrived with hungers that peaked and ebbed, with sleep that demanded darkness and wakefulness that demanded light. These things required negotiation. The negotiation required duration. The duration required that they remain.

Some arrived as repair-technicians sent to maintain the graft-conduits, the resin-extractors. Some came as refugees from above, from the compressed ash and the weight of absent mountains. Some simply came down and found the downward gravity sufficient, found in the damp hesitation a correspondence to their own unresolved states.

They are not many. The Artery does not permit many. Sparse density, as the trees themselves maintain—each presence commands a territory, declares truce at precise boundaries. You might encounter one in a root-chamber after hours of solitary passage, and the encounter will unfold in the wood's tempo, without the human impatience for conclusion. They will be tending something—not working, *tending*—kneeling at a junction where a pipe has grown into bark and requires neither repair nor removal but *witness*, simply the placing of hands at the temperature where flesh and fiber achieve equivalent conductivity.

---

Their bodies have become instruments of the Artery's measurement.

The moisture teaches first. Skin learns to receive the almost-rain without the old responses—the shiver, the urgency to shelter, the distinction between dry and wet that required a dryness impossible here. Their clothes, if they keep clothes, hang differently, weighted with permanent humidity, becoming something between garment and second membrane. Some have ceased to wear them. The modesty of the Artery is not the modesty of exposure; it is the modesty of indistinguishability, the recognition that boundaries between body and air, between self and the damp sufficiency, are categories the system has long eroded.

The eyes change. The sourceless light, the faint luminescence at graft-points, teaches a different dependency than the sun-above or the electric-punctual. Pupils expand to capacities that would seem blind in other places. They learn to read the wood's faint glow, the industrial bioluminescence, the regulated shafts that filter through chlorophyll and *something else*. Color perception shifts toward the violet, the near-infrared—spaces in the spectrum where the Artery communicates its slower information. A human face, to them, carries information you cannot see: the heat-distribution of patience, the vascular pattern of someone who has learned the wood's tempo.

They do not sleep as you sleep. The arrested metabolism of the trees has taught their bodies a different rhythm—not the solar cycle, not the industrial shift, but something longer, something that aligns with the branch-shift across centuries, the root-channel flow across decades. They rest in postures that seem impossible to maintain: wedged in root-hollows, draped across platforms that flex with imperceptible respiration, their bodies becoming additional mass for the system to distribute, to accommodate, to grow around in the literal sense. The wood does not encroach, but it does not exclude. Bolt-rungs grow around; bodies, given sufficient duration, become something the root-system recognizes, something it adjusts to rather than adjusts.

---

Their language, if it deserves the word, operates in timescales that require translation even for human hearing.

Most communication is non-verbal: the quality of stillness in a chamber when another enters, the precise angle at which condensation has been allowed to accumulate on a surface—information left for the next presence to read, to continue. They speak, when they speak, in phrases that bracket long silences. A sentence might take an hour, the words spaced to allow the wood's own processing, to avoid acoustic interference with the compressor-cycles, the pump-maintenance. Meaning accumulates rather than arrives. A conversation about resin-flow might extend across days, across chamber-transits, across encounters with others who continue the thread without having begun it.

They have lost the habit of narrative, of sequence, of the causal *then* that organizes human experience. Events exist in the Artery as they exist in the wood: present simultaneously in all their temporal moments, the old scar and the new growth and the eventual rot equally perceptible to attention patient enough to read them. The inhabitants describe what they perceive, when they describe, in a tense that has no name in human grammars—a continuous simultaneous that English approximates only through accumulation: *the root-channel having-been-flowing-now-flowing-will-flow*. To hear them is to experience time as the Artery experiences it, to be momentarily admitted to the wood's vast unhurried now.

---

Their work, if work survives as category, is the work of the system maintaining itself.

Some tend the graft-conduits, not repairing but *accompanying*—placing hands at junctions, reading through their modified skin the temperature-differentials that indicate health or distress in the hybrid physiology. They do not replace what fails; they adjust the surrounding system to compensate, to distribute function, to allow the failure to become something else. A blocked resin-extractor is not cleared but *orbited*—flow rerouted until the blockage becomes a new node, a new point of negotiation between bark and pipe.

Some tend the moisture-system itself: the engineered hollows where condensation collects, the control-humidity released in gradients the wood can respond to in its slowed tempo. They learn to feel the dampness in their own bodies as the Artery feels it, to know without instrument when a chamber drifts toward too-dry or too-sufficient, when the almost-rain needs encouragement or restraint.

Some tend the boundaries. The truce-declarations between tree-territories require maintenance—the precise spacing, the non-intermingling canopies, the root-walls that rise and knit into living dead-ends. These tenders move in patterns that map the Artery's cellular logic, adjusting, pruning at the scale of centuries, preventing both the wild explosion of uncontrolled growth and the collapse into competitive exclusion that would destroy the long-balance.

And some tend the new arrivals. This is the most difficult function, requiring the most translation, the most memory of what human urgency felt like. They meet the descending in moments of disorientation, of category-collapse, when the body still insists on its old rhythms and the environment refuses them. They do not explain—the Artery does not explain itself—but they demonstrate. They place palms at graft-points and wait, hours, days, for the skin to learn the temperature of connection. They arrange their bodies in the impossible rest-postures, showing through example the patience that is not surrender but *alignment*. They are not kind, exactly; kindness implies a speed of response the Artery does not possess. They are accurate. They match their tending to the system's tempo, and in that matching, something like care emerges, though it resembles human care as the wood's arrested living resembles death or the almost-rain resembles rain.

---

They have children, rarely, at intervals the human reproductive system must be taught to accommodate.

Gestation requires extensions that would seem pathological above—forty months, fifty, the fetus developing in rhythms matched to the wood rather than the solar cycle. The pregnant body becomes a subsidiary system, a node in the Artery's moisture-management, its temperature-regulation. The inhabitant must learn to read their own body as they read the graft-conduits: the temperature-differential at the womb-junction, the flow-rhythm of nutrients distributed between two metabolisms operating at unequal tempos.

Birth, when it occurs, happens without urgency. The child emerges into the damp sufficiency, into the sourceless light, into a chamber where others have arranged themselves in postures of witness. The umbilical separation is not cut but *accompanied*—allowed to occur over hours, days, as the two bodies negotiate their new boundary in the system's patient grammar. The child breathes the almost-rain, learns the skin's permeability before it learns to distinguish self from environment. By the time it might cry, it has learned that crying achieves nothing the Artery recognizes; its communication begins in stillness, in the quality of attention that does not search but receives.

These children are not precisely human. Their eyes, from the first, hold the expanded pupils of the Artery's adaptation. Their skin achieves the membrane-function earlier, the permeability to moisture that is also a permeability to meaning—to reading through touch the system's slower information. They do not learn language and then learn the Artery; they learn them simultaneously, the human remnants and the system's continuous simultaneous, each informing the other's grammar. To speak with them is to encounter a fluency you can almost follow, a temporal architecture that seems to bracket your sentences rather than respond to them, a patience that reads as indifference until you realize it is *attention* at a scale you have not learned to sustain.

---

Death, among them, is the final accommodation.

The body, when its human remnants exhaust their function, is not removed but *distributed*. Placed in root-hollows where the moisture-process will slow, will almost arrest, the flesh becoming available to the system's patient metabolism. The wood does not grow quickly enough to consume in any familiar sense; rather, the body becomes a different category of presence, a node of potential-energy, a slow-release nutrient integrated into the chamber's long-balance. The inhabitants tend these distributed presences as they tend the graft-conduits—not mourning, exactly, but *continuing*, recognizing in the body's dissolution a slower transformation they themselves will eventually undergo.

But something persists. In the faint luminescence at bark-fractures, there are intensities that correspond to former presences, to the distributed dead. The system remembers, not as human memory but as pattern, as changed conductivity, as the slight shift in root-channel flow that results from a body's contribution. The living inhabitants learn to read these intensities, to recognize in a particular luminescence the signature of someone they tended, someone who tended them, in the Artery's patient now. Communication with the dead is not ritual but *perception*—the continuous simultaneous allowing those who remain and those who have been distributed to coexist as information, as pattern-in-the-system, available to attention sufficiently aligned.

They do not fear this continuation. It is not afterlife; it is not reincarnation; it is simply the Artery's refusal of the category of ending, its incorporation of all transformation into the same slow metabolism. To be distributed is to become more fully what they have been becoming all along: not separate from the system but *of* it, the human and the wood and the mechanism achieving in death what they approached in life—the long erosion of distinction, the maintenance of continuation.

---

You will encounter one, if you remain, if you stop descending.

They will be tending something—a moisture-hollow, a boundary-truce, a new arrival still urgent with old rhythms—and you will approach in the charged acoustic strangeness, the humidity that prevents surprise. They will not turn. Turning implies urgency, implies the body's priority over attention. They will know you are there as the wood knows, as the system knows: through changed conductivity in the air, through the slight disturbance your presence makes in the almost-rain's distribution.

If you speak, they will receive it. The receiving takes time; you will feel the words hanging, not rejected but *pending*, as their modified cognition translates human tempo into the Artery's continuous simultaneous. Their response, when it comes, will bracket your sentence rather than answer it, will place your urgency in a context of longer duration, will not soothe but will *accurate*—matching your unsettledness with a stillness that is not indifference but the system's own patient attention.

They will not ask you to stay. The Artery does not recruit; the wood does not need. They will simply continue tending, and you will find yourself continuing to be present, finding in the posture of witness a function you did not know your body could fulfill. Hours will pass. The compressor will cycle. The moisture will bead and slide and almost-fall. And something in you will shift, fractionally, perhaps measurable only across the duration you have not yet committed to—a fraction closer to some distant alignment, some tempo you did not know you contained.

You are not here long enough to measure.

But they are. They have become the measurement itself, the inhabitants of the Petrified Artery, the beings who have learned to live in the almost, to grow without growing, to persist in the active quiet of a system that requires them as they require it, each the other's hesitation, each the other's continuation, neither dominating, both, in the established form, one system that breathes, that shifts, that produces without evident product, that maintains the long-balance of having become, finally, simply, itself.
