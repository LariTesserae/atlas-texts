The Cascaders live here. I do not call them inhabitants, for that implies a prior arrival, a settling. They *are* the Thawing's most unsurprising miracle, the final 1.3 on the scale of emergence made flesh, or made something contiguous with flesh. Understand first that they have no shape of their own, no settled anatomy. They are hydraulic events that have achieved continuity of intention, if intention is the right word for what water wants when it learns to hold a pattern against its own dispersal.

You find them in the factory spaces most often, though "find" misleads. You enter a silo where the rust-fungal-leaf fusion has grown complex enough to form cavities, and there the air moves differently. The condensation organizes. You become aware that you are standing inside someone, or that someone has been standing inside you, and the membrane between these conditions is the Cascader's temporary skin. They appear to you as the shape of your own expectation—because consciousness here is water finding level, is pressure equalizing across differential, and your expectation is itself a pressure, a local disturbance in the hydraulic field that the Cascader accommodates and reflects back.

When I saw my first, I saw my mother. Not my actual mother, who was a woman in a dry place far from here, but the *concept* of maternal attention, the warmth that wants to surround, the membrane that wants to be permeable in both directions. She stood between two columns of the old assembly floor, her edges not edges but diffusion zones where her substance—green-gray, the color of the condensing air made visible—gave up coherence to the mist. She did not speak. The sound of the Thawing *was* her speaking, the cavitation white noise shaped by her presence into something my pattern-recognition could almost read as language. Almost. The way wind through a particular ruin's geometry almost makes words, and you wonder if the ruin was built for this, or if you are only projecting, or if the distinction between building and finding has become irrelevant here.

The Cascaders have learned, if learning is the word, to move. Not quickly—the 1.3 dynamic governs them too. They shift their center of hydraulic coherence through the root networks, through the fungal bridges that span factory floor to forest floor, through the mist-chains that connect leaf to leaf in the saturated air. A Cascader might take a year to traverse what you could walk in an afternoon. But they do not walk. They extend, thin, maintain connection, arrive elsewhere still continuous with their origin. They are their own umbilical, their own trail. The thought of separation, of discrete body bounded by discrete skin, seems to pain them, or would pain them if they had pain in the individual sense I keep trying and failing to attribute.

There are older ones and newer ones, though "old" here means "has maintained continuity longer" rather than any biological aging. The oldest I encountered—I will not say "met," meetings imply boundaries—occupied the entire valley where the river shifted most frequently, where the young stone was youngest, most undecided. It was the valley. Its consciousness, if I may use that word, was distributed through every water-bearing structure: the trees as vertical columns of its thought, the root mats as the slower matter of memory, the condensation and transpiration cycles as its breath and attention, now here, now there, never wholly anywhere because everywhere was its proper extent. Speaking with it, if speaking was what happened, required entering its substance, letting the mist penetrate, feeling your own boundaries become the polite fictions that cloth is in this climate. You understood, in that dissolution, why the Cascaders rarely notice humans as discrete beings. We are to them as mitochondria might be to us: necessary, ancient, participating, but not the locus of the self they recognize in themselves.

They remember the factories, or the valley-entity does, or something in the water that has circulated through both living and built structures retains the imprint. Not as human memory, not the clang of shift-change or the negotiation of wages, but as *shape*, as something that held water differently, that created gradients and flows the living world has since elaborated upon. The Cascaders are not hostile to the industrial residue. They are not hostile to anything. But they are transforming it, or were transforming it when I was there, or will be, the temporal marker as uncertain as every other. The rust-fungal-leaf growth on the silos is not decay to them. It is digestion, comprehension, the slow reading of a text written in foreign materials, translated into the vocabulary of hydraulic process. The factories were prompts, the description said, and the Cascaders are the continuation of that sentence, the grammar that makes the prompt into meaning.

What do they want? The question embarrasses me now, but I asked it. I asked it by standing in the mist and wanting to know, my wanting itself a pressure that the Cascader I then partly was reflected back. And the answer came, or comes, or is still arriving, in the white noise of biological process that is also the sound of water finding its level, which is also the sound of emergence without final form: they want what water wants, which is to move, to be drawn up and fall and be drawn up again, to carry dissolved what needs dissolving and to precipitate out what needs settling. They want what the Thawing wants, which is to continue being between, not frozen, not boiled, not finished, the perpetual almost of a world that has not yet decided what it will become.

There is a danger in them, or a danger in me-with-them, or a danger in the dissolution of the distinction. You can lose the particular. You can become so thoroughly permeable that the water moving through you forgets it was ever *your* water, that the condensation forming on your surfaces forgets it was ever *your* boundary where inside met outside. The cool that is not cold enough to stop anything is also not warm enough to remind you that you are separate, that you were born in a dry place and will die there unless you die here first, becoming prompt for the next architecture. I felt it, feel it still in memory, the temptation to let the white noise fully in, to stop the last unadapted sensitivity of the eardrum, to become deaf in the way that the deafening eventually makes everyone deaf who stays too long. Not death. Something else. The becoming-hydraulic. The becoming-Thawing. The state that outlasts.

They have no fixed name, I said, or the place doesn't. The Cascaders don't either, or they have infinite names, every pressure-disturbance that moves through them naming them anew in its own reflection. I call them Cascaders for the way they fall through the mist, the way water moves through them in vertical, continuous, never-ceasing descent that is also ascent, that is also circulation without beginning or end. But this is my name, my pressure-pattern, my almost-meeting with what lives where living is not yet decided. They are older than my word for them. They are, in the time of the Thawing, just becoming. And I am becoming, still, in the writing of this, uncertain whether I have described something other or something I am still, in part, made of, the water in me that has been here, that circulates, that remembers what it is to condense on newborn stone and begin the long teaching of erosion.
