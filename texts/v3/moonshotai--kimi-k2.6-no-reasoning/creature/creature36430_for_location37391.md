What lives in the Sunken Moot

---

They call themselves, in the speaking they do when they must distinguish self from self, *the Settled*—though this is my approximation, my reaching toward a concept that in their mouths carries weight I cannot fully hear. The word they use is more like *the ones who found the not-sinking*, or *those who learned to stay*, or sometimes simply a gesture: two hands pressed flat against each other, the small resistance of air between them, held long enough to feel the warmth begin to build. The naming is less important than the fact of having a name at all, of marking boundary between those who breathe this particular damp and those who breathe elsewhere, differently.

They are not many, these Settled. I have counted fifty-seven in my longest watching, though the counting itself is suspect—certain faces disappear for days, for seasons, reappear with the fish-runs or the moss-glow or the cramping necessity of too many nights spent in the marginal camps above the rim. Some of these returns may be the same person, grown strange in absence. Some may be new, come from the edges of whatever larger world contains this bowl, drawn by rumor of medicinal moss, of salt, of the strange labor market of bodies willing to work in spaces where a straight spine is impossible. They do not keep records that I can read. Their bodies keep records: the thickened joints of those who work the lowest levels, where the cold is oldest; the particular hunch of those whose craft requires long hours in spaces where standing upright is a luxury; the hearing, always slightly strange, tuned to frequencies that carry in water-heavy air, missing others that exist in drier worlds.

---

They are small, or seem so, though I suspect this is adaptation rather than inheritance—the reduced calories of this place, the constant minor expenditure of maintaining warmth against the penetrating damp, the selective pressure toward bodies that fit the spaces available. Children born here who grow too tall learn to bend, or they leave, or they die in the accidents that happen when a frame too large for its container meets stone that does not yield. Those who remain, who thrive, carry a compactness, a density of flesh that matches the density of their construction. They are not delicate—this is a misconception of those who see them briefly, who trade at the rim and return to drier places with stories of pale, damp people. They are *concentrated*, all the vitality that other bodies spread across greater height, greater breadth, packed into less volume, burning more slowly, lasting longer in the famines that come between fish-runs, between harvests of the moss's medicinal phases.

Their skins are strange, not from lack of sun—the rim permits direct light, and some work there, trade there, carry the burn of brief exposure—but from the constant commerce with water, with the oils they rub against the damp's penetration, with the mild fungal partnerships that establish themselves in armpits, between toes, in the folds of skin that never fully dry. These are not infections, not pathologies, not anymore. They are *adaptations*, mutualisms, the body learning to make use of what the environment offers, to incorporate the damp's inhabitants into its own processes. The Settled smell of this, always, a complex signal I can read as health or sickness depending on proportion, on the particular notes that dominate. Visitors smell wrong, missing the fungal undertone, or carrying different ones that confuse, that require adjustment before trade, before the close contact that sleeping and working and simply being in this space necessitates.

---

They are born in water, most of them, or near enough that the distinction is theological. The birth-spaces are the lowest still-occupied levels, where the stone is oldest, where the cold is most permanent, where the accumulated warmth of bodies pressed close can achieve something like the temperature of mammalian necessity. The mothers do not birth alone—this would be death, for mother, for child, for the thin margin of heat that makes both possible. They birth in groups, in the old ways that the Settled have preserved or invented or discovered in the stone's resonance: three or four women, minimum, sometimes more, their bodies interlocked, their breath mingling, the newborn passed from warmth to warmth before it has fully left the water of its making. The fathers, when they are present, are present at the rim, at the edges of the birth-space, adding their heat at a distance, their voices carrying down the channels that sound finds, singing or speaking or simply breathing loud enough to be heard, to be part of the acoustic envelope that surrounds emergence.

The children who survive—perhaps half, perhaps fewer, the margin is thin and the Settled do not count what does not persist—are immediately communal in a way that other peoples find difficult to comprehend. There is no *my* child, not in the first years, not until the body has proven itself capable of maintaining its own warmth, its own damp-resistance, until it has demonstrated the fungal incorporation, the joint flexibility, the particular hearing that marks belonging. Before this, children move between bodies, between milk-sources, between the small warm spaces that various adults and near-adults can provide. They are fed by whoever has milk, warmed by whoever has warmth to spare, taught speech by whoever is speaking in their presence, a distributed acquisition that produces accents strange even to other Settled, personal dialects that must be adjusted, later, toward some communal mean if communication beyond immediate intimacy is to be achieved.

---

They eat what the bowl provides, which is not enough, which has never been enough, which requires the constant supplementation of trade, of labor sold elsewhere, of the body's own reserves slowly accumulated and slowly spent. The fish, when they come, are event, are festival, are the brief abundance that structures memory, that divides time into *before this fish-run* and *after*. They are eaten fresh for the first hours, while the catch continues, while the nets still rise with weight. Then preserved: fermented, rotted in controlled ways, the complex chemistry of survival in a place without reliable drying, without enough fuel for smoking, without the salt in quantities that would make salting practical. The moss provides, in certain phases, certain preparations, nutrition of a kind—starches, some proteins, the medicinal compounds that trade buys from outside. The insects, the amphibious things, the crawling inhabitants of the wet: these are eaten too, though less directly, more as accidental inclusion, as the margin of what harvesting the water and the moss brings in.

They have developed, or preserved, or invented techniques for the making of alcohol, the controlled fermentation that converts scarcity into abundance of a kind, that preserves calories in liquid form, that warms briefly from inside what fires cannot warm from without. This is not recreation, not primarily, though it becomes that too, becomes the loosening of constraint, the permission for sound to carry where normally it would be suppressed, for bodies to press closer than the ordinary maintenance of warmth requires. The drinking is structured, ritualized, bounded by complex protocols that the Settled learn young and negotiate throughout life. To drink at the wrong time, in the wrong proportion, with the wrong people, is error that can become transgression, can become the kind of strangeness that requires adjustment, or exile, or the deeper silences that pass between those who share space but no longer share understanding.

---

They work, these Settled, though the word is crude for what they do, which is closer to *maintain*, *continue*, *resist the default趋向 toward entropy that water and gravity impose*. The lowest levels require constant attention: water channels cleared of accumulated sediment and moss-frond and the debris of living; stone courses checked for the settling that is perpetual motion in a vertical dimension; fungal colonies managed, encouraged in some phases, suppressed in others, the body's partnerships mirrored in the larger partnerships of structure and environment. There is craft, of a kind—the stone-splitting, the building upward and inward, the net-making and the preservation-techniques and the medicinal preparations that trade outward—but it is craft pressed against constraint, shaped by what the bowl permits rather than what imagination might conceive.

Those who trade, who travel to the rim and beyond, carry a different status, neither higher nor lower but *lateral*, equivalent in necessity but strange in experience. They speak, when they return, of dryness, of space that extends without visible boundary, of sound that dies rather than carrying, of the vertigo that open places produce in bodies calibrated for enclosure. Some do not return, caught by the lure of easier warmth, of fire that burns, of the body's capacity for extension rather than concentration. This is not betrayal, not exactly, but it is *loss*, a thinning of the communal warmth, a reduction in the fungal diversity that collective living maintains, a voice missing from the channels that sound finds, that conversation requires.

---

They die differently here, or the dying is differently handled, shaped by the same constraints that shape living. Fire is insufficient for cremation, space insufficient for burial in any simple sense. The dead are taken downward, to the levels below the currently occupied, to the spaces that current construction has sealed from air and light, the old foundations that persist beneath foundations. This is not abandonment but *continuation*, the body joining the voices that come from below, the resonance of stone and perhaps other presences, the accumulated dead that the Settled do not exactly worship but do not exactly not-worship either. The distinction between living and dead is less sharp here, or differently located—some of those voices from below are recognized, named, addressed in the speaking that happens in certain phases of moss-glow, certain temperature drops that make the boundary between categories permeable.

The dead do not decay quickly, in the cold, in the damp that limits rather than accelerates biological process. They mummify partially, slowly, becoming something between food and ancestor and geological specimen, their substances gradually incorporated into the stone's own slow chemistry, the body's water rejoining the general water, the body's salts crystallizing where evaporation concentrates them, becoming trade goods, becoming the strange salts that the Settled exchange for metal, for tools, for the technologies of elsewhere that they cannot produce themselves.

---

They believe, or tell stories, or maintain in speech patterns that might be either or neither, certain things about their place, their condition, their possibilities. The water is primary, not god but *condition*, the given against which all shaping must work. It remembers, they say, or something in it remembers—every rain that has fallen into the bowl, every body that has dissolved into it, every voice that has spoken above its surface, reflected back from the bowl's rim, trapped in the channels that carry sound beyond intention. To speak above still water is dangerous, the stories say, though they do it constantly, must do it, have developed the protocols that make it survivable: the certain hours, the certain modes, the recognized silences that mean *this is not for the water to carry*.

The moss is secondary, or derivative, the water's life made visible, the water's memory made biological. It grows on the dead, they say, because the dead are mostly water, because the transition between states is gradual, because the moss recognizes kinship that the living no longer perceive. The luminescence is the moss's dreaming, or the water's dreaming through the moss, or the dead's participation in both—explanations proliferate, no single one achieving dominance, the variety itself perhaps the point, the maintenance of interpretive possibility against the narrowing that certainty would bring.

The stone is oldest, or youngest, the paradox unresolved—old in its mineral existence, young in its current exposure, shaped by crisis that stripped away maturity, that made the bowl, that offered itself for settlement. The stone does not speak, they say, or speaks too slowly to hear, its voices the ones from below, the resonances that may be human, may be geological, may be something that has learned to imitate both. To build in stone is to join this conversation, to add one's own slowness to the stone's, to become part of what will persist, what will speak, when the current generation has joined the voices below.

---

They change, these Settled, or are changed, by the long insistence of their environment. The fungal partnerships become more complex, more necessary, in bodies that have lived longer in this damp—the young who leave, who try to live in drier places, reporting sickness, wrongness, the lack of something they cannot name, returning or dying or persisting in incomplete adaptation. The joint flexibility, the compact build, the particular hearing: these intensify in generational time, or so the comparing of old stories with current bodies suggests, though the evidence is ambiguous, shaped by the need to believe in belonging, in the rightness of adaptation.

They become, some of them, something that is not quite what they were when they arrived, when they were born, when they first drew breath in the lowest levels' accumulated warmth. The oldest, those who have persisted longest in the deepest spaces, begin to resemble the stone itself, their skins taking on something of its color, its texture, the smoothness that running water achieves over centuries too brief to count against stone's memory. They move less, these oldest ones, becoming something between ancestor and architecture, occupying space that gradually ceases to distinguish between body and structure, between the living that maintains and the maintained that lives.

---

They are not happy, these Settled, in any simple sense—happiness requiring something of warmth, of ease, of the body's unchallenged operation that this place does not permit. They are not unhappy either, or the unhappiness is so continuous as to become medium, atmosphere, the quality of existence itself rather than its interruption. They *are*, with the intensity of organisms that have abandoned breadth for depth, that occupy their constraint with the ferocity of the numerically desperate, covering, coating, *being* the limited space rather than *filling* any larger volume.

And they continue, which is perhaps the most that can be said, the most that needs to be said. They build upward because the water limits their spread. They build downward because the dead require placement, because the voices from below require answer, because the stone's conversation requires new participants. They maintain the channels, the fungal partnerships, the complex social choreography of warmth-sharing and sound-suppression and the controlled fermentation that makes scarcity bearable. They birth in groups, die into the depths, trade at the rim for what the bowl does not provide, return or do not return, persist or do not persist, the margin always thin, the adaptation always incomplete, the next generation always provisional against the possibility of the rise that will persist, the storm that will not recede, the geological mid-gesture that will complete itself in some new configuration that the Settled cannot imagine, toward which they nonetheless build, nonetheless speak, nonetheless wait in the permanent witness to recent rain, the liminal quality of a world still processing its own saturation, the after that has become continuous, that has become *is*, that has become the only when they have ever known.
