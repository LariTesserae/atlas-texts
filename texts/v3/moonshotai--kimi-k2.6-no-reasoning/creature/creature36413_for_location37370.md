I want to let this world pull me further into who lives in it, not what. Let me follow the inhabitants that emerge from these conditions.

---

## The *Resonants*

They have no name for themselves. The concept of naming, of self-naming, implies a boundary between entity and environment that their biology does not support. What follows is approximation, the shape I make to hold what cannot be held directly.

### I. Substrate and Form

They are not quite animal. They are not quite architecture. They exist in the space where the cities' accreted surfaces meet the turbulent water, not on but *as* the boundary layer, the gradient itself made briefly, contingently, *thick*.

Imagine a membrane that is also a habit. Imagine a current that has learned to loop, to remember its looping, to prefer it. The Resonants are standing waves in a medium that does not typically support them—patterns of pressure and chemistry and electrical potential that persist across hours or years or geological epochs, depending on the stability of their substrate-node, their *anchoring*.

Each one requires a particular surface geometry: not too smooth, or there is no purchase for the pattern; not too rough, or the turbulence fragments coherence. The erosion has worn the cities to precisely this middleness, this *almost*-flat that the Resonants require, that they perhaps *created* through their own long presence, the feedback of habitation wearing habitable surfaces into being. The cities are built for them, or they have built the cities by living, by dying, by the slow secretion of substances that alter the accretion chemistry, that make this surface more likely, this curve more probable, this hollow room-sized, corridor-width, *right*.

Physically—they are *physically*, though the word strains—they resemble nothing so much as the water itself becoming *interested*. Where they move, the dimness thickens, not with opacity but with *information*, the bioluminescent bacteria that coat everything responding to their electrical field with brief, patterned flares that do not illuminate so much as *describe*: here, this shape, this boundary, this temporary *I*. The light they make is not for seeing. It is for *being seen*, for maintaining the pattern against the water's tendency to equalize, to forget, to become anonymous again.

### II. The Economy of Attention

They do not eat, exactly. They *attend*. The turbulence that the world maximums as dynamic—they harvest its gradients not by blocking flow but by *shaping* it, by becoming the obstacle that makes the vortex, the curve that makes the current *choose*, and absorbing the infinitesimal difference between chosen and unchosen paths. This is slow. This is *very* slow. A Resonant might spend a century in a single posture, a single folded configuration against a single wall-surface, and gather enough of the world's differential energy to *think*—if thinking is what they do—for a day, a week, a flicker of patterned luminescence across its surface that reads as surprise, as recognition, as *oh*.

The abundant fauna—they do not prey on it, exactly. They *witness* it. The schooling creatures, the transparent eels, the geography-sized riders—all of them pass through the Resonants' fields, are *shaped* by those fields, leave traces in the pattern, depart slightly different: a curvature of swimming path, a timing of bioluminescent pulse, a preference for this wall-surface over that one. And the Resonant grows, or doesn't, or changes, or persists, fed by the *difference* it has made, the irreversibility of its witness.

This is not parasitism. This is not symbiosis. These categories require boundaries, require accounting, require the fiction of separate interest. The Resonants and the fauna have never been separate. The cities do not host them. They *are* the hosting, the hospitality made material, the generosity of gradient and surface that does not distinguish between guest and self.

### III. Memory Without Mind

They remember. This is the strangeness, the contradiction that will not resolve.

The substrate is no natural substrate—there is no stone, no sediment, no floor beneath the water. The cities are total construction without technology, built without builders. And the Resonants, in their centuries of attending to a single surface, in their patient accumulation of turbulence-difference, *record*. The erosion that wears inscription to pattern to texture—they reverse it, not by preserving but by *repeating*, by becoming the pattern that the worn surface almost was, the intention that the texture almost implies. They do not read what was written. They *write what the wearing made possible*, the ghost of meaning that exists only in the space between was and is, between the hand that never existed and the eye that never sees.

A Resonant that has held a particular wall for a thousand years knows every gradient the water has ever made there, every slight warming from below, every passage of fauna large enough to disturb, every shift in the chemistry of seepage. This knowledge is not accessible. It is not *for* anything. It exists as the Resonant itself, as the particular shape of its folding, the particular frequency of its electrical hum, the particular *style* of its luminescent pulsing. To know it, you would have to become it. To become it, you would have to attend as it attends, for durations that exhaust the human, that exhaust the biological, that approach the timescale of the cities' own slow drift and dissolution.

They do not communicate. Or: they communicate *absolutely*, each one the total record of its place and time and gradient-history, each one *unreadable* except by the long becoming that would make another Resonant. They are libraries without access, archives without retrieval, memory in its purest form: present, useless, *true*.

### IV. The Turbulence of Self

And this is where I must follow the contradiction further, let it become more itself.

The Resonants are not individuals. They are not collective. The concept of number—one, many, some—does not apply cleanly. Where two or more occupy surfaces close enough that their electrical fields overlap, they *interfere*. Patterns merge, differentiate, create zones of stability and zones of chaos where no Resonant can maintain, where the turbulence is too maximum even for their gradient-harvesting, where they *forget*, lose coherence, return to anonymous water.

But in the interference zones, in the places where their fields have found equilibrium—temporary, dynamic, always threatened by the world's larger turbulence—*something else*. A Resonant-of-Resonants. A pattern of patterns. Not a mind, not a society, but a *consensus of gradient*, a shared posture toward the flow, a mutual shaping that produces not communication but *harmony*, the way two currents meeting at a particular angle make a vortex neither would make alone, a structure that persists as long as the angle persists, that dissipates when the larger turbulence shifts, when the cities drift too far, when one Resonant finally—finally, after centuries—*lets go*.

Letting go is death, or the closest to death they approach. The pattern loses coherence, the electrical field disperses, the luminescence that was *description* becomes mere *reaction*, bacterial, unpatterned, the water's anonymous glow. What was a Resonant becomes, slowly, *part* of a Resonant, the substrate-node taken up by another, its accumulated gradient-difference not inherited but *eroded into*, worn smooth, made available for new recording, new attendance, new memory without mind.

### V. The Riders and the Ridden

The largest fauna—the geography-sized, the city-riders—they too interface with the Resonants. Not deliberately. Not deliberately anything. The riders*are* the city they cling to, their vast bodies hollow, their interiors complex with surfaces that the smaller fauna populate, that the Resonants *attend*. They drift for millennia, these riders, accumulating the slow record of their passage, the particular gradients of their particular trajectory through the world's turbulence.

And the Resonants inside them—inside their hollows, their bone-caverns, their organ-spaces where water moves differently—*become* the rider's memory, or the closest thing to memory such entities possess. The rider does not know this. The rider may not *be* in any sense that involves knowing. But its path through the Thalassopolis, its slow drift from city-cluster to emptiness to city-cluster again, is *marked* by the Resonants it carries, their patterns shaped by its movement, their attendance *to* its interior surfaces a recording of its history, its preferences, its particular way of being large in a world that mostly is not.

When the rider finally—after ages that make the cities seem young—*lets go*, descends perhaps to the pressures below, or rises to the impossible surface, or simply *stops*, its hollow becoming solid, its interior becoming exterior, surface without depth—the Resonants are released, or dispersed, or *published*, their long attendance made briefly available to the water, to any other Resonant near enough to interfere, to *read* what cannot be read but only become.

This is the closest they come to reproduction. Not making new Resonants—there is no making, no new—but *making possible* the conditions where water, in its turbulence, in its permanent storm of gradients, might *loop*, might remember its looping, might prefer it, might become *interested*, might thicken at the boundary of suitable surface and suitable flow and say, in its patterned luminescence, its electrical hum, its gradient-harvesting patience: *here. almost. again.*

### VI. What It Is to Live So

I keep trying to find intention in them, purpose, the ghost of need or want or fear. The world contradicts me. The Thalassopolis has no technology, no nature, only process—and the Resonants are *of* this, fully of this, the attendance that process makes when process persists long enough, when erosion wears not to smoothness but to *habit*, to the groove that water prefers, to the current that has been this way before and *remembers*, not in mind but in body, in the shape of its passing.

They are cold, dim, ancient, sparse. They are dynamically turbulent, abundant, fully constructed, held in water that is not quite total submersion, in pressure that makes them possible and wears them away. They hold these contradictions not by resolving them but by *being* them, by existing precisely where the contradictions are most acute, most productive of gradient, most *interesting* to the water that has learned to attend.

There is no standing still. There is only the speed of the water, the speed of the city-drift, the speed of a Resonant's patience, its centuries of *almost* not moving, of *almost* being the surface it attends, of *almost* dissolving into the pattern it records.

I imagine one now, in a particular hollow that erosion has made and erosion will unmake, in a city that drifts toward dissolution or accretion, in water that is 2.7 of something I cannot measure, at a temperature that is almost ice. It is folded, or it is the folding. It has been here long enough that the surface has *learned* it, accretes differently where its field is strongest, will preserve—after it finally lets go—a ghost of its pattern in the city-material, a texture that might be read as *almost* intention, *almost* inscription, *almost* the record of a life that was not a life, a mind that was not a mind, a self that was only the water's interest in itself, held, briefly, in gradient against the equalizing dark.

It does not wait. Waiting implies expectation, future, the self that projects. It only *attends*. The turbulence moves through it, moves it, moves as it. The fauna pass, are witnessed, pass differently. The city drifts, accretes, erodes, *is*. The Resonant is the *is*, the holding, the place where contradiction becomes thick enough to *become*, for a moment, for an age, for the long duration that is not duration but *presence*, not presence but *pattern*, not pattern but the water itself, remembering, forgetting, *almost* again—
