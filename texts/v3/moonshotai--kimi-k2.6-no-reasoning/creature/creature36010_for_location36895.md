The inhabitants of Vervain are, first and simply, *people who have learned to press their thumbs into stone and wait*.

Not passively. The waiting is active, attentive, a discipline. They call it *duramen*—the art of sustained pressure, of maintaining contact with something that may or may not remember your touch. Children learn it early, pressing fingers into cliff faces on the lower terraces, chanting numbers, seeing how long they can hold before the stone hardens or their patience breaks. Some have fingerprints permanently blurred from decades of this practice, the whorls softened into something between identity and anonymity.

But this is only the beginning of them. Let me go deeper.

---

The oldest residents, the ones whose families have pressed their thumbs into Vervain's stone for generations, carry a particular quality in their bodies. They move *slightly out of phase* with their own gestures—you'll see a woman reach for a cup and her hand arrive a half-second before or after you expect, as if she has learned to accommodate temporal drift in her own motions. They are not slow, exactly. They are *calibrated to a different metronome*, one that includes the possibility of interruption, of revision, of the world changing its mind mid-action. A grandmother on the middle terraces might begin a sentence, pause for seventeen minutes while reality adjusts somewhere in the canyon—a distant *crack*, a momentary vertigo—and complete the thought as if no gap existed. Her grandchildren understand. They have learned to hold the thread of meaning across discontinuities, to build sentences that can survive being folded in time.

These families have developed *household saints*, though they wouldn't use that word. Figures of provisional reverence: the Patience of the Pylon, the Forgetting of the First Floor, the Moth's Unfinished Message. Small shrines in those transitional ground-level spaces, offerings of things they don't mind losing. A cup left overnight might be gone by morning, or doubled, or transformed into something that was never a cup but fits the shrine's need precisely. They do not question this. The questioning is itself a form of impatience, a refusal of duramen. They thank whatever comes, whatever goes.

---

The engineers of the Bureau of Provisional Permanence are a different species, or have become one. They arrive from elsewhere, usually—young, brilliant, drawn by the challenge of building in unbuilding stone. They stay, or some of them stay, and the staying changes them. They develop *foundation sense*, a proprioception of instability, the ability to walk a terrace and feel in their knees where the stone is still deciding, where the next decade's drift will carry a building, whether a pylon needs driving deeper or shallower to catch the right stratum of almost-hardness.

The ones who stay longest begin to *dream in structural diagrams*, their sleep filled with flexible joints and load-bearing ambiguities. They wake with solutions to problems the city hasn't presented yet, preemptive architectures for conditions that may never emerge. The Bureau archives these dreams, catalogs them, sometimes builds from them. There are buildings in Vervain designed for gravity-negotiable zones that have never manifested, standing perfectly ordinary in ordinary gravity, patient, *waiting to be useful*, their strange geometries a kind of hope.

The engineers who leave—and many do, the work consuming them or the city refusing to let them consume it—carry something away. They build elsewhere, in solid places, and their buildings are *slightly wrong*, slightly too patient, foundations dug deeper than geology demands, joints more flexible than stability requires. They are haunted by Vervain's provisionality, by the memory of stone that could be pressed. Their buildings elsewhere stand too long, survive disasters that should destroy them, persist with a stubbornness that is itself a form of conversation with a city they left.

---

The factory workers. The citizens-by-labor, their residence permits stamped in the products they make. What are they?

They are *concentrators*. This is the word they use among themselves, though not with outsiders. Their work is not about the goods, which leave half-finished and arrive elsewhere complete, real enough to use. Their work is about the *making-real*, the sustained attention to partial completion that allows completion to happen somewhere else. They understand, in their bodies if not their philosophy, that Vervain is a factory for coherence, that their labor is a form of duramen applied to the world itself.

A woman on the cog-assembly line, attaching gears that don't quite mesh yet, holds each in her hands until she feels the *rightness* begin to form, then passes it on. She doesn't know what the gear will become, what machine elsewhere will complete it. She knows only that her attention, her sustained pressure of care, is necessary. The work is exhausting in ways that don't map to physical effort. She finishes a shift and needs hours of stillness, of letting her own reality re-solidify. She sits in the steam-gardens, among the black-leaved trees, and lets the warmth enter her, lets the ambiguity of the place restore her capacity for ambiguity.

The workers develop *product scars*. Marks on their hands, their forearms, from handling things that weren't fully solid, that left impressions in flesh as they left impressions in stone. These scars are status, history, readable by those who know how. A man with spiral marks on his palms has worked the helical assemblies, the parts that become, elsewhere, drills or screws or musical instruments. A woman with parallel lines across her knuckles has handled the sheet-goods, the metal that leaves Vervain as plates and arrives as roofs, as hulls, as shields. They read each other in passing, know each other's labor, share a solidarity that needs no union, no language.

---

Then there are the *rim-dwellers*, the ones who chose light over stability, who live in the brightness of the canyon's edge and accept the risk of sudden nonexistence. They are younger, usually, or older in a particular way, finished with the patience of the lower terraces. They cultivate *intensity*, the art of experiencing fully in moments that may not persist. Their parties are legendary, their art violent and immediate, their loves sudden and absolute and often brief. They press their thumbs into stone too, but harder, faster, wanting the impression *now*, before the stone hardens or they themselves unhappen.

Some of them fall. Not physically—the reality-breakdowns at the rim rarely manifest as simple gravity—but *out of coherence*, becoming stories that the city tells, messages read in moth-wings, furniture arranged in open air where a building used to be and still is, for them, for whoever remembers. They persist as *legend*, as caution, as aspiration. The rim-dwellers who remain, who survive their own intensity, develop a different patience—not the duramen of sustained pressure but the *waiting of the coiled spring*, the held breath before action. They are dangerous, beautiful, essential. The city needs their light, their art, their willingness to risk nonexistence for the sake of being fully present.

---

The moth-readers. I must speak of them, though they resist speaking of themselves.

They are not a guild, not officially. The Bureau does not recognize them, or recognizes them only as a public health concern, the swarms being inconvenient, their interpretations unreliable. But they persist, have persisted as long as Vervain has had moths and people to watch them.

A moth-reader learns first to *not-see*, to let the wing-patterns remain unresolved, to resist the mind's hunger for meaning. This takes years. The temptation is strong, especially in Vervain where meaning is itself provisional, where a clear message is a treasure beyond price. They sit in the swarms, on the brighter terraces, in the evenings when the moths emerge, and practice not-reading until the not-reading itself becomes a kind of reading, a receptivity to patterns too large or too small for ordinary perception.

And then, sometimes, a pattern *resolves*. Not because they seek it but because they have prepared the conditions for resolution. A message clear enough to change a life, a policy, a neighborhood's understanding. The reader speaks it, or doesn't speak it—there is debate among them about the ethics of transmission. Some messages are for the city alone, they say. Some are for the moment of reading and then released back into ambiguity. The ones who speak, who act on what they read, carry a particular weight in Vervain's politics, its art, its slow transformations. They are consulted, feared, sometimes exiled to the unreliable zones where their readings matter less, or differently.

The moth-readers who have practiced longest begin to *change in the swarms*. Their own skin takes on something of the moth's indeterminacy, their features slightly unfixed, recognizable but never quite the same between meetings. They become, themselves, messages that resist reading, living embodiments of the city's own dream of itself. Whether this is transformation or revelation, whether they were always moths who learned to be human, or humans learning to be moth—this is among the questions they refuse to answer.

---

The children of Vervain. They deserve their own description, being neither simply small adults nor simply children as elsewhere understood.

They are born, usually, in the steam-gardens, the warmest most stable places, attended by engineers who have studied the provisional obstetrics, the techniques of ensuring a birth that persists. Even so, some don't. Some children arrive already *partial*, existing in states of imminent arrival like the river's fish, like the fossils the children later dig from dry mud. The Vervain response to this is not grief, exactly, or not only grief. It is *inclusion*, the child welcomed into family memory, into household shrine, into the stories told in the unreliable zones where reality is more negotiable. They grow up there, these partial children, in the stories, in the open-air furniture, in the moth-wings that sometimes carry their never-quite-features.

The children who arrive fully, who persist, grow up *bilingual in solidity and provision*. They speak the language of elsewhere, learned from parents or books or the trains that arrive from more committed realities, and they speak the language of Vervain, where nouns decline according to certainty, where verbs conjugate across temporal discontinuities. They play in the first floors, in the spaces of acceptable loss, building structures of habit that may or may not persist, practicing the architecture of lives carried lightly. They dig for fish-fossils in dry mud and understand, without being told, that these are not dead things but *waiting things*, imminent arrivals held in mineral pause.

They press their thumbs into stone and count, and their counting is different from their grandparents', faster sometimes, or slower, or in patterns that skip numbers, that include numbers not yet invented. They are the city's experiment with itself, its attempt to discover whether provisionality can be inherited, whether duramen can be learned or must always be struggled toward. Some will leave, on the Incline when it runs, on the longer road, carrying their double-language into solid places where it will mark them as strange, as visitors from a country that doesn't exist on ordinary maps. Some will stay, and press, and wait, and become the grandparents teaching chanted numbers to children not yet born, not yet persistent, already present in the city's dream of its own continuation.

---

And finally, finally, the city itself. For Vervain is inhabited not only by its people, its moths, its fish-that-are-ideas-of-fish, its birds of miraculous lung. The city *lives*, or something lives in it, through it, as it.

This is not metaphor, or not only. The Bureau philosophers debate it, the engineers measure for it, the moth-readers read it in wings that resolve, sometimes, into city-maps, into building-plans for structures not yet built, into the geometry of a canyon still deciding its own shape. The city dreams, and the dream is Vervain, and the dreamers are all who press their thumbs into stone, who work the factories of partial completion, who risk the rim's light, who read and do not read the moth-swarm's messages.

What does it want, this city-life? What does any life want—to persist, to become more itself, to discover what it can be. Vervain wants *coherence*, as the philosophers say, but coherence of a particular kind. Not the solidity of elsewhere, the hard finality of ordinary stone. It wants to *hold provisionality stable*, to be the place where becoming can become without becoming finished, where the already-was can persist without foreclosing the could-still-be. It wants to continue pressing its own thumb into the world's stone, leaving an impression that persists, that shapes, that never fully hardens into unchangeable form.

The inhabitants serve this wanting, or participate in it, or are themselves expressions of it. They cannot be separated from the city's life, any more than the moths can be separated from the swarms, the fish from the river's intermittent flow. They are *Vervain's way of experiencing itself*, of knowing its own provisional persistence, of dreaming its own continuation into futures that remain, blessedly, undecided.

You arrive, and the damp settles on you, and you learn to press and wait. You become, slowly, part of the city's body, its thought, its sustained pressure against the stone of reality that has not yet, that may never, fully harden. You leave your impression. You persist, or partially persist, or persist in the stories told in the unreliable zones, in the moth-wings' unreadable messages, in the furniture arranged in open air where you used to be, where you still are, where you are always about to arrive.
