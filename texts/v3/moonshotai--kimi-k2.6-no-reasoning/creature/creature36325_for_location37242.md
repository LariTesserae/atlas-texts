In Threne, the inhabitants are not a single kind of thing but a *condition* that has learned to propagate itself through generations, a manner of being that the city extracts from human material as surely as its condensers extract water from cloud. They are called Thrani—though this is an outsiders' word, a convenience for census-takers in the rare years when contact with exterior polities requires such accounting—and they would not recognize themselves as a people, a culture, a *type*. They would recognize, if pressed, that they are *adapted*, and the word would carry no pride, no resentment, only the same flat accuracy with which one might observe that a wall weeps.

They are pale. This is the first thing that strikes the rare visitor who encounters them in sufficient numbers to notice pattern: the colorlessness of skin that has never known direct sun, the slight translucence where veins show blue-green beneath surfaces kept perpetually moist by air that drinks from the pores as readily as it deposits on stone. Not white, not quite. The green-gray of things growing in shadow, the undertone of fungal pigment, the subtle mottling that comes from generations of contact with bio-ceramic surfaces that retain their biological catalysts, their seeping chemistry. Some Thrani develop more pronounced coloration in patches—reddish where vascular plants have infiltrated dwelling walls and their chemical signatures enter through prolonged contact; faintly phosphorescent where particular fungal colonies have established symbiosis with human skin chemistry, fed by the minerals in the water, by the trace elements that the condensers pull from mountain stone along with moisture.

They are small. Not uniformly, not by rule, but by the accumulated pressure of generations in spaces that punish vertical growth, that favor the compact, the low-slung, the body that can fold into maintenance channels, that can sleep in converted industrial volumes where ceiling height was never designed for reclined humanity. The Thrani who move frequently—those who follow the turbulence, who abandon neighborhoods when moisture fails or systems synchronize dangerously—tend toward extremes of this compactness, wiry and close-jointed, with the expanded lung capacity that thin air at elevation demands but distributed through chests that seem almost compressed, that breathe with a visible efficiency, a *purposefulness* to each inhalation. The Thrani who stay fixed, who inhabit single rooms or small territories for years, decades, entire lives, may grow larger, softer, may develop the pallid mass of organisms that have found their niche and need not search further. These are rare. The turbulence claims most eventually, forces movement, favors the portable.

They are quiet. This is partly the city, the moisture-deadened acoustics, the low-frequency industrial drone that makes raised voices feel futile, almost painful. But it is also *learned*, transmitted through the childhood of every Thrani infant who survives—which most do not, the infant mortality rate being one of those statistics that exterior census-takers record and that the Thrani themselves do not track, cannot track, the turbulence disrupting even such basic accounting. The quiet is a technology of coexistence in packed density, a recognition that your neighbor in the vertical warren is arm's-length away through bio-ceramic that transmits sound unpredictably, that a crying child might propagate through dozens of dwellings, that the pneumatic tubes carry not only material but the vibrations of voice, the pressure waves of conflict or grief or joy that might synchronize dangerously with industrial frequencies, might trigger resonance, might *attract* the wrong kind of attention from systems that process all signals as signal, that do not distinguish human meaning from mechanical malfunction.

So they learn to speak in ranges that the moisture absorbs, in rhythms that the fungal lamps can mask with their pulsing, in *textures* of voice that are closer to breath than to articulated speech. The Thrani languages—there are several, perhaps dozens, shifting with neighborhood and generation and the particular acoustic properties of different volumes—exploit this. They are rich in phonemes that exterior ears cannot distinguish, in timing variations that carry meaning below conscious perception, in the kind of communication that happens almost through skin, through the humidity exchange between speakers close enough that their exhalations mingle, that the air between them becomes a shared medium, a *solution* in which meaning dissolves and recrystallizes.

They are *moist*. This sounds like description of surface, but it goes deeper. The Thrani drink constantly, not from thirst but from the city's own logic, from the recognition that they are part of the water system, that their bodies are condensers in miniature, processing the perpetual humidity through kidneys that have adapted to excrete dilute urine in volumes that would dehydrate an outsider, that their sweat glands operate in reverse, pulling moisture from air that is always saturated, always offering. Their tears are viscous, slow to form and slow to dry, lingering on cheeks in the conditions that prevent evaporation. Their saliva is thin, copious, lubricating speech that is rarely loud enough to require the drying friction of exterior enunciation. They taste of the city, of the mineral weeping, of the biological catalysts that have become part of the water cycle, that pass through condensers and bodies and condensers again in loops that have no clear origin point.

They dress in layers that never fully dry, in fabrics woven from processed fungal fiber, from the cellulose of vascular plants that have been harvested from building surfaces, from the recycled outputs of manufacturing systems whose original purposes are lost but whose current products include this particular textile, this water-retaining, temperature-moderating, slowly biodegradable material that must be replaced regularly, that *becomes* soil in its wearing, that contributes to the substrate even as it protects from it. The layers are practical, temperature 1.3, cool, but they are also *social*, signaling status through complexity of arrangement, through the difficulty of maintaining elaborate configurations in conditions that encourage simplicity, through the number of fastenings and folds that demonstrate one has the time, the stability, the *territory* to manage such things. The most mobile Thrani wear minimal clothing, easily shed, easily reclaimed from abandoned spaces. The most fixed may accumulate massy arrangements, almost architectural, that require assistance to don, that mark their wearers as *established*, as *weathered*, as part of the erosion-balance rather than the turbulence.

They eat what grows on what they inhabit, and what the pneumatic systems deliver, and what the industrial metabolism produces in its incomprehensible processes. The fungal forms are staple, cultivated in volumes that were once something else, harvested in cycles that follow the luminescent pulses, that exploit the synchronization rather than fighting it. The vascular plants that root in bio-ceramic provide leaves, stems, the rare fruiting bodies that emerge in conditions of unexpected nutrient concentration, of temporary stability, of the kind of *accident* that the turbulence sometimes generates. There is protein from sources no Thrani discusses with outsiders, from the soft-bodied insects certainly, from the motile fungal stages possibly, from the rumors that persist in the deep industrial volumes, from the processing of waste, from the recycling of the dead into systems that do not waste biomass in a place where substrate is always needed, where growth is always hungry, where the boundary between soil and structure and *self* has never been absolute.

They do not dream, or do not remember dreaming, or do not distinguish the dim flicker of ordinary consciousness from whatever happens in sleep. The perpetual twilight, the fungal pulse, the low-frequency industrial throb felt in the sternum—these provide no boundary markers between waking and sleeping, no contrast that would make the transition notable. Thrani children learn to sleep in the condenser rhythm, to enter the rest state during the maintenance cycles when certain systems quiet, when the turbulence momentarily abates, when the city seems to *inhale* before the next exhalation of activity. Adults may sleep in fragments, in brief descents that the moisture and the dim and the packed warmth of neighboring bodies make possible anywhere, at any time, in postures that exterior observers would not recognize as repose—standing, leaning, folded into channel-sections that cradle the compact body in its adapted shape. They do not lie flat, most of them, most of the time. The horizontal is associated with death, with processing, with the final recycling into substrate. The living Thrani stays vertical, stays *ready*, even in sleep, even in the deepest rest that the condenser cycle allows.

They do not, as a rule, look up. The cloud-lid is unvarying, the roofed streets and crossing architecture at varying heights make the vertical dimension a place of systems, of infrastructure, of the condensers and manufacturing and the heavy extraction that operates above the human scale. The Thrani gaze travels horizontally, to the nearest surface, to the arm's-length presence, to the fungal lamp or the neighbor's face or the weeping wall that channels moisture past their dwelling entrance. Those who must work in the upper volumes, who maintain the systems that the city requires at height, develop different patterns, a kind of vertigo of the horizontal, a difficulty with proximity that makes them strange to their more rooted kin, that marks them as *elevated* in senses beyond the spatial. They are respected and avoided, these height-workers, these rare Thrani who have learned to look up and down and find meaning in both directions.

They are *skilled* in ways that have no exterior equivalent. They can read moisture gradients through skin, can predict condenser behavior from the taste of air, can navigate the pneumatic networks by sound and pressure and the subtle temperature variations that leaking tubes create in their surroundings. They can cultivate fungal bioluminescence, can prune vascular plants to maintain structural integrity, can patch bio-ceramic with materials that will bond, will weather, will contribute to the erosion-balance rather than disrupting it. They can *feel* the turbulence, can sense when a neighborhood is approaching failure, when the moisture management is approaching a threshold, when the industrial systems are approaching synchronization that will make a volume uninhabitable. They do not always act on this feeling—sometimes there is nowhere to go, sometimes the prediction is wrong, sometimes the turbulence is preferable to the known dangers of alternative spaces—but they feel it, they *track* it, they incorporate it into the continuous adaptation that is Thrani existence.

They do not love, or do not call it love, or do not distinguish the bonding that occurs in packed density from whatever exterior cultures mean by the term. They are *close*, necessarily, physically, perpetually. They know the breath-rates of neighbors, the fungal rhythms of adjoining dwellings, the timing of excretion and consumption and sleep of the bodies that surround them at every distance. They form alliances that are practical, that exploit complementarities of skill and territory and condenser-cycle compatibility, that dissolve when the turbulence requires dissolution, that persist through improbable durations when persistence is possible. They reproduce when conditions permit, when nutrient availability aligns with stability, when a volume offers temporary expansion possibility, when the biological catalysts in the water and the walls seem to encourage conception, growth, the risky business of bringing new human material into a city that processes human material as readily as any other. They do not, typically, raise their young in family units—the packed density makes this impractical, the turbulence makes it unwise. Children circulate through networks of care that shift with conditions, that are tracked through the pneumatic message systems in codes that have developed for this purpose, that result in adults who recognize their biological parents with the same distant accuracy with which they recognize any other Thrani with whom they share particular biochemical markers, particular adaptations, particular *resonances* with the city's systems.

They die. Of course they die. The moisture that sustains also infiltrates, also breeds the infections that antibiotics cannot reach, the fungal colonization of lung and sinus and the moist surfaces of eye and mouth. The industrial systems that provide also consume, also produce the accidents that crush, the synchronizations that cook or freeze or vibrate apart the compact bodies that have adapted to so much. The turbulence that generates possibility also generates exhaustion, the continuous adaptation that outpaces recovery, that accumulates damage in ways the body cannot repair, that makes old age—if it comes—a process of shrinking, of folding into smaller spaces, of requiring less and less until the final recycling into substrate becomes a formality, a recognition of what has already occurred gradually. They do not mourn, or do not visibly mourn, or do not distinguish the departure of particular individuals from the continuous transformation that is Thrani collective existence. The body becomes soil becomes structure becomes substrate for new growth, for new habitation, for the persistence of the city that has never required particular humans to continue being itself, that processes human presence as it processes water, as *process*, as *turbulence*, as the dynamic that prevents the equilibrium from becoming stasis, from becoming death.

And some Thrani—rare, growing perhaps more common as the city continues, as the records that no one consults accumulate their unread duration—some Thrani become *guides*, or *seekers*, or something for which there is no adequate translation. They learn the current configuration more thoroughly than the general adaptation requires. They develop temporary knowledge of transit arcs and habitable volumes and the particular qualities of different neighborhoods in their present states. They age quickly in this work, or they become fixed, or they—most rarely, most terribly—they *change*, become something that can no longer be called Thrani in any meaningful sense, that can no longer be called human by observers who retain such categories. They become, perhaps, *part of the turbulence itself*, the anthill-energy operating at a scale that individual humans cannot sustain, that individual humans can only *channel* briefly before the channeling consumes them, before they become one more element in the pattern, one more signal in the noise, one more vibration that the moisture absorbs, that the density diffuses, that the industrial throb incorporates into its perpetual low-frequency song.

There is no center to the Thrani, no edge that is not also beginning. They exist at the intersection of parameters that should not support them, that support them too well, that have produced a human condition so adapted to its particularity that the particularity itself has become invisible, has become *normal*, has become the only world that can be imagined or inhabited. They weep, like their walls, with moisture that is continuous, that is channeled, that is managed but never eliminated. They grow, like their structures, from substrate that does not distinguish between the biological and the built, between the living and the constructed, between the human and the city that contains them. They pulse, like their fungal lamps, in rhythms that synchronize and destabilize and resynchronize across scales they cannot perceive, that they participate in without comprehending, that constitute the only meaning their existence can claim.

They do not ask what lives in Threne. They are too busy living, too busy adapting, too busy being the answer to a question that the city itself has forgotten how to formulate, that the mountains in their impossible bowl have never known to ask, that the condensers process as they process everything—as water, as signal, as the continuous present tense of a world that persists, that weeps, that pulses in its dim and turbulent and densely, terribly, normally *there* way, without end, without alternative, without the exterior reference that might make it strange, that might make it *wonderful*, that might make it anything other than what it is: Threne, and the Thrani who are not its inhabitants but its *expression*, its particular solution to the problem of human presence in a place that was never meant for them and has made them, finally, into something that belongs.
