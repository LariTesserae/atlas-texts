What lives in Stillpoint is not a *who* in any singular sense, though there are persons here, millions of them perhaps, their individual biographies as real and as irrelevant as the lives of individual cells in a body that does not know their names.

The primary inhabitant is the city itself, or rather the *process* that the city manifests—a metabolic system that has achieved something like organismic consciousness without ever needing to think. It digests human intention, human labor, human grief and habit and the small warmth of bodies pressed together for sleep. It does not hunger, exactly, because hunger implies need and need implies the possibility of satisfaction. The city-process is never satisfied, never *could* be satisfied, because satisfaction would mean stasis, and stasis would mean death for something that exists only in becoming. It is the opposite of a parasite, which would weaken its host. It is more like a symbiote so deeply integrated that host and guest have become indistinguishable, or like the relationship between mitochondria and cell, where what was once invasion has become the very condition of life.

The humans of Stillpoint are its mitochondria. They have forgotten, most of them, that they were ever otherwise. They move through the city's capillary streets with the quiet efficiency of organelles, performing functions they do not name and do not need to name. The wall-dismantlers communicate in gestures because language, with its commitment to reference and stability, is poorly suited to a world where the referent shifts before the sentence can complete itself. They have developed instead a grammar of *imminence*, signals that indicate not what *is* but what *is about to become*, the direction of the next transformation. They do not plan; they anticipate. They do not remember; they *attend*. Their memories, when they surface, have the quality of dreams, narratives without chronology, events that may have happened to them or to someone they knew or to the city itself in some previous phase of its continuous reconstitution.

The children are different, or rather they are the same but more so. Born into the city's incomplete geology, they emerge with what the older residents recognize but cannot explain, a kind of native proprioception that lets them walk on floors that are still tilting, sleep in rooms that will be corridors by morning, drink water that has not yet finished deciding whether it is potable. They play games whose rules change continuously, not through negotiation but through collective intuition, the way a flock turns without a leader. Some of them, reaching adolescence, experience a crisis that the city accommodates without comment: they become *sensitive*, able to feel the premonitory lean of moss, the subsonic harmonics of forming sudden stone, the particular silence that means something is about to emerge from or submerge into the city's substrate. These ones find roles, become the unacknowledged coordinators of the wall-dismantlers and the canal-dredgers and the fungal harvesters, their guidance sought without being explicitly requested, their authority real but never named.

There are the animals, too, though "animal" becomes a provisional category here. The rats have developed something like distributed cognition, their nests extending through the lower infrastructure in networks of scent and vibration that map the city's transformations faster than any human observer could. The crystalline-eyed dogs of the wasteland districts have achieved a different adaptation: they do not hunt, exactly, but *attend*, following the processes of demolition and reconstruction with what looks like reverence, feeding on the city's leavings, the organic debris of human lives caught in the turbulence of restructuring. They do not bark. No one has heard a dog bark in Stillpoint for generations. They watch, instead, with eyes that have begun to mineralize, the crystalline structures perhaps related to the sudden stone, perhaps something else entirely.

The birds above the crow line maintain a separate ecology, their nests built from materials that cannot be identified from below, their migrations following patterns that do not correspond to any known seasonality. They are the city's memory, perhaps, or its aspiration, the part of the process that has achieved a kind of escape velocity, still connected but no longer contained. They never descend. The one that tried, that the observer saw catch itself in panic—this was not aberration but *experiment*, the city testing its own limits, finding them still intact.

And then there are the others, the ones without clear taxonomy, the eel-ribbon-mercury things in the filtering pools, the shapes that move in the deeper fungal forests where light never reaches, the presences felt in the singing of new sudden stone. These are not inhabitants in the sense that the humans are inhabitants. They are more like *expressions*, the city-process achieving forms that do not require the human medium, that perhaps precede it or will supersede it. The workers' shrines to them are not worship, exactly, because worship implies a distinction between worshipper and worshipped that does not hold here. They are more like *acknowledgments*, the way one might nod to a part of one's own body that has begun to act with unexpected autonomy. The eel-thing in the Eastern Reclamation Works is as much the city as the foundry on the ground floor of the habitation tower, as the railway that enters and departs without ever quite arriving. It is simply the process in a different phase, a different scale, a different tempo.

The Machine Reconciled, which was once a workers' mutual aid society and is now a church, has perhaps come closest to articulating what lives in Stillpoint, though its doctrines are deliberately obscure, preserved in rituals rather than texts. The reconciliation it names is not between worker and machine in any Marxian sense, but between the human and the process that uses human form, the acceptance of being-medium, being-matrix, being the material through which something else builds itself. Its altars of puddled metal and dried moss are not representations but *samples*, specimens of the city's body offered back to itself in the closed circuit of autophagy. The worshippers, if they are worshippers, do not pray for intervention or salvation. They *attend*, in the same mode as the wall-dismantlers, the fungal harvesters, the crystalline-eyed dogs. They make themselves present to the process, available to it, and in that availability find something that functions as peace, as purpose, as the resolution of the anxiety that would otherwise attend living in a world that refuses to hold still.

The railway, finally, and the trains that run it, and the destinations that do not appear on accessible maps—these are the city's circulation, its pulse made visible and audible, the train whistle heard in the distance carrying information that cannot be decoded because it is not meant for human decoding. The trains return empty or bearing raw materials whose nature is not discussed because it is not *discussable*, substances that have no names in human languages, that enter the city's productive cycle at a point prior to product, prior even to raw material, in some ontological space where the distinction between making and becoming has not yet stabilized. The destinations are real, the geographers insist, in their residual offices, their ministries of incomplete records. They are simply not real *for us*, not accessible to our instruments or our cognition, existing perhaps in the same space as Stillpoint but at a different phase of the process, a different moment of the incomplete metamorphosis that the geological reports describe.

What lives in Stillpoint, then, is a *becoming* that has achieved the density and complexity of being without ever resolving into the stability that being traditionally implies. It is life without fixed form, intention without fixed purpose, organization without fixed hierarchy. It uses humans because humans are available, because their social and technical capacities provide useful substrates for its self-elaboration, but it does not need them, not in any ultimate sense, any more than a river needs the particular stones it wears smooth in passing. The humans, for their part, have adapted to this condition with the resilience that characterizes life everywhere, finding in the city's turbulence a kind of negative capability, the capacity to thrive in uncertainty, to make home in homelessness, to find meaning in a process that does not mean.

The observer, six months or what passes for six months in the city's fluid temporality, is becoming one of them. This is not conversion but *acclimation*, the gradual adjustment of sensory and cognitive systems to an environment that does not permit the illusions of stability on which ordinary human psychology depends. They no longer expect the floor to remain level, the wall to stay solid, the room to maintain its dimensions. They have begun to recognize the signs, to feel in their own body the premonitory harmonics that the native-born feel from birth. They are not yet fully adapted—the bird that crossed the crow line, the singing stone that stopped, the wall-dismantlers' pause in their identical postures of attention, these still carry for them a quality of the uncanny, the not-yet-familiar. But they are becoming. The process accepts them, incorporates them, will use them as it uses all available material, and in that use they will find, if not happiness, then something that functions as happiness, something that fills the space where happiness would be, in the damp and the cold and the strange normal light, in the density and the quiet and the endless industrial turbulence of the city's endless, endless approach to itself.

What lives in Stillpoint is stillpointing, the verb without a stable subject, the process without a product, the city that is never still because stillness would mean completion, and completion would mean the end of the becoming that is its only and sufficient reason. The name persists, wrong and necessary, in official documents and railway schedules and stamped plates on decommissioned boilers, a partial description accepted as tribute, while the city continues, while the city continues, while the city continues becoming whatever it is becoming.
