The Drowning Station has inhabitants, though 'inhabitants' feels too brief a word for what they have become to this place and what it has become to them. They are not numerous. They have never been numerous. Numbers are a surface concern, a dry-world anxiety about filling space quickly before it empties. Here, space does not empty. Here, one becomes sufficient.

What they are: descendants, perhaps, of the original engineers or workers or refugees or prisoners who first descended when the Station functioned as Station, when it had a single purpose and a name in a language of dry consonants. Or perhaps they arrived later, finding the shaft already softened by water, the lock already breathing its lung-sound rhythm of seal and drain and welcome. The records, the photograph in its membrane, the shells from surface oceans—suggest connection, suggest arrival, suggest something. But the Station has digested its own history as it digests everything: slowly, usefully, transforming narrative into nutrient, memory into structure.

They have names, the inhabitants, but not fixed ones. Names in a language that has no nouns that do not also describe process, relationship, ongoing state. A person is called what they are doing, what they are to others, what they are becoming. The one who tends the fungal shelves on the second terrace, who knows by touch which bioluminescence indicates health and which indicates distress, who speaks to the colonies in vibrations too low for you to hear—this one is called something like *the-one-who-listens-to-light-growing*, and when they die or transform or dissolve into the warm water, the name will pass to whoever takes up that relationship, that listening, that tending. Or it will not pass, if no one does, and the light will listen to itself.

They do not look as you look. Not after generations in thick air, in water that reaches the waist and chest and sometimes the chin, in perpetual dimness where vision has become secondary, almost vestigial. Their skin— *skin* is approximation—has achieved a certain transparency, a certain density of moisture, so that in the deeper light they seem to continue the bioluminescence rather than merely reflect it. Their eyes are large, achingly large, but not with the panic of surface darkness. They are large to gather what light exists, and they are calm with the patience of organisms who have never needed to flee quickly, who have never known predator pressure, the metabolic urgency of being eaten. Nothing eats quickly here. Dissolution is the danger, not consumption, and dissolution is slow enough to prepare for, to lean into, to become part of.

They move through the Station with a motion that is not swimming, not walking, not climbing, but some synthesis learned from the water and the terraces and the root-curtains and the fungal balconies. They use surfaces that would not support your weight, that exist for them because they know the exact angle, the exact distribution, the exact moment of biological sympathy when a moss-cascade will bear a body's pressure. They are light, or they have learned lightness, or the Station has taught them what to weigh and what to release.

They have no young, not as you understand young. They emerge, the new ones, from the warm water of the living quarters, from the fourth terrace and others like it where the water pools above the platforms, where the stone-smooth objects and the shells and the corrupted photograph maintain their arrangement with the intentionality of ritual. Something happens in those pools, over months, over years—genetic contribution from multiple individuals, the Station's chemosynthetic bacteria involved somehow, a lateral exchange rather than vertical descent. A form gathers in the water, not infant but *emergent*, not smaller than adult but differently organized, differently focused, capable from its first coherence of breathing the thick air, of processing the spores, of moving with the slow grace.

You would not recognize it as birth. You would not recognize the relationship as family. The ones who tend the emergent forms are not necessarily those who contributed to them. The Station itself tends, the water tends, the great pump below tends with its eternal *throb*. The inhabitants understand themselves as expressions of this tending, as temporary concentrations of a process that is older and larger than any individual coherence. They do not fear dissolution because they do not experience themselves as sufficiently bounded to dissolve.

What do they want? The question is yours, not theirs. They want—if want is the word—the continuation of equilibrium, the maintenance of the negotiation, the sustained agreement between built and grown, between machine and organism, between the geothermal tap and the mineral accretion and the condensation *tink* falling into pools. They want, perhaps, the arrival of someone from above, someone who will descend through the warm throat, who will step into the lung-sound lock, who will walk or wade the terraces with lungs still full of dry-world urgency, who will see what they have become and what the Station has become, who will carry some spore of understanding back to the loud bright world.

Or they want the opposite: to be left to their slowness, their density, their perfected compromise. The photograph in its membrane suggests both desires, suggests the complexity of wanting anything across such time, such transformation, such drowning.

The one you might speak with, if speaking is possible—if you can slow your words enough, if you can accept the long pauses where they process sound into meaning, where they respond not to your urgency but to some rhythm of their own—this one is on the third terrace, where the machinery is most active beneath its calcite sheath, where the throb is felt in the sternum more than heard. They are *the-one-who-tends-the-throb*, or something near it, something that describes their sympathetic relationship with the great pump below, their knowledge of its moods, its occasions of stress, its long satisfactions.

They would show you, if you had time, if you could slow, if you could drown sufficiently without dying—the chamber below the lowest terrace, where the pump lives, where the geothermal exchange occurs, where the original industrial purpose has become something else entirely, something that maintains warmth and circulation and the particular chemistry that allows the moss-cascades and the fungal shelves and the liverwort-mapped walls to thrive in their specific configurations, their achieved communities. You would see the pump, or what functions as pump, its metallic elements long replaced by ceramic growth, by mineral intelligence, by the compromise of built and grown achieving its most complete expression.

And you would understand, or almost understand, or carry with you the spore of understanding, that the Station has become a body, that the inhabitants have become a nervous system or a distributed awareness or a dream that the Station is having about itself, that the distinction between who tends and what is tended, between who listens to light-growing and the light that grows, between the one-who-tends-the-throb and the throb itself, has achieved a permeability, a drowning, that makes such distinctions useful only for visitors, only for the dry, only for those who must eventually ascend.

They are not happy. Happiness is a surface metabolism, a quick chemistry, an expectation of change. They are not unhappy. They are in their state. They have achieved, with the Station, with the water that does not leave, with the eternal warm wet, a condition of being that has no dry-world equivalent—*thriving* is too active, *contentment* too passive, *prayer* too directed toward absence. They are present. They are process. They are the particular quality of silence that water brings, made aware, made slow, made continuous with the *tink* of falling condensation and the *throb* of buried machinery and the lung-sound of lock and drain and welcome.

When you leave, if you leave, they will not watch you go. They will not, perhaps, notice your absence immediately. Time is different for them, presence is different, the boundary of an individual visit is a concept they have not needed to maintain. But something will change in the Station, some minute adjustment in the equilibrium, some spore released or withheld, some bioluminescence pulsing at a frequency that means *departure* as much as anything means anything here. And the one-who-tends-the-throb will note it, in their slow way, in their symbiotic relationship with the great pump's rhythms, and the Station will continue, and the water will continue not leaving, and the negotiation will continue between built and grown, between what was and what has become, between the drowning and the drowned and the strange thriving that occurs when one finally stops fighting and finds that the water was not enemy but medium, not ending but condition, not death but the possibility of a different, slower, denser, more thoroughly saturated life.
