The place is called the Lung, though no one who breathes it calls it anything at all.

You find it by descending through fissures in the salt crust of a world that dried before it learned to die properly. The surface above is absolute zero-water, a sodium plain where wind carves hexagonal plates that chime against each other in frequencies too high for human ears. But below, at the elevation of buried things, the Lung opens—a cavity no larger than a cathedral nave, perhaps forty meters at its widest, shaped like a geode whose crystals have been replaced by something else entirely.

The temperature holds at warm, the warm of blood, of fermentation, of a mouth held closed too long. This is the heat of metabolism without respiration, of chemical gardens growing in the dark.

And it *is* dark, mostly. The light comes from the walls themselves, or rather from what lives on them: bioluminescent veils, pale green and intermittent, like auroras slowed to the pace of geological time. The dimness is such that you learn to navigate by the *absence* of phosphorescence, by the darker patches where something has eaten the light-emitting film away.

The vegetation—lush, impossibly lush for a place without water—fills the Lung in strata. From the floor rise trunk-like structures that aren't trees, aren't fungi, aren't anything with a name in surface taxonomy. They are hollow, perforated with pores, and through these pores move the sounds. The "bark" is fibrous, almost textile, warm to touch, and when you press your ear against it you hear the internal commerce of the place: the rush of whatever circulates where water would circulate, the clicking of symbiotic populations, the low groaning of growth itself.

These trunk-forms are sparse in their arrangement, each one a kind of estate with territory around it, space that seems necessary, almost sacred. Between them the floor is covered in a mat of finer growth, something like moss crossed with neural tissue, pulsing with slow peristaltic waves. Step on it and it compresses, springs back, emits a brief bright flash of bioluminescence that travels in ripples to the nearest trunk, which responds with a deeper tone, a bass note felt in the sternum.

The fauna is abundant and *loud*. This is the strangeness: in a place without water, without weather, without day or night, the creatures have evolved toward acoustic saturation. There are things like crabs made of ceramic segments, their joints clicking in constant rhythm. There are ribbon-forms that hang from the upper reaches and whip through the air, their passage making sounds between thunder and cloth tearing. There are populations of what appear to be mineral nodules that *sing*, that have been singing the same notes for—how long? The dynamic is never-changing, frozen equilibrium. Nothing evolves here, nothing dies out, nothing arrives. The songs are the same songs, the clicks the same clicks, the growth at the same pace, the bioluminescence pulsing to the same rhythms. It is a recording that plays itself, a clockwork ecosystem sealed in warm amber.

The built structures are scattered, primitive, and *wrong*. Someone came here, or something emerged here, capable of tool-use. You find them wedged between trunk-bases, half-swallowed by the neural-moss: shelters made from shed ceramic segments, lashed together with fiber extracted from the perforated bark. The lashing is hand-done, pre-metal, the knots sophisticated in their simplicity. But the shelters are empty, have always been empty, were perhaps never meant to be occupied. They are models of shelters, practice-shelters, or offerings to a concept of shelter that this place never needed. Some contain arrangements of singing nodules, curated, composed. Some contain nothing but smooth hollows where something has lain for durations that leave no mark.

The weirdness permeates everything. The air itself is strange—not air, precisely, but a dense gas mixture that supports the non-aqueous chemistry of this place, that carries sound with unusual fidelity and directionality. You can hear a ceramic crab clicking at the far end of the Lung as if it were at your elbow, while a ribbon-form passing overhead sounds distant, underwater. The gas has a taste, metallic and sweet, that lingers in the throat for hours after departure.

And the rules of this world: no water, ever, not even bound in chemistry you would recognize. The lushness is supported by a metabolism of sulfur compounds, of mineral oxidation, of processes that on the surface would be called volcanic, here called merely *life*. The warmth is the waste heat of these reactions, stable because the reactions are stable, frozen in their equilibrium like a dancer in a photograph. The erosion is young because the Lung is young, recently emerged from processes you cannot reconstruct, sharp-featured in its architecture, nothing yet worn smooth by time—though time moves strangely here, or doesn't move, the dynamic never-changing, the same processes, the same sounds, the same bioluminescent ripples spreading from your footstep as from the footstep of whatever came before, if anything came before, if the footstep and its response are not themselves eternal, uncaused, the fundamental activity of the place.

You leave. The salt crust seals above you. The Lung continues, warm, dim, loud, strange, lush and sparse and scattered and primitive and young and hidden and never changing, breathing without breath, singing without singer, growing without growth, a pocket of impossible persistence in a world that dried too early, that forgot to finish dying, that holds this one warm secret in its collapsed chest, not a heart, not a lung, something else, something that has no name because it has no need of one, because nothing here needs anything, because need itself is a concept from elsewhere, from surfaces, from water, from change.
