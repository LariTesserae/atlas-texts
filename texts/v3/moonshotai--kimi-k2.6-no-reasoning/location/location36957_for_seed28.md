The Singing Vault of Keth-Umâr

---

They call it a city, but you would not recognize it as such from the outside. The Vault lies embedded in the basalt shield of a dead volcanic plain, its existence betrayed only by a single aperture: a crack in the earth perhaps twenty arm-spans across, from which light blooms upward at dawn and dusk like a wound in the world that refuses to heal. The surrounding territory is mineral-dead, a black glass desert where nothing grows for thirty leagues in any direction, where water has not fallen as liquid in ten thousand years. What moisture exists here arrives as mineral oils seeping from deep fractures, or as vapor dragged up from the Vault's own lungs by the thermal bellows of its breathing.

Descend through that aperture. The passage spirals downward, carved not by water—there is no water, has never been water in Keth-Umâr's chemistry—but by the patient scraping of ten thousand generations of hands, hard keratinous tools, and slow thermal expansion of the stone itself. The walls are grooved with those spiral striations, polished to a satin sheen by bodies passing, passing, always passing. The descent takes hours. The air warms as you go down, grows thick with the scents of petrochemical sap, of heated copper, of something green and urgent growing in impossible profusion.

Then the passage opens, and you emerge onto a balcony of living stone, and Keth-Umâr spreads before you in all its impossible intimacy.

---

The Vault is enormous by the standards of things hidden, yet tiny by the measure of cities. Perhaps three thousand paces across at its widest, perhaps eight hundred from the highest cultivated terrace to the floor of the central chasm. But within that pocket, that contained world, the builders have achieved a vertical density of habitation that mimics thecompressed complexity of a termite mound or the layered chambers of a wasp's nest. The space is not hollow, not truly—rather, it is *hollowed*, a bubble of relative emptiness punched through solid rock, its walls terraced and carved and grown into a topography of habitation that climbs every surface.

And everywhere, growing everywhere: vegetation that has never seen sunlight, never tasted rain.

The light source is the ceiling, if such a term applies to a dome of stone so distant and so irregular. The rock there glows. Not with any single quality of illumination—different regions pulse at different frequencies, different temperatures, different colors that shift through a slow cycle based on processes no Keth-Umâri scholar has fully described. The brightest patches burn at a brassy white-green that hurts to look at directly; the dimmest regions settle into a crimson so deep it is nearly darkness. Together they create a perpetual twilight-brightness, warm, omnipresent, casting no sharp shadows because the light emanates from all directions at once from that stony sky.

This light feeds the gardens. And what gardens.

They cascade from every terrace, tumble from every crevice, pour in vegetal waterfalls down the carved channels that thread the city's walls. The plants themselves are nonsense to any surface botanist—structures of cellulose and silica hybrid, photosynthesis based not on chlorophyll but on *rhodopsin-like* pigments in complex matrices, metabolism driven by chemosynthesis at the root and photosynthesis at the leaf in simultaneous operation. They grow as vines, as sheets, as hollow tubes, as lacy filigree, as solid woody masses that have grown over centuries to become structural elements, living beams and buttresses integrated into the architecture itself.

The most common form is the *velth*: a rope-thick vine with leaves like purple-black fans, each leaf broad as a spread hand, its surface micro-pitted to capture and amplify the dimmest phosphorescence. Velth grows at extraordinary rates, meters per month, and the Keth-Umâri harvest it constantly, weave it while living into structural cables, into baskets, into the membrane of their famous breathing-halls. Other plants—*gromm* with its explosive seed-pods used for percussion instruments, *ishka* whose hollow stems conduct water-fluids through the terraces, *the-the* that grows as a solid sheet over entire cliff-faces and flowers with bioluminescent organs that supplement the ceiling's glow—these fill every niche, every crack, every cultivated surface.

Yet the total biomass, for all this lushness, is constrained by the Vault's finite geometry. The gardens are *intensive*, not extensive. A single terrace of twenty paces square might contain two hundred distinct species in carefully maintained layers, but the next terrace might be bare rock, its ecology failed or never established. Gaps of emptiness yawn between the zones of profusion. The overall density of life is sparse—this is no jungle, no continuous biomass, but rather a *spotty* lushness, a punctuated brilliance, an ecology of isolated oases each fiercely maintaining itself against the stone.

---

The Keth-Umâri themselves—if you can call them a single kind—are rare. Perhaps three thousand individuals inhabit this space, scattered across the terraces, clustered in family-groups or solitary by choice. They are primarily tool-users, metal-poor, their technology based on the endless *processing* of their botanical wealth. They weave velth into structures stronger than wood, softer than stone. They ferment ishkainto adhesives, solvents, medicines. They breed gromm for specific seed-pitch, using generations of selection to tune their instruments. Their metals come from deep thermal vents, limited copper and iron extracted with enormous labor, precious enough that a single metal knife might represent a family's wealth accumulated across centuries.

They are small. This is the scale of their world, and their bodies match it—most stand less than four feet tall, slender, their skin a mottled gray-green pigmentation that shifts with emotional state and dietary chemistry. They have no eyes as such; the light they navigate by is detected through dermal patches, a distributed photosensitivity that gives them awareness of illumination direction and intensity without focused vision. Their primary sense is *vibrational*: they feel the city through their feet, detect conversations through wall-conduction, locate each other at distance through deliberate seismic signals.

Which brings us to the sound.

---

Keth-Umâr is deafening.

The Vault's geometry—a rough oval, irregular, with surfaces at every angle—creates acoustic properties that would drive a surface-dweller to madness within hours. Every sound persists, reflects, builds. The Keth-Umâri do not merely tolerate this; they orchestrate it.

The central chasm, a hundred paces deep, functions as the city's resonating chamber. Across its width stretch cables of woven velth, tensioned with precise care, each tuned to a specific frequency. The inhabitants play these cables—striking them with gromm-seed mallets, plucking them with keratinous picks, setting them into sympathetic vibration with their own voice-organs. The resulting sound fills the Vault at all hours, a continuous complex drone interspersed with rhythmic patterns, with melodic fragments, with sudden explosive percussive events that echo for minutes in the stone.

But this is merely the *intentional* sound. Beneath it, behind it, through it: the city's infrastructure noise. The ventilation system, if it can be called that, relies on thermal differentials between the aperture and the deep floor, on the breathing of thousands of plants, on deliberate wind-baffles carved to direct flow. The result is a perpetual rush, a roar, a *weather* of moving air that howls through narrow passages and whispers across broad terraces. Water-fluids—oils and saps and condensed vapors—move through channels under pressure, gurgling, hissing, occasionally bursting through worn seals in percussive spouts.

And the stone itself sings. The thermal expansion and contraction of the Vault's mass, responding to cycles in the ceiling's light-output, creates groans and cracks and deep subsonic rumbles that the Keth-Umâri feel in their bones, that their instruments sometimes capture and amplify into audible range. Seismic events from the distant surface—minor volcanic adjustments, impacts, the slow settling of geological ages—propagate down and resonate in the city's structure.

A visitor, could one survive the atmosphere and the chemistry and the sheer sensory overload, would experience Keth-Umâr as a continuous physical assault of sound. The Keth-Umâri experience it as the medium of their thought, the condition of their consciousness. They sleep in specially dampened chambers—velth-walled cocoons where sound is absorbed rather than reflected—and emerge daily into the sonic immersion that maintains their orientation, their social connection, their sense of place in the world.

---

The strangeness of the place accumulates in particulars.

The Keth-Umâri practice a form of gardening that is also architecture, also ritual, also memorial. When an individual dies, their body is not buried—there is no soil as such, only rock and accumulated organic detritus—but rather grafted into the velth. Specific vines are cultivated around the corpse, grow *through* it, incorporate its substance into living tissue. After some years, the original form is unrecognizable, but the plant that persists is understood to contain the person, to *be* them in some transformed state. These memorial-vines are tended for generations, their location carefully recorded, their growth patterns interpreted for messages from the deceased. A family might maintain twenty or thirty such ancestor-plants, consulting them before major decisions, arguing with them, singing to them through the stone-conduction of their roots.

The city's vertical placement is socially coded but not in the simple hierarchies of surface cities. Highest terraces, nearest the brightest light, are zones of experiment and risk—new plant varieties tested, new architectural forms attempted, the young and reckless establishing themselves. Lowest levels, near the warm, humid, dark floor, are the places of deepest stability, where the oldest families maintain their ancestor-groves, where the most traditional practices persist. But movement between levels is constant, expected, part of life-phase. One descends with age and achievement, but might ascend again in crisis, in innovation, in spiritual renewal. The city's social structure is thus a continuous circulation, a vertical churn, individuals and families tracing spiral paths through the generations.

There is no government as such, no central authority. Decisions emerge from the interaction of multiple systems: the technical requirements of maintaining the gardens and structures, the interpretive traditions around ancestor-vine consultation, the seismic communication network that allows rapid consultation across the entire Vault but only in certain rhythmic patterns, during certain acoustic windows. A proposal might take years to propagate, to be tested in dream-communication with ancestors, to emerge as the consensus of a hundred simultaneous local decisions. The Keth-Umâri find the concept of rapid, individual choice-making bizarre, perhaps insane.

---

The fauna is rare, as the parameters suggest, but significant in its rarity.

A few animal species have found their way down through the aeons—perhaps carried by the original settlers, perhaps evolved from microorganisms in the deep thermal systems. Most important are the *thrum*, small hexapodal creatures that inhabit the velth-vines, that feed on sap and in turn produce a waste-product essential to certain plant metabolisms. The Keth-Umâri cultivate thrum colonies with precision, move them between gardens, have bred distinct strains for distinct functions. Thrum are not exactly domesticated—they remain wild, unpredictable, occasionally devastating—but they are integrated into the ecology in ways that make them indispensable.

Larger predators exist in the deepest, darkest, warmest cracks of the Vault floor. The Keth-Umâri rarely encounter them directly, but interpret their seismic signatures, their occasional migrations upward as temperature gradients shift. These creatures—they are called *veth-veth*, a term that refers to their sound-signature rather than any visual form—are understood as necessary to the system's health, their predation preventing any single thrum strain from dominating, their waste-products contributing to lower-floor fertility. They are dangerous when encountered, but encounters are managed through careful acoustic monitoring, through deliberate garden-design that channels movement, through occasional ritual sacrifices of surplus thrum colonies.

And there are stories, persistent in the ancestor-vine interpretations, of something else. Something that moves in the stone itself, that is neither plant nor animal, that communicates in frequencies below Keth-Umâri perception but above veth-veth sensing. The stories are inconsistent, dream-like, perhaps merely the accumulated noise of generations of vibration-interpretation. But maintenance crews report occasional problems—channels blocked by unknown crystalline growths, acoustic properties shifting in ways that resist analysis, the rare disappearance of an individual in circumstances that suggest neither accident nor predator. The Keth-Umâri do not deny these phenomena, but they do not exactly *investigate* them in ways a surface-dweller would recognize. They sing to the stone, tend their gardens, consult their ancestors, and accept that some processes operate at scales or frequencies outside their immediate comprehension.

---

The Vault is old. The *established* quality of its erosion is part of its essence—not the weathering of water and wind, but the slow thermal fatigue of expansion and contraction, the chemical corrosion of organic vapors, the biological weathering of root-pressure and growth-stress. Surfaces are rounded, softened, *comforted* into curves. Even the sharpest architectural interventions of past generations have settled into something like natural form, their edges worn, their functions adapted to subsequent needs. The city has been in its current configuration—this specific arrangement of terraces, this garden ecology, this social order—for perhaps ten thousand years. Before that, other configurations, now detectable only in certain acoustic anomalies, in the genetic diversity of the velth strains that preserves ancestral forms, in the deepest, most reticent ancestor-vines whose consultation requires days of preparation and produces only ambiguous, archaic response.

Whether it will persist, whether the thermal systems that sustain it will shift, whether the ceiling-phosphors will eventually fade—these are matters of ancestor-consultation, of long-term trend-interpretation, of songs whose full performance takes decades. The Keth-Umâri do not plan in the short term. They maintain, they adapt, they sing, they tend their impossible garden in its impossible hollow, and the sound of their city rises through the crack in the dead world's surface, a brightness and a thunder audible, if anyone outside were listening, across the mineral desert of their isolation.

---

*From the field notes of the Seventeenth Expedition: "I emerged after forty days in the Vault, my ears damaged perhaps permanently, my skin pigmented greenish-gray that took months to fade, my dreams filled with vibration and root-pattern for years afterward. I cannot say I understood what I experienced. I can say only that Keth-Umâr is itself, is *ongoing*, is a process of becoming that has achieved a kind of sustained, singing equilibrium in a place where equilibrium should be impossible. They have asked me not to return, not because I am unwelcome, but because my surface-chemistry, my water-biased biology, introduces instabilities into their careful systems. I respect this. I miss them. I still wake sometimes with the sensation of seismic communication, of messages passing through stone that I almost, almost, can interpret."*
