I need to hold some contradictions here: a *built* environment at maximum (3.0) with *primitive* technology (0.9), *hot* and *lush* yet *never changing*, *bright* light with *mostly quiet*, and *reality breakdown* weaving through everything. Let me follow where this pulls.

## The Gardens of Permanent Drying

### First Appearance

You arrive at the moment the plaster always finishes cracking. Not *has* cracked—*always finishes*. The crack runs from the northeast corner of the receiving court to the central drainage channel, and it has run there since the Gardens first dried into their final form. You can feel the dust in your throat already, though no dust moves. The air holds it suspended, lime-white and talc-fine, at that precise instant of inhalation before the sneeze that never completes.

The plaster is the color of old teeth. Someone—some *thing*—mixed it from burnt shell and volcanic ash, ground it with stone rollers, floated it onto walls that curve like something grown rather than laid course by course. But nothing grows here, not in the way you understand. The walls *are* the growing, arrested. The curves are root-forms captured mid-thrust, the moment of seeking water before the root found it or failed. You can follow a wall with your palm and feel the vegetal intention still pulsing in the plaster's coolness, though the hands that smoothed it have been mineral for longer than your language has words for moisture.

### The Water That Is Not Water

The water rating here—1.9, frequent, marsh-edge—is not wrong, but it requires attention. There is water *in* everything. The plaster weeps. Not drips, not streams, but *weeps*: a continuous surfacing of moisture that never accumulates, never evaporates, only perpetually arrives at the skin of things and hangs there in beads that catch the unwavering light. Touch a wall and your fingers come away slick with something too viscous for water, too clear for sap. It smells of copper and something greener.

The central channels run with this substance. They slope at that precise angle where liquid should flow, and the liquid *does* flow in the sense that it is forever at the edge of flowing—meniscus trembling, surface tension drawn tight as a drumskin—yet never advances. Stand at the channel's edge long enough (and time here accommodates standing) and you see your own reflection doubled: once in the stationary surface, once in the deeper surface that the surface pretends to conceal. The lower reflection sometimes moves when you do not.

There is no marsh, but there is marsh-*memory*. Certain corners of certain courts hold the *idea* of saturated ground: the smell of anaerobic decay, the particular squelch-footed resistance of peat, the iridescent film of bacterial life. You can press your heel into these corners and feel the give, the almost-yield, before the plaster's absolute hardness asserts itself underneath. The Gardens remember being wetland. The remembering is structural, architectural, a load-bearing element in the design.

### Vegetation of a Kind

Lush, yes—2.5—and utterly still. The plants are plaster too, or plaster is plant, or the distinction collapsed here before categories existed to distinguish. There are fronds in the lower courts that spread wider than a sleeping body, each leaflet defined in low relief, each vein a channel where the weeping water collects and does not run. Their color is malachite in the bright light, though if you chipped one (no tool you carry will chip it; the bronze you might have brought home from soldiering bends against this surface, the stone flakes) you would find the white of gypsum all through.

Higher, in the courts where the channels branch narrower, there are *trees*: columns that flare at cap height into canopies of interlocking geometric leaves. From below, in the absolute noon that never becomes afternoon, the leaves are black with the intensity of transmitted light. The shapes between them are not sky but something *held against* sky, a blueness that has been captured and displayed rather than glimpsed through. The leaves do not fall. The season that would release them never arrives. They are lush in the manner of a cough suppressed for years, green pressed to the edge of greenness without the relief of autumn or the honest death of drought.

Birds—*abundant* fauna, 2.2—perch in these plaster canopies. They are quick, more quick than anything in the Gardens has right to be, and their calls are the only sound that violates the prevailing quiet (0.6, mostly silent). But attend more carefully: the birds are not separate from the architecture. Their feet grip branches that continue into their claws without clear transition. Their shadows on the plaster below sometimes show wings spread where the actual birds sit folded. Sometimes you hear two birds calling answer to answer, and trace the sound to a single throat. Sometimes a bird launches from a branch and the branch *reaches* with it, maintaining contact, a long elastic limb of calcium and hair-thin silica that snaps back only when the bird has reached the limit of its flight and settled, always, on another branch that extends to meet it.

The birds eat the weeping water. You can watch the beak-dip, the head-tilt, the swallowing motion. Yet the water does not diminish at the point of drinking. The bird's throat bulges with intake, and the meniscus at its source remains exactly where arithmetic would not allow it to remain.

### The Temperature of Arrested Process

Hot: 2.6, nearly tropical in intensity. But not humid, not dry, not anything with a name in meteorology. The heat is the heat of chemical reaction interrupted. Something was *happening* here—hydration, crystallization, the slow exothermic embrace of lime and water and air—and it was stopped at its most active moment. The Gardens exist in the thermal instant of setting plaster, the warmth of enclosure, the fever of becoming-solid. You move through it as you might move through the flank of something very large that has not yet decided whether it is alive.

There is no sun. The light (3.0, absolute brightness) comes from everywhere and nowhere, the color of overexposed bone, and it casts shadows that do not consistently indicate direction. Your own shadow may walk ahead of you at morning, behind you at what should be noon, occasionally beneath you like a second body pressed into the plaster by your weight. The light does not change quality. There is no golden hour, no blue, no dusk. The birds call at intervals that suggest circadian rhythm but do not map to any cycle you could track.

The heat makes you want to remove your clothes, your armor, your skin. The Gardens permit this. The plaster is warm to the touch, blood-warm, and does not abrade. But those who have stayed long enough to become naked report that their shadows begin to lag behind, to carry the outline of garments they no longer wear, to walk separately in the peripheral courts where the architecture grows more complex and less visited.

### The Youngness of Ancient Things

Erosion at 0.7: young, fresh, recently emerged. This is the most difficult parameter, for the Gardens are old by any conscious measure. Yet they are *newly* old. They have not been worn; they have been *preserved*. The sharpness of the relief—each leaflet, each feather on each bird, each geometric flower in the pavement—is the sharpness of first completion, not of careful maintenance. Nothing has been refreshed, retouched, repaired. Nothing has needed to be.

The youngness manifests as *impatience*. The plaster curves strain outward still, seeking their final dimension, held in the moment before settling. The channels slope with the confidence of engineering not yet tested by failure. The shadows fall where they were designed to fall, not where centuries of deviation would place them. In a human construction, this would read as recent, as just-finished. In the Gardens, where the finish has lasted longer than memory, it reads as something else: a determination against erosion that operates by existing in the moment where erosion has not yet begun, and *remaining* there.

There are no chips. No wear patterns at doorways, no polished depressions where generations walked. The doors (there are openings that function as doors, though they close with nothing visible, operate with nothing mechanical) have no hinges to wear, no thresholds to groove. The plaster flows around the concept of passage without dividing into moving and fixed parts.

### The Scale of Confined Immensity

Open: 1.5, town-or-valley-size, extending past the immediate. Yet the density is sparse (1.3), much space between presences, balanced with emptiness. The Gardens solve this by *refracting* scale. Each court opens into larger courts; each larger court contains, nested at its center, a smaller court that contains the memory of the larger in its proportions. You can walk for what feels like hours and reach a boundary—a wall, a channel's source, a tree-column's base—only to find, turned around, that the court you left is the same size, or larger, or inverted in some dimension you can feel as wrongness in your inner ear but not identify.

The sparseness is architectural. The elements—wall, channel, tree, bird—are few in any given view. But the *arrangement* proliferates. Two walls define a court; three walls define, impossibly, six courts, some of which you can enter only by not intending to enter them. The emptiness is functional. It is where you, the walker, are meant to be. The presences—built to maximum, everything made, no natural substrate—hold their places and do not crowd, do not encroach, leave you the negative space that the design requires you to occupy.

There are no other walkers. The faunal abundance is all non-human, or *para*-human: the birds, the occasional slithering in the channels that might be eels made of the same weeping-water substance, the insects that crawl the leaves and are indistinguishable from the veins they traverse until they move. You are alone in the spaciousness, and the spaciousness seems to have been waiting for aloneness, designed around its contours.

### The Built Absolute

At 3.0, built to total, no natural substrate: this is the governing principle, the gravity of the place. There is no ground beneath the Gardens. There is plaster, then channels, then plaster descending to channels, then the channels' lower surfaces which are also plaster, also weeping, also bearing the faint warmth of interrupted reaction. If you could drill (you cannot; nothing drills this) you would find no stone, no earth, no core. The Gardens are depth without substrate, construction all the way down, or perhaps construction *as* down, the direction of descent being simply another architectural decision.

What built them? The primitive tech rating—0.9, pre-metal, hand-tool, manual-craft—seems contradictory until you examine the surfaces closely. The smoothness of the plaster, the precision of the relief, these read as machined, as impossible without instruments of measurement and application that your own civilization has only recently developed. But look closer. The veins of the leaves are not identical. They vary within a tolerance that suggests hand-work, the slight tremor of a wrist guiding a stylus, the minute variation of pressure that a machine would not reproduce. The curves of the walls, mathematically continuous from a distance, reveal on close approach the incremental straightness of short sections joined, each section the length of a forearm, the reach of a worker standing on something to reach higher.

They were plastered from the inside, these walls that curve inward. There is no scaffold evidence, no hole for a support, no mark of external access. The workers—hundreds, thousands, their number calculable from the arm-length sections—worked from within the space they created, and created it *around* themselves, and left by means not preserved in the record of the structure. Or did not leave. Perhaps they are the birds. Perhaps they are the weeping water, the memory of marsh, the heat of reaction sustained beyond biological possibility.

The tools that made this were stone, wood, fired clay. You can find them, occasionally, in the lower courts where the plaster has—no, *holds the form of*—cracking. The rollers, half-buried in their own product, stone worn to smoothness by the grinding of shell and ash. The wooden floats, mineralized now, their grain still visible, still holding the oil that once seasoned them against the wet plaster's thirst. The clay forms, broken at the edges where they were stripped away too early or too late, leaving their textures printed in negative on the walls they shaped.

### Reality Breakdown: The 2.6 Unraveling

This is not a place where the laws of nature are violated. It is a place where they are *completed*, finished, set like the plaster itself, and the completion reveals something about the laws that their operation elsewhere concealed.

Time, for instance. The never-changing dynamic (0.4, frozen equilibrium) is not stasis but *resolution*. All processes that began here have reached their final state simultaneously, which means that causes and their effects occupy the same instant. The crack in the receiving court's corner is the result of thermal stress; the thermal stress is the result of the day's heat; the day's heat is the present condition; the crack is present too, without the interval of propagation. You can put your finger in it and feel, if your nerves were sensitive enough, the entire history of its formation compressed into the texture of its edges, the slight widening at the top where the initiating impulse was strongest.

Space operates similarly. The courts that contain courts, the shadows that walk separately, the birds that are also branches—these are not impossibilities but *solutions*. The Gardens solve for enclosure without boundary, for growth without decay, for water without flow. The solutions are rigorous, internally consistent, and visibly *wrong* to perceiveurs trained on a more permissive physics.

Most unsettling is the *social* breakdown, the reality of relation. You enter alone, and the Gardens accommodate one. But you are not the first, and the Gardens remember, not as history but as *structure*. In certain courts, your shadow is not yours alone. It carries the posture of previous walkers, the angle of their shoulders, the length of their stride. If you move quickly, you can sometimes see them separate, your shadow and theirs, the two figures diverging before the geometry of light reasserts its single-source illusion and collapses them together again.

The birds know you. Not individually—they do not track your return, for return is not reliably possible—but as *type*. They call differently to human presences than to emptiness, a pitch that resonates in the nasal cavity, that seems to originate inside your head and project outward rather than entering from without. If you are still long enough, they will settle on you. Their feet grip your shoulder, your head, and you feel the moment of transition where bird becomes branch, where your body becomes extension of the architecture, where you too might weep the not-water and never complete the weeping.

### The Quiet That Is Almost Sound

0.6, mostly quiet. The quiet is not absence. It is the *shape* of sound, the plaster cast of auditory experience. The birds' calls exist in it like relief sculpture: positive against negative, but the negative is primary, the matrix from which the positive emerges and into which it subsides.

There is a frequency to the silence. You feel it in your sternum when the channels are empty of your footsteps, when your breathing slows to match the non-cycle of the Gardens' non-time. It is the pitch of crystallization interrupted, the hum of water molecules aligned in meniscus tension, the subsonic drone of heat maintained without source. It does not vary. It does not respond to your presence. But it *acknowledges* you, in the way that absolute zero acknowledges the last degree of warmth before it too is drawn in and averaged out.

### What the Gardens Want

They do not want. This is the final strangeness, the completion of the breakdown. A built environment, total, primitive in its making, sustained past biological possibility, holding its own conditions invariant: this *should* be willful, should be the expression of intention, the monument to some need. But the Gardens are without desire. They are the *result* of process, not its goal. The workers (plasterers, bird-shapers, vein-carvers) did not build for eternity. They built for the next stage, the stage after plastering, the inhabitation and use and weathering and repair that never arrived because the conditions for arrival were interrupted, frozen, *solved*.

You can inhabit them, in a manner. There is no food; the fauna does not eat in any way that would sustain you, and the flora is mineral. There is water, after a fashion, but drinking the weeping substance produces not hydration but *memory*: the memory of water, the body's recollection of quenching, which satisfies for a time the need that the substance itself does not address. You could live here, perhaps indefinitely, in the state of being perpetually almost-dead, almost-thirsty, almost-hungry, almost-sane.

Some do. You find them, occasionally, in the higher courts where the channels narrow to finger-width and the tree-columns cluster close enough to form something like rooms. They have stopped moving. Their postures suggest waiting, or listening, or that final relaxation into architecture that the Gardens slowly effect. Their shadows have separated and walk independently in the lower courts. Their bodies remain, warm still, weeping slightly from the pores, indistinguishable in texture from the plaster they rest against.

They are not dead. The Gardens do not permit death, which is change, which is process, which is the continuation that the frozen equilibrium excludes. They are *finished*, in the plasterers' sense: complete, final, no more work required.

### The Exit, If There Is One

You leave by not wanting to leave, or by wanting it with absolute specificity. The Gardens accommodate both. The doors (openings that function as doors) appear where your path has brought you to the necessity of discontinuity. Passing through, you may find yourself in the marsh that was, or the city that surrounded the construction, or the quarry where the ash was burned, or none of these, or your own beginning, your own plaster moment, your own crack in the corner of something that has not yet finished cracking.

Most who leave do not return. This is not because return is impossible; the Gardens are not selective, not malicious, not anything that would recognize selection or malice. It is because leaving requires becoming the kind of person who can leave, and that person no longer needs, no longer seeks, no longer *moves* in the way that return would require.

Those who do return—returning once, twice, cultivating the capacity like a garden themselves—become more difficult to distinguish from the structure each time. Their footsteps leave no mark, but they are anticipated by the plaster's slight warming. Their shadows arrive before them, settle into familiar positions, wait. Their voices, when they speak, carry the frequency of the silence, and the birds answer less and less, as if the distinction between human call and architectural resonance has finally become too fine to maintain.

Eventually, they too are finished. The Gardens do not grow, but they *extend*, and each completion is an extension. The latest court, the highest tree, the most recent crack running from northeast corner to central channel: these are the contributions of walkers who became workers, who joined the manual craft, whose primitive tools are their own bodies now, their own persistence, their own heat of interrupted becoming.

*Enter, then, if you will. The plaster is warm. The water weeps. The light holds steady, and your shadow walks ahead of you, already knowing the way.*
