The sound comes first, before you see anything at all—a constant, bone-humming pressure that fills the air so completely it seems to *be* the air. Not the sharp violence of explosions, but something older, deeper: the endless grinding of tectonic patience, translated into frequencies that the body feels as much as hears. Then you see where it comes from.

The city—if it is a city, if that word can stretch far enough—hugs the bottom of a shallow basin, perhaps forty kilometers across, that gathers everything down to itself like water finding a drain. The elevation here is low, yes, but *strange* low: not a valley carved by time, but a place where the ground is still *deciding* to be ground. The basin floor shimmers with fresh mineral skin, cooling still from its most recent birth. Basalt nodules bulge upward through half-solidified silicates like knots in young wood. The erosion scale reads wrong here because there is no weather to speak of, no rain yet to wear anything smooth, no wind to polish—only the perpetual grinding sound that seems to be the rock speaking itself into thicker existence.

And it is warm. Warm in the way of a body's interior, warm with metabolic impatience. In places, the water pools in depressions that are not yet ponds, not yet lakes, just *holdings* where liquid waits to understand its relationship to the new ground beneath it.

There are no plants. Zero-point-four on the vegetation scale, and that fraction is generous—perhaps a smear of mineral stain that someone might mistake for lichen, but nothing that feeds on light, nothing that grows by division and aspiration. The life here is otherwise. Their carapaces range from hand-sized to chest-high, and they move in streams, in rivers, in *traffic* that has patterns too old to be random and too young to be called instinct. The buildings are low, hunched, almost organic in their irregularity—no two the same because no two moments of emergence are the same, no two patches of cooling ground offer identical gifts.

The city has neighborhoods, after a fashion. There are districts where the crab-things cluster thicker, where the grinding sound is modulated by their own continuous movement, by the clicking of their jointed legs and the scraping of their carapaces against the rough new walls. It will grind until the material runs out or the world finishes its decision to exist.

The illumination comes from the cracks, from the seams where the deeper warmth leaks through, orange and slow and ancient even in this young place. It comes from phosphorescence in the steam, in the water, in the thin films that grow on the crab-shells. The crab-things are not alien; they are *logical*, evolved for this exact moment of geological hesitation. The city is not supernatural; it is *necessary*, the only response to density and warmth and the availability of building-material still cooling from its birth. The only strangeness is that it exists at all, that the parameters have aligned to make this moment, this holding-pattern between not-world and world, stable enough to become home.

But it *is* stable. Dynamic 0.2. Never changing. The crab-things have been building for—how long? Time moves strangely in equilibrium. Their cities fill the basin, extend up the uncertain slopes, layer upon layer of chitinous architecture and half-mineralized organic matter and the fresh leavings of a ground that keeps producing more ground. Nothing grows because growth is not yet necessary. Everything *is*, sustained in this moment of becoming, this extended threshold.

Walk carefully, if you walk here. The ground may still be deciding. The crab-things will not harm you, probably—they have no aggression toward what does not compete for the warm wet mineral seams—but they will flow around you like water, like necessity, and their attention is elsewhere, on building, on the endless patient construction of a city that will never be finished because it exists in the moment before finishing became possible.

Listen. The sound is not deafening to them. The sound is *the voice of the place*, teaching them what to build, where to gather, how to hold this moment open against the pressure of time. They are the keepers of emergence. Almost familiar.
