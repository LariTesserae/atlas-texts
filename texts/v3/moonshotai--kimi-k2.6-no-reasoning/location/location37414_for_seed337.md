The Hollows of Keth Morran

---

You descend through a crack in the world that does not know it is finished being made.

The entrance betrays nothing from above—a mere fold in scrub-grass country, a limestone lip that cattle avoid. But the crack widens as you go, and the walls do not behave as stone should. They are *new*, still speaking their first words in the language of geology. Strata that should have taken millennia to settle lie in soft bands like unbaked pastry, pale and slightly warm to the touch. In places the rock weeps a thin, milky fluid—not quite water, not quite mineral—that leaves no residue when it dries. The erosion parameter here reads zero because erosion has not yet been invited to the party. This place is *emergent*, still dreaming itself into solidity, and the dream is crowded.

---

The first chamber opens like a lung expanding.

You expected cavernous emptiness, the cathedral hush of deep spaces. Instead: *density*. The Hollows pack life and structure into every available volume with the desperate ingenuity of a city built by people who have never known there was space to spare. The chamber extends perhaps two kilometers in its longest dimension, ceiling lost in a haze of condensation and cookfire smoke perhaps eighty meters up. The scale is regional, town-sized, yet it feels intimate because there is no *unclaimed* air. Every cubic meter holds something breathing, burning, dripping, or waiting.

The architecture is urban without being planned. Structures cling to the raw walls like barnacles—platforms of lashed bone and half-cured hide, wedged into notches in the embryonic stone. Rope bridges spiderweb across the open space at six different levels, some sagging with the weight of hanging gardens (sparse, desperate gardens: lichens cultivated on urine-soaked rags, a few stunted tubers in leather pouches of imported soil). The builders work in primitive tech—flint, antler, sinew, the occasional soft copper beaten thin—but they have had generations to refine their constraints. A pulley system of interlocking deer scapulae hauls water from the seeps that thread the chamber's lower reaches. Hide bellows, operated by children too small for other labor, push air through leather tubes to fires that would otherwise suffocate in the packed atmosphere.

---

The damp is intermittent, strategic.

Water does not flow here so much as *negotiate*. The chamber's geology is too young for proper aquifers; instead, moisture condenses on the cooler upper walls in rhythmic cycles, gathering into rivulets that the inhabitants have learned to predict with the precision of tide-tables. Every twelve hours or so, a particular patch of ceiling above the eastern platforms weeps for twenty minutes. The families there have developed a choreography of vessels—gourds, bladders, folded-leaf cups—to capture what they can. Other water comes from deeper, from seeps where the raw stone still bleeds its formation fluids. This water tastes of something not quite mineral, not quite organic. The oldest inhabitants claim it sustains them differently than surface water, and perhaps it does: they are pale, slow-moving, long-lived by their own accounting, though they have no written records to verify.

The vegetation, what little exists, has adapted to this rhythm of scarcity. Mosses dominate, species that can desiccate to paper and rehydrate in minutes when the drips come. A few fungi, pale and without color, grow on the platforms where organic waste accumulates. The rarest and most prized are the "stone-flowers"—lichen-like growths that appear only on the newest, warmest rock faces, their chemistry somehow interfacing with the chamber's ongoing formation. They are harvested for medicine, for dye, for the religious ceremonies that mark each new platform's construction. The ecology is sparse but *intense*, every organism locked into specific relationships with the still-cooling stone.

---

Temperature hovers in the cool range, but unevenly.

The newer walls, the places where the chamber's edges still grow (and it does grow, slowly, almost imperceptibly, the crack widening by a finger's width each year), radiate warmth from their ongoing mineral conversations. The established platforms, the oldest constructions, sit in persistent coolness, their inhabitants wrapping themselves in layers of hide and woven moss. The temperature gradient drives air movement, subtle currents that the inhabitants navigate by instinct, knowing which passages will chill and which will warm, which updrafts carry cooking smells from the central hearths, which downdrafts smell of the deeper dark.

---

The fauna is rare because the niche is *occupied*.

What ecological space exists has been claimed by the human inhabitants and their few domesticates. There are no bats, no cave fish, no troglobitic insects in the usual sense. Instead, a single species of pale, eyeless cricket has adapted to coexist, its chirping so faint—near the threshold of hearing—that it registers more as a texture of silence than as sound. The inhabitants cultivate these crickets in woven cages, not for food but for *divination*: the patterns of their near-silent song, interpreted by elders who have spent decades learning to hear, guide decisions about platform construction, marriage, the timing of expeditions to the surface. The crickets are the only animals that thrive here; everything else is marginal, visiting, or extinct in the attempt.

The soundscape, consequently, is dominated by human activity at its quietest registers. The scrape of bone tools on soft stone. The wet rhythm of bellows. Breastfed infants, the soft percussion of hide being worked, the almost inaudible drone of prayer or complaint or lullaby. Loud sounds are dangerous in the packed space—startle responses have caused bridge collapses, fire spreads, falls. The culture has evolved toward *quietude* as survival strategy, as aesthetic, as moral value. To speak loudly is to claim space others need. To listen carefully is to participate in the collective necessity.

---

The light is normal, which is to say: fire, and the strange luminescence of the stone itself in certain phases.

The raw geology, in its unfinished state, occasionally phosphoresces. Not dramatically—no glowing walls, no fungal fairy lights—but a faint, sourceless ambient illumination that makes complete darkness rare. The inhabitants have learned to supplement this with fires kept small and numerous rather than large and few, distributing both light and heat and risk across the chamber's volume. During certain periods, which they track but do not understand, the stone-light brightens to something approaching overcast day, and these are times of festival, of major construction, of the rare communal gatherings that require visual contact across distances. The rest of the time, life proceeds in a twilight of embers and stone-glow, faces known by voice and silhouette, intimacy enforced by the limits of reliable vision.

---

The dynamic is active in ways that belie the apparent stillness.

The chamber *breathes*. Air pressure shifts with temperature gradients and the slow exhalation of the forming rock. The water seeps migrate, new ones appearing, old ones drying as the stone completes some internal negotiation. The platforms require constant maintenance—the raw walls do not hold anchors reliably, structures must be adjusted, re-wedged, rebuilt on timescales of years rather than generations. Population pressure drives continuous micro-construction, the subdivision of existing platforms, the brave or desperate expansion to newly stable wall sections. Disease moves quickly in the packed space, met with quarantine protocols that have evolved through bitter experience: infected platforms are sealed with hide barriers, supplied by rope from above, their inhabitants speaking through membranes of stretched intestine until recovery or death resolves their status.

And deeper, always, there are the sounds of *making*. The stone is not finished. Somewhere below, perhaps accessible through passages the inhabitants have not yet found or have found and do not return from, the chamber continues its emergence. Occasionally there are tremors, the soft birth-pangs of new space, and after these the geography may have shifted—a new seep, a closed passage, a fresh wall warm to the touch and ready, perhaps, for platforms not yet built.

---

The rules of this world are simple and absolute: *there is no elsewhere*.

The inhabitants know of the surface, theoretically, as the place where a few traders descend from, where exiles are sent to survive or not, where the materials they cannot make must be acquired through dangerous exchange. But the surface is not *real* in the way the Hollows are real. It is abstraction, threat, the source of leather and wood and occasional strangers with hard eyes and surface-hardened bodies. The Hollows are the world, the full extent of meaningful space, and the ongoing fact of their emergence—their not-yet-completedness—is the foundation of culture, religion, daily practice.

They believe they live inside a body not yet born. The warm stone is womb, the intermittent water is the pulse of something growing. Their quiet is the quiet of listening for the first breath. Their density is the density of cells before differentiation, of potential not yet resolved into form. They do not know if the emergence will complete, if the chamber will one day stabilize into finished geology and begin, finally, to erode like proper stone. They do not know if they will be crushed in the process, or expelled, or transformed into something that can inhabit whatever comes next.

They know only that they are here, packed together in the damp cool dark, building with bone and hope on walls that are still learning to be walls, listening to crickets almost too quiet to hear, waiting for the stone to finish its dream of becoming.
