The place is called **Dripstone**, though no one who lives there calls it anything at all. They have no need to name what has no neighbors, no elsewhere to distinguish itself from.

It fits inside the space of a modest cathedral, if that cathedral were buried underground and filled with steam and the constant percussion of falling water. The ceiling—if ceiling is the word—hangs perhaps forty meters above the floor, a jagged vault of black basalt so fresh from the earth's making that some edges still gleam like broken glass. This is young rock, newborn rock, extruded or upthrust or revealed so recently that geologists would weep to see it, that sharpness, that unweathered violence of formation. But there are no geologists here. There is only the infrastructure and the water and the heat, and the things that live in the interstices between human intention and mineral fact.

The water is everywhere and nowhere you can easily point to. It falls. That is the first thing you notice, entering Dripstone—the sound, the *loudness* of it, not a roar but a constant dense percussion, thousands of individual impacts per second, water falling from the ceiling onto the roofs of buildings, onto the rock between buildings, into the channels and troughs that make up the settlement's circulatory system. The rock weeps. The atmosphere itself weeps. The air is saturated, supersaturated, carrying more water than physics suggests it should, and that excess condenses on every cooler surface—pipe, wall, the bare skin of your forearm—running down in continuous streams that join the falling water, the channel water, the water that has nowhere else to go and so invents new paths through the young stone, beginning already, despite the rock's infancy, the work of erosion that will not complete for epochs.

It is hot. 2.8 on the scale means near the top, and the heat is not dry heat, not desert heat, but the wet compression of a laundry press, of a mouth held closed too long. The industrial processes generate it, certainly—the foundries and the catalytic baths and the electrochemical cells that are Dripstone's purpose and its prison—but the heat seems older than the industry too, rising from below, as if the young rock still remembers the magma it came from and speaks that memory in temperature. Steam rises from channels where falling water hits heated surfaces. The air shimmers even in the dimness. You do not walk through Dripstone so much as wade through it, through the thickness of its atmosphere, through the visible humidity that makes every light source into a haloed moon, every distant figure into a smear of possible humanity.

And the light is dim, 1.1, barely above darkness. The original designers—there must have been designers, though no one now claims the title—installed sodium vapor lamps, the industrial standard, but the humidity has defeated them over time, or perhaps the heat has, or perhaps they were simply not maintained. They glow now in amber patches, each one surrounded by its own luminescent fog, light struggling through water to reach the eye. In the spaces between lamps, darkness holds complete dominion, not the darkness of a sealed room but the living darkness of a place where light is consumed, where photons drown. The residents navigate these intervals by memory, by the sound of falling water, by the faint bioluminescence that has begun, unauthorized, unplanned, to colonize the wet surfaces.

The buildings. I must describe the buildings, for built is 2.2, urban, organized construction, layered, infrastructural. They are not tall—scale is small, 0.9, this whole world compressed to a single chamber, a hamlet of industry. Perhaps two dozen structures, none more than three stories, though stories is generous when floors sag and foundations shift on the fresh and settling rock. They are made of what was available: the black basalt itself, quarried from the chamber's edges and re-erected; precast concrete panels brought in through the single tunnel that connects Dripstone to whatever exists outside; brick, ceramic, the occasional desperate gesture toward organic material that the wet heat claims within months. They crowd toward the center of the space, leaving the margins to the rock and the water and the things that live in the margins.

Layered construction. This is important. The buildings accrete. A foundry built for one purpose is converted, a residential module grafted to its side, then another above, then a cooling tower that was never part of any original plan but became necessary, became inevitable, became architecture through the logic of heat dissipation. The layers do not match. Materials speak different eras, different intentions, different desperate solutions to problems that no longer exist or have become permanent. Pipes run everywhere, surface-mounted because the walls are too thin, too saturated, too young to hold embedded infrastructure. They carry water, yes, but also condensed steam being returned to the cycle, chemical effluent, the blood-warm coolant that keeps the electrochemical cells from thermal runaway. The pipes sweat. Everything sweats. The buildings appear to be weeping too, weeping in sympathy with the rock, with the air, with the whole saturated system.

Industry. Tech 2.2, industrial, mechanized, electrified, scaled production. But scaled to what? The scale is small, the chamber is small, and so the industry is dense, compressed, intense rather than extensive. They make things here, or made things, or make something that is not quite the thing they started making. The foundries produce ingots of an alloy that reacts with the humid air, that must be immediately coated, immediately shipped, immediately removed from this place that wants to reclaim all metal to rust, all stone to solution, all human intention to the entropy of wet heat. The electrochemical cells produce hydrogen, or did, or produce something that contains hydrogen, that burns with a flame invisible in the sodium light. The catalytic baths—there are three of them, open vats like swimming pools filled with reagent, steaming constantly, their surfaces disturbed by the falling water from above, by the condensation from the air, by the drips from the inadequate roofing that was added later, that was layered on, that is itself already failing.

The industry runs. That is the dynamic parameter: 0.3, never changing, stable, equilibrium, frozen, time-invariant. It does not run well. It runs *constantly*, at the same rate, in the same way, responding to no variation in demand, no change in input, no human decision to increase or decrease. The foundry pours ingots at intervals measured by some internal logic that has nothing to do with orders or markets. The electrochemical cells draw current that flickers but never ceases. The catalytic baths bubble at a constant rate, their temperature held in some dead zone between efficiency and catastrophe by control systems that were installed, that run, that no one adjusts because no one knows how, because the knowledge of their adjustment has been lost or was never transmitted, because the people here are not operators but inhabitants, living in the equilibrium that the machines have found without them.

And the people. I have not mentioned the people. They are sparse, density 1.3, much space between presences, balanced with emptiness. Perhaps two hundred in a space that could hold more, that could hold thousands if it were differently arranged, if the industry did not claim the center, if the margins were not given to the rock and the water and the fauna. They live in the buildings, in the layered accretions, in spaces that were never intended for habitation but became necessary, became home, became the only dry—dry is relative, everything is wet, but the *relatively* dry—places in the constant precipitation. They do not interact much. The density permits solitude, enforces it, makes each person an island in the falling water, connected by the sound of that falling, by the shared humidity, by the occasional necessary passage through the industrial zones where the machines run, have always run, will always run.

The fauna. 1.7, abundant, more abundant than the sparse human presence, more abundant than the sparse vegetation. For there is vegetation, 0.8, sparse, struggling against the dimness and the wet and the heat. Algae, primarily, in every pool and channel, in the thin films that make the rock surfaces treacherous, that colonize the concrete and the brick and the sweating pipes. Lichens in the cooler margins, the pale green and gray of early succession on young substrate. And mushrooms, fruiting bodies pushing through the saturated walls, through the floors, through the very mattresses where people attempt sleep in the loud dim wet heat. The mushrooms are not the vegetation, or they are, or the distinction collapses here where life categories blur in the constant moisture.

But fauna. Abundant. Insects, first, always insects in wet heat, midges and gnats and the larger beetles that navigate by the sodium lamps, that die in such numbers their bodies form drifts in the corners, that are eaten by other fauna before those drifts can accumulate too far. Spiders, then, in the high corners of the buildings, in the spaces between the layered construction, webs heavy with condensation, webs that catch not just insects but the water itself, that become visible architecture of thread and droplet in the dim light. Rats, or rat-equivalents, something mammalian that has found the food streams, the waste streams, the constant temperature that permits no season of scarcity. They are bold, these mammals, moving through the industrial zones with the confidence of creatures that have never known a predator, that have found their equilibrium too, their niche in the never-changing system.

And other things. Things that may have come with the original construction, stowaways in the concrete or the reagent. Things that evolved, or were selected, or that emerged from the wet chemistry of this place. Filter-feeders in the falling water, sessile animals that look like stone, like the basalt itself, that open to catch the organic matter in the constant precipitation, that close when disturbed, that have begun to colonize the industrial equipment, that must be scraped periodically from the pipes and the channels and the surfaces of the catalytic baths. They are not dangerous, exactly. They are *present*, abundant, part of the system that does not change, that has found its equilibrium with them as with everything else.

The sound. 1.8, loud, and I have described the water, the falling, the percussion, but there is more. The industry makes sound, the machines in their equilibrium, the foundry's pour, the cells' hum, the compressors that move gas through pipes that ring like bells with each pressure pulse. The fauna makes sound, the insects' constant frequency, the mammals' occasional cry, the strange wet clicking of the filter-feeders as they open and close. And the buildings make sound, the settling of young rock, the creak of layered construction adjusting to the heat and the moisture, the ping of cooling metal, the drip of water finding new paths through materials that have not yet learned to be permanent. The sound does not vary. It is loud, and it is constant, and it is the same loudness, the same composition, at every hour of every day in this place that has no day, no night, no cycle, no change.

Weirdness is 0.4, normal, and this is the hardest thing to hold, this normalcy, because the place seems so strange, so *other*, to any outside description. But to be there, to live in it, is to understand that it is not weird, not really, not to itself. The water falls. The heat presses. The machines run. The people move through their sparse density, their balanced emptiness, finding food, finding rest, finding the small negotiations of social existence that require no more density than this. The fauna lives and dies in abundance. The vegetation struggles and persists in sparseness. The rock weeps its youth, its fresh formation, its unweathered sharpness that will, in millennia, in epochs, become something else, but that now is only itself, only new, only *here*.

The rules of the world. This is what I have been circling toward. The rules are simple: nothing changes, and everything persists. The equilibrium is not comfortable but it is stable. The heat does not increase, though it is always hot. The water does not rise, though it is always falling. The industry does not expand or contract, though it is always running. The people do not increase or decrease, though they are always present, sparse, spaced in their balance with emptiness. There is no future in Dripstone, but there is no past either, no memory of change, no anticipation of it. The young rock will eventually age, but not in any timescale that matters to the system. The layered construction will eventually collapse, but the equilibrium finds ways to shore, to accrete, to add another layer that addresses the failure of the previous.

To enter Dripstone is to enter a moment that has been extended indefinitely, a photograph of industrial desperation that has become, through persistence, through the absence of any outside force to disrupt it, a kind of habitat, a kind of *place*, with its own ecology, its own fauna, its own rules of being. The water falls. The heat presses. The machines run. The people move through the loud dim wet, finding their paths by memory, by the sound of falling water, by the faint bioluminescence that is not weird, that is normal, that is simply what light does here, what life does, what everything does in Dripstone, in the equilibrium, in the never-changing now.
