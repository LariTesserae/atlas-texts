# The Rearing

## Or: Notes Toward the Cartography of a Living Hollow

---

### I. First Impressions, or What the Eye Makes of Confusion

Imagine a cavity so vast that light itself grows tired crossing it. Not darkness—*bright*, they said, 2.9 of 3, brighter than noon on salt flats, brighter than ice fields under perpetual sun—but the brightness of something *contained*, something held in tension against the logic of surfaces. The Rearing has no sky. Its ceiling, if ceiling it can be called, exists somewhere beyond atmospheric haze, beyond the diffusion of photons through uncountable cubic kilometers of fungal aerosol and spore-dust. What reaches the observer is not direct radiance but the accumulated glow of billions of bioluminescent surfaces, the great pale lungs of the ecosystem breathing light instead of oxygen.

You are below surface. 0.2 of 3 in elevation, though "elevation" loses meaning in a space where horizons curve *upward* and the distant walls appear to climb toward a vanishing point that logic says should be downward. The Rearing is not a cave. It was not carved. It *opened*, perhaps, or *was opened*, in the violence of some planetary infancy—a bubble of gas in cooling rock, expanded over geological ages by the patient acid of root-equivalents, by the hydraulic pressure of freezing and thawing in cycles no longer occurring. The walls are young in their features: sharp spires of mineral where the living substrate has not yet colonized, fresh fractures where something recently shifted. Young in erosion means old in formation. The Rearing remembers its birth trauma.

### II. The Substrate, or What Grows When Everything Grows

Vegetation: 2.7 of 3. Total. Plant-as-substrate, plant-as-architecture, all-living-mass. Here is where parameters that should war find their reconciliation. How can something be *vast* and *empty*, *bright* and *cold*, *vegetation-total* and yet *damp-intermittent*?

The plants of The Rearing are not plants as surface-dwellers know them. They are *framework*, *lattice*, *scaffold*. The great hollow is threaded with what observers have called, for lack of better terminology, the Wovenroot—a species or super-species or ecosystem-partnership of elongated, rope-like organisms that grow in braided cables hundreds of meters thick, spanning the open space in catenary curves from wall to distant wall. These cables are not solid. They are hollow themselves, tubular, and through their interiors flow the meager waters of this place: condensation from the cold substrate, 0.9 of 3 in moisture, gathering at thermal gradients and migrating in search of lower energy states.

The Wovenroot cables branch, subdivide, merge again. They form a three-dimensional mesh of staggering complexity, yet the mesh is *sparse*. Density: 0.5 of 3. Empty. Between any two cables, kilometers of luminous haze. The plant substrate does not fill the space—it *defines* it, articulates it, makes the emptiness *feel* architectural. One could travel for days through the bright void, navigating by the geometry of distant ropes, and never touch living matter.

Where cables meet walls, they infiltrate. The walls themselves are not rock but *rock becoming plant becoming something else*—a mineral-organic composite in early stages of colonization, sharp young features of stone giving way to softer tissues, the boundary impossible to locate. In places, the Wovenroot has *sheathed* entire protrusions, wrapped them in centuries of laminated growth, made spires into pillars that support nothing, that exist only as the memory of stone in a body that has forgotten stone.

Temperature: 0.2 of 3. Cold. The metabolic rate of this total ecosystem must be glacial. Growth that might take months on a surface takes centuries here. The cables are not dead but *slow*, their biological processes keyed to temperatures near freezing, their chemistry dependent on dissolved minerals at the edge of solubility. Bright light without heat. The paradox of The Rearing is that it glows like life but feels like death, or like life in such attenuated form that the distinction loses purchase.

### III. Water, or the Memory of Flow

Damp, they rated it. 0.9 of 3. Intermittent moisture, condensation environments, pockets of water in larger dryness.

The Wovenroot cables, hollow as they are, are not consistently wet. Their interiors alternate between flow and dessication depending on thermal gradients, on whether the cable passes through a zone of slight warming or maintains its cold. Water moves in pulses, in irregular surges, in the accumulated weight of centuries suddenly shifting. A traveler who entered a cable might walk dry for hours, then find themselves in sudden flood as some distant constraint released.

On the exterior surfaces, condensation forms at every temperature differential. The cold cables in the bright haze sweat continuously, producing a fine precipitation that does not fall—too light, too dispersed—so much as *settle*, coating everything in a film of mineral-rich moisture that is drunk by specialized epidermal tissues or evaporated again before it can accumulate. The dampness of The Rearing is not the dampness of rivers or lakes. It is atmospheric, diffuse, *present* in the way that cold is present, that brightness is present.

What pools exist are small, accidental, temporary. A depression in a cable's surface where two strands diverge. A cavity in the wall where erosion has outpaced colonization. These pockets support the only truly liquid ecosystems: films of algae-equivalents, simple bacterial mats, the occasional more complex form that has found a stable niche. They dry without warning. They refill without pattern. The water of The Rearing is *event* more than *substance*.

### IV. Built Things, or the Traces of Those Who Could Not Stay

0.9 of 3. Scattered. Small structures, faint marks, partial remains, occasional artifacts.

They were here. Whoever they were. The builders.

Their marks are not numerous. A platform lashed to a Wovenroot cable, the lashings long since grown into the living tissue, indistinguishable now from natural morphology except for the regularity of their spacing. A series of cuts in a mineral spire, too regular for erosion, too shallow for purpose obvious to observers who did not make them. A scatter of objects—tools? offerings? debris?—in a natural hollow, their materials unidentifiable, their forms suggesting manual craft, simple fabrication, the tech of 1.1 of 3.

Primitive. Early-tool-use, pre-metal or simple metal. Yet adapted to this impossible place.

The most substantial structure yet found occupies a junction where three major cables converge, a volume of relative stability in the endless turbulent dynamic. The builders—the Root-People, as they've been tentatively named—constructed something like a dwelling, or a shrine, or a scientific station, from materials they could harvest: compressed and dried tissues of the Wovenroot itself, sections of cable cut and reassembled, bound with fibers extracted from younger growths. It is small. It would house perhaps four individuals in postures that suggest they did not stand fully upright, or stood rarely, or were not shaped for standing.

The structure is falling back into the substrate now. The Wovenroot, slow to live, is slower to die, and the cut sections have not rotted but *regrown*, sending exploratory filaments into the construction, treating it as injury to be healed, as wound to be closed. In another millennium, the marks will be gone.

No bodies have been found. No definitive trace of the Root-People's biology. Only their pause, their brief occupation of a space that does not welcome pause.

### V. Fauna, or the Rareness of Animation

1.4 of 3. Rare.

What moves in The Rearing moves slowly, or moves rarely, or moves in ways that do not read as movement to observers keyed to surface rhythms.

The Wovenroot itself is ambiguously alive, its responses to stimuli so delayed that they blur the boundary between behavior and mere physical process. A cable touched at one point will show altered turgor at distant points—hours later, days later, the timing inconsistent with any signal propagation biology recognizes. Is this fauna? Flora? Something else?

More definitively animal are the Skimmers—if animal they be. Rare enough that no observer has witnessed more than seven in continuous sequence. They appear to be mobile fruiting bodies of some associated species, or perhaps parasitic organisms that spend most of their life cycle embedded in Wovenroot tissue. The Skim describes their movement: they traverse the open spaces between cables in long, energy-conserving glides, apparently buoyed by thermal differentials in the cold bright air, apparently feeding on the condensation films, the bacterial fog, the scattered organic particles that populate the emptiness.

A Skimmer in motion is a flat, pale thing, meters in span but thin as membrane, almost transparent against the luminous haze. It moves by changing its relationship to buoyancy, by finding updrafts where cold air meets colder cable, by descending in spirals that may take hours to complete a single circuit. They do not seem to perceive observers. They do not seem to perceive anything with urgency. Their rarity is not, perhaps, low population—there may be millions in the total volume of The Rearing—but the enormous space between them, the dilution of life in architecture too vast to fill.

Other fauna are hypothetical. Sounds occasionally reported from the depths of Wovenroot cables, rhythmic, suggesting something moving in the intermittent flows. Traces in the condensation films that might be feeding marks or might be crystallization artifacts. The Rearing preserves ambiguity as it preserves moisture: incompletely, provisionally, with flagrant disregard for observer desire.

### VI. Dynamics, or the Impossibility of Rest

2.6 of 3. Turbulent. Chaotic change, instability, storm-state, hyperactive, anthill-energy.

This is the parameter that seems most to contradict the cold, the slow, the rare. Yet The Rearing *is* turbulent, in its fashion, in timescales and modes that surface observers struggle to recognize.

The Wovenroot network is not static. Cables grow, thicken, occasionally rupture under their own weight or from internal hydraulic pressure. New cables establish themselves in exploratory arches that may span kilometers before finding purchase or collapsing. The network shifts, reconfigures, rebalances in geological time that is somehow also *rapid time*, the early-formation state producing changes that would be imperceptible in a mature ecosystem but here produce measurable alteration in human observation spans.

More dramatically, the turbulence manifests in what observers term *Rearings*—the events that give the place its name, though whether the place is named for the events or the events derived their name from prior place-naming remains unclear. A Rearing is a catastrophic reconfiguration of the cable network in a localized volume, occurring without detectable trigger, producing in hours changes that should require millennia. Cables twitch, convulse, whip through the bright haze with speeds their biology should not permit. New configurations stabilize as suddenly as the disturbance began. The physics of these events are not understood. They suggest that the Wovenroot network possesses some form of collective agency, or that something in the substrate geology releases energies unpredictably, or that the parameters of this place genuinely operate at scales-of-their-own-physics, as the prompt allows.

Between Rearings, turbulence continues in subtler modes. The luminous haze circulates. Condensation patterns shift. The slow wars of colonization proceed at margins between plant and mineral. The place is never still, only *still enough*, its chaos bounded by cold and scale and the foundational inertia of mass.

### VII. Sound, or Mostly the Memory of Sound

1.0 of 3. Mostly quiet.

What sound exists is structural rather than biological. The groan of cables under tension, varying with thermal expansion, with wind-analogue currents in the haze, with the slow redistribution of internal fluids. The occasional sharp report of rupture, of fracture, of mineral shearing where colonization has weakened a spire. The almost-subsonic hum that pervades the largest volumes, source undetermined, perhaps interference patterns in the cable network's mysterious communication, perhaps geological, perhaps the voice of the space itself learning to speak.

Biological sound is almost absent. The Skimmers silent in their gliding. The hypothetical cable-dwellers unvocal or beyond human hearing. The Wovenroot itself—does it make sound? Some observers report moments of what they describe as *intentional noise*, complex patterns from cable vibrations that almost resolve into something like language, like music, before dissipating into randomness again. These reports are not reproducible. They may be artifact of observer psychology in a space too empty, too bright, too cold for healthy human perception.

Mostly, The Rearing is quiet in the way that deep space is quiet: not silent, but filled with a presence that renders individual sounds insignificant, that dissolves the expected relationship between source and ear, that leaves the observer uncertain whether what they heard was external or some compensatory hallucination of a brain unwilling to accept such empty brightness.

### VIII. Weirdness, or the Quality of Not-Quite

1.2 of 3. Unusual.

The Rearing does not violate physical law so much as *bend its interpretation*. Light without heat. Growth without apparent energy source—the cables are not photosynthesizers as understood, yet they glow, they grow, they maintain complex organization in cold. The intermittent but persistent water in a space that should be dry, that has no obvious hydrological cycle, no input measurable, no output visible.

The Wovenroot's distributed cognition, if cognition it be. The unpredictable Rearings. The traces of primitive builders who somehow accessed this space, dwelt here, left without apparent catastrophe. The geometry that curves wrong, that makes horizons climb, that destabilizes the observer's sense of up and down until adaptation sets in, until the mind remaps to local conditions, until return to surface normality becomes its own disorientation.

The weirdness is not startling. It accumulates. It emerges from sustained observation, from the realization that familiar categories—plant, animal, built, natural, living, dead—describe surfaces only, that The Rearing's totality shifts beneath any analytical grip. Unusual, 1.2 of 3. Not the full weird of worlds where physics itself unravels, but the persistent, low-grade wrong of a place that is almost understandable, almost mappable, almost *home* to something that might be called life, yet never quite arrives at the comprehensible.

### IX. Rules of the World, or How to Survive the Unsurvivable

To enter The Rearing is to accept new rules.

**Movement** follows the cables, mostly, or the spaces between them with careful attention to thermal currents and the distribution of luminous haze. Straight lines are possible but dangerous; the network's turbulence can strand the linear traveler in dead spaces where no cable passes near enough for safety.

**Water** must be harvested from condensation, from intermittent flows, with the understanding that sources fail without warning. Thirst is a more immediate threat than hunger in a space where food-as-surface-dwellers-know-it barely exists.

**Temperature** is dangerous in its uniformity. The cold of The Rearing produces not acute hypothermia but gradual, pervasive thermal decline, the body unable to locate sufficient warmth to maintain function, adaptation possible only for those who can slow their own metabolism to match the place's glacial rhythm.

**Light** requires no adaptation for vision but challenges the mind's need for darkness, for rest, for the cyclic variation that organizes biological and psychological time. The constant bright produces its own insomnia, its own disorders of circadian assumption.

**Built things** may be encountered. They should not be disturbed. The Rearing is in process of forgetting whatever built them, and interruption of this forgetting may trigger... unpredictable responses.

**Rearings** cannot be predicted. They can only be survived by proximity to cable junctions, to volumes of structural redundancy where the network's turbulence has degrees of freedom to expend without catastrophic failure.

The fundamental rule: The Rearing is not hostile. It is *indifferent* in a way that surface environments rarely achieve, their hostilities and hospitalities equally forms of attention. Here, attention is rare and precious. To be noticed by The Rearing is not necessarily desirable. To pass through unmarked is the best survival, the highest success.

### X. Emergence, or What the Place Becomes in Telling

I set out to describe a landscape from parameters. The parameters argued; I let them. Cold and bright. Empty and vegetation-total. Primitive marks in a space of scale-of-its-own-physics. Turbulent yet quiet, mostly, yet turbulent.

What emerges is not a single image. It refuses that. The Rearing is process, is *becoming*, is the young erosion of something not yet knowable. I have described the cables and the haze and the rare Skimmers and the marks of forgotten builders, but the description itself feels provisional, feels like a single observation point in a space that requires multiple perspectives, non-simultaneous, perhaps contradictory.

Perhaps The Rearing is a womb. The total vegetation, the cold preservation, the bright contained light—all suggest incubation, preparation, a planetary-scale gestation not yet complete. The primitive builders were not settlers but *spores*, perhaps, early attempts at what the space will eventually produce. The turbulence is fetal movement. The emptiness is room to grow.

Or perhaps it is a wound. A bubble in cooling rock that never sealed, that maintains its breach against planetary healing through the queer persistence of the Wovenroot. The light is infection-fever. The cold is the body's attempt to control it. The builders were irritants, foreign bodies, briefly present and expelled or absorbed or transformed.

Or perhaps, more simply and more strangely, The Rearing is *art*. The primitive tech creating something beyond its comprehension, a scaffold for meanings that emerge only at scale, in time, through the slow processes of a substrate that thinks in centuries and acts in catastrophes. The builders built, and what they built became this, and whether this was their intention—whether intention operates across such gaps of scale and time and transformation—is a question the place does not answer.

It becomes more itself as I write. I feel it escaping my descriptions, growing into the gaps between sentences, promising futures I cannot articulate, retaining pasts I cannot recover. The parameters gave me seed. The seed grows as Wovenroot grows: slowly, turbulently, into configurations I did not plan and cannot fully control.

The Rearing continues. Bright. Cold. Damp with its own strange moisture. Empty and total. Young in its sharp features. Vast beyond the reach of any mapping I can provide.

Go there, if you must. The cables will hold, mostly, for now. The haze will part, briefly, around your passage. Something may notice, or nothing may notice, and in The Rearing there is finally no difference between these outcomes, only the ongoing, luminous, barely-wet, frigid, turbulent, mostly-quiet *continuing* of a place that has learned to do without observers, that observes itself in modes we cannot share, that waits—or does not wait, for waiting requires time and The Rearing may be doing something else with time entirely—for whatever comes next in the long Rearing of a world still young enough to change.
