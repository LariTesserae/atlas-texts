**The Drowned Grove of Keth-Vorosh**

At first you notice the pressure—not crushing, but *present*, like a held breath. Then the light: pale green-gold filtering from above, from a surface you cannot see, scattered through forty meters of water column and further diffused by the living canopy overhead. You are suspended. You are *held*.

This is not the open ocean. This is not a reef. This is a forest that forgot to die when the waters came, or perhaps—a forest that grew *into* the flood, that welcomed it, that learned to drink the sea itself.

The trees are not trees. Or they are trees that became something else, something between mangrove and kelp and cathedral pillar, something that joins wood to water without the boundary of bark. Their trunks rise thirty, forty meters from substrates you cannot name—root-masses the size of houses, root-masses that have *become* houses. The architecture of Keth-Vorosh is not built upon the grove; it is *grown from* it, indistinguishable, the same tissue performing structure and life.

The old ones call them *vorosh-nai*, the drinking-woods. They are not single organisms but *assemblages*—central columns of heartwood dense enough to sink, sheathed in pneumatophores that pulse faintly, that conduct gas from leaf-surface to root-tip in slow peristaltic waves you can feel if you press your palm flat and wait. The heartwood is dark, almost black, streaked with copper oxides that the inhabitants have learned to leach and work: not true metallurgy but *encouragement*, the way you might encourage a vine to trellis. The tools of Keth-Vorosh are wood, bone, compressed leaf-matter laminated in layers, and the soft red metal that forms where the vorosh-nai's vascular tissues concentrate minerals from the ancient deep.

You swim through spaces that were streets, once, or that function as streets now without ever having been planned. The settlement-scale here is not of blocks and intersections but of *chambers*—hollows where heartwood has been coaxed to grow in rings, leaving centers open; spaces where root-buttresses diverge to form natural vaulting. The "urban" quality emerges from density of use, not density of construction. There are perhaps four hundred dwelling-hollows in the central mass, another two hundred in the outrider thickets, each one a negotiation between inhabitant and tree, each one slowly adjusting as the tree grows, as the inhabitant's needs change, as the water chemistry shifts with season and depth.

The temperature is cool. Not cold—this is not polar water, not the crushing dark of abyssal trenches. Perhaps fourteen degrees at the canopy, eleven where the oldest root-masses descend to substrates that predate the flood. The vorosh-nai generate metabolic heat, not much, but enough to create weak thermoclines you can navigate by feel, enough to keep the deepest hollows from becoming lethal. The inhabitants wear little—skins tanned in bark-extracts, membranes harvested from the large gas-filled organisms that drift at the canopy interface, trapped air in bladders that let them rest in dry-ish chambers without the constant expenditure of buoyancy.

The fauna is *abundant*, loud, *present* in ways that open ocean never permits. This is the advantage of structure in water: surfaces for attachment, niches for specialization, the acoustic complexity of hard and soft boundaries. You hear the *click-click-click* of the marrow-snails that clean the vorosh-nai's outer tissues, each shell resonant as a bell. You hear the low-frequency thrumming of the tuber-fish, who bury themselves in the root-masses and sing through the water, through the wood itself, their songs traveling farther and faster in the dense medium than in the open sea. You hear the colony-organisms—"coral" is wrong, they are something closer to assembled shrimp, to communal ascidians—whose rasping feeding structures create a constant granular static, a rain of tiny sound.

Loudest of all: the air-breathers. Creatures that must rise to the canopy, to the strange surface-world of trapped gas and floating mats, to breathe. Their passage through the water columns creates wakes of turbulence, pressure-changes you feel in your teeth. When they dive back down, they carry air in modified throat-pouches, in repurposed swim-bladders, and their exhalation at depth creates *boluses* of atmosphere that rise ringing, chiming, unmistakable. The inhabitants of Keth-Vorosh navigate by these sounds, read them as weather, as time-of-day, as warning or invitation.

The vegetation—they do not have a separate word. The vorosh-nai *are* the world. Their leaves, broad membranous things at the canopy, host epiphytes that host further epiphytes, three, four degrees of parasitic specificity. The root-masses grow *through* older structures, digesting heartwood that has ceased its active processes, recycling the architecture of centuries into new growth. The "established" quality here is not stasis but *momentum*: forms that persist because they have persisted, because the system has found configurations that work, that repeat, that the trees remember in their growth patterns even when the original reason is forgotten.

But there is weirdness here, unusual quality, something that does not resolve.

The light. The light is wrong at certain depths, at certain times. Not the diffuse green-gold of normal days. Something *directional*, something *focused*, that enters through channels in the canopy that close behind it, that seems to *search*. The inhabitants avoid these beams, swim through them quickly if they must, make signs against what they call the *vorosh-vel*, the tree's-eye, though whether they mean the eye *of* the tree or the eye *within* the tree is unclear. The beams do not harm. But things that persist in them too long... change. Grow more woody. More *patient*. The old ones in the deepest hollows, the ones who have stopped moving, who pulse now only with the trees' own pneumatophore rhythms: had they chosen this? Had the light chosen for them?

And there is movement in the substrate. Not the tuber-fish. Something that rises sometimes through the oldest root-masses, something that leaves channels behind it, something that the trees grow *around* rather than through, that the trees seem to know, seem to have always known, the way you know your own bones. The inhabitants do not speak of it directly. They speak of *balance*, of *long durations*, of processes too patient to perceive. The erosion here is not wearing-down but *wearing-in*, the long settling of water into a form that water itself recognizes.

The density is sparse. Much space between presences. You can swim for minutes through green-lit water and encounter nothing but the trees themselves, their surfaces, their slow processes. Then suddenly a *node*: a market-hollow where root-buttresses happen to form a natural amphitheater, where inhabitants gather to trade gas-bladders for pressed-leaf tools, for marrow-snail bells, for the rare dried meats of surface-hunting. The sound there is overwhelming after the emptiness: voices, the click of tools, the constant wet percussion of bodies moving in water. Then dispersal again, back to scattered hollows, to solitary patrols of tree-health, to the deep dreamers who have begun their long merge.

Slow dynamics. Gradual cycles. The water level changes across years, decades—the vorosh-nai adjust their pneumatophore ratios, grow thicker or thinner heartwood, and the inhabited chambers shift accordingly, are abandoned, reoccupied, modified. Populations of the air-breathing megafauna boom and decline on century scales, their bones accumulating in hollows where the trees grow *respectfully* around them, incorporating them as structural elements, as memorials the trees do not understand but do not forget. The copper-red metal forms in tissues slowly, leached by specific techniques that require years of application, that must be *negotiated* with the tree's own mineral transport, that cannot be rushed without killing the host and losing everything.

Primitive tech. Simple fabrication. But *attuned*, *relational*, technologies of persuasion rather than extraction. The inhabitants have not domesticated the vorosh-nai; they have entered into something like mutualism, something that might be symbiosis or might be parasitism or might be a category that requires the tree to have desires, to have *preferences*, which perhaps it does, expressed in growth patterns and chemical signals that the most skilled practitioners learn to read. Their tools are extensions of negotiation. Their built environment is *grown encouragement*. Their urban quality emerges not from imposition on landscape but from *participation in* landscape, from the slow accumulation of arrangements that the trees tolerate, that the trees perhaps—very slowly, very coolly, very wetly—*prefer*.

At night, or what passes for night in the perpetual submarine twilight, the bioluminescence of certain epiphytes creates false constellations in the canopy, patterns that slowly shift as the host-leaves move in thermal convection. The inhabitants read these too, less for navigation than for *weather*, for the sense of being within something larger that knows they are there, that responds to their presence even if that response takes years to manifest in modified growth, in hollows that open or close, in the availability of copper-red, in the frequency of the *vorosh-vel* and its searching beams.

This is Keth-Vorosh. This is the Drowned Grove. It has been here long enough that "drowned" has lost its meaning of loss, has become merely description, has become perhaps a kind of *aspiration*. The water is not enemy. The trees are not victims. The inhabitants are not survivors. They are simply *this*, this configuration, this cool high-elevation forest that found the sea and grew into it, that built without building, that waits without impatience in the green loud patient dark, where the sound of tuber-fish thrums through your sternum and the light sometimes searches and the old ones dream their woody dreams and the world continues, slow, sparse, abundant, strange, and very thoroughly wet.
