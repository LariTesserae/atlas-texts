The Siphon Terraces rise in stepped shelves from a basin that never fully dries, though it is never fully wet either. Each level catches what moisture falls—not from frequent rain but from a persistent, invisible breath that condenses on every surface, that beads on the million million leaves and drips, drips, drips into collection pools that feed downward through ceramic channels to the level below, and the level below that, a thousand steps descending into mist that obscures whatever lies at the bottom. No one has seen the bottom. The moisture cycle is ancient, patient, sufficient.

This is young land. The terraces themselves are new—geologically infantile, perhaps three centuries since the uplift that raised them from seabed or compressed them from limestone, the geology remains disputed. What matters is the freshness: edges knife-sharp, pools glass-bottomed and still too young for much silt, cliffs unweathered to any softness. And yet lush. The vegetation has wasted no time. Moss carpets every horizontal in verdant impatience. Ferns unroll fiddleheads the size of your arm. Trees of no known family grow in such density that their canopies merge into a continuous ceiling, root systems interlocking in the thin soil like fingers clasped in prayer or struggle. You cannot walk in a straight line for more than three meters without encountering trunk, vine, dripping frond, or the pale suggestion of a mushroom cluster breathing spores into the wet air.

The light is extraordinary. The brightness parameter is near maximum, yet the sun never directly strikes most surfaces. Instead, some atmospheric condition—water vapor suspended at precise altitudes, particulate from the industrial works above, a property of the rock itself—diffuses and multiplies the light until it seems sourceless, omnipresent, a white-gold haze that eliminates shadow and flattens depth perception. You could read fine print anywhere. You could not, however, reliably judge the distance to that tree, that structure, that figure standing motionless among the ferns.

The structures are scattered, yes, but this understates their strangeness. They are not ruins. They are not precisely functional either. A tower of riveted iron stands alone on a terrace edge, its base already being consumed by banyan-like roots, its summit crowned with a mechanism that turns, turns, turns with no evident purpose, water dripping from its joints. A greenhouse of fractured glass houses only more of the same jungle that surrounds it. A railway track emerges from a tunnel in the cliff face, runs for forty meters on a trestle that sags but holds, and disappears again into another tunnel with no station between. The industrial technology is present, was present, but operates on logics difficult to recover. The machines run. Water is pumped, perhaps, or condensed, or transformed. Power lines—ceramic insulators still perfect, copper cables greened but intact—thread through the canopy. But to what end? The current flows. Somewhere, something is satisfied.

The fauna is rare, they said when I arrived. This is wrong. The fauna is absent in the way that makes you feel watched. I have found tracks in the moss, the delicate punctuation of something lighter than I can imagine. I have heard—no. The sound parameter is meant to be silence, near-silence, and so it is. The drip of water. The creak of that iron tower in wind I cannot feel. The occasional, sourceless mechanical exhale, perhaps from below, perhaps from everywhere. But no birdsong. No insect hum. No rustle of small things in the undergrowth. The density is vegetation, the density is the structures and their unexplained purpose, the density is the weight of light in your eyes. But the animals—there is a theory that they are there, always there, pressed between leaf and leaf, between the ticks of those turning machines, moving so slowly as to be imperceptible, patient as the geological processes that made this place, watching with eyes that do not need to blink.

The dynamic is slow. I have watched a pool for three days as its level rose almost imperceptibly, as a root found a crack in the ceramic lining and began, with vegetable patience, to widen it. The water will find a new path. The terrace will fail, eventually, in centuries. The process has already begun. Everything here has already begun. Nothing here finishes.

I found a journal in one of the scattered structures. Its pages were saturated, the ink bloomed into beautiful irrecoverable patterns. But on the cover, still legible, a single word in a language I almost recognized: *Drink.* Or perhaps *Drown.* The characters allow both readings.

The weirdness is not in any single thing but in the coherence of incoherence. The way the industrial and the vegetal have not struggled but negotiated, reached terms, intergrown. The way the light removes the possibility of shadow and thus of hiding, and yet nothing is revealed. The way the silence is not empty but occupied, held in reserve, waiting for some future sound that will retroactively explain all the quiet that preceded it.

I am going down. The terraces continue below the mist line, I am certain of it. The water flows down. The light penetrates, I am told, though I have not yet seen how. There are structures below, denser, more complete, their purpose perhaps more recoverable. Or perhaps more thoroughly lost. The slow dynamic suggests I need not hurry. The density suggests I will not travel alone, whether I will or no. The damp is constant on my skin, in my lungs, a permeation rather than a condition.

I will follow the water. I will follow the light that has no source. I will follow the railway that connects nothing to nothing, in case it knows something I do not.

The Siphon Terraces do not discriminate. They receive. They channel. They distribute. They do not, in any human timeframe, release.

I have been here long enough to no longer be certain when I arrived. The light does not change. The drip continues. Somewhere, a machine I have not found turns, and turns, and turns.
