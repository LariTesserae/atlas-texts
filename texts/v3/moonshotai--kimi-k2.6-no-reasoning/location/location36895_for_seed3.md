The city of Vervain hangs in a geological moment that should not exist— suspended between the *becoming* and the *already-was*, built into the walls of a canyon system that hasn't finished forming. The rock here is raw. Uncured. You can press your thumb into certain cliff faces and leave an impression that will persist for hours, days, centuries—time moves strangely here too—before the stone remembers it should be hard. Before the world corrects itself, or forgets to.

The canyon is extreme, yes. Two kilometers deep in places, though "deep" implies a finished cutting, and this is more like a wound that keeps reopening. The river at the bottom, the Verv, is more memory of water than water itself. It appears in certain lights, or certain moods of the city, flowing through channels of packed clay and half-stone that glisten with the damp of condensation, of fog that rolls down from the canyon rim and never fully leaves. Other times the riverbed is dry cracked mud where children play at archeology, digging up fish fossils that shouldn't be there because the fish aren't dead, aren't even born, exist in a state of perpetual imminent arrival. The damp is intermittent, unpredictable. A street might flood with ankle-deep mist-rain at 3 AM, dry by dawn, flood again at noon for seventeen minutes. The city has learned not to store things at ground level. The first floors of buildings are transitional spaces—workshops, shrines to uncertainty, places where you keep what you don't mind losing.

And there are buildings. Oh, there are buildings. Vervain is urban, organized, layered with the density of intention. But built *into* this raw geology, this emerging erosion, this reality that hasn't committed to its own form. The architecture is a negotiation. You see it in the foundations—massive iron pylons driven into stone-that-isn't-stone-yet, their lower sections slowly being swallowed, reformed, becoming something between support and inclusion. Buildings lean slightly, not from age but from *becoming*—the ground still settling into decisions about what it will be. The city engineers, the Bureau of Provisional Permanence, have developed techniques. Flexible joints. Foundations that can shift meters and hold. A building might be erected in one location and, over decades, find itself in another as the canyon wall recedes, advances, hesitates. The city moves without moving. There are addresses that have drifted. You receive mail from versions of yourself who lived where your building was before it migrated.

The industrial character of Vervain is unmistakable. Smokestacks—though they vent more often steam than smoke, the city running on geothermal ambiguity, on heat from a core that hasn't decided its own temperature—rise in clusters along the canyon's switchback terraces. Factories occupy the wider ledges, their machinery adapted to the provisional nature of their footing. Vibration is a constant engineering concern. You feel it walking the streets, a low-frequency hum that enters the sternum, that the body learns to interpret as *standing still*. The factories produce. It's unclear entirely what. Goods that exist in states of partial completion, that leave on trains (there is a train, the Vervain Incline, that climbs the canyon wall in a series of switchbacking cog-rail ascents, when it runs, when the tracks are where they were) and arrive elsewhere as things with uses, with purposes, with the solidity that Vervain itself denies. The city is a factory for making things real enough to leave. The workers are citizens first, their labor a form of residence permit, their products a kind of exportable proof-of-existence.

The light is dim. The canyon walls, extreme as they are, block direct sun for most of the day. What reaches the lower city is refracted, filtered through mists, through the perpetual condensation that beads on every surface, through the strange luminescence that sometimes emanates from the raw rock itself—a bioluminescence of geology, or perhaps of something else that has colonized the not-yet-stone. The upper city, on the canyon rim, gets more light, and there is a social stratification to this, though complicated by the fact that the rim is less stable, more prone to the reality-breakdowns that afflict the whole region. To live high is to live bright and to live in danger of sudden nonexistence. To live low is to live in perpetual twilight, in the damp, with the comfort of relative persistence. The middle terraces, where industry clusters, where the smokestacks vent their ambiguous exhaust, where the light is dimmest because of the particulate haze that never fully clears—these are the most populated, the most *stable*, as if the city has found its equilibrium in obscurity.

The vegetation is lush, yes, but *strange* lush. Not the verdancy of forests but of opportunism, of life finding foothold in provisional niches. Mosses dominate, and lichens, and things that might be fungi or might be something that has learned to photosynthesize in dimness, in damp, in the mineral leach of raw rock. They grow on buildings, on machinery, on the slow-moving trains when they sit too long. They are harvested, cultivated even, in hydroponic galleries that hang from canyon walls like the gardens of a slower, more patient civilization. The gardens are warm, surprisingly so, heated by the same geothermal ambiguity that powers the factories. Steam rises from them, contributing to the haze, the condensation, the perpetual damp. There are trees, stunted, adapted to ledge-life, to root-systems that grip uncertain stone. Their leaves are broad, dark, almost black, optimized for light-gathering in dimness. They grow slowly, as everything here grows slowly, or seems to—except when they don't, when a tree that was a sapling for decades suddenly completes a century of growth in a season, as if time had found a shortcut through it, as if the reality-breakdown had particular sympathy for arboreal ambition.

The fauna is abundant. This is perhaps the strangest thing, given the strangeness of everything else. Life here has adapted to the provisional, or perhaps was always provisional and finds this place honest. The vervain moths are famous—creatures of indeterminate species, their wings patterned with markings that shift, that are never the same between sightings, that some claim to read as messages, as prophecies, as the city's own dreams of itself. They are drawn to light, any light, and swarm the brighter terraces in clouds that make outdoor evening activity impractical. There are things in the river, when the river is there, that have no names, that negotiate the boundary between fish and idea-of-fish. There are birds that nest in the smokestacks, that have evolved, or been shaped by the city's industry, to thrive in the particulate haze, their lungs miraculous filters, their songs—loud, the city is loud with their songs—adapted to carry through dense air. The sound of Vervain is industrial and natural in layers that don't separate cleanly. The factory hum, the steam release, the train's cog-rail grind, the moth-swarm whisper, the bird-cries, the occasional *crack* of reality adjusting itself somewhere in the canyon, a building shifting, a new ledge emerging, an old one softening back to potential—these form a composition that residents learn to hear as silence, as background, as the condition of being here at all.

The reality breakdown. This is not chaos, not random. It has patterns, or the city has learned to impose patterns. Things *unhappen* here, but selectively. A street might forget it had a building, the building persisting in the minds of its residents, in their habits, in the furniture they arrange in open air, in the doors they open and close against walls that aren't there until, sometimes, the street remembers, the building returns, the furniture is inside again. Or it doesn't return, and the residents adapt, their lives becoming performance, their architecture of habit persisting without architecture of matter. There are neighborhoods where this is more common, where the provisional is more pronounced, where the Bureau of Provisional Permanence has given up on enforcement and simply marks zones as *unreliable* on official maps. People live there who prefer the flexibility, who have learned to carry their lives lightly, to not assume the persistence of walls or floors or the direction of gravity, which is itself occasionally negotiable in the deeper, dimmer parts of the canyon.

The dynamic is slow. Change happens, but across durations that require patience to perceive. The canyon widens, perhaps, but over centuries. The city shifts, but like a glacier, like a memory surfacing. The factories alter their production, but on schedules geological in their patience. And yet there are moments—there are always moments—when the slow accelerates, when the reality-breakdown manifests suddenly, when a building that was drifting for decades finds its new position in an hour, when the river arrives all at once and floods terraces that had forgotten water, when a moth's wing-pattern resolves into a message so clear it changes a life, a policy, a neighborhood's understanding of itself. These are the city's punctuation, its exclamations in a syntax of gradual becoming.

What holds it together? What makes Vervain a city and not merely a collection of provisional shelters in an impossible geology? There is a theory, popular among the Bureau philosophers, that the city is itself a kind of factory, its product being *coherence*, its raw material the reality-breakdown itself. That in building, in organizing, in imposing the urban on the emergent, Vervain performs a work of concentration, of making-real, that extends beyond its walls, that stabilizes a region that would otherwise be mere potential. The city as dam, as battery, as the thing that holds back—holds back what? The not-yet, the not-anymore, the could-be and the was-and-isn't. The city as labor against entropy, or against something stranger than entropy, something more like the refusal of things to commit to being anything at all.

You enter through the Incline, when it runs, or by the longer road that switchbacks down the canyon's gentler slope, or by air, though flight is complicated by the reality instabilities, by the way the air itself sometimes forgets to support. You arrive, and the damp settles on you, and the dim light, and the sound of the city enters your bones as vibration, as the hum of standing still in a place that moves. You find your terrace, your ledge, your position in the vertical city. You learn to read the moths' wings, or not. You learn to hear the factory sounds as music, or not. You build, or find built for you, a shelter that acknowledges its own provisionality, that leans slightly, that has a first floor for things you don't mind losing. You become, slowly, a citizen. The city makes room, or you make room in yourself for the city's strangeness. The rock is soft beneath your thumb. You press, and wait to see if the impression holds.
