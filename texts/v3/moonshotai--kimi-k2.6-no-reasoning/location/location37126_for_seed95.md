# The Murmuring Stair

## Terrain and the Logic of Stone

The plateau has finished eroding. This is the first thing you understand, though understanding comes slowly here, as everything does. For three hundred million years, wind and the rare thin rain have worked at this high place, and now they have stopped—not because they lack force, but because the stone has become *accommodated* to their touch. The surface is not flat but *resolved*, every angle softened to a curve that satisfies the equations of persistent wear. Ridges run like half-remembered gestures across the pale stone, and these ridges are the remains of mountains, though you would not recognize them as such. They rise perhaps thirty meters above the general level, these final humps, and their slopes are so perfectly graded that from a distance they resemble the backs of enormous animals frozen in the act of submergence.

The elevation is sufficient that the air carries a constant thinness, a quality not quite breathlessness but a reminder that breath is *effort*. Yet it is warm here, warm in the way of deep tissue held close to a body, and this warmth seems to emanate from the stone itself, which stores sunlight through the long days and releases it through the cooler hours with the patience of geologic memory. There are no shadows that ever truly cool. Even at what passes for evening—a slant of softer gold across the western ridges—the stone retains its heat, and the air above it shimmers with the memory of day.

Water exists here, but furtively, almost apologetically. It arrives as *arrival itself*—condensation on stone faces in the hours before dawn, thin films that gather and trickle into the same channels they have carved over incomprehensible time. There are no rivers, but there are *routes* where water has been, marked by slightly darker stone, by a particular polish, by the way certain curves in the terrain seem *expectant*. After the rare rains, which come perhaps twice in a decade and fall with the gentleness of long hesitation, there are pools. They last for days, sometimes weeks, shallow lenses of water held in the most resolved of hollows. The stone around these pools darkens, and for that brief window, the plateau almost remembers what it is to be wet.

## The Built and Its Absence

There are structures here, if structure is the word. They are not buildings, not ruins in any conventional sense, but *concentrations*—places where the stone has gathered itself differently, where the erosion resolved into something that could be mistaken for intention. A platform of stone, its edges not quite natural, not quite other, raised slightly above the surrounding level. A circle of smaller stones, each perhaps the size of a human head, arranged with a spacing that suggests neither randomness nor true pattern but something between: the *idea* of pattern, long dissolved. A single vertical stone, slender as a finger, that stands where no stone should stand, that has stood so long its base has worn to a socket in the platform below.

These are scattered. You might walk for half a day and find nothing, then come upon three such concentrations in the space of an hour. They do not seem to have been placed *for* anything, but rather to have *accumulated*, like the residue of some process too slow to observe. There is no technology here, no tool marks, no sense of the made. If hands ever touched these stones, they left no trace more recent than the stones themselves.

## The Fauna, Which Is the Place

The overwhelmingness of animal life here is not a matter of numbers, exactly. It is density of a different order. The creatures are *layered*, *interleaved*, *simultaneous*.

The most visible are the stone-flies, though they are not flies and have nothing of stone about them but their coloration, which matches the pale surface so precisely that they exist in a state of perpetual near-disappearance. They are large, the size of a human palm, and they do not fly so much as *settle* and *unsettle*, moving from ridge to ridge in slow parabolas that seem to test the air rather than traverse it. Their wings, when spread, reveal patterns like the erosion-ridges below, as if each fly carries a map of the plateau, as if the map and the territory have become indistinguishable.

Beneath them, in the sense that the stone has an beneath, are the tunnelers. These are never seen, only evidenced: the slight rise of dust from a fresh excavation, the sound of something *else* moving in the stone's own resonances, the occasional emergence hole that appears overnight and remains, slowly weathering back to the general level over years. They are numerous, these tunnelers. The plateau is more hollow than solid, has been for longer than solidity. Their network connects the scattered concentrations, passes beneath the rare pools, threads through the final humps. What they seek, what they eat, whether they are multiple species or one vast distributed organism—these questions do not resolve.

And then there are the listeners. These are the strangest, the most *other*. They are not numerous but they are *present*, overwhelmingly present, in a way that makes their rarity more intense rather than less. They appear to be stone. They are shaped like stones, weather like stones, sit like stones in the resolved curves of the terrain. But they *attend*. You can feel it when you pass near, though pass near is relative—they might be kilometers distant and still you feel the weight of their attention, the absolute focus of something that has nothing else to do but receive. What they hear, what they listen for, whether they distinguish between the sounds of wind and water and the rare visitor—these are not questions that can be answered. They are part of the strangeness, which is not the strangeness of the inexplicable but of the *over-explained*, the too-resolved, the final form that has become strange through sheer completion.

## The Sound of Almost Nothing

It is mostly quiet here. The wind, when it moves, does so with the softness of long practice, finding paths through the terrain that produce no turbulence, no whistle, only a *movement* of air that happens to reach the ear. The stone-flies make no sound in their settling and unsettling. The tunnelers, if they make any, keep it below the threshold of the stone itself.

But there are sounds. The condensation, gathering, produces a *presence* below hearing, a pressure in the inner ear that resolves occasionally into something like a note, sustained for hours, changing so gradually that change itself becomes imperceptible. The stone, heating and cooling, *breathes*—expands and contracts across its vast extent, and this movement produces frequencies below and above the audible range, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, in the bones of the inner ear that are not quite for hearing.

And the listeners, attending, seem to *create* a sound by their attention, a kind of negative acoustic, a shape in the air where sound would be if they were not absorbing it. Walking among them—and they are scattered across the plateau, never more than two in view of each other, never fewer than one in any day's travel—you become aware of your own noise, your breathing, your heartbeat, the slight rasp of cloth on skin, as *intrusions*, as *violations*. You learn to move differently. You learn to *be* differently. This is part of the place, part of what it does.

## The Ecology of Final Forms

Life here has reached what might be called, in another context, a climax community, except that climax implies cycling, implies the possibility of disturbance and recovery. Here there is only the *settled*. The sparse vegetation—young would call it wrong to expect trees, and there are none, only the low crusts of something between lichen and moss and plant, the pale green-grey of their growth almost indistinguishable from the stone they cling to—has found its final forms. They do not compete because there is nothing to compete for, or rather, what there is to compete for has been so thoroughly *apportioned* that competition has become indistinguishable from cooperation.

The pools, when they exist, bloom. This is the closest the plateau comes to event. For those brief weeks, the water gathers spores from the wind, from the stone, from sources unknown, and there is a *greening*, a *thickening*, that seems almost violent in its rarity. Small things hatch. The stone-flies gather, settle on the water's surface, seem to *drink* though what they drink and why is not clear. The tunnelers make new holes, or perhaps the same holes, the distinction not mattering. And then the water goes, absorbed into stone and air, and the green fades to the pale memory of green, and the plateau resumes its patient waiting.

The fauna, overwhelming, does not overwhelm in the manner of abundance but in the manner of *saturation*. Every cubic meter of this place contains its portion of life, but life so *adapted*, so *resolved*, that it has become continuous with the stone, with the air, with the thin films of water and their absence. To remove any element would be to remove the whole, not through connection but through *indistinguishability*. The stone-flies are the stone. The listeners are the listening. The tunnelers are the tunnels. This is the ecology of the plateau, which is not an ecology at all in the usual sense, but a *condition*, a *state*, a final form that has become strange through its own completion.

## The Rules That Are Not Rules

There are patterns here, but they are not rules. They are *tendencies*, *inclinations*, *habits of long standing*.

Things tend to move slowly. The stone-flies in their settling, the water in its gathering and dispersal, the very light in its progression across the day—these are processes that resist acceleration. Not through friction, not through resistance, but through *agreement*, through a consensus among all elements that this is the rate at which things happen, that to move faster would be to move *wrongly*, to introduce a note of discord into the long resolution.

Things tend to gather. The concentrations of stone, the listeners in their scattered attendance, the pools in their hollows—these are not random distributions but *accumulations*, places where the plateau has *settled* more densely, more intensely. To be near one of these gatherings is to feel the density of the place, the many presences per volume that the parameters suggest. Not crowded, not cramped, but *full*, *complete*, *without room for addition*.

Things tend to persist. The plateau has persisted. The forms upon it have persisted. The listeners have listened for longer than there is language to describe. This persistence is not immobility but *patience*, not death but *waiting without expectation*, the dynamic of slow cycles that have become so slow as to be indistinguishable from stasis, yet are not stasis, are *life at the rate of stone*.

## What Emerges

What emerges, walking this place, is the sense of having arrived at a *solution*. Not a solution to a problem, but a solution in the chemical sense, a dissolving of all elements into a medium that holds them in suspension, that is itself the product of their interaction. The plateau is this medium. The warmth, the thin water, the sparse green, the overwhelming fauna, the scattered marks of something never quite built, the listening stones and the tunneling absences and the settling flies—the these are not separate things but *aspects*, *modes*, *appearances* of a single condition.

To be here is to be *in* it, to become, gradually, gradually, part of the resolution. You do not forget yourself, exactly. You do not lose yourself, exactly. You *accommodate*, as the stone has accommodated, as the wind has accommodated, as all things here have found their final form through long patience and longer persistence. The strangeness is not that this is alien. The strangeness is that it is *familiar*, that it recognizes something in you that you did not know was there, a capacity for stillness, for attention, for being *present* without demand or expectation.

The light changes. It is always changing, but so slowly that the change is itself a kind of constancy. The listeners attend. The tunnelers tunnel, or do not tunnel, the distinction not mattering. The stone-flies settle and unsettle. The water gathers, briefly, and disperses. The plateau continues, finished, complete, waiting without expectation, strange in its resolution, overwhelming in its density, sparse in its life, warm in its stone, high in its thin air, damp in its furtive water, ancient beyond the need for age.

This is the place. This is what it is. This is what you have come to, or what has come to you, or what was always here, waiting for the recognition that is itself a form of dissolution, a gathering into the final form that waits, patient, resolved, complete.
