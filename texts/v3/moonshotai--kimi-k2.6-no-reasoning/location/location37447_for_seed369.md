The Weeping Terrace

---

You come upon it after the trail, such as it is, simply ends. Not dramatically—there is no cliff, no warning. The ground merely graduates from packed earth to something else, something that gives slightly underfoot like a lung breathing out, and you are *in it*.

The Terrace.

It is small. You could walk its length in perhaps forty minutes, its breadth in twenty, though you won't. You won't want to. The scale deceives; it feels larger inside itself, as though its boundaries curve away from traversal. Cathedral-grove, yes, but a cathedral built by no hand, a single room whose walls are distance itself. From the center, you cannot see any edge at all, only the repeating gesture of ferns and the white noise of water on stone.

The elevation is high—nearly two kilometers by any measure—but you would not know it. There is no thinness to the air, no wind-scour. The Terrace sits in a pocket, a thermal and atmospheric anomaly, warm as lower valleys, warm as skin. Condensation beads on your forearms the moment you stop moving. The warmth breathes upward from the stone itself, which is pitted and cratered like something lunar but *active*, weeping, alive with seepage.

Long ago, something shaped this bowl. Not hands. Water, perhaps, working across millennia, or the slow settling of volcanic stone into its own exhausted form. The erosion is *finished*, or rather, it has achieved equilibrium. Nothing sharp remains. Everything rounded, softened, weathered into collaboration with its own decay. Boulders the size of houses rest half-submerged in moss, their undersides dark with perpetual moisture, their tops hosting gardens of liverwort and the tiny white flowers that have no name you would know.

And water. Water at 1.7—not a lake, not a river, not even properly a wetland, though it shares qualities with all three. The water lives *inside* things here. It condenses on the underside of fern fronds and drops, *drop*, in slow percussion. It weeps from the stone faces, hundreds of seeps, thousands, each tracing its own pale mineral path down into the general saturation. The ground *reflects* without being mirror-like—some quality of mica in the soil, perhaps, or some deeper strangeness, so that walking across certain stretches gives the vertiginous sense of walking on sky, on the inverted image of your own body suspended above cloud.

The vegetation is lush, almost aggressive in its abundance, yet the density is sparse. This is the paradox of the Terrace. Growth *explodes* but *spreads*. Each fern, each fungal colony, each nodding orchid has claimed territory sufficient for hundreds of its kind, yet grows alone, in state, attended by its own drip of water and its own patch of luminescent moss. The space between presences is not empty but *charged*, held in balance, as though each plant exudes a field that repels competition. You can walk freely. Nothing tangles. Nothing walls you. Yet you are always *among*, never *through*.

---

The sound is loud. This is the shock of the place, after its quietude of aspect. The loudness is not of volume but of *persistence*, of density. Every drop, amplified by the bowl's acoustics. The seepage from ten thousand pores. The collapse of saturated moss under your step. The *movement* of growth, which here you can almost convince yourself you hear—the cellular stretch of fiddleheads uncoiling, the hydraulic rush in fern stems, the almost-subsonic pulse of mycelial networks threading through stone. No single sound dominates. It is composite, *orchestral*, a drone with ten thousand partials, never varying in its variation. You stop noticing it, then notice it again with a start, as you notice your own heartbeat.

Fauna is rare. Not absent—the parameters permit nothing so absolute—but *rare* as a reigning quality, a statistical truth made atmospheric. You will not see birds. You may, if you sit motionless for an hour, observe a single amphibian, something pale and translucent, moving through the moss with the slow patience of the place itself. There are insects, but they make no sound, or their sound submerges into the greater water-drone. Once, I am told, a traveler found a bone. White, water-smoothed, belonging to no creature anatomists could reconstruct. She left it where it lay. The rarity of fauna does not feel like absence but like *concentration elsewhere*—as though the life of the Terrace has pooled into the vegetal, the fungal, the liquid, and abandoned the animal as an experiment concluded.

---

The weirdness is moderate, present, *uncanny-without-threat*. I have mentioned the walking-on-sky. There are other qualities. Time moves strangely; not faster or slower, but *thicker*, each moment containing more of itself. You will check your watch—if you wear one, and it functions here—and find hours passed in what felt like minutes, or minutes endured like hours, with no correspondence to experience. The ferns remember. This is the only way I can phrase it. Walk a path, return the next day, and the ferns have *not moved* but have *noticed*, have *recorded*, and something in their posture, their collective orientation, acknowledges your return.

There is also the matter of the deep pools.

Not many. Three, perhaps four, depending on the season and whether they choose to reveal themselves. They form where seeps converge in depressions worn smooth as pottery. The water in them is not especially deep—rarely more than a meter—but *looking into them*, you will see depths that should not exist. Light behaves incorrectly, or your perception of it does. You will see, I am told, the sky from below, or the stone from above, or your own face young and old superimposed, or nothing at all, only a color you lack the name for. The pools are the most primitive of technologies, the simplest of fabrications: hole, water, light, eye. Yet they function as *instruments*, as *tools* for seeing something otherwise unseeable. Whether this is property of the pools or of the mind that looks into them, no one can determine. The distinction may not obtain here.

---

The rules of this world, then. The laws that emerge from its specific gravity.

First: *saturation without drowning*. Everything is wet, will be wet, yet nothing rots improperly. The decay is clean, participating in immediate recycling, fungal to fungal, water to stone to water. The lushness feeds on its own ending.

Second: *space without loneliness*. The sparsity of density, the cathedral interval between presences, yet no abandonment, no desert quality. Each entity *attends* to its neighbors across the distance, in some medium not yet measured.

Third: *change without urgency*. The dynamic is slow, patient, cycle upon cycle, yet not static. The ferns grow. The seeps shift their paths by millimeters per decade. The pools migrate across the stone, following some hydrologic logic invisible to short-lived observers. The Terrace is *going somewhere*, but its destination requires a timescale that makes human ambition a kind of nervous tic.

Fourth, and last: *welcoming without possession*. You may enter. You will not be prevented, not by wall or warning or inhospitable condition. The warmth receives you. The water touches you. Yet you will not want to stay—not because it rejects, but because it *has no use for you*. Your presence is noted, filed, left without consequence. The Terrace continues its slow breathing, its water-drone, its vegetal contemplation. You are a momentary condensation on its surface, a drop that will fall and be forgotten in the general recording.

---

I have described what I know, what has been told, what can be inferred from the parameters that shaped this account. The rest belongs to the Terrace itself, which preserves itself in its own saturation, its own slow becoming, its own strange and balanced solitude.

If you go, go with patience. The only tool required is attention. The only craft, endurance. Take nothing. The water will provide if you need. Leave nothing. The stone records more completely than you know.

Walk to the center. Stop. Listen to the loudness of seep and drop and growth. Feel the warmth rising, the condensation beading, the sky inverted underfoot.

Wait.

Something may come. Something may not.

The Terrace does not hurry.
