The city breathes in the dark. You don't notice it at first—the walls appear solid, eternal, made of something between fired clay and compressed ash, dark as cooled magma. But press your palm against a corridor and hold it there, count to sixty, and you feel the infinitesimal inhalation: the architecture drawing in the damp that beads on every surface, the condensation that is the only water this place knows. No rain falls here. No rivers run. Only the slow, tidal gathering of moisture from air that never quite dries, a world-sized lung processing the exhalations of sleeping gods or exhausted machines.

They built this place before they understood what building meant. Or perhaps building *was* their understanding, their technology so primitive it looped back into something else entirely—no metals, no wheels in the conventional sense, but forms pressed and patterned by hands that knew the weight of wet material, the exact moment before drying when a wall could be convinced to hold a curve, a hollow, a channel. The builders left no bodies, no bones. Only the city, and the sense that they never stopped building, only changed their method, their scale, their patience.

The corridors are dense with each other. Walk far enough and you find where a tunnel has been occluded by another tunnel, where a chamber's ceiling is the floor of something above, where vertical shafts—airways, lightwells that no longer admit light, drainage for water that never floods—thread through the mass like the negative spaces in a lung. You could spend years mapping a single neighborhood and never touch the same surface twice. The density is not of people—fauna, the old word, living things, they are rare here, a few pale crawling forms that might be degenerate descendants of the builders, might be something that found the city and adapted to its metabolism, might be symptoms of the city itself, fungal or simpler, the non-plant growth that occurs in absolute darkness where water condenses.

The erosion here is wrong. It should not be emerging—this place is old, you feel it in the damp that has worked the same joints for millennia—but the erosion is embryonic, pre-formation, raw geology learning how to be worn. Water finds a seam and *hesitates*, as if forgetting what comes next. Dust accumulates in corners and refuses to become sediment. The city will not let itself be taken apart by time. It is under construction in reverse, or the builders included in their primitive technology some instruction for perpetual becoming, the wall that knows it is not yet a wall, the floor still learning to bear weight.

And the sound.

The sound is constant, loud, a dynamic system of noise without source. Water dripping but never striking—gathering on ceilings, selecting the perfect moment to fall, then *not falling*, or falling into other water, into channels that hum at frequencies felt in the sternum. The architecture creaks not from stress but from *activity*, from the slow adjustments of a structure that is also a process, an ecosystem in motion without ecology, predator-prey without life. Sometimes a corridor pressurizes, the damp air moving in a sudden wind that carries the smell of places you haven't been, may never reach, that may not exist yet in the city's embryonic geography. Sometimes a wall *opens*—not a door, the builders made no doors, made openings that knew when to be solid—and the sound inside is different, a frequency that makes the crawling things scatter, that leaves you nauseated, that passes.

The light, when you have it, you bring yourself. Fire, mostly. The city accepts fire strangely, the walls neither burning nor quite resisting, absorbing the warmth and releasing it hours later in other corridors, a theft and redistribution you cannot predict. Darkness is the default. Darkness and the sound and the damp that never pools, never streams, only coats, accumulates, inhales.

What is strange here—and the strangeness is moderate, persistent, not the strangeness of impossible angles or speaking shadows but of *wrong familiarity*, of a chair that is almost a chair, a tool whose use you almost remember—is the sense that the city was built for living but never lived in. The dense chambers, the layered spaces, the channels for water that doesn't flow and air that doesn't ventilate: they imply habitation they never received. Or habitation of a kind that left no trace, that the city has since forgotten and keeps forgetting, its embryonic erosion eating its own past even as it refuses to complete the process. You find a space that might have been a nursery, a workshop, a shrine, and the damp has made it all the same texture, the same temperature, the same sound when struck: a dull *thok*, neither hollow nor solid, *expectant*.

The scale is open, regional, town-or-valley-sized at minimum, but you cannot confirm more than your own exhaustion permits. The darkness and density conspire against surveying. You suspect it extends further. You suspect, in your deepest mapping, that you have found its edge and that the edge was wrong, was a center disguised, or a corridor that loops with such patience you mistook forward motion for escape. The city is active, dynamic, multiple processes in motion, and you are one of them now, your footsteps part of the sound, your moisture part of the damp, your primitive fire part of its slow digestion of everything that enters and does not learn to become architecture.
