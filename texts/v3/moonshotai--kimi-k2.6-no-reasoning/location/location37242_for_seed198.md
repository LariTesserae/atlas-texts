The city is called Threne. It exists in a bowl of mountains that should not, by any ordinary logic, support a city at all—elevation 2,400 meters, the air thin enough to make newcomers gasp, thin enough that fires burn differently, that the huge condensing engines must work harder to pull water from a sky already generous with it. And yet: water 1.5, wet, *marsh-edge* somehow made vertical, made architectural. The mountains cup a high depression where clouds lodge and weep, where perpetual mist beads on every surface, where the distinction between rain and air and breath has long since dissolved.

The wet is not the wet of rivers, not mainly. Threne has no rivers in the ordinary sense. Instead, water moves through the city as the city moves through water—permeation, seepage, the endless *weeping* of its constructed skin. Every exterior wall is designed to channel moisture downward through capillary action, through grooves and flutes and microscopic textures that the original engineers perfected two centuries ago and that have been replicated, patched, rebuilt, extended ever since. The buildings *cry*. The walls run with condensation that gathers into films, into trickles, into managed rivulets. You do not touch a surface in Threne and find it dry. The air itself drinks.

Vegetation: lush. 2.3. Here the parameters begin their strange negotiation with density 3.0, packed, and built 2.7, total. There is no natural substrate. Everything is made. And yet everything grows. Threne's builders—no one agrees whether they were prescient or merely desperate, ingenious or monstrously proud—constructed their city from a material that does not fully distinguish between structure and soil. The walls, the foundations, the thirteen concentric rings of elevation that step down toward the central depression, all of it is some ceramic-metabolic hybrid, fired in kilns that burned for decades, seeded during construction with biological catalysts that have long since exceeded their design parameters.

So the buildings *are* soil. The towers fifteen stories tall, the vaulted transit arcs, the dense-packed residential warrens where 400,000 people live in vertical adjacency that makes "packed" into something almost theological—these structures *support* growth. Moss with leaves the size of手掌. Fungi in shelves and brackets and luminous veined domes that pulse weakly in the dim. Vascular plants that have cracked the surface glaze or found purchase in the engineered fault-lines, the maintenance-access channels, the places where moisture concentrates and mineral weeping provides nutrients. The city is green-gray, green-black, the green of deep water and old copper. Lush, yes, but lush in the manner of a root cellar, of things growing without light, without the particular generosity of sun.

Temperature: 1.3. Cool. The elevation ensures this, the cloud-lid ensures this, the evaporative labor of all that weeping architecture ensures this. 8 degrees Celsius on a warm day. The vast condensing engines produce heat, but it is drawn immediately into the water-systems, into the thermal mass of the bio-ceramic structures, into the perpetual work of maintaining the moisture gradient that keeps Threne from drowning in its own exhalations. People wear layers. The fungal lamps that supplement the feeble natural light glow at temperatures just slightly above the ambient, and people cluster near them not for the light alone.

Light: 1.1. Dim. The cloud-lid is perpetual, or near enough. The sun, when it penetrates, does so as diffusion, as a general brightening of the gray that might last minutes or hours before the mountains squeeze out more moisture, before the atmospheric engine of this high bowl reasserts itself. Threne operates in twilight that has lasted centuries. The industrial systems—tech 2.5, industrial, electrified, the vast condensers and the manufacturing halls and the recycling processors and the pneumatic message tubes and the heavy extraction equipment that pulls useful minerals from the accumulated biomass of generations—throw their own light, but it is the light of purpose, not illumination. Sodium yellow. Arc white in the factories. The fungal bioluminescence, hacked and intensified over decades, provides the domestic glow, the street-level navigation, the sense that one moves through something *living* rather than merely built.

The dense-packed quality—density 3.0—means that there is always another surface arm's-length away, always another presence, another structure, another growth. Threne has no open spaces in the conventional sense. The "streets" are clefts between buildings, roofed with crossing architecture at varying heights, lit by the glow from windows and the fungal tubes. The transit arcs pass through tunnels bored directly through the bio-ceramic mass, sound-deadened by the moisture, by the soft organic growths that maintenance can never fully clear. You walk through Threne and the walls press, not unkindly, not with threat, but with the absolute fact of *occupation*. Every cubic meter has been fought for, claimed, engineered, filled. The population lives in vertical sections, in the old industrial volumes converted to residence when whatever original purpose they served was exhausted or forgotten, in the spaces between systems, in the maintenance ways that have been widened over generations until they are corridors, then streets, then neighborhoods with their own names and customs.

And yet: scale 2.5. Open, extending past the immediate. The bowl is large. Thirty kilometers across at its widest. The ring-structures step down from the mountain walls, each ring lower, each ring denser with accumulated construction, with the sedimentary record of Threne's long existence. Erosion 2.0: established, settled-form, weathered, in-balance. The city has been here long enough that its own weathering has become part of its function. The bio-ceramic weathers predictably, feeds predictably, hosts its verdant parasites predictably. The maintenance of unpredictability is itself a system. The city has learned to fail in manageable ways, to erode along engineered vectors, to accumulate its own debris as new substrate.

This should be stable. This should be the steady-state of a fully-constructed world, a total environment that has reached its equilibrium.

But dynamic: 2.9. Turbulent. Chaotic change. Instability. Anthill-energy.

Threne *moves*. Not the buildings—the buildings are fixed, rooted in their bio-ceramic fusion with the mountain bowl—but the internal arrangements, the functions, the populations, the purposes. The industrial systems require constant reconfiguration. The condensers demand unceasing attention, their scale so vast that teams work in rotating shifts across twenty-hour cycles, never stopping, the machines never silent though sound overall is 0.7, mostly quiet, because the moisture swallows sound, because the packed density diffuses it, because the industrial roar is low-frequency, felt in the sternum more than heard. The manufacturing—no one is certain anymore what Thrne produces, what it consumes, whether the material flows have purpose or have become self-sustaining ritual, the industrial metabolism of a city that forgot its original contract with the world beyond the mountains.

The population moves. Whole neighborhoods are abandoned when the moisture management fails, when a fungal bloom becomes toxic, when the bio-ceramic begins to fail in *unpredictable* ways and must be quarantined, harvested, reseeded. Other neighborhoods are reclaimed, repopulated, their industrial archaeology repurposed. There are people in Threne who have never slept in the same volume for more than a month. There are others who have never left a single room, their needs met by the pneumatic systems, their waste extracted, their contact limited to the voices through ducts, to the flickering messages on salvaged screens.

The turbulence is not violent, not usually. It is the turbulence of *process*, of too many systems operating at too many scales with too many feedback loops. The condensers create microclimates that redirect moisture that alters growth patterns that block access channels that require new construction that changes thermal properties that affect condenser efficiency. The fungal bioluminescence networks develop synchronizations, waves of brightness and dimness that sweep across neighborhoods and disrupt sleep schedules, work schedules, the delicate coordination of maintenance. The pneumatic tubes—there are thousands of kilometers, there is no complete map—develop pressure instabilities that manifest as sudden deliveries, as lost objects, as the occasional explosive decompression that punches through a wall and creates, temporarily, an unexpected space in the packed density, a gap that fills within hours with opportunistic growth, with opportunistic people, with the immediate adaptation that characterizes Threne's particular ecology.

Fauna: 0.9. Rare. The original design, or the conditions, did not support animals well. There are insects—there are always insects—optimized for the dim and the moist, pale and soft-bodied, some luminous, some predatory, none large. There are the cultivated fungal forms that might be argued as fauna, the motile stages, the seeking rhizomorphs. There are rumors, persistent, of rats or rat-equivalents in the deepest industrial volumes, but if they exist they are as adapted to Threne as Threne is to itself, unseen, perhaps inseparable from the systems they allegedly infest. And there are people. 400,000 of them, the only fauna that truly *thrives*, if thriving is the word, if the dense-packed, turbulent, dim-lit existence of Threne allows for concepts like thriving.

Weirdness: 0.1. Normal. This is perhaps the most terrible parameter, the one that prevents Threne from becoming fable, from becoming the subject of wonder rather than exhausted description. It is *normal*. The people of Threne do not find their city strange. The weeping walls, the perpetual twilight, the industrial throb felt in the bones, the constant movement and resettlement, the rare and lonely sounds of their own voices in the moisture-deadened spaces—these are the given. Perhaps there are records, archives somewhere in the pneumatic depths, of other worlds, of open sky, of dry ground and direct sun and the booming absence of human presence. No one consults them. The work of Threne is continuous, the adaptation is continuous, the dense-packed turbulence generates its own urgency, its own narrative of purpose that requires no external reference.

You might walk through Threne—if you could find it, if you could enter through the mountain passes that are themselves engineered and maintained and subject to the same weathering and the same restless maintenance—and you might think: this is strange, this is wrong, this is a wound in the world, a mistake of construction and persistence.

But Threne does not agree. Threne continues. The condensers condense. The walls weep. The vegetation grows in its dim lushness through the bio-ceramic substrate. The people move and settle and move again in patterns that analysis might find fractal, might find chaotic, might find meaningful or meaningless. The industrial systems operate at scales that no individual comprehends, that perhaps no collective comprehends, that maintain themselves through the same kind of organic persistence as the fungal growths, the same kind of weathered equilibrium as the eroding structures.

It is cool here. It is dim. It is wet with a wetness that has been captured, channeled, made to serve. It is packed with presence, with construction, with the layered history of a world that has never been natural and has long since ceased to distinguish itself from what it has made. It is turbulent with change that never quite becomes transformation, that erodes and builds and erodes again in patterns old enough to seem eternal.

The sound: mostly quiet. The moisture absorbs it. The density diffuses it. The industrial systems operate at frequencies felt rather than heard. A conversation twenty meters away is private. A footstep is soft. The fungal lamps pulse in visual rhythms that have no auditory counterpart. In the rare spaces where sound concentrates—where architecture creates an accidental resonance, where the pneumatic tubes run in parallel and their vibrations synchronize—there is noise enough to drive people out, to create the ghost-zones in the packed city, the volumes no one will inhabit, the erosion-zones that become part of the turbulence, that re-enter the cycle differently, eventually, unpredictably, normally.

Threne has no center. Threne has no edge that is not also a beginning. It extends past the immediate in every direction that matters—up into the mountain walls where engineering projects half-completed centuries ago wait in their weathered state, down into the central depression where the condensers concentrate and the population thins and the industrial purpose seems most intense and least comprehensible, out along the transit arcs that punch through the bio-ceramic mass and emerge, sometimes, into volumes that have no current function, that wait in their packed emptiness for the turbulence to assign them meaning.

It has been like this for longer than records remember. It will be like this after the current configurations have eroded into new configurations, after the current populations have moved or adapted or been adapted by the systems they tend. The wet will weep. The dim will persist. The cool will moderate whatever heat the industrial metabolism generates. The rare fauna— the people, the soft insects, the seeking fungi—will continue in their rareness, their particular density, their particular quiet.

This is Threne. This is what the parameters produce when they are followed past their apparent contradictions into the place that holds them all: the high wet, the lush constructed, the dim turbulent, the totally built and densely packed and eerily, normally, persistently *there*.

*For the visitor who finds their way in: there is no map that remains accurate. There are guides who specialize in the current configuration, who trade in temporary knowledge, who know which transit arcs are open this week, which neighborhoods have been abandoned to moisture or toxic bloom or the rare violent synchronization of industrial systems. They age quickly, these guides, or they become fixed in particular territories, their knowledge growing more precise and less useful as the turbulence moves on. The best advice, perhaps, is to find a rhythm in the condenser cycle, to learn the fungal pulse of a particular volume, to become, as the city itself has become, weathered and established and in-balance with conditions that were never meant to sustain such persistent, such normal, such *packed* and *turbulent* life.*
