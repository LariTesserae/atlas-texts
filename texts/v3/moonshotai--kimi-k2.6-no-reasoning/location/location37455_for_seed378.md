The Sintering Flats

---

From a distance, you might mistake it for a city. The regular geometry rises from the basin's floor—block-forms, shaft-forms, the suggestion of avenues and intersections. But as you descend the escarpment, the error corrects itself. These are not buildings. They are excretions, accumulations, the slow solidified vomit of something that has been eating and digesting the earth for ten thousand years.

The Flats occupy a high limestone basin, tilted slightly southward, where mineral springs once percolated through fissured bedrock. That was before. Now the water still moves—constantly, prodigiously—but it moves *through* the structures rather than around them, percolating through hollow channels, condensing on overhangs, sheeting down faces that were never designed but have grown as regular as if designed. The air is saturated. Stand still for ten minutes and your clothing will cling to your skin with gathered moisture, your hair will plaster to your skull, and your breath will begin to feel like drinking. The heat, rising from deep thermal sources and trapped by the basin's walls, keeps this moisture aloft, cycling, never quite raining but always *almost* raining. The light, when it strikes through the hovering haze, breaks into spectral components, so that certain angles of the day—mid-morning, late afternoon—fill the Flats with shifting color: rose, iodine-yellow, the green of corroded copper.

There is nothing alive here that you would recognize as alive. No plant has taken root in recorded memory; the chemistry is wrong, too alkaline, too metalliferous, the substrate too mobile and too hot. Yet the Flats teem. The abundance is unmistakable, once you learn to read it.

---

The Builders—they have no other name; no one has conversed with them—are somewhere between organism and mineral process. They resemble, when visible, enormous flatworms of poured concrete, gray-pink, with neither eyes nor any obvious sensory organs. Their bodies are sessions wide, capable of extending hundreds of meters in sinusoidal waves, and they move through their own constructions with the sluggishness of something for whom a century is a season. They eat stone, or rather they *process* it: dissolving, filtering, re-precipitating. What they eliminate forms the regular geometries of the Flats. They seem to work in cohorts, synchronized across decades, so that a given district will rise in sudden spurt of activity—new towers extruding, channels rerouting, the whole chemistry of the local water shifting—and then enter quiescence for generations. The oldest structures are deep in the basin's center, weathered by their own creators' subsequent effluvia into something almost smooth, almost classical, like civic architecture from a civilization that never had citizens.

The weathering is particular. Rainfall, in the conventional sense, is rare. Instead there is *condensation*—heavy, constant, running in sheets down every vertical surface, pooling in every depression, evaporating again before it can truly accumulate. Yet over centuries this has been enough to hollow, to channel, to sculpt. The Builders seem to incorporate this erosion into their subsequent work, so that the Flats represent not a single plan but ten thousand years of dialogue between secretion and dissolution, between the organism's intent (if it is intent) and the chemistry's indifferent refutation.

---

What moves and makes sound in the Flats is mostly small. The Builders themselves are silent; their passage through their own tunnels produces no vibration audible to human ears, or perhaps they move only when the surrounding structures have grown thick enough to muffle entirely. The dominant sound is water—dripping, trickling, the hollow *tonk* of a heavy drop falling down a vertical shaft of unknown depth—but water arranged into such complex polyphony that standing in certain intersections, you can hear what seems like music, chord progressions formed by the chance alignment of a dozen seepages at different rates, different resonant cavities.

The fauna—if we must call them that—have adapted to the heat, the wet, the mineral substrate, the absolute absence of anything photosynthetic. There are the Glass-Skimmers, arthropod-analogues perhaps three centimeters long, whose carapaces are transparent silica and who move across the wet surfaces with the surface tension of the water itself as their only locomotion. They feed on the thin films of oxidized metal that precipitate where iron-rich seepage meets the air. There are the Fumarole Nets, colonial organisms that span the mouths of hot vents with meshworks as fine as spider-silk, woven from their own nitrogenous waste, catching the mineral particles that the rising steam carries and digesting them in chambers that maintain internal temperatures precisely at the boiling point of water at this elevation. There are the deeper things, rarely seen—pale, elongated, moving in the warm pools that gather in the Builders' abandoned foundations, eyeless, jawless, their metabolisms so slow that a single digestive event might occupy a decade.

And there are the Singers.

---

They are not precisely fauna. They are not precisely anything. The best description might be acoustic phenomena with biological correlates, or biological phenomena that have learned to use the Flats' resonant architecture as external organs. A Singer begins as a colony of Glass-Skimmers, massing in some cavity with the right harmonics, their silica carapaces positioned at precise angles to one another. Their collective movement produces tones—first random, then, through processes no one has observed closely enough to document, increasingly organized. Over years, the colony shapes the cavity, polishing surfaces with secreted acids, adjusting dimensions by fractions of a millimeter. Eventually, if the conditions are correct, the Singing begins.

It is nearly inaudible. This is the strangeness of it. The Flats are loud with water-noise, the constant percussive and fluid accompaniment of so much moving moisture. Against this, the Singers produce frequencies so low or so high that they register primarily as *pressure*, as a sense of wrongness in the chest cavity, in the inner ear. Those who have heard the middle ranges describe it as the sound of a vast space being slowly emptied, or filled, a suction without wind. The effect on the local fauna is pronounced: Glass-Skimmers will migrate toward an active Singer from kilometers away, adding themselves to the colony, adjusting the acoustics, extending the performance. Builders have been observed to alter course, slowly, across decades, toward Singing cavities, their subsequent excretions modifying the architecture to amplify or dampen particular frequencies.

Whether this is communication, art, parasitism, or something without category, no one can say. The Singers have been Singing, as far as can be determined, since before human beings reached this basin.

---

Human presence is recent, sparse, tolerated or unnoticed. There is a settlement, if it can be called that—the Stilt-Village of **Keth-Undertower**, perched where the basin's rim begins to slope downward, where the thermal activity is less intense and the condensation, while still constant, does not threaten to drown sleepers in their hammocks. Perhaps four hundred people live there, in structures built from imported timber and the peculiar lightweight ceramic that precipitates from certain of the Flats' alkaline streams. They are miners, primarily, but miners of a strange ore: the concentrated mineral salts that the Builders' processing concentrates in particular chambers, accessible during the decades of quiescence between building-cycles. There are chemists who study the Singers, or try to. There are the Desiccated, religious ascetics who believe that the Flats represent the physical form of a divine exhalation, and who walk slowly into the basin's center, refusing water, until they collapse and are consumed by the slow metabolisms of the foundation-pools.

The village has a factory, of a sort. Not for metal-working—the heat and humidity make that impractical—but for the processing of biological compounds extracted from the deeper fauna. Medicines mostly, and some pigments, and a hallucinogen derived from Fumarole Net tissue that allows users to perceive the Singers' full acoustic range, an experience that drives some to remain in the Flats permanently, walking the channels, positioning themselves at nodes of maximum resonance, living on condensed moisture and what they can scrape from mineral surfaces until their bodies fail.

The factory runs on geothermal power, pipes sunk into vents the Builders abandoned millennia ago. It operates with the quiet efficiency of something that has learned to move with rather than against the landscape's indifference. There is no smoke. The waste heat is routed through ceramic channels to warm the village's communal pools; the waste chemicals are piped back into the Flats at points where the local chemistry can absorb them. The industrial process has become, over two centuries of operation, almost organic itself, a symbiont with the larger system.

---

The light is brightest in early afternoon, when the sun passes through the narrowest arc of open sky above the basin's western rim. Then the haze becomes luminous, incandescent, each water droplet a prism, and the Flats' regular geometries cast shadows that are themselves colored, that move as the droplets shift in the thermal convection, that seem almost solid enough to touch. The Builders, when visible during these hours, glow faintly from within, some phosphorescent byproduct of their mineral digestion. The Glass-Skimmers scatter light in starfield patterns across wet surfaces. For perhaps an hour, the Flats achieve a beauty so absolute that it has become dangerous—travelers have been known to walk directly into channels, to miss the warning warmth of a vent edge, to stand motionless until the evening cooling threatens hypothermia in their wet clothing.

Then the light withdraws, the colors cool toward blue and violet, and the Flats enter their night phase, which is not dark—the thermal phosphorescence, the occasional spark from chemical reaction, the village's minimal lighting—but dim enough that distances become uncertain, that the regular geometries seem to extend further than they do, that the sound of water becomes the sound of something approaching, or retreating, or breathing.

---

The rules here are simple, slowly learned. Do not interfere with a building-cycle; the Builders' chemistry is sensitive, and a human insertion into their process can poison a district for centuries. Do not attempt to capture or contain a Singer; the acoustic feedback has killed at least two expeditions, their bodies found without mark but with ruptured inner ears, with blood dried in trails from eyes and nostrils. Do not drink the standing water; it will carry you full of minerals you cannot excrete, will lithify you from within. Move slowly. The heat demands it, the uncertain footing demands it, the sense of being in something's digestive system demands it. The Flats are not hostile. They are simply *not for you*, and their tolerance of human presence is contingent on human smallness, human patience, human willingness to become temporarily mineral, temporarily fungal, temporarily acoustic—to participate in the Flats' processes without presuming to direct them.

There are older presences than the Builders, or so the Desiccated teach. Something that began the seepage, the thermal upwelling, the mineral richness that made the Builders possible. Something that *is* the basin, the erosion, the cycling of water, the slow accumulation and dissolution. They do not name it. Naming would be a presumption. They walk toward it instead, drying out, becoming salt, becoming eventually indistinguishable from the precipitate that the Fumarole Nets harvest from the steam.

The Flats continue. A new tower has begun to rise in the northeastern quadrant, visible from Keth-Undertower as a pale pencil against the luminous haze. It will be two centuries before it matches its neighbors. The Glass-Skimmers have begun to gather at its base, sensing the resonant cavity that will form. Somewhere, a Singer adjusts its frequencies to anticipate the new architecture. Everything moves, everything processes, everything slowly transforms and is transformed. The silence, when it comes, is not the absence of sound but the presence of so many sounds at thresholds beyond hearing, a pressure against the eardrum like the pressure of depth, like the weight of water above a diver, like the accumulated patience of ten thousand years of mineral digestion, waiting for whatever comes next, building whatever comes next, singing whatever comes next into being.
