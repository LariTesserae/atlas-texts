The city-in-the-swamp exists at a frequency that makes visitors check their own pulse. It is called Keth Vo, though the residents call it only *here*, or *the breathing*, or sometimes *the argument*, depending on which quarter you ask and what season the cypress is in.

The place is small—you could walk its circumference in a morning if the bridges cooperated, if the bridges didn't occasionally decide to be elsewhere. Cathedral-scale, perhaps, though cathedrals dream of such density. The living structures press against each other at shoulders, at hips, at the elaborate joints where one generation's growth has been trained through another's. There are perhaps four hundred distinct chambers, platforms, hollows, and arboreal streets, occupying a volume that would barely contain a modest human town. But humans do not build like this. The Vo do not build at all, exactly. They negotiate.

The temperature rides the cool edge of perpetual autumn, the kind of chill that makes you catalogue your own warmth as a resource. The air itself seems to have passed through miles of limestone and yesterday's rain; it carries the mineral tang of water that has forgotten the sun. Yet the light is normal, diffuse, adequate—filtering through the canopy in the particular way of clouded afternoons, neither gloom nor glare. The leaves here are small, numerous, dark-waxy, designed for efficiency rather than display. They carpet the upper architecture in scales of bronze and deep green, and they tremble, always, with the passage of something.

Because the fauna is overwhelming. This is the first truth and the last. The plants may be substrate and architecture both, but they pale beside the animal presence that saturates every cubic span. Insects in strata—midges at the foggy bottom where the cypress knees rise like prayer, beetles in the rotting exclusion zones between walls, moths the size of hands patrolling the gas-lit hollows after dusk. Frogs in the gutters, frogs in the joints, frogs whose bodies have flattened to the thickness of bark to slip between the living beams. Birds in migratory waves that the locals have learned to read as weather, as prophecy, as simply *conversation*—the sharp-billed ones that drill for larvae in the season's wood, the heavy ones that land with disastrous weight on platforms rated for lighter traffic, the tiny iridescent hundreds that move as one visible current through the understory.

And mammals. The Vo themselves, of course, though they resist the category with the particular stubbornness of primates who have never had the word applied to them by outsiders. But also the others: the bark-readers with their prehensile tails and too-many fingers, the swamp deer that have learned to stand on hind legs to reach the sweet inner layers, the things in the water that surface only to exhale and regard. Everything feeds, everything is fed upon. The food web here is not a web but a briar, hopelessly tangled, and the Vo have learned to read its vibrations as others read the stock market or the morning news.

The erosion of the place is established. This is the second truth. Keth Vo has been *settled* in its form, not by conservation but by dynamic equilibrium. The structures rot at the rate they grow. The great platform that serves as central gathering-space has been falling in its current configuration for perhaps eighty years; it will complete its collapse in approximately forty more, and the Vo have already begun the patient negotiation with the surrounding growth to replace it, not as emergency but as continuity. There is no ruin here, only becoming-ruin, only pre-ruin, only the long moment of balanced decay that the residents inhabit like a home.

The water is wet in that particular way of landscapes where water has forgotten to stay put. It rises from below, seeps from every surface, condenses on the cooler bark-faces and runs in channels that were pith once and have been trained into plumbing. It weeps from the moss that serves as insulation, as textile, as minor deity. The Vo collect it, direct it, drink it with the reverence of those who know the entire supply is presently passing through the body of something else. It is never clean in the sterile sense. It is alive in the way that makes sterilization an absurdity, a violence, a joke in poor taste.

The technology is primitive, or perhaps the Vo simply have different priorities. They work with hand-tools of bone and stone and the occasional meteoric iron, with wedges and levers and the careful application of fire in controlled burns that take a decade to plan. Their material science is one of patience: knowing when a particular length of vine will be ready for weaving, which hollow to tap for the resins that serve as adhesive and antibiotic and mild hallucinogen. They have no wheels, or rather, the wheels they have are grown, slow spirals of root-stock that complete a rotation across a season, useful only for the grinding of certain seeds at certain times. Their infrastructure is the negotiation itself, the accumulated knowledge of what will hold, what will yield, what can be asked of the living city and what must be allowed to pass.

And the sound. The sound is loud.

It is not loud like machinery, though there is that—the rhythmic hollow knock of the water-engines, the beetle-shell click of the message-devices, the percussion of communal work in distant chambers. It is loud like a conversation that has been going on for millennia and has no intention of stopping. The frogs alone would be deafening. The frogs in their reproductive billions, each species tuned to a different band, a different hour, a different weather. The insects provide the harmonic bed, the endless granular static of living. The birds punctuate, argue, translate. The mammals add the registers of distress and comfort and territorial assertion. And beneath it all, the perpetual wet sound of water in motion, in transition, in stubborn refusal to be still.

The turbulence of the place manifests in its constant renegotiation. Not storm, exactly, though storms come. Rather the hyperactive instability of a system that has too many participants, too many desires, too many hungry mouths and growing edges and chemical signals passing in incomprehensible profusion. A platform that was sound yesterday will be soft this morning, invaded by a root that found purchase, home now to a colony that rebuilds it in their image. A route that served as main thoroughfare will be blocked by a single fallen branch, which rots, which feeds, which becomes the foundation of something else. The Vo navigate this with the particular expertise of those who have never expected stasis. They carry maps in their heads that update themselves, that forget as insistently as they remember, that privilege the present truth over yesterday's certainty.

The weirdness is low. I want to emphasize this, because the temptation is to make such a place strange, other, *weird* in the cosmic sense. But Keth Vo is normal. It is simply itself, simply *more* of what is, simply the ordinary processes of life and death and growth and decay carried to their dense conclusion. If it seems strange to visitors, that is the strangeness of any ecology fully inhabited, any economy fully realized, any culture that has found its equilibrium and sees no need to explain itself.

The Vo themselves are small, dark, quieter than their environment would suggest. They have learned that survival here requires a certain economy of presence, that to call attention is to become part of the soundscape in ways that may not be advantageous. They move through their city with the particular grace of those who have memorized every yielding surface, every living handhold, every place where the structure is currently negotiating with itself and should not be disturbed. They speak in frequencies below the frog-threshold, or above the insect-ceiling, or simply do not speak at all, having developed kin-navigated alternatives.

They will, on occasion, build. This is the closest they come to what outsiders recognize as technology, as progress. A new platform, a new bridge, a new chamber for a new purpose, a new family, a new need. The building takes years. The negotiation with living materials takes longer. The result is always compromise, always partial, always already beginning its long negotiation with decay. And the Vo find this sufficient, find this beautiful, find this to be the nature of making in a world where everything is already being unmade and remade in every moment.

I have been trying to describe the sound of evening, when the day-shift insects give way to the night-shift and the frogs conduct their exchanges with increased urgency, and the water-engines slow their work as the temperature drops and the condensation eases, and the Vo themselves become more visible, more audible, taking their portion of the temporal spectrum now that it is available. The sound is not music, though it has structure. It is not noise, though it resists meaning. It is the sound of a city that is entirely alive, entirely present, entirely engaged in the ancient argument that is existence in a wet place, a green place, a place where everything is becoming everything else in the slow turbulence of ecological time.

The light is leaving now, in the particular way this place knows twilight, and the moss-bioluminescence that the Vo have cultivated rather than invented is beginning its slow brightening, and the first of the night-birds has called something that translates roughly as *here* or *now* or *the same thing, continuing*. The argument continues. The water continues. The life, dense and loud and cool and turbulent and established and small and wet and green and overwhelming and normal, continues.

This is Keth Vo. This is the breathing. This is where you are.
