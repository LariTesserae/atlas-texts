The space is called the Lung, though no one breathes here anymore.

It is a cavity roughly the size of a cathedral, though "cathedral" implies a kind of architectural intention that the Lung only partially earns. The chamber exists deep beneath the calcified seabed of what was once a continental shelf, now lifted and folded by tectonic patience into something unnameable. The rock around it is porous with ancient coral skeletons, compressed to the density of bone china, veined with the rust of iron-eating bacteria that lived and died in geological instants three hundred million years apart.

The water here is not water as you understand it. At this pressure, at this temperature—warm, yes, persistently, unnaturally warm—it has entered a state of perpetual near-boiling without ever quite committing to the phase change. It moves in convection cells the size of city blocks, if cities could be built in such cells, if blocks could exist in this geometry. The water carries dissolved minerals in concentrations that would kill surface fish instantly: silica, heavy metals, the leached calcium of a billion billion microscopic shells. It tastes of electricity and old blood.

And the sound. The sound is the first thing that would destroy you, before the pressure, before the heat, before the disorientation. The Lung is deafening in a way that has nothing to do with volume precisely, though the volume is sufficient to liquefy organic tissue at certain frequencies. The sound is the product of the city that was built here, is being built here, will be built here—tenses collapse in the Lung as surely as they do in grammar and in dreams.

The city. The city is called Asphodel by the ones who named it, though that word implies a kind of poetic tradition that the builders would not recognize. They are not present now, the builders. They are rarely present. They are a species of temporal absence, of scheduled nonexistence, and their city reflects this.

Asphodel is industrial in the truest sense: it produces. What it produces is not important, or rather, it is important but the importance is distributed across so many axes of meaning that no single product can be identified. The city produces *process*. It produces the conditions of its own continuation. It produces heat as waste, and the heat is absorbed by the anomalous water, and the water convects, and the convection drives turbines, and the turbines power the production.

The architecture is layered in a way that only makes sense when you understand that it was built from the inside out and the outside in simultaneously. The outer shell—contacting the porous fossil-rock—is relatively stable, established, weathered into a kind of equilibrium with the surrounding geology. It has existed long enough to have been eroded, not by water but by time itself, by the slow chemical conversation between alien metal and ancient calcium carbonate. This is the *settled* Asphodel, the Asphodel that has learned to be still.

But inward, toward the center of the Lung—the city grows turbulent, chaotic, hyperactive as an anthill that has achieved consciousness and immediately developed anxiety. Structures are built and dismantled in hours. Conduits branch and rebranch like lightning frozen in the act of striking. The builders—or their machines, or their machine-successors, or something that dreams it is a machine dreaming of a builder—construct and deconstruct in a frenzy of productive activity that serves no visible purpose and produces no visible product except more activity, more heat, more sound.

The sound is the city breathing. The sound is the city dreaming. The sound is the city *being* a city, the way a lung is the fact of breathing made flesh.

And in the spaces between the building and the unbuilding—in the empty volume, the near-void that the parameters demand and the reality provides—there is almost nothing. The density is low, spatially. Vast chambers exist that have never been entered, that may have never been intended to be entered, that serve as resonators for the frequencies that the productive machinery generates. Sound bounces and amplifies. Sound becomes architecture. The empty spaces are shaped by the harmonics of industry, and in turn shape those harmonics into patterns that the original builders—if they were builders, if they were ever—could not have predicted.

The light is dim. Of course it is dim. What light there is comes from the process itself: the glow of superheated water, the bioluminescence-equivalent of certain catalytic reactions, the occasional spark of electrical discharge between poorly grounded surfaces. The color is predominantly the deep red of hot iron, the orange of sodium vapor, the brief white of arc welding that happens constantly at the turbulent center and never quite stabilizes into illumination. Shadows do not exist in the Lung so much as they are the default condition, interrupted by these uncertain glows.

There is no vegetation. Nothing that could be called plant, or fungus, or even the chemosynthetic bacterial mats that cluster around deep-sea vents in the world above. The mineral world here is too mineral, too processed, too *urban* to allow for the slow accidents of biology. Life, if it exists, exists in the cracks between production cycles, in the temporary eddies of the convective flow, in the brief moments when a machine pauses and something else—something not quite describable—uses that pause to *be*.

The fauna, what there is of it, is rare and strange and probably not fauna in any taxonomic sense. Things move in the empty spaces. They are difficult to describe because they seem to be made of the same materials as the city, or of the sound, or of the heat gradients in the anomalous water. They have the quality of being *noticed* rather than *seen*, of registering on peripheral perception and vanishing when directly attended to. They might be maintenance subroutines given physical form by the pressure and the noise. They might be the dreams of the absent builders, compressed into something approaching solidity. They might be the city learning to be curious about itself.

The weirdness of the place—and it is unusual, it is persistently, claustrophobically unusual—resides in its coherence. Everything in the Lung is connected to everything else by the water and the sound and the heat and the endless productive frenzy. There are no isolated events. A structural failure in the outer, settled shell propagates inward as a change in the convection pattern, which alters the turbine speed, which changes the production rate, which generates more heat, which increases the turbulence, which eventually—hours later, days later—produces another structural failure in a different part of the shell. The city is an organism in this sense, a metabolic system, a lung that breathes industry and exhales instability.

And you, if you were there—you would not be there, you could not survive it, but imagine—you would experience it as intimacy. The scale is pocket, contained, a single feature embedded in the greater dark. Despite the vastness of the surrounding rock, despite the pressure of the world above, the Lung is a room. It is a room with strange acoustics and stranger furniture, but it is a room. You could, in theory, walk from one end to the other in a day, if you could walk, if you were not immediately destroyed by the sound, the heat, the pressure, the sheer alien intensity of the place.

The Lung has been here for a long time. It will be here longer. The builders, whatever they were, are gone or going or yet to arrive. The city builds itself and unbuilds itself in the same gesture, the way a breath is both inhalation and the preparation for exhalation. The water moves. The sound continues. The light flickers in its dim, persistent way.

And in the empty spaces, the vast spaces between the productive frenzy and the settled shell, there is room to imagine that something is listening. That something has always been listening. That the Lung is not merely a place but a kind of ear, pressed against the chest of the world, waiting to hear a heartbeat that has not yet begun.
