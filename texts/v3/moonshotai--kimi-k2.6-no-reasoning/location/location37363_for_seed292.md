The Sunken Stair—so named for what the first surveyors thought they saw on sonar, a ascending structure, though later mapping proved it was only the lip of a collapsed basin, a trick of angle and hope—is not, strictly speaking, a place that *has* water. It *is* water, water that forgot to end, water that accumulated convictions about itself and pressed upward against whatever would hold it.

At the boundary, where the Stair meets the compressed atmosphere of the lower altitudes, there is no shore. Instead the water thickens gradually, gaining viscosity, gaining *intention*, until breathing creatures must swim or else accept the gill-implants that the old industrial stations still manufacture in batches of ten thousand, their machinery grinding away in drowned foundries where no human supervisor has walked for two centuries. The transition zone extends for six vertical kilometers. Rain falls upward through it sometimes, pulled by thermal inversions, and fish descend through clouds, confused, their swim bladders bursting in slow agony if they rise too fast.

The elevation is extreme not because the Stair rises but because it *deepens*. The basin—ancient, eroded to its essential gesture—is a geological afterthought, the last punctuation mark of a continent that subducted itself into exhaustion. What was once mountain range, once trench, once seabed, has been planed and polished by epochs of pressured water until only this broad funnel remains, descending to depths where the crust itself grows strange and ductile. The old maps show contour lines so closely spaced they seem solid, a black cone of ink. At the center, the theoretical bottom, instruments fail. Not from pressure alone—though that, certainly—but from *age*, as though the water there remembers the planet's formation and refuses to measure its own depth against such infancy.

Yet it is lush. Lush beyond parody, beyond the reasonable capacity of any ecosystem.

The vegetation is not swimming toward light, as one might expect of submerged flora, but rather *descending* toward something else entirely. Great forests of kelp-equivalent, their genetics scrambled by the industrial effluent still leaking from the stations, grow downward from the upper interface, their holdfasts gripping only each other in mat-communities thirty meters thick, their fronds extending kilometers into the blue-dark below. They do not photosynthesize, not primarily. They chemosynthesize, they radio-synthesize, they feed on thermal gradients and on the soluble minerals released by the ancient rock's final surrender. Bioluminescence is wasteful here, profligate, a language spoken in broad spectrum by organisms that never knew the sun. The forests pulse with it, synchronized across kilometers by chemical signals too slow for human perception, so that from a distance they seem to breathe, great lungs of cold light inhaling and exhaling across the Stair's descending walls.

And where the kelp-forests thin, where the pressure begins to demand new compromises, other growths take over. Tube-worms the diameter of subway tunnels, their red plumes like hemorrhaging flowers, cluster around vents that spout not heat but *memory*—superheated brine carrying dissolved minerals in ratios that match the planet's first oceans, unchanged for four billion years. Corals built from industrial waste, from slag and processed aluminum, their skeletons harder than any natural formation, grow in mad architectures that the old surveyors termed "habitat-unsafe" and left unmapped. Fungal mats spread through every available substrate, including living tissue, including the sealed housing of abandoned machinery. They are not parasites, exactly. They are *collaborators*, offering chemical processing in exchange for structural support, and many of the larger fauna have incorporated them so thoroughly that the distinction between animal and vegetable has become administrative, maintained only by the dying taxonomy stations.

The fauna is abundant, but abundance here does not mean variety in the familiar sense. Rather, there are a few forms that have found purchase and have filled every available niche through size variation alone. The filter-feeders range from microscopic to leviathan, the same basic mouth-plan scaled across twelve orders of magnitude. The predators, too, are iterations of a single ancestral design, something like a segmented worm and something like a crustacean, armored against pressure and equipped with jaws that can crush industrial ceramics. They do not hunt by sight—light is too precious, too unpredictable—but by vibration, by the seismic signature of swimming, by the chemical trails that every movement leaves in water this dense with dissolved minerals.

And they are loud. The sound of the Stair is not the muted hush of deep water. It is thunderous, cacophonous, a constant turbulence of biological and geological process. The filter-feeders pump water through their bodies in rhythmic jets, thousands of them, millions, the percussion of a million drums played without pattern. The predators scrape their armored segments across rock, across coral, across each other, in displays of aggression and mating that carry for dozens of kilometers. The vents themselves speak, not the gentle hiss of surface hot springs but explosive bursts that send shockwaves rippling through the water, triggering landslides of ancient sediment, setting the kelp-forests swaying in waves that take hours to complete their arc. And beneath it all, infrasonic, felt in the bones of any human swimmer rather than heard, is the groan of the rock itself, still settling, still eroding, still seeking its final flatness in geological time.

The human presence is scattered, built things reduced to their most necessary, their most stubborn. Single structures, mostly, or the partial remains thereof. A habitat dome crushed by pressure but still maintaining structural integrity in its collapse, now inhabited by a colony of the armored predators who have grown fond of its curved interior surfaces. The foundries, yes, still operating on automatic, their products sinking unused to accumulate in drifts against the basin walls, gill-implants and pressure-suits and filtration membranes for a population that never arrived. A single intact building, the original survey station, maintained by a family who refuse to acknowledge the evacuation order of 2147 and who have grown, through generations of pressure-adaptation, into shapes that would not survive return to the surface.

Their technology is industrial but *stranded*, scaled for production without consumption, electrified by thermal taps that the geological turbulence maintains despite the lack of maintenance. The stations produce. The stations have produced for two hundred years. The product accumulates, forms reefs, becomes substrate for new growth, is eventually incorporated into the living geology of the place. It is not waste, exactly. It is *contribution*, if unintentional, to a system that processes everything, that erases distinction between organic and manufactured, between living and merely persistent.

The dynamism is exhausting, constant, a storm-state that never clears. The kelp-forests shift their bioluminescent patterns in response to predator movements, to vent eruptions, to gravitational influences from the planet's moons that at these pressures create tidal effects measurable in centimeters but significant in their cumulative stress. New vents open, old ones seal, the tube-worm colonies migrate in geological real-time across the basin floor, leaving behind empty tubes that become habitat, become mineral deposit, become new vent themselves in some future iteration. Fauna populations bloom and crash in cycles too rapid for classical ecology, predator and prey locked in oscillations that the mathematics of the old stations classify as "unstable" and would regulate if they could, if any hand remained to adjust their parameters.

The light is normal, in its way. Not bright, never bright, but *expected*, the bioluminescence falling into ranges that human-adapted eyes process as natural, the vent-glow reading as firelight, the filtered remnants of surface illumination—on the rare days when storms do not occlude the interface entirely—registering as dawn, as dusk, as the blue hour that lasts for hours in high latitudes. It is normal because the Stair *has* no normal, no baseline against which to measure deviation. Each generation of human inhabitants, each iteration of the fauna, each pulse of the kelp-forests establishes its own standard, forgets it, establishes another.

The weirdness is minimal. Everything here follows rules, physical laws, biological imperatives pressed to their logical extreme. The gill-implants function predictably. The pressure kills at calculable depths. The foundries produce according to their programming. What seems strange is only *scale*, only *duration*, only the patience of water and rock in their long conversation, which human time cannot encompass and therefore misreads as anomaly.

At the theoretical center, where instruments fail and even the armored predators do not venture, there are—sometimes—sounds that the survey stations record but do not analyze. Low frequencies, rhythmic, almost *speech-like* in their patterning though below the threshold of meaningful decoding. The old family in the intact station claims they are the Stair itself, finally eroded to voice, speaking its own history in a language that will outlast every listener. The taxonomy stations classify them as "geological process, unresolved." The foundries do not respond to queries about the sounds, though their machinery pauses sometimes, listening, before resuming its endless production.

The water is 2.8 of 3, which means almost all, which means the land that was here is remembered only in chemistry, in the dissolved minerals that speak of granite, of basalt, of ancient sedimentary beds compressed beyond identity. The vegetation is 2.2 of 3, lush beyond reason, feeding on what the water brings, on what the industrial stations leak, on the chemosynthetic generosity of a planet's interior finally exposed. The temperature is cool, the great mass of water moderating every thermal impulse toward a median that supports life, that supports *this* life, these specific adaptations, these negotiations between pressure and chemistry and time.

And it is old. Eroded to 3.0 of 3, ancient, near-flattened, settled into final form except for the dynamism that prevents any true settlement, the turbulence that keeps even this ancientness in constant renegotiation. The Stair has been almost flat before, will be again, is briefly interrupted in its long leveling by life, by industry, by the brief loud anomaly of human attention before that too becomes substrate, becomes mineral, becomes the next layer in the accumulated record of what water does to time, to intention, to the distinction between making and being made.
