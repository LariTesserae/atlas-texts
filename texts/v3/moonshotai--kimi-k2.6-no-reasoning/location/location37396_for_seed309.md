**The Sump of Babel's Roots**

It's called the Throat, though it swallows nothing. Or perhaps it swallows everything, eventually.

You descend through forty meters of reinforced concrete shaft in a cage elevator that shudders and screams against its guides—a sound like a giant filing its teeth. The shaft is *wrong*, angled not quite vertical, and the elevator lurches against the tilt, metal groaning in a language of complaint you've somehow learned to understand after enough trips down. This is the sound: industrial music, the loudness of machinery that never sleeps, pumps and compressors and the eternal chewing of something you're never shown. The sound wraps around you like a wet blanket, makes conversation into shouting, makes shouting into a kind of intimacy.

Then the doors crank open and you step out into *warmth*—unexpected, biological warmth, the heat of composting, of metabolism at massive scale, of engines venting into spaces that were never meant to hold them.

---

The Throat is small. Cathedral-small, which is to say: small enough to hold in your mind's architecture, small enough to walk across in twenty minutes if any path allowed straight passage, but large enough to lose yourself in for hours. It occupies perhaps three hectares of excavated volume, though "excavated" implies intention and much of this hollow was *grown* into being, then reinforced, then grown again in the gaps where reinforcement failed.

You're below surface. The ceiling—if you can call it that—arches between four and twelve meters overhead, a irregular dome of packed earth and the dense net of *roots* that make up perhaps forty percent of its mass. These aren't tree roots, exactly. The botanists argue about what to call the organism, or organisms, or whether the distinction means anything here. What you see: cables of pale matter, some thick as your thigh, some fine as hair, all of it alive, all of it *moving*, slowly, imperceptibly unless you watch for minutes, unless you return to the same spot after a week and find the corridor subtly narrowed, the passage subtly shifted.

The plants are total here. They are substrate and architecture and ecosystem and something like intention, though the biologists punish themselves with debates about that word. Every surface that isn't actively maintained against them—every surface that isn't metal heated to exclusion temperatures, or sprayed with the expensive defoliants that smell like copper and regret—every other surface hosts life. Roots descend from above. Mycelial mats carpet what passes for floor, in the unmaintained sections. In the maintained sections, the floor is grating, and you can look down through it to the next level down, and sometimes the level below that, to where the Throat narrows to something like a root-clogged pipe and, presumably, continues further than anyone has mapped.

---

Water is intermittent here. Not scarce—never quite scarce—but *capricious*. Condensation beads on every cool surface, drips, collects in pools that the mycelium drinks visibly, the pale mats darkening, pulsing, moving on. Rain falls in the Throat, sometimes: not from weather, but from failures in the cooling systems above, from steam condensing in masses too large for the drainage to swallow, releasing all at once in warm sheets that smell of minerals and the particular green of this place's metabolism. The water that pools and stays pools is always shallow, always temporary, always being consumed by something. You learn to walk with your mouth slightly open, drinking the air, because the air drinks you back—pulls moisture from your skin, from your breath, and gives back warmth and spores and the taste of ongoing digestion.

---

The built environment: this is urban, somehow, though you might not recognize it as such if you came expecting streets and buildings. The Throat was settled—constructed—grown into—perhaps two centuries past, in the aftermath of some surface conflict that the archives describe only as "the Collapse of the Northern Reaches." People needed to be *inside*. People needed to be *away*. And the roots were already here, in some older excavation, some natural cave system or mine worked by hands no one remembers.

So they built *with* the roots, *around* the roots, *between* them in the spaces that the slow growth hadn't yet claimed. Platforms of welded steel, bolted into the living wood-analogue. Walkways of grating. Chambers where the roots were trained—or fought, or negotiated with—into arching supports, into hollow spaces that became rooms, then neighborhoods, then something like a town.

It's layered construction, vertical and horizontal both. Five main levels, though "level" is generous—the floors slope, follow the irregular geometry of the space, step up and down where roots have grown through or been cut away. And within each level, subdivisions: the Heating District, where the great engines vent and the warmth concentrates; the Wet Warrens, where condensation pools and certain industries thrive; the Dry Line, aggressively maintained, where the spore-counters work and the medical facilities try to keep ahead of the infections; the Midden, where the roots are densest and the human presence thins to a few hardy or desperate souls.

Infrastructure density: pipes run everywhere, some carrying steam, some carrying condensed water, some carrying the defoliant sprays, some whose purpose has been forgotten, their contents a mystery that occasionally becomes urgent when one ruptures. Cables, too, for the electrification that makes the industrial scale possible. The power comes from somewhere deeper, or further, a reactor or geothermal tap that no one you've met has seen directly. The cables are armored, rooted around by the patient growth above, requiring constant maintenance, constant patrol.

---

The fauna. This is the parameter that overwhelms, that makes the Throat what it is.

You expected, perhaps, some animals. Rats, roaches, the hardy companions of human settlement. Or perhaps the romantic expectation: bats in the upper reaches, fish in the deeper pools.

The reality is *stranger* and *more total* and *louder* than anything you prepared for.

The roots themselves are faunal, in a sense that blurs categories. They move. They *hunt*, or something like hunting—the fine root-hairs quest toward sources of nitrogen, of phosphorus, of the particular compounds that the greater organism craves, and where they find concentrations, they concentrate in turn. There are stories, urban legends or suppressed reports, of people found wrapped in root-mass, desiccated, fertilizing. The official position is accident, intoxication, misadventure. The unofficial whisper is *predation*.

But the roots are only the beginning, the substrate from which everything else emerges.

The *arthropods* first: beetles the size of your thumb that click and whir in the root-mass, that emerge in swarms when the warm rain falls, that carpet the walls in glittering carapace. They are loud, collectively, a dry continuous rattle like懒惰 rice in a sieve. They eat the roots, are eaten by what comes next.

The *larvae*: pale, segmented, found in the water pools, in the condensation that collects in machinery, in the thin films that coat everything in the Wet Warrens. They *pulse*. They are always pulsing, a movement that draws the eye, that becomes hypnotic, that the long-term residents learn not to watch. Some of the larvae are of the beetles. Some are of other things. Some are of things that don't seem to correspond to any adult form anyone has found.

The *fliers*: moth-analogues, though with too many wings, with wings that catch the dim light—*dim*, always dim, the lighting is industrial, failing, supplemented by phosphorescence that the roots have learned to produce in patches, in streaks, in patterns that might be mapping or might be language or might be the byproduct of some metabolism no one understands. The fliers are prey to *bats* that are not quite bats, that echolocate in frequencies that make your fillings ache, that hunt in shifts so the Throat is never without their ultrasonic screaming.

The *deeper* things.

You hear them more than see them. Something in the root-mass below the lowest mapped level, something that moves with purpose, that the roots avoid, that leaves tunnels behind it. The sound of its movement is part of the infrasonic background, felt in the sternum, in the gut, before it's heard in the ear. Occasionally someone sees *parts* of it—an appendage, scaled or chitinous or neither, retreating into shadow; an eye, perhaps, reflecting back the dim light with too many facets, too much *organization*.

The fauna overwhelms not in diversity but in *presence*, in *pressure*, in the sense that everything here is alive and most of it is *hungry* or *growing* or *moving toward you* with purposes that intersect your own only incidentally, if at all.

---

The strange. The weirdness. This is not a quantity but a *quality of relation*, a way that things connect that shouldn't, or don't connect that should.

The roots respond to sound. Not in the slow way of plants turning to light—immediately, visibly, the fine hairs retracting from loud noise, the thicker structures *tensing*, doing something like attention. Musicians have experimented: certain frequencies cause growth surges, cause root-tips to extend toward the source, cause the phosphorescence to brighten or shift color. The industrial noise is tuned, partially, to discourage this—to keep the roots in their patterns, to prevent the architectural supports from *reaching* toward the living.

The fauna *cooperates* in ways that suggest something like planning. Beetle swarms coincide with root growth surges, the increased root vulnerability exploited, the root defenses—chemical, physical—predicted and avoided. The fliers gather in certain chambers at certain times, and afterward those chambers show increased phosphorescence, increased growth, as if *worship* or *negotiation* had occurred.

And the *human inhabitants*, the settlers, the industrial workers, the desperate and the hiding and the devoted—some of them have *changed*. The long-term exposure, the spores, the larvae in the water, the constant warmth and damp. You see them in the deeper levels, in places where the defoliant sprays don't reach. Skin pale as root-matter, eyes adapted to dimness that spill tears in brighter light. Some of them *feed* differently, you're told, though the details vary by teller: photosynthesis via skin patches, or root-insertion direct into the vascular system, or a diet of the beetles, the larvae, the things that crawl.

They are still human, legally. Still counted in the census, still vote in the surface elections they no longer attend. But they speak of the Throat differently. As *home* in ways that the industrial workers on three-year contracts never understand. As *alive* and *aware* and *welcoming*.

---

The active, the dynamic. This is not a static place, not a ruin fossilized in its moment of abandonment. The roots grow, are cut, grow again. The fauna cycles through its populations, responding to warmth, to moisture, to the accidental introduction of new substrates. The human settlement *changes*, new platforms built, old ones abandoned to reclamation, political disputes about resources and access and the slow creep of the living architecture.

The weather cycles, even underground. The rain from failed cooling, irregular, unpredictable. The steam releases, scheduled or emergency. The warm fronts that move through when the engines below increase output, the cool pockets where root transpiration dominates.

Predator and prey play out continuously, though the categories slide: roots and beetles, beetles and larvae, the deep things and anything that strays too low, humans and the environment that sustains and threatens them. The biologists would call it an ecosystem, though they'd argue about whether it's *one* ecosystem or many overlapping, whether the human industrial component is part of it or parasite upon it or host to it.

---

What does it feel like, to be there?

Walking the grating of the Dry Line, the warmed air full of spore-dust that your mask filters imperfectly, the sound of the nearest engine a constant bass note beneath the rattle of beetles, the screech of distant metal, the ultrasonic cries of hunters you can't see. The light: dim enough that your eyes strain, that colors reduce to suggestion, that movement in peripheral vision becomes constant uncertainty—is that a root-tip growing, a beetle crawling, a human watching, something else?

And always the sense of *pressure*, of *presence*, of being inside something that is aware of you in ways you can't reciprocate. The Throat was named before the full strangeness was understood, a joke about its shape, its descent. Now the name seems prophetic, seems to acknowledge what the settlers have learned: that they are inside something *digesting*, slowly, that they participate in whether they choose to or not. That the warmth is metabolic heat. That the moisture is exhalation. That the roots, the fauna, the strange cooperative emergences are all aspects of something that doesn't distinguish clearly between itself and its contents.

The industrial scale, the electrified machinery, the pumps and compressors that maintain the livable spaces—this is not conquest of the Throat. This is *negotiation*, ongoing, with a partner whose goals are opaque, whose methods are biological and patient and *there* in ways that human industry, for all its power, can never quite match. The engineers speak of equilibrium, of balance, but what they mean is *stalemate*, is the current moment of a war that has no final victory, only continuation or collapse.

And in the Midden, in the Wet Warrens, in the spaces where human maintenance has faltered and the roots have grown through, where the fauna concentrates and the phosphorescence paints everything in shades of green and blue and the occasional startling red—there, they say, you can feel it most clearly. The *aliveness*. The *attention*. The sense of being in a body larger than architecture, older than the settlement, stranger than any individual component suggests.

They say if you stay long enough, you stop wanting to leave. They say the ones who've changed are the lucky ones, the ones who've been *accepted*, who've found a place in the metabolism of the place.

They say many things. The sound makes it hard to hear clearly. The dimness makes it hard to see. The warmth and the moisture and the constant motion, the growth and feeding and predation and cooperation, the strange emergence of meaning from systems that shouldn't be capable of it—

The Throat continues. You could descend, still. The elevator shrieks its willingness. The roots have grown through the shaft in places, but cleared, or been cleared, or *arranged*.

What would you find, if you went down?

What would find *you*?
