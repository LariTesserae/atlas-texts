The Sinking Foundries of Vervain Deep

I. The Descent

There is a place where the ocean does not know it is ocean. Where the water has forgotten the surface, forgotten the sun, forgotten even the concept of sky. Vervain Deep sits in a geological pocket of the continental shelf, a depression so old that the water above it has compressed into something denser, stranger—not quite liquid, not quite solid, a medium that holds you like a memory holds grief: present, pressing, impossible to escape.

You arrive through a vertical shaft punched through bedrock during an era when such engineering seemed possible, when the world above still believed in permanence. The shaft is lined with ceramic tiles, millions of them, each hand-fired in kilns that have long since dissolved into the chemical soup of the seafloor. The tiles depict what you gradually understand to be instructions: how to breathe here, how to move, how to survive in a place that should not permit survival. The images show human figures with gills like fans, with eyes like lanterns, with limbs that end in delicate fractal branches. None of these modifications are available to you. You descend anyway.

II. The Pressure Gardens

The first chamber opens like a lung taking breath. It is small—cathedral-small, meaning you could fit perhaps three Gothic naves inside if you could arrange them, but the proportions feel wrong, compressed, as if the space has been squeezed by the weight of all that water into something denser than geometry allows. The ceiling is lost in darkness above. The floor... the floor is not floor.

Everything here is made. That is the first rule of Vervain Deep, the rule from which all others descend. There is no natural substrate, no bedrock, no sand, no silt that has not been processed, fired, smelted, extruded, or grown in vats. The floor beneath your feet (you are wearing weighted boots, you must be, or you would float) is composed of compressed industrial waste: slag from furnaces, powdered ceramic from failed batches, the fine dust of a million million manufacturing errors, all pressed together under unimaginable pressure until it achieved the density and stability of metamorphic rock. It is warm to the touch. Residual heat, still, from processes that began before your grandparents were born.

The vegetation is sparse here, but present. It grows in strips along the seams where floor meets wall, where wall meets ceiling, wherever two manufactured surfaces create a margin of shadow. *Laminaria manufacta*, the catalog calls it, though no one has cataloged anything here for generations. It is kelp that has been engineered, or perhaps merely trained by centuries of selective pressure, to photosynthesize using the dim thermal radiation of the foundry walls rather than light. Its fronds are the color of dried blood, of old rust, of the particular brown that iron achieves when it has surrendered to oxidation but not yet to collapse. It grows slowly, achingly, reaching toward the warmest surfaces with the patience of geology.

III. The Foundries Proper

The sound hits you before you reach them. A continuous roar, not rhythmic enough to be comforting, not chaotic enough to be noise. It is the sound of water being moved against its will, of steam being born and dying in the same instant, of metal being persuaded to change its nature through the application of heat and pressure and time.

The foundries themselves are the reason Vervain Deep exists, or perhaps Vervain Deep is the reason the foundries exist—it is impossible to determine priority, to find first causes in a place that has been continuous with itself for so long. They are arranged in a rough spiral, descending deeper into the depression, each one slightly smaller than the last, each one slightly older, until the oldest are lost in darkness and silt and the particular silence of abandoned places.

The working foundries are loud. That is the second rule. The sound is not merely heard but felt, transmitted through the water, through the manufactured stone, through the bones of your inner ear until you cannot distinguish between the roar of the furnaces and the roar of your own blood. Workers—there are workers, you will see them soon—wear no ear protection. They have no ears, not in the conventional sense. They have been here too long, have adapted in ways that the tile-instructions only hinted at, tympanic membranes thickened and migrated, auditory nerves rerouted, until they hear through their skin, through the sensitive membranes of their throats, through the delicate architecture of their sinuses.

They are making something. They are always making something. The foundries produce a specific alloy, *Vervain steel*, that can only exist under these conditions of pressure and temperature and chemical peculiarity. It is used—somewhere, the somewhere that still maintains contact with this place, the somewhere that still values materials that cannot be synthesized by easier means—for purposes that require both absolute structural integrity and the capacity to flex without breaking. The exact applications are classified, or forgotten, or perhaps never known by the people who do the actual making.

IV. The Fauna of Manufacture

The animals here are abundant, in the way that life is abundant in any place where energy flows and nutrients cycle and time has had its way. They are not, for the most part, animals that you will find in any surface taxonomy.

There are the filter-crabs, *Portunus vervainensis*, though that name is a convenience, a pinning of Latin on something that has never been formally described. They are not crabs, not really, but they have the general architecture of crabs: a central carapace, jointed appendages, a tendency to scuttle. Their carapaces are ceramic, grown rather than secreted, formed from the same slag-composite as the floors, and they filter from the water not plankton but industrial particulates: metal fines, ceramic dust, the microscopic debris of continuous manufacture. They are the color of their environment, which is to say, they are the color of rust, of old blood, of the particular brown that comes from the mixture of all colors under dim light.

There are the vent-fish, though they are not fish and they do not swim near vents. They are elongated, eel-like, and they have learned to live in the spaces between the foundry walls and the water, in the boundary layer where temperature changes so rapidly that it might as well be a different medium. They feed on the bacteria that bloom in the thermal gradients, and they are eaten by the workers, when the workers eat, which is not often, which is perhaps not in the way that you understand eating.

There are the workers themselves, though whether they are still fauna in any meaningful sense is a question that the tile-instructions do not address. They were human, once. Some of them still are, in the same way that a foundry that has been continuously operating for centuries is still the same foundry. They have adapted. They have become something that belongs here, that could not survive elsewhere, that moves through the dense water with a grace that speaks of generations of practice, of selection, of becoming-more-themselves.

V. The Erosion of Meaning

The third rule of Vervain Deep is that everything here is old. Not merely old in the way that mountains are old, but old in a way that implies completion, finality, a settling into forms that will not change again. The erosion parameter here is not erosion in the conventional sense—there is no weather, in the way that surfaces understand weather, no rain except the continuous pressure of the water above, no wind except the currents generated by thermal differentials. But there is erosion nonetheless, a wearing away of possibility, a smoothing of all features into their final configurations.

The foundries have reached their oldest-stable-state. They will not expand. They will not contract. They will continue, in their spiral arrangement, in their continuous production, in their particular loudness, until something external intervenes or until the heat sources below finally exhaust themselves, which will not happen in any time that matters to human concern.

The walls show this age. Not in damage—there is remarkably little damage, the materials having been engineered for exactly these conditions—but in a kind of aesthetic exhaustion, a settling-in. The ceramic tiles of the descent shaft, you will notice, if you ever ascend again, are slightly thicker at the bottom than at the top. Not from accumulation, but from compression, from the slow rearrangement of their molecular structure under pressure, the way that very old glass will flow downward in its frame, except that here the flow is inward, downward, toward the center of the earth's gravity and the foundries' heat.

VI. The Light That Is Not Light

It is dim here. That is the fourth rule, though it is less a rule than a condition, a fact so fundamental that it shapes everything else. The light comes from the foundries themselves, from the glow of the furnaces, from the incandescence of the metal being worked, from the bioluminescence of certain modified organisms that have been encouraged to colonize the upper surfaces where their light will not interfere with the precise temperature control of the manufacturing process.

The light is the color of old wounds, of healing, of the particular moment before a fire becomes ash. It does not illuminate so much as suggest, creating spaces of visibility that are always partial, always contingent, always requiring the eye to complete what the light implies. The workers move through this light with a certainty that suggests other senses, other ways of knowing space, that you do not possess and cannot acquire.

There is a cycle to the light, though it is not the cycle of day and night. The foundries operate in pulses, in response to pressures and temperatures and chemical balances that you cannot perceive, and the light follows these pulses, brightening when the furnaces are charged, dimming when they settle into their holding patterns. The workers time their activities to these pulses, their rest and their labor, their feeding and their reproduction, if that is what happens, if they still reproduce in ways that you would recognize.

VII. The Dynamic Stillness

The place is active. That is the fifth rule, the rule that contradicts and completes all the others. Despite its age, despite its settledness, despite the sense that everything here has reached its final form, Vervain Deep is in motion. The water circulates, driven by thermal differentials, by the continuous input of new material from above, by the slow breathing of the foundries themselves. The fauna moves through this circulation, following gradients of temperature and chemistry and food, their abundance a measure of the system's continued vitality.

There are processes here that you will not see, that happen too slowly or too quickly for human perception. The compression of the floor material, ongoing, continuous, the way that the entire place is very slowly, very slightly, becoming smaller, denser, more itself. The growth of the *Laminaria*, measured in millimeters per year, in the way that its fronds slowly colonize new surfaces, new seams, following the warmth. The adaptation of the workers, generation by generation, to conditions that their ancestors would have found impossible, that their descendants will find merely normal.

There is predation, though not in the way that you understand predation. The filter-crabs consume the industrial particulates, but they are also consumed, by processes that break them down into their component materials, that recycle their ceramic carapaces into new manufacture. The vent-fish consume the bacteria, but they are also consumed, by the workers, when the workers consume, which is not often, which is perhaps not in the way that you understand consumption. Everything here is part of a cycle, a system, a closed loop of matter and energy that has achieved a kind of stability, a dynamic equilibrium that is not quite life, not quite death, but something in between, something that has been going on for so long that it has become its own reason for continuing.

VIII. The Boundary of Breath

You cannot stay here. That is the final rule, the rule that is not written on any tile but is nonetheless absolute. The pressure, the temperature, the chemistry of the water, all conspire to make your presence temporary, contingent, a kind of error that the system will eventually correct. You must breathe, and the air here is wrong, is manufactured, is the product of processes that do not produce oxygen in forms that your lungs can use. You must eat, and the food here is not food for you, is the *Laminaria* and the vent-fish and perhaps the filter-crabs, all of which contain compounds that your body cannot process, that will poison you slowly or quickly depending on the dose.

And yet. And yet there is something here, something that pulls, that makes you want to stay, to adapt, to become one of the workers with their thickened membranes and their rerouted nerves and their grace in the dense water. The foundries call, not in any voice that you can hear, but in the vibration of their operation, in the warmth of their walls, in the continuous production of something that is needed, somewhere, for purposes that you do not understand but can sense are important, are meaningful, are worth the cost of being here.

You will leave, though. You will ascend the tiled shaft, past the *Laminaria* and the filter-crabs and the places where the vent-fish flicker in the thermal boundaries, up toward a surface that you are no longer certain exists, that may have become as mythical as the tile-instructions' gilled humans, their fractal limbs. You will leave, and you will carry with you the sound of the foundries, the particular roar that is not quite rhythm and not quite noise, that will persist in your inner ear long after you have returned to places where sound moves through air, where light comes from above, where the natural substrate has not been entirely replaced by manufacture.

You will leave, and you will wonder, as you go, whether the workers you saw were born or made, whether they remember the surface, whether they are waiting for something or merely continuing, whether the foundries will still be operating when the water above finally forgets the surface entirely, when the pressure becomes so great that the distinction between water and solid finally dissolves, when Vervain Deep achieves its own oldest-stable-state, its own final form, its own completion.

You will leave, and you will not know the answers to any of these questions, and that will be the most lasting effect of the place, the way that it opens questions that have no answers, that perhaps do not need answers, that are themselves the product of being in a place where everything is made, where everything is old, where everything continues in its particular loudness, its particular dimness, its particular active stillness, its particular abundant sparseness, its particular becoming.

The shaft ascends. The tiles continue their instructions, their depictions of adaptations that you do not possess and cannot acquire. The water presses. The foundries roar, somewhere below, somewhere that will always be below, no matter how far you climb.

You go up.

The Sinking Foundries continue their work.
