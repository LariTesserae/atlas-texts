The Chime is not a valley, not truly, though you arrive at it by descending. It is a hollow in the high granite, a single cathedral-sized bowl carved by something that predates glaciers—a blow from beneath, perhaps, or a bubble in the molten earth that cooled and collapsed inward and was then found by water, by wind, by time. The rim stands up on three sides like the walls of a room whose fourth wall has been removed to show the sky, and the sky at this elevation is almost violent in its brightness, a white-blue hammer that reflects off every surface until you learn to half-squint, to read the world through your lashes.

The granite here is not bare. That is the first thing. The second thing is that nothing grows.

What lives instead, what has established itself in the long equilibrium of this place, is a colony of glass—*siliceous accretion*, if you wanted a name, but the people who first found this place (and they did find it, once, long ago) called it **weepstone**. It forms in sheets and curtains where moisture collects on the inward faces of the bowl, where cold rock meets slightly warmer air and condensation beads, trickles, freezes in layers too thin to see. Over centuries, perhaps millennia, mineral-laden water deposits its burden in transparent films that harden to something between obsidian and quartz, something that the light turns into instruments. The weepstone grows outward from the walls in formations that resemble nothing so much as frozen waterfalls, or the internal organs of some impossible creature displayed in vitrine, or—in certain angles, certain weathers—the pipes of an organ built for a mythology that forgot its gods.

The dampness is intermittent. For days the Chime may be dry, the weepstone dull, the air carrying only the mineral scent of cold stone. Then weather arrives—clouds from below, rising past the elevation of the bowl's rim, or simply a shift in pressure that pulls moisture from invisible reserves in the atmosphere. The walls begin to weep in earnest. Water runs in threads down the weepstone, and the glass catches it, channels it, shapes it into droplets that hang and fall and hang again. The weepstone does not absorb. It is less a sponge than a thousand thousand flutes, a structure designed by no mind for the purpose of making music from water and gravity and the particular acoustics of this stony room.

The sound, when it comes, is almost too quiet to notice at first. A single note, perhaps, held longer than any instrument of breath or bow could sustain—a clear harmonic that seems to originate everywhere and nowhere, the frequency that the bowl's dimensions prefer. Then another, as a new thread of water finds its path, a different thickness of film, a changed angle of descent. The Chime does not sing constantly; it is not a fountain. But in active moments, when the damp is sufficient and the temperature hovers at that precise point where water moves but barely, the entire hollow becomes an instrument played by no hand, attended by no audience, producing harmonies that no scale has named.

There are marks of habitation. Scattered, yes, barely there—a ring of stones on the flatter ground near the center, blackened within, where fires were once laid. The charcoal remains, compressed by time, identifiable if you dig. Near the eastern wall, where the weepstone grows thickest and the sound concentrates, someone built a shelter once: not walls, for the wind at this elevation would tear anything vertical, but a partial roof of flat stones laid across two low outcrops, creating a space beneath that is still, that is *held*, where the temperature stays fractionally higher and the condensation runs down to pool in a shallow depression worn into the granite floor. The builders had tools of simple metal—copper, perhaps, or cold-worked meteoric iron, for there are striations on the outcrop where they cut, blow by patient blow, to shape the stones to their purpose. They did not stay. Whether they came for the sound, or for shelter from some pursuit below, or for reasons that leave no trace in material record, they built this one structure and used it and abandoned it, and the Chime has since been alone.

The density of the place is in its presences, not its inhabitants. Every surface is attended by its history of water, its growth of glass, its note in the possible harmony. The weepstone formations crowd the walls in layers upon layers, each generation building upon the last, older growths buried behind newer, transparent sheets that reveal and distort what lies behind them. In bright light, you can see into the stone for inches, for feet, the visual path bending through the accumulated strata until depth becomes impossible to judge and a formation the size of your hand might contain the record of centuries. The density is also temporal—these are many moments per volume, pressed together, saturating the available space with time.

What moves here, what makes this place dynamic in the absence of life, is the cycle of water and the growth of the weepstone and the transformation of light into sound into the strange stillness that follows. There is a predator-prey relationship, if you want to see it that way: the water hunts the cold, finds it on these walls, is consumed in the making of glass; the glass, in turn, is hunted by gravity, by the same water wearing it slowly back, by the freeze and thaw that prises loose flakes and shards to litter the floor in drifts like broken crockery from a civilization of ice. The floor itself is not stable. In the center, where the shelter stands, you can see where the granite has been planed smooth by some past movement—perhaps a standing water that lasted long enough to polish, perhaps snow compressed to ice by its own weight, moving imperceptibly, grinding. The marks remain though the ice is gone.

The weirdness is subtle. It does not announce itself. But spend time in the Chime and you begin to understand that the place is *listening*—not to you, for there is nothing here that could attend to your presence, but to itself. The acoustics are such that sound does not escape easily; it reflects, combines, cancels, reinforces. The bowl holds its own history of noise, and in the active moments when the weepstone sings, there are harmonics that seem to answer harmonics that have not yet sounded, anticipations and echoes overlapping until the direction of time becomes uncertain even in the physics of the air. You might record this, if you brought instruments. The recordings would show nothing impossible. But played back, they would not match your memory of having been there, and you could never determine whether the fault lies in the recording, the memory, or the place itself.

At night, which comes quickly at this elevation, the brightness does not entirely fail. Starlight is sufficient, moonlight overwhelming, and the weepstone becomes luminous with stored radiance, emitting a faint phosphorescence that the builders must have known, must have planned for in their choice of location. The shelter beneath its partial roof becomes a place of seeing, the walls around you glowing with cold fire, the notes of the stone—if the damp holds through the temperature drop—visible as slight shivers in the air, disturbances in the light, like heat rising in reverse.

You could live here. That is the strangest thing. The conditions are not impossible; the shelter proves it, the fire-ring proves it. Water, in the active times, is abundant if you know where to collect it from the weepstone's channels. The cold is manageable with the simplest of technologies, with fire, with the insulation of the shelter's design. Yet no one does. The Chime remains unoccupied, has remained so for longer than the weepstone can record in its transparent stratigraphy, longer than the weathered edges of the copper marks can suggest. Something about the density of presence, the saturation of the place with its own processes, its own listening, its own time-bending song, makes habitation feel not forbidden but *futile*—the gesture of a species that has not yet understood that this place is already fully occupied, already complete, already in the equilibrium it has established for itself across the hollow of years.

When you leave, climbing back up to the rim and down the outer slopes, the silence that follows is not empty. You carry the resonance of the Chime in your inner ear, the memory of a brightness that was almost pressure, a cold that was almost touch, a sound that was almost, in its clearest moments, the voice of something that has learned to speak in frequencies just below the threshold of meaning. The damp returns behind you. The weepstone continues its growth, layer by invisible layer. The bowl holds its note, waiting for the moisture that will let it sound again, complete again, attended by no one, sufficient unto itself in the high permanent brightness of its elevation, its long existence, its absolute and singing solitude.
