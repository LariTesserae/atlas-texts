The Oxidation Chapel

---

You descend through a hatch whose seals wheezed their last decades ago, dropping into a space that refuses the utter darkness it deserves. The light — what there is of it — comes from somewhere else entirely, not from above but from *within* the walls, a phosphorescent rust-glow that wakes only where air touches metal. Corrosion as illumination. Decay that sees.

The chamber, when your eyes adjust enough to resolve it, reveals itself as modest: perhaps forty meters in its longest dimension, a flattened ovoid not carved but *grown* in some mineral sense, the product of ancient water finding hairline fractures in bedrock and patiently hollowing. The ceiling hovers just above reach — you could almost touch it, but doing so would mean standing on tiptoe, and the floor discourages such ambition. Specifically, the floor is a skin of water no deeper than your ankle, but the water carries in suspension ochre particulates that swirl lazily around your intrusion, revealing currents that move at speeds measured in hours per meter. The temperature holds at that precise point where the air stops being merely warm and becomes a presence, a wet blanket of atmosphere that has forgotten the existence of seasons. Your skin finds no purchase for evaporation; sweat accumulates rather than departs.

Yet the water is not universal. It collects in the basin of this space, leaving the walls to rise in shoulders and alcoves where condensation beads and drips with geological leisure — perhaps a dozen falls per minute across the entire visible surface, each droplet finding its own path down strata of copper-sulfate blue and ferrous orange. The colors are *fresh*, that is the word that insists. This chemistry has not had time to settle into the muted harmonies of older formations. Something woke this place recently, geologically speaking. Within a handful of millennia, perhaps less.

And the vegetation — because there is vegetation, lush against all expectation — has colonized these dry shoulders with the enthusiasm of first arrivals. Not the green of forests but the *green of copper patina*, biological and mineral become indistinguishable: mats of moss that have evolved, or perhaps engineered themselves, to metabolize the metal ions that saturate every available surface. They grow in plush verdure, impossibly thick, forming cushions and draperies that pulse with slow hydraulic rhythm. Spore cases at their tips release periodically — you witness one, a soft *phut* almost below hearing, a cloud of darker green ascending through the fetid air. These plants do not photosynthesize, not primarily. Their chlorophyll is a deep accessory; their true metabolism is *electrochemical*, feeding on the gradient between the oxidized walls and the reducing muck below. They are factories, in essence. Self-sustaining foundries of organic chemistry, industrial in their efficiency, scattered in their distribution across the available dry surfaces.

For scattered they are, these biomes-in-miniature. The chamber's walls offer only intermittent purchase — too steep, too smooth, too actively corrosive — and so the vegetation clusters where chance topography has created ledges, where falls of earlier rock have created rubble fields, where some past event cracked and pitted the surfaces into hospitality. Between these gardens, the walls run bare and glowing, rust-light finding no interruption. The density feels deliberate, composed, a conversation between presence and absence rather than mere sparseness. Where the moss grows, it grows *lavishly*, almost obscenely; where it does not, the emptiness holds equal weight.

The built structures: you notice them slowly, as your eyes learn to parse decay from intention. A railing of porcelain insulators, still faintly white, traces what must have been a walkway — now submerged to knee height along one wall, now emerging to waist height where the floor shallows over accumulated sediment. The insulators hold nothing; the wire they once separated has corroded to colored stains in the water. Further along, a pump housing, recognizable by its fundamental geometry despite the vegetable draperies that have colonized its cooling fins. It operates still, or something operates *in* it — a vibration you feel first through your feet before you hear anything, a subsonic churn corresponding to nothing visible in the still water. The pump's exhaust, if that is what it is, emerges as periodic bubbles of hydrogen or oxygen or some unguessable compound, breaking surface with soft *pops* that arrive too infrequently to establish rhythm.

Single-building, this place. Not a complex but a *fragment*, a chapel of industry, something constructed for a purpose now illegible. The machinery does not connect to anything outside itself; it merely *persists*, in the manner of an arrested process, a single frame held from a longer film. The tech is industrial, yes — scaled for production, electrified in some lost configuration — but miniaturized to this single chamber's needs, whatever they were. Water processing? Mineral extraction? Something the vegetation has inherited and continued, or replaced, or parasitized?

The sound, what there is of it, arrives from the dynamic processes: the slow drip, the periodic spore release, the subsonic pump, the occasional *tick* of thermal expansion in the still-hot air. These sounds do not accumulate into texture. They remain *events*, each separately countable, the silence between them populated only by the blood-hum of your own presence. You have never been somewhere so quiet that your own swallowing registered as disturbance.

No animal life. You expected insects at least, the ubiquitous inheritors of human absence, but something in the water chemistry — copper toxicity, perhaps, or the lack of any energy base they could metabolize — has kept even these excluded. The ecosystem is purely vegetable, purely *slow*, operating on timescales where a year is a breath and a century a conversation. The moss does not compete; it *spreads*, it *waits*, it *chemicals*. When two colonies meet, they fuse without boundary rather than contest. The only drama is geological, the only deaths are saturation and drowning when water levels shift.

And the weirdness — the parameter that asks not for consistency but for *texture* — lives in the symmetries. The way the rust-light pulses, almost in a pattern, almost carrying information. The way the pump's bubbles sometimes surface in near-unison, as if responding to pressure changes from somewhere deeper, somewhere unmapped. The way the moss grows in shapes that repeat at different scales: the same branching, the same cupping, whether you examine a single colony or the entire chamber's distribution. It is not quite fractal, not quite intentional, but it is *unusual*, a reminder that the boundary between pattern and meaning is itself a construction, thin and local.

You stand in water that remembers being elsewhere, carrying in suspension the products of walls that remember being underground, elsewhere, *before*. The chamber breathes in its slow fashion, temperature and moisture held in equilibrium that requires no correcting, no homeostat beyond the mass of stone and water and strange vegetable industry. Time here is not circular — too young for that, too freshly emergent — but *patient*, a vector stretched until direction becomes indistinguishable from duration.

What emerges, finally, is a place that has not yet decided what it is. The erosion that formed it is young; the built structures that modified it are old but unmourned; the vegetation that occupies it is neither native nor invasive but *novel*, a third category for which no word yet exists. It lives in the interval between definitions, cathedral-scale and room-intimate, industrial and organic, dark and self-illuminated, silent and resonant with potential sound.

You could wait here. The parameters suggest you might wait very long, and change very little, and be absorbed not into death but into *slowness*, into the patient processes that are the only dynamic this place permits. The water would rise, perhaps, fractionally. The moss would advance, or retreat, following chemical gradients your presence has already altered. The pump would continue its unseen labor, for purposes that might become clear in millennia, or never, or might already have been fulfilled a thousand times over in ways too subtle to announce completion.

This is the Oxidation Chapel. It does not need you. It does not need anything, not in any hurry. It merely *is*, in the manner of new things learning to continue, and the continuing is — for now, for this span — enough.
