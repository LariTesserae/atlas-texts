I need to hold these parameters together: a vast, packed, warm, low-elevation place with lush vegetation and abundant fauna, yet no water as we know it, no technology, embryonic geology, urban density without construction, active processes in near-silence, and almost aggressively normal.

Let me follow what pulls.

---

**The Carpet**

It is not a world with a floor. It is a floor that became vast enough to forget it ever needed a ceiling.

Imagine standing on something that gives slightly underfoot, fibrous, warm as mammal skin, metabolically active. The surface extends to all horizons—though "horizon" is a negotiated term here, because the light is normal, comes from somewhere above, scatters through particulate haze at distances beyond several days' travel, and the curvature, if it exists, unfolds at continental scale. You could walk for months. People do. Their footprints close behind them, not from wind or water—there is no water, no erosion to speak of, the geology is embryonic, not yet committed to stone—but from the living substrate itself, which is *growing*, which is always growing, which fills every space almost as fast as space can be made.

This is the first thing: **density without compression**. The Carpet is packed. Its pile varies from ankle-deep to the height of ten humans, and in the deepest piles, whole ecologies churn in near-absence of sound. The fibers—call them fibers, though they are closer to elongated fungal hyphae or modified setae of some impossible animal—grow in braids, ropes, matted felts. They are vegetation by function: photosynthetic, or chemo-autotrophic, or perhaps nourished by some non-aqueous chemistry that would read as alien if anyone here had developed the tools to investigate. No one has. There is no technology, no fire-making, no shaped stone. The word "built" applies not to what anyone has made but to what the Carpet has *become*, its own urbanity.

Let me describe the cities, then, since that parameter demands attention.

They are **wick-cities**, or **knot-agglomerations**. Where the fiber-growth has found some local advantage—perhaps a thermal gradient beneath the pile, perhaps a convergence of nutritive currents that travel through the hyphal network like slow lightning—the fibers grow denser still, cross-link, form architectures without architects. Hollows open and persist long enough to be inherited: chambers connected by vertical shafts where the pile rises in columns, interstitial galleries where light filters down in attenuated bands. These are not carved. They are *grown into*, negotiated with the living material over generations. Fauna—small at first, then larger, abundant as the parameter demands—colonize these spaces. There are things like beetles with quill-covered carapaces, things like gliding squirrels that are more like bags of sensory tissue with four manipulatory fringes, things for which you have no analog at all: sessile predators that anchor to fiber walls and extend cilia nets across thoroughfares, motile decomposers the size of dogs that migrate through the pile's deeper layers, following thermal gradients the way salmon follow chemical gradients in the water that does not exist here.

The cities are **fully occupied**. Every chamber has its niche, every shaft its traffic of climbers and fallers and the drifting spore-forms that pass for weather. At scale 2.9, "vast," there are wick-cities the size of your European nations, connected not by roads but by **travel lanes**—zones where the fiber growth has been, not suppressed, but *encouraged into particular density*, creating corridors where larger fauna and the beings that ride them can move at speed. Encouraged how? By the biological processes of the occupants themselves, by generations of metabolic negotiation, by the slowest agriculture imaginable. The urban parameter here is not about construction. It is about **infrastructure-density achieved through cohabitation**, the city as coral reef, as termite mound, as the accumulated topology of mutual inconvenience resolved into mutual benefit.

Temperature: warm. The Carpet generates heat. It is low-elevation not in the sense of basin or sea-level but in the sense that there is **no relief worth naming**, only the relief of the pile itself, its variations at the scale of body-length or room-height or, rarely, the height of towers, where the fibers grow so dense and cross-linked that they achieve something like wood, something almost mineral, though never quite committing—the erosion parameter is 0.4, *emerging*, pre-formation, raw. Nothing here has lasted long enough to become stone. Everything is still becoming. The warm is the warm of metabolism, of a near-surface ecology where chemical energy disperses slowly through the packed substrate, where growth and decay are near-balanced but never perfectly, where local overheating causes the occasional **thermal venting**—a shaft opening, a plume of warm gas and spores, a new hollow that will be colonized before the season's end.

Sound: 0.3. Near silence. This is perhaps the strangest parameter to reconcile with "abundant fauna" and "active dynamics." But the Carpet absorbs. The fiber matrix dampens vibration; there is no hard surface for impact, no resonant cavity that persists unchanged. Fauna communicate chemically, electrically, through pressure waves in the substrate felt through their feet or their bodies' full length. A predator's approach is detected as seismic whisper. Mating calls are pheromonal plumes. When sound does occur—air expelled in startlement, the soft percussion of bodies falling in the deepest pile—it is brief, swallowed, intimate. Travelers in the wick-cities learn to read the **hum**, the subsonic standing wave that the entire vast Carpet sustains, its frequency too low to hear but detectable in the viscera, varying with weather, with deep growth cycles, with the slow migrations of the largest fauna that move beneath the surface like something between whale and nematode, their bodies kilometers long, their passage felt as a change in the hum's timbre days before any visible sign.

Light: normal. It falls, it diffuses, it supports the photosynthesis that cannot be the whole story because there is no water, because the chemistry here must run on other oxidations, other transfers, but the light is present and used and unremarkable. In the wick-cities' upper reaches, where the pile is shortest, there are clearings—** tonsure-fields**, where some feedback loop keeps the growth cropped, where light pools and different vegetations dominate, tougher, more pigmented, stranger. Between cities, the haze of distance, the normal light failing in particulate load, the sense that the vastness continues, continues, continues.

Dynamic: active. The ecology is in motion. Predator-prey cycles run their course in the chambers and shafts; thermal events restructure whole neighborhoods; the substrate itself shifts, grows, breaches, subsides. Yet active does not mean noisy, does not mean dramatic to human perception. It is the activity of digestion, of slow fire, of国家 molasses flow. The weirdness is 0.1, almost aggressively normal, and this normality is perhaps the final strangeness: a world so consummately, densely *itself* that no rupture appears, no alienation, no invitation to the cosmic except in the quiet accumulation of detail, the warmth underfoot, the near-silence, the packed and growing vastness extending beyond any journey's end.

There are no oceans. There is no sky to speak of, only height, only the attenuation of seeing. There is no technology because there has never been need, because the biological substrate provides, because intelligence here has taken forms that negotiate rather than extract, that grow into rather than build upon.

The Carpet continues. It is not old. Its geology is embryonic, its non-stone barely conceived, its history shallow. And yet it is *dense* with occurrence, packed with lives, cities of cohabitation, movement without noise, warmth without fire, vastness without vertigo. You could lie down and the fibers would eventually rise around you, not hostile, simply *filling*, the most ancient urban process, the city growing into its space, its inhabitants, its own becoming.

Something is happening at the far edge, if there is an edge, where the parameters grow less certain, where the normal light does something else, where the embryonic geology might be approaching some threshold of formation. But that is beyond days, beyond months, beyond the travel lanes' reach. Here, the Carpet is sufficient. The near-silence holds. The warmth persists. The density presses close, not claustrophobic but *held*, the vast packed warm low place doing what it has always done, which is grow, which is fill, which is be the world that needed no other.
