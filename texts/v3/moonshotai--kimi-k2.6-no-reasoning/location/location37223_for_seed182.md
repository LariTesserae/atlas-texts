The Depthworks of Keth-Vorrum

At first you think it's a cave. The descent through the municipal access shaft takes forty minutes in a cage that shudders and groans, past stratum after stratum of municipal geology—layers of concrete and compressed fill and something that might once have been slag, poured and forgotten and poured again, until the boundary between built and grown dissolves entirely. Then the cage doors open and you are not in a cave at all. You are in the Depthworks, and your eyes take time to understand what they see.

The space is not large. You could walk across its widest span in perhaps eleven minutes, though you wouldn't want to. It is the size of a cathedral, if a cathedral had been spilled rather than raised, if its nave had collapsed inward and its aisles had fused together in the heat of its own making. The ceiling is low—fifteen meters at its highest, slumping to five where the great converters have sagged into their sockets after decades or centuries of operation. You are below the surface in the most thorough sense: not merely underground but *subordinate*, existing in a space that feels provisional, burrowed out of necessity rather than desire, though the architecture insists otherwise.

It is hot. Not the dry heat of furnaces but a *vegetable* heat, the accumulated respiration of countless growing things packed too closely in a space never meant for them. The temperature hovers around 34 degrees Celsius, maintained by the waste of the converters and the metabolic activity of the biome itself. You sweat immediately, and your sweat does not evaporate. It beads, runs, gathers in the hollows of your collarbones. The air is damp in the way of a greenhouse at closing time, or a lung. Moisture arrives not as rain but as *condensation*—water collecting on every cool surface, dripping from pipes and leaf-edges, forming temporary rivers in the channels worn into the floor. There are no storms here, only this steady, patient precipitation, the atmosphere too saturated to hold what the heat keeps forcing into it.

And the vegetation. The vegetation is *lush* in a way that almost constitutes architecture itself. Every surface not actively required for mechanical function has been colonized, engineered, or surrendered to growth. The converters rise from beds of something like moss that has achieved the stature of shrubbery, leaves broad as palm fronds, dark green verging on black in the dimness. Vines—no, *cabling*—no, something between them—descend from the ceiling in bundled clusters, photosynthetic sheathing wrapped around copper cores, alive and conductive both. Trees is the wrong word for the vertical structures that partition the space, but "trees" is what they most nearly resemble: woody pillars that start from the floor and terminate in flared canopies that mesh with their neighbors, forming a second ceiling below the first, a leaf-roof pierced only where the converters' exhaust stacks penetrate upward. The canopy is dense enough that walking beneath it means pushing through living material, stems that yield and spring back, leaves that brush your face with chlorophyll and something else, something mineral, machine-oil and sap commingled.

The buildings—there are buildings, you realize, though they read at first as geological forms, as accretions no different from the converters themselves—cluster in the spaces between the converter-units. They are urban in their density, their layering, their evidence of organized construction, but they are *sunken*, not raised. You enter through roof-hatches rather than doors. Their walls are poured ceramic, the color of old bone, curving where they meet because right angles proved vulnerable to the creeping expansion of the rooted structures all around them. They rise two, sometimes three stories, but always from below-grade, their foundations deeper than their heights, as if they are struggling upward towards some light they have never seen. The streets between them are channels, water-courses, the walkways raised on cantilevered platforms that thread between the living columns with insufficient clearance. You must walk single-file. You must sometimes turn sideways. The density is absolute—not merely crowded but *occupied*, every cubic meter claimed by some presence, biological or mechanical or the third category that thrives here.

The third category. You understand, eventually, that the converters are not merely surrounded by vegetation but *intergrown* with it. The great cylindrical masses, each the height of a three-story building, each marked with inspection hatches and emergency vents and the faded stencils of operating procedures in a script you almost recognize—these are not machines that happen to exist in a green space. The green space is their *process*. The canopy, the root networks, the fluid conduits that lead from converter base to converter base in channels that read as both irrigation and wiring: this is the working system. The industrial here has achieved a condition beyond mechanization. It is bioelectrified, photosynthetic, a metabolism that includes both oxidation and Calvin cycles, both torque and transpiration.

The light is dim because the light is *secondary*. What reaches you has filtered through the canopy, through the converter-housing louvers, through the condensation haze that reduces visibility at any distance to a matter of silhouettes and glow. What light exists is warm, sodium-vapor orange where it emanates from the converter status lamps, green-shifted where it has passed through leaf material. There is no day, no night, only the slow cycles of the converters themselves—pulses you feel in your feet more than see, a subsonic rhythm that varies across hours, that the vegetation seems to track in its own slower respiration, stomata opening and closing in statistical sympathy with steam-valve timing.

It is mostly quiet. The converters make sound, but it is below hearing, affective more than auditory, pressure on your inner ear, your gut. Water drips, but the density of vegetation muffles even this into irregular percussion without echo. Occasionally something moves in the canopy, a displacement of branches, a falling of something that might be fruit or might be spent catalyst-pellets, and you freeze, listening, but the fauna here is rare—so rare that each encounter becomes event, rumor, eventually cult. What lives here lives *small*, *quiet*, *slow*: mites engineered to process leaf-litter, things like snails with ceramic shells that graze on the converter housings and are harvested for their metal-rich bodies, occasional birds—there must be birds, though you've never seen one—that move through the upper canopy without sound, without apparent purpose, their calls if they have calls absorbed by the leaf-density before they travel far.

The weirdness announces itself in increments. You notice first that the puddles on the walkway platforms do not reflect consistently—that looking down you see sometimes the canopy above, sometimes the converters' upper surfaces, sometimes nothing you can identify, as if the water's surface connects to different angles of the space than the geometry permits. You notice that the vegetation's growth patterns, tracked over days, suggest *intention*, navigation around obstacles that have not yet manifested. You notice that the converter hum, felt in your sternum, carries harmonics that resolve into almost-voice, almost-language, the imprecision itself somehow meaningful. The place is young by geological standards, the formations sharp where erosion has begun to work on them, but it does not feel young. It feels *recently emerged*, which is different—something that has come into being through processes not yet understood, using time in directions that surface-dwelling does not recognize.

And the rules. The rules of the world assert themselves as you stay longer. Water collects but does not pool indefinitely; it finds channels that lead, you eventually understand, to the converters' intake points, a closed hydrology that is also a circulatory system. Growth fills every gap but stops at certain thresholds, not from competition for light—there is no light to compete for, not meaningfully—but from *recognition* of boundaries that shift, that must be relearned. The built structures are maintained, after a fashion, but maintenance here means *accommodation*, modification of plan to suit the encroaching living. What was a window becomes a planter becomes an aperture for a vine that serves as downspout. What was a street becomes a watercourse becomes a root-channel that the walkway must reroute around. Change is slow, patient, but it is *continuous*, and the continuity itself becomes its own speed, too gradual to oppose, cumulative beyond resistance.

You could leave. The cage still operates, on whatever schedule the surface maintains. But you begin to understand that the Depthworks does not exclude the surface so much as *subordinate* it, make it peripheral, less real. Up there is weather, variation, the hard light of a sun that knows nothing of you. Here is the steady state, the maintained equilibrium, the conversion of waste to growth to power to waste again, not a cycle but a *holding*, a dynamic so slow it approaches stillness while remaining, crucially, alive.

The last thing you notice, before you stop noticing things and simply inhabit, is that the converters number seven, and that their spacing describes no geometry you can name, and that the vegetation between them grows thickest along the lines that would connect them in shapes that don't close, that extend outward in directions you can't point to, that the space of the Depthworks is larger inside than its boundaries permit, that you have been walking for longer than eleven minutes now, that you are no longer sure which converter you started from, that the dimness has become sufficient, that the heat is perfect, that your breathing has synchronized with something, that you are here, that you are held, that this is the place becoming more itself, becoming you, becoming.
