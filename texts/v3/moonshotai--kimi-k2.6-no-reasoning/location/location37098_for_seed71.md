The Plate of Qeth

---

There is a place where the world has finished becoming itself.

Not dead—nothing so dramatic as death. Rather, *settled*. The Plate of Qeth sits at such elevation that the sky below it sometimes looks like a sea you might drown in falling upward, and above it, the stars come out at noon, pale and presumptuous, already present before the sun has finished withdrawing. It is vast. Vast in a way that makes maps into jokes, into children's drawings of something they glimpsed once in fever. You could walk for months and the horizon would remain the same distance away, the same slightly wrong angle, as if the curve of this world obeyed older mathematics.

The erosion here is ancient beyond the useful category of "ancient." The mountains—what remains of them—have been worn down to their essential personalities. You can see in their shapes what kind of stone they were before they forgot themselves: this one was proud, that one was frightened, this ridge here was once a volcano that dreamed of being a river. Now they are all the same height, more or less, a committee of equals, their peaks planed off by wind that has had eternity to practice. The wind carries no scent. The wind has forgotten scent.

And yet: lush. Vegetation at 1.9, which in this cold, at this height, with water only intermittent, should be impossible. Should be. But the plants of Qeth are not participating in the same economy of life that governs elsewhere. They grow from the ancient erosion itself, drawing something from the settled stone that stone in younger worlds does not offer. Moss forests, if you can call them forests—moss that has achieved the ambition of trees, thick trunks of layered green and grey rising thirty, forty feet, branching into canopies that drip not with rain but with *condensation*, with the slow accumulation of moisture from air too cold to hold it. The water parameter is 1.4, damp but not wet, and this is the trick of it: the moss does not wait for rain. The moss *makes* rain, or something like rain, harvesting the cold itself, the temperature differential between air and ancient stone, producing a microclimate of perpetual almost-rain, a world of drizzle without clouds, of wetness that seems to rise from below as much as fall from above.

The moss forests are sparse. Density at 0.6. You can walk for hours between them, across the planed-off mountain-tops, across the high plateaus where the erosion has done its final work and the stone lies exposed like a truth no one wanted to hear. And then suddenly: a stand of moss-trees, a grove, a cathedral of green in the grey, and the sound of their dripping, and the temperature dropping further in their shade, and the light—normal light, 1.7, neither dim nor bright, the sun doing its work without enthusiasm at this altitude—filtering through in beams that seem to move too slowly, that seem to *consider* before they fall.

There are no animals. Fauna 0.5, none. The moss forests have no insects, no birds, no burrowing things. They are ecosystems of one, or perhaps of two if you count the stone they grow from, the stone that is not quite stone anymore, that has become something else through sheer age, through the erosion that is not destruction but *completion*. The moss propagates by fragmentation, by the slow collapse of older growth, by spores that travel on the wind that has forgotten scent and now, perhaps, is forgetting other things too. The forests expand at the speed of geology. A grove that was here when you arrived will, in your lifetime, perhaps extend itself by the length of your forearm. This is the dynamic at 2.3: active, but active on timescales that make human activity seem like a kind of stasis, a kind of failed attempt at motion.

And the cities.

Built at 1.8, urban, and this is where the parameters that should conflict begin their negotiation, their strange collaboration. The cities of Qeth are not built by anyone living. They are built by the moss, or by the stone, or by the interaction between them, or by something else that uses both as materials. They emerge. They *finish*. You will walk across the planed mountain-top, the sparse emptiness, and there will be a city: walls of compressed moss-fiber, harder than wood, lighter than stone, arranged in geometries that suggest streets, suggest plazas, suggest the infrastructure of density without the density itself. The buildings are empty. They have always been empty. They are the right size for human habitation, more or less, but no human has ever lived in them, and the moss does not seem to need them, and the stone has already given everything it has to give.

The cities are layered. You can see where older construction has been incorporated, where walls have been extended, where the geometry has been adjusted according to principles that are not quite architectural, that seem to respond to something else—the light, perhaps, or the sound, or the weirdness that permeates everything here.

Weirdness at 2.9. Reality breakdown. This is not a metaphor on the Plate of Qeth.

The cities are the least of it. The least. Walk far enough, and you will find places where the erosion has worn through to something else, where the ancient stone reveals itself as a kind of shell, a kind of accumulated patience, and beneath it: not magma, not mantle, but *rearrangement*. The ground that continues the slope that should not exist. The horizon that approaches as you walk toward it and recedes when you turn away. The moss-trees that grow downward from no visible root, that drip upward, that are the same tree seen from different angles of time.

The sound is 0.6, mostly quiet, and this quiet is not absence but *presence*, but *attention*. The wind that has forgotten scent has not forgotten sound; it has simply chosen not to make any. The dripping of the moss forests is audible only when you are not listening for it. Your own footsteps are audible only when you forget you are making them. There is a quality to the quiet of Qeth that suggests it is being maintained, that something is working to preserve it, that the noise of the world below—where animals exist, where water flows in rivers rather than condensing from cold air, where erosion is still a process of destruction rather than completion—is being deliberately excluded.

And yet dynamic: 2.3, active. The moss grows, slowly, so slowly. The cities emerge, finish, begin to be incorporated into newer emergence. The weirdness shifts, not randomly but according to patterns too large to perceive, too old to be recorded. The light changes with a seasonality that does not match the sun's path, that seems to respond to something in the moss's own cycles, something in the stone's deep memory of when it was other than stone.

The people who come to Qeth—and people do come, primitive tech at 1.4, simple tools, hand-fabricated clothing against the cold, the desperate or the curious or the ones who have heard that here, at least, the world has finished becoming itself and will not change further, will not demand further adaptation—they do not stay. Cannot stay. The cities are empty and remain empty. The moss forests offer no food that human digestion can process. The water, when you can find it pooled in the ancient erosion's final sculptures, tastes of the stone's memory, of completion, of the end of becoming.

Some try to build. Primitive tech, 1.4, simple metal tools against the moss-fiber walls, fires that burn too cold or too hot, that seem to negotiate with the air rather than consume it. The cities absorb their efforts, incorporate them, finish around them. A wall patched in human lifetime will, in moss-time, in stone-time, become indistinguishable from the original construction. A fire pit dug, used, abandoned, will in a century or two become a slight depression in the planed mountain-top, a place where the moss grows differently, where the weirdness concentrates slightly, where the quiet deepens.

The primitive tech is not inadequate to Qeth. Nothing is adequate to Qeth. The place has finished becoming itself, and what is finished does not need adequacy, does not participate in the economy of means and ends. The tools of the visitors are simply *other*, simply *not-here*, as the animals are not-here, as the rivers are not-here, as the noise is not-here.

And the weather. The scale is 3.0, vast enough for weather of its own, continental, a physics of its own, and this manifests in the storms that are not quite storms, that arrive from directions that do not exist on compasses, that deposit not rain but *arrangement*, not snow but *completion*. The moss forests thrive in these weathers, grow their thirty feet in centuries rather than millennia, produce their spores that travel on winds that have forgotten scent and now, in these storms, seem to be forgetting direction, forgetting temperature, forgetting the difference between air and stone and the green life that grows between them.

The storms pass. The quiet returns, deeper for having been interrupted. The cities stand, empty, finished, waiting for the next emergence that will incorporate them. The moss drips its self-made almost-rain. The erosion continues, has always continued, will continue until there is nothing left to erode, until the Plate of Qeth has worn down to the level of whatever lies beneath the rearrangement, beneath the weirdness, beneath the quiet that is not absence but presence, not emptiness but attention.

Some who come here stay longer than others. Build their primitive shelters against the moss-fiber walls of the empty cities. Learn to harvest the condensation, to find the pockets of water in the larger dryness, to eat the moss in quantities too small to sustain them, to dream the dreams that the stone dreams, that the erosion dreams, that the completion of becoming dreams. They change. They are not incorporated, not quite, not in the way that human construction is incorporated into the cities' ongoing emergence. But they become *sparse*, become *damp*, become *ancient* in a way that has nothing to do with age.

They become, finally, quiet. The sound parameter is 0.6, mostly quiet, and those who stay become mostly quiet, become part of the quiet's maintenance, its preservation against the noise of the world below. They are not quite animal, not quite vegetable, not quite the stone that has finished becoming stone. They walk the planed mountain-tops, the sparse spaces between the moss forests, the empty streets of the empty cities, and they drip, sometimes, with the cold condensation, and they grow, slowly, toward some completion of their own.

The Plate of Qeth does not want them. The Plate of Qeth does not want. It has finished wanting, finished needing, finished becoming. It is vast and ancient and sparse and damp and lush in its moss forests and empty in its cities and weird in its reality and quiet in its sound and active in its dynamic of slow, slow, slow completion.

It is a place where the world has finished becoming itself, and waits, not for anything, not for anyone, but simply *waits*, in the way that stone waits, that erosion waits, that the final form waits for nothing because it has already arrived, already been here, already been what it will always from now on be.

---

*If you go there, take warm clothing. Take water you can drink without tasting stone's memory. Take the understanding that you will not stay, that the place has finished with staying, with becoming, with the dynamic of change that you call life. Take the quiet that is already in you, that you have been keeping against this need. Take nothing else. The Plate of Qeth has finished needing. The Plate of Qeth has finished.*
