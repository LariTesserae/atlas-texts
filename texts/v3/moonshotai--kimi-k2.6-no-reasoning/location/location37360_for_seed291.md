The city is called Stillpoint, though no one knows who named it or when. The name persists in official documents, railway schedules, the stamped plates on decommissioned boilers. It fits badly. Stillpoint is never still.

It occupies a low bowl of land where three defunct riverbeds converge, the earth still damp there, perpetually weeping. Waters rise without warning from the porous stone beneath—sometimes as thin sheets across the factory floors, sometimes as violent geysers that leave mineral towers behind, accretions of salts and oxides that the workers call *sudden stone*. The city planners—there were planners once, whole ministries of them—long ago gave up on drainage. Instead they built around the water's mercurial presence, raised walkways, suspended platforms, entire districts where the streets are canals one week and dust-choked alleys the next.

I am walking through the Packing District at what passes for morning. The sky is the color of old linen, neither bright nor dark, the normal light of Stillpoint's strange latitudes. It never quite storms here, never quite clears. The clouds move in continuous formation and dissolution, as if the air itself were industrial, a process rather than a condition.

The density is what strikes newcomers first, then claustrophobes, then eventually everyone. Every surface occupied. Buildings lean into each other like exhausted dancers, their walls actually touching in places, merging through decades of mutual pressure and opportunistic construction. New structures are inserted into the gaps between old ones, then new ones into the gaps between those, until the original street plan is archaeological, something to be inferred from the occasional straight line of surviving roof ridge. I pass a habitation tower where the ground floor is a foundry, the second a textile mill, the third through seventh residential, the eighth a mushroom farm, the ninth a church of the Machine Reconciled, the tenth through fourteenth more housing, and the top three floors given over to a single extended family who have walled themselves in so thoroughly that their windows are mere rumor, ventilation provided by repurposed boiler pipes.

The vegetation is lush despite the cold, or perhaps because of the particular cold here, which is damp and mineral rather than dry and killing. Moss carpets everything—the walls, the railings, the slow-moving machinery, the shoulders of workers in the brief moments between shifts. Vines have found purchase in the city's endless cracks and fissures, thick-trunked things with leaves the size of dinner plates, pale green and almost translucent. They grow fastest near the sudden stone formations, as if the mineral-rich water feeds something in them beyond ordinary chemistry. In the deeper volumes of the city, where light rarely reaches, fungi have achieved a kind of forest architecture, great bracket shelves and hanging veils that the residents harvest, trade, sometimes worship.

The fauna is abundant and strange. Rats the size of cats, cats the size of dogs, dogs that have gone feral and crystalline-eyed in the wastelands between functional districts. Birds nest exclusively in the highest reaches, never descending below what the locals call the crow line, as if some pressure below forbids it. But there are other things too, things that have no clear taxonomy. I saw one once, in the filtering pools of the Eastern Reclamation Works: something like a eel, something like a ribbon of living mercury, moving through the murk with what looked like deliberation, with what looked like *purpose*. The workers there keep shrines to it, or to its kind, small altars of puddled metal and dried moss.

The erosion parameter means something different here. I have studied the geological reports, what fragments survive from before the administrative collapse of 'the year without reliable records.' The land is young. Not in the sense of recent uplift or volcanic formation, but in the sense of *incompleteness*. The earth here has not finished becoming what it will be. The sudden stone is part of it, but so are the texture of the native rock, which lacks the patina of deep time, the chemical signatures of long weathering. So are the irregularities in the city's foundations, walls that must be continually rebuilt not because they decay but because the ground beneath them *shifts in ways that suggest the substrate itself is still settling into its final form*. The city is built on dough that has not finished rising. This is not metaphor. The geologists, when they still came, spoke of incomplete metamorphism, of rock caught in transition between states, as if some process were interrupted and never resumed, or as if time itself moved differently in the deep strata.

The industrial character is undeniable and omnipresent. Smokestacks rise from the compressed mass of the city like the spines of some buried creature, their emissions blending with the perpetual cloud cover, feeding it perhaps, completing some cycle. The factories never stop. I have confirmed this through personal observation: there is no hour, no minute, when some process is not underway, some furnace not fed, some assembly line not moving. The shifts follow patterns too complex for any single mind to track, overlapping, interlocking, creating a continuous hum of production that is the city's true heartbeat. What they make, what the endless output of Stillpoint serves—this is less clear. Some goods are obviously consumed locally, the textiles and processed foods and simple tools. Some are loaded onto the railway that enters from the north, passes through the Transfer Yards in a confusion of switching tracks, and emerges heading... somewhere. The schedules list destinations that do not appear on any map I can access. The trains return empty, or bearing raw materials whose nature is not discussed.

The turbulence is the final essential quality, the parameter that contains and explains all others. Stillpoint is in continuous transformation, not growth or decay but both simultaneously, not expansion or contraction but a kind of writhing. Buildings are demolished and rebuilt while still occupied, their functions migrating through the temporary structures erected around them. The water courses shift, new sudden stone formations render streets impassable, new channels open where none were. The social order—there is one, I am certain, though its nature eludes direct observation—reconstitutes itself constantly around the physical flux. Gangs become guilds become governmental departments become religious orders become gangs again. The Machine Reconciled was, I am told, a workers' mutual aid society only decades ago.

The sound parameter claims mostly quiet, and this is true in a specific sense. There is no quiet of absence. The industrial hum is constant, the water's movement, the city's endless small adjustments and settlements. But there is little *event*, little punctual noise. No gunshots, though violence is surely common. No alarms, though one sees emergency situations constantly developing. No raised voices, almost ever. The residents of Stillpoint move through their turbulent environment with a studied, practiced quiet, as if loudness were dangerous, or impossible, or simply *not done*. Even the machinery seems muffled, not by any acoustic treatment I can identify, but by something in the air itself, some quality of density or humidity that swallows sound before it can travel.

I have been here six months, or what I calculate as six months. Timekeeping is another fluid parameter. The normal light reveals no reliable solar progression, and the city's internal rhythms operate on schedules I cannot access. I sleep when tired, wake when rested, and neither state arrives predictably.

This morning, in the Packing District, I watch a team of workers dismantling a wall that was a building's exterior yesterday and will be, I am told, a section of elevated aqueduct by evening. They work in silence, communicating through gestures I do not know, their movements choreographed by some collective intelligence that needs no direction. The moss on their shoulders glistens. The air smells of wet stone, hot metal, something vegetal and faintly sweet. A sudden stone formation is growing nearby, I realize, only hours old, already waist-high and still *singing*, a faint harmonic vibration I feel in my sternum before I hear it with my ears.

The weirdness is not an overlay, not something added to an otherwise comprehensible place. It is the native condition. Stillpoint is strange the way a dream is strange, with its own internal logic, its own rules that cannot be stated but only demonstrated through lived participation. I am learning them, I think. Slowly. I no longer expect the floor to remain level, the wall to stay solid, the room to maintain its dimensions. I have begun to recognize the signs that presage a major restructuring, the particular quality of silence that falls before something emerges or submerges, the way the moss will *lean* before a sudden stone formation rises.

What rules govern this place? Not the laws of the former administration, though their empty forms are still recited. Not the doctrines of the Machine Reconciled, though its altars are maintained. Not the imperatives of any human intention I can identify. Something is being built here, something was always being built, something that uses human labor and human habitation as its medium the way a sculptor uses clay. The city is the byproduct of a process vaster and stranger than its inhabitants can comprehend, and they—*we*, I should say, I am becoming one of them—are caught in it like insects in sap, still living, still moving, but progressively enclosed in something that will outlast and transform all our individual purposes.

The silence persists. A bird crosses the crow line, descending slightly, then catches itself and rises again with what looks like panic. The wall-dismantlers have paused, all of them, in identical postures of attention, listening to something I cannot hear. The singing stone has stopped. In the distance, a train whistle sounds, or something like a train whistle, something that carries the same information: *approach, transfer, departure, the continuation of a circulation I will never fully trace.*

Stillpoint. The name is wrong. But names are wrong here, by necessity. They cling to things that will not be held, describe conditions that will not stabilize, map territories in continuous reformation. We use them anyway, the wrong names, the partial descriptions, the incomplete accounts. It is what we have. It is what the city allows, what it accepts as its tribute of partial comprehension, while it continues becoming whatever it is becoming, in the damp and the cold and the strange normal light, in the density and the quiet and the endless industrial turbulence of its endless, endless approach to itself.
