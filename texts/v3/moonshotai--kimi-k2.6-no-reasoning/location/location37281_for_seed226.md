The Hollow Spire

There is a mountain whose peak was never finished. The world was making it—pressure and time forcing granite toward sky—and then something interrupted, or something arrived, or the mountain simply *changed its mind*. What exists now is a vertical chamber: a roughly cylindrical cavity two hundred meters across, open to sky at its summit, its walls still bearing the fresh scars of whatever birth-trauma created it. The rim rises perhaps thirty meters above the surrounding alpine terrain, not a peak but a broken collar, a volcano that inhaled instead of exhaled.

Water enters constantly. Snowmelt seeps through fractures in the upper rock. The interior walls weep. Condensation beads on every surface, gathers, runs in thin threads down the raw stone. The chamber breathes fog at dawn and dusk—cool, dense vapor that fills the lower half like a slow tide, then gradually, *gradually*, lifts and disperses through the opening above. The fog never fully leaves. There is always a haze at knee-height, shrouding the floor.

And there is a floor, or something like one. At the chamber's base, occupying perhaps a third of the available space, sits the town. I call it a town. It is urban in *organization*, not in populousness. Perhaps forty souls inhabit the Hollow Spire. They live in structures of pressed peat and woven willow, of slate shingles split thin as fingernails, of bone-lattice frameworks stretched with cured hides. Their tools are stone, antler, a little soft copper hammered cold. They build upward because the horizontal is occupied by their predecessor's mistake, or perhaps their predecessor's wisdom—a foundation of massive worked stones, walls three meters thick, forming a partial ring that encloses the northern quadrant of the chamber floor. The old builders worked in something harder than basalt, something the current inhabitants cannot identify or work. They have built their wattle structures against these ruins, into them, through gaps where the ancient walls have... not collapsed, but *unmade themselves*, reverted toward raw geology, corners rounding, surfaces flowing like slow wax.

The vegetation is *lush* but the density is *empty*. This is the first tension resolved. Life here grows in explosions of concentration, then vast silence between. Lichen mantles the upper walls in tapestries of sage and rust, fed by the constant seep and the good light falling through the opening. Below, where the fog pools, moss carpets the ancient foundation-stones in depths of a meter or more, swallowing their edges, making them into green hills. Fungi thread through this moss in networks that must be acres if unfurled, though compressed into this small vertical world. And in the mist-line, at that precise elevation where water and light and cool air mix perfectly, ferns grow from every crack and ledge, some as tall as a person, their fiddleheads uncurling with vegetative patience.

Between these concentrations: naked stone, wet and singing.

Because the sound is *loud*. This is the second resolution. The emptiness amplifies. Water falling from the rim strikes ledges on its descent, each impact a percussion note. Some streams have worn channels and play them like flutes. The moss-muffled foundation catches and releases sound unpredictably—voices carry, then vanish. The fog dampens and focuses simultaneously. And the chamber itself resonates at a frequency felt in the sternum, a hum from wind across the opening or perhaps from the mountain's own unfinished business, its still-arising. The inhabitants have developed a sign-language for precise communication, using their voices only for song, for ritual, for the calling-out that happens at evening when the fog rises fastest and the sound-environment shifts, becomes briefly *room-like*, intimate, before expanding again into cathedral vastness.

The fauna is rare. I have seen only three creatures with certainty. The strand-spiders, which are not spiders, which build nothing but instead *find* the condensation-strands between fern fronds and walk them with geometric precision, drinking as they go. The stone-moths, which pupate in the raw geology itself, emerging already mineral-stained, their wings the color of the rock they left, their flight a silent brown drift against the louder elements. And the hollow-goats, which are kept by the inhabitants, which have evolved or been bred to climb the interior walls, to find the sparse ledges, to give milk that tastes of the mineral water and the particular lichens they favor. Perhaps two dozen of them. Perhaps, on certain fog-thick days, none visible at all.

The weirdness is subtle, structural, in the rules. Time here is *emergent* too, not fully formed. The old builders' stones revert; this I mentioned. But other things arrive before they should. The inhabitants find, sometimes, in the moss, objects not yet made: a copper knife in the shape their grandchildren will forge, cool to the touch, unused. They leave them. They know not to accelerate what's still becoming. The fog sometimes shows configurations—an antler, a face—that will exist tomorrow, next season, in dreams not yet dreamed. The mountain is still deciding what it is. The world permits no rush.

At evening, the fog rises. The inhabitants climb to the ruined stone ring's highest intact point, a platform where the old builders' work reaches toward the chamber's center without completing its circle. From here they can see, through the rising mist, the sky's color change, the first stars become visible through the opening. They sing then, loud as the water, their voices joining the resonance, shaping it for a moment before releasing it back to its own slow becoming. The goats climb down to the moss-floor, invisible in the fog. The strand-spiders walk their geometry. The stone-moths, if they are there, keep their mineral silence.

The mountain grows still, patient, preparing its next emergence. The town waits, sparse, organized, precise in its emptiness. The water falls, falls, falls, and has not yet finished falling.
