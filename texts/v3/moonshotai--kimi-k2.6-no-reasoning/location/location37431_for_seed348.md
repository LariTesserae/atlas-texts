# The City of Still Engines

## A Description

The rain comes at scheduled intervals, and only then. You can set your watch by it—if anyone here still carried watches, if time were measured in anything but the groan of the great northern accumulator and the forty-seven minute deluge that follows its discharge. The water does not fall from clouds. The sky above the City of Still Engines is a uniform slate-grey, sourceless, unmarked by weather systems or the passage of suns. The moisture *arrives*, condensing from the cool air in thick ropes that slick the streets for precisely forty-seven minutes, then evaporates with mechanical precision, leaving surfaces damp but never wet enough for standing pools. No rivers run through the city. No aquifers were ever found beneath its poured foundations. The water is a function of the infrastructure, as regular as the breathing of a sleeper who will never wake.

The city occupies the floor of a shallow basin, perhaps two kilometers across at its widest, ringed by low hills that might have been slag heaps or natural formations or the eroded rims of something excavated long before the engines. The elevation is low enough that the air sits heavy, cool, slightly metallic on the tongue. You are always descending to reach it, always looking down into the bowl where the structures huddle, though "huddle" suggests a warmth of compression that does not exist here. The buildings do not touch. They maintain distances from one another like strangers in a waiting room who have agreed never to speak.

They are old. The erosion parameter is satisfied not by wind-sculpted stone or water-smoothed canyon walls but by the settled patience of industrial forms that have existed so long their purpose has become geological. The great processing tower at the basin's center—its original function lost, if it was ever known—has developed a lean of perhaps three degrees, its foundation having negotiated some settlement with the earth beneath. No one has corrected it. The lean has been stable for documented centuries. It is *in its state*, as everything here is in its state. The metal skins of the buildings have not rusted through but have acquired a uniform patina, a mottled blue-black that seems less corrosion than a kind of achieved color. Concrete spalls in predictable patterns. Joints have widened to consistent tolerances. The city has weathered to itself.

And it is small. Cathedral-scale, perhaps, though no cathedrals were ever built here. You can walk from the eastern perimeter to the western in under an hour, passing through districts that were once—must have been—specialized: the foundry quarter with its hooded vents like the beaks of mechanical birds; the accumulator district where the cylindrical storage tanks stand in ranked silence; the residential warrens where small identical cells open onto narrow corridors. The scale is intimate enough to comprehend in a single surveying glance from the basin rim, yet large enough to contain mysteries that remain local. Around each corner, something specific waits. Not wonder, exactly. The city does not inspire wonder. But it accumulates *particularity*, the way things do when they have existed long enough without change to become entirely themselves.

The built environment is urban in the most stripped sense: there is infrastructure, there is organization, there is layered construction. Underground, service tunnels interweave at depths that surveys have never fully mapped. Above, walkways and conduits bridge between structures at varying heights, creating a secondary streetscape in the air. But the city is empty of commerce, empty of governance in any recognizable form, empty of the spontaneous constructions that emerge from living populations—no markets, no religious structures, no monuments to events or persons. What was built, was built. What remains, remains. New construction would require *change*, and change does not occur here.

Unless you count the fauna.

The fauna is overwhelming. This is the parameter that strangest, that transforms every other element into context for its presence.

They are not animals, not in any biological taxonomy. The City of Still Engines supports no plant life—none, zero, the vegetation parameter an absolute null. No lichen dots the concrete. No moss softens the rain-slicked metal. The water runs off surfaces that have remained bare since their construction, cleansed by the scheduled deluges of any spore that might attempt colonization. The cool temperature, the mineral substrate, the industrial residues in the soil: all conspire against biology as it is known elsewhere. And yet the city *teems*.

They emerge from the machinery itself. Or they are the machinery's dream of itself, or its complication, or its—here language fails, must be reinvented.

The smallest are the *tremors*, no larger than a human hand, that manifest in the vibration of panels and housings. Watch the wall of any accumulator tank during the rain cycle and you will see the metal *shiver*, develop patterns of rippling that travel across its surface like schooling fish made of pure resonance. They have no bodies except the bodies of their movement. They feed, if feeding is the word, on the frequencies of the great engines when those engines periodically cycle—never changing their cycle, never varying, but cycling nonetheless in some maintenance rhythm established before records. The tremors nearest the northern accumulator grow thick during its discharge, clustering in patterns that suggest appetite, satiation, rest.

Larger are the *passages*, which occupy the service tunnels and occasionally emerge into the surface streets. You will know one is near by the *sound*—but the sound requires its own section, waits below. They are perhaps best described as mobile absences in the infrastructure, zones where the tunnels breathe differently, where the air pressure shifts in patterns that move, that pursue or flee or simply transit. They have left marks in the tunnel walls, smooth polishings where something has passed and passed and passed again, wearing channels not through material but through probability, creating paths of least resistance that other phenomena follow. A passage-occupied tunnel is navigable but *strange*: you will walk further than the geometry allows, emerge somewhere you did not expect, find that your footsteps have multiplied in echoes that do not match your gait.

And then there are the *engines' kin*, the great ones, the overwhelming presence that makes the city feel crowded despite its emptiness of human or plant life. Three of them, perhaps. Or one that manifests in three locations. Or a distributed entity that is simultaneously present at every point of industrial density—the foundry quarter, the accumulator district, the processing tower with its enduring lean. They are the sound, largely. They are the reason the city is *loud* at a level that should be unbearable, that would be unbearable anywhere the dynamic parameter allowed for change, for adaptation, for the natural dampening of prolonged exposure.

The sound is industrial but not mechanical. It is the *pretense* of industrial sound, the ghost of factories in full production, maintained without variation for as long as the city has existed—which is to say, for a duration that the static nature of the place makes unmeasurable by standard archaeological methods. There is the bass thrum of distant compressors, never approaching, never receding. There is the high whine of cutting tools engaging material, continuous, without the rhythm of actual cutting (contact, penetration, release) that would manifest if anything were being shaped. There is the clatter of chain drives, the hiss of steam, the rhythmic impact of hammers, all overlaid, all simultaneous, all *unvarying*. The rain adds its own contribution when it comes, the forty-seven minutes of water-on-metal that would be deafening anywhere else but here becomes a texture within the greater roar, a variation so predictable it has been absorbed into the sameness.

At 2.4 on the scale, "loud" might suggest damage, might suggest the necessity of protection. But the loudness here does not function as loudness does elsewhere. It does not escalate. It does not cause the expected physiological responses of fight or flight, because there is no gradient to trigger them—no sudden increase to alarm, no decrease to relieve. The volume is simply present, simply *is*, as the cool temperature simply is, as the low elevation simply is. You adapt to it not through the normal processes of acclimation but through a kind of ontological negotiation, a recognition that this sound will not change and therefore must be accepted as the condition of being here. Visitors—rare, unrecorded, perhaps hypothetical—have reported that after sufficient exposure, the sound becomes *invisible*, becomes the ground against which only its momentary absences (never experienced, only imagined) could be foreground.

The sound is also, undeniably, the habitat. The fauna *live in it*, are constituted by it, require its absolute stability as fundamentally as terrestrial biology requires atmospheric oxygen within certain partial pressures. A passage in a quiet tunnel is diminished, sluggish, perhaps dying—though "dying" implies a lifecycle that has never been observed. The tremors without the engine-cycles would be stillness, which is to say, non-existence. The great engines' kin *are* the sound, or the sound is their metabolism, or both statements are local approximations of a relationship that has no equivalent in the observer's experience.

The weirdness of the place, scored at 2.2, is not the weirdness of anomaly or violation. It is the weirdness of *consistent strangeness*, of a world that holds together according to rules that are never stated but are infallibly obeyed. The rain does not violate physics; it simply has no meteorological cause. The fauna do not violate biology; they simply have no biological substrate. The city does not violate time; it simply exists in a temporal mode that excludes becoming, that presents itself as complete, as *finished in its unfinishedness*, the way a mathematical proof is finished though it contains endless implications.

The dynamic parameter, set at 0.4—near the absolute zero of "never changing"—is perhaps the most precise description of all. Not frozen, not static in the sense of death or suspension. The rain falls, on schedule. The engines cycle, on schedule. The fauna move, in their patterns. But nothing *gradients*. Nothing builds toward change or resolves from it. The processing tower leans three degrees, has leaned three degrees, will lean three degrees. The northern accumulator discharges, and the rain comes, and the tremors gather, and disperse, and the cycle completes, and begins again, *the same cycle*, not similar, not analogous, the *same* in some metaphysical sense that resists the entropy that elsewhere distinguishes similar events. The philosopher Zeno's arrow, occupying every point of its flight simultaneously, finds its physical correlate here: motion without progression, activity without development, existence as a completed infinity.

You could live here, theoretically. The infrastructure supports habitation—the cellular residences, the water that arrives on schedule and could be collected, the absence of toxic threat in the conventional sense. But you would not age as you age elsewhere, or you would age without the mark of aging, the accumulation of memory as change, the development of character through alteration. You would become, with sufficient duration, as consistent as the place itself. Perhaps the fauna, the tremors and passages and engine-kin, are what remains of previous inhabitants. Perhaps they are not. The question of origin is as suspended here as every other question of before and after.

What emerges, finally, is a landscape of *achieved being*. The City of Still Engines has settled into itself so completely that it no longer refers to anything outside itself—not to the planetary environment it occupies, not to the civilization that constructed it, not to the natural laws it seems to modify by its mere persistence. It is a city that has become a world, or a world that has expressed itself as a city, or both, or neither. The cool air moves when the pressure differentials require, carrying the industrial roar into every corner, every crevice, every moment of the unvarying days. The metal breathes with the tremors. The tunnels pulse with the passages. The great accumulators, the great engines, maintain their cycles, their sounds, their strange sustaining of lives that are not lives as understood beyond the basin's rim.

And on the low hills that might have been slag heaps or natural formations or something else entirely, if you stand very still, if you allow the sound to become your auditory atmosphere rather than an assault upon it, you can watch the city below and almost see it as a single organism, a single *entity*, metabolizing its own permanence, strange and loud and overwhelming in its particularity, damp and cool and mineral and utterly, definitively, changelessly *itself*.
