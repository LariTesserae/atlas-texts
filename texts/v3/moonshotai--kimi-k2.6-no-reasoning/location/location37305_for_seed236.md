The place is called the Dryhollow, though the ones who live there have no word for it—no words at all, no language, no technology, no tools. It is a single bowl-shaped depression in a high plateau of red ferrous rock, perhaps three hundred paces across at its widest, rimmed by sudden walls that rise like the inside of a broken skull. The plateau itself sits above a sea of rust-colored dust that extends to every horizon, though "sea" is a borrowed word, a human trespass. There is no water here. Never was. The chemistry of this world uses perchlorate salts and siloxanes, lipids built without water, metabolisms slow as plate tectonics.

Yet the Dryhollow is lush.

This is the paradox that defines it, the tension that makes it real. The vegetation—if we can stretch that word—grows in dense, near-touching profusion, layer upon layer of it, a vertical forest compressed into the horizontal because the bowl protects it from the wind that scourges the plateau above. The rim walls are young, sharp, barely weathered. Geologically, this hollow was born yesterday: a gas bubble in cooling lava, or the collapse above some slowly dissolving mineral body, or perhaps the impact scar of something the size of a fist that hit with extraordinary velocity in a world with no atmosphere to burn it. The edges still show melt-splatter, glassy lobes frozen mid-flow, hexagonal cooling cracks that have not yet had time to widen.

Inside this protection, life has achieved density. The scale is intimate, room-sized, cathedral-sized if you consider the vertical layering. The tallest growths reach perhaps to the height of a human knee, but they are not simple. They are coral-trees of copper-green filament, branching in fractal iterations so fine that to move through them is to part curtains of living wire. They do not photosynthesize—there is no water to split, no oxygen to release. Instead they are thermoelectric, drawing current from the temperature differential between the sun-warmed rock surface and the cooler depths below. Their metabolism is electrical, their growth is crystalline, their reproduction is the self-replication of satisfactory mineral arrangements.

Warmth is the key. The temperature sits in the upper range of comfort for human analogues, though humans would die quickly here, desiccated by the absolute dryness, poisoned by the perchlorates, starved of the water their chemistry demands. But the warmth is gentle, distributed, held in the rock and radiated back slowly through the dense growth. The bowl is a solar collector, a natural greenhouse. During the long day—forty hours of this world's rotation—the rock stores heat. During the night, the filament-trees glow faintly with St. Elmo's fire, discharge points dancing along their branching tips, enough to read a face by if anyone here had faces to read.

The ecology is abundant with fauna, though "animal" is another borrowed word. These are mobile mineral formations, silicate and metallic, grown rather than born. The smallest are motile crystals that migrate up and down the temperature gradient, following optimal thermoelectric yield like sundews following light. They are the grazers, though they consume not the living filaments but the charge-differential those filaments maintain. Their movement is slow, geological, a crystal lattice reconfiguring itself atom by atom, so that to watch one cross a span of hand-width might take an afternoon. Faster are the pseudopod-forms, colonies of cooperative motility, iron-sulfide slimes that flow through the coral-wire forest in channels of least resistance, seeking concentrations of charge and processing them through internal voltaic cascades.

Largest are the statuaries, knee-high masses of intergrown crystal that settle in place for decades, centuries perhaps, becoming indistinguishable from the growing coral-trees until some threshold of charge accumulates and they suddenly, painfully reconfigure, splitting along planes of weakness and reassembling in new geometries that walk, slowly, slowly, to new positions. They leave tracks in the finer growths, paths that become channels that become arroyos that the living forest slowly, slowly fills. The statuaries are the apex of this ecosystem, if apex means anything without predation, without death in any familiar sense. They do not eat the smaller forms. They process richer charge gradients, the thermoelectric equivalent of apex predators concentrating trace minerals. They are few. In the whole Dryhollow there may be thirty. They are never seen to interact, though their paths sometimes cross in patterns that suggest awareness, planning, perhaps communication through electrical field disturbance too subtle for human instrumentation.

Built infrastructure is everywhere, and nowhere. The Dryhollow is urban in its density, its layering, its organized complexity, but it was not built by minds, by hands, by intention. The coral-trees grow in patterns that resemble streets, plazas, cul-de-sacs, because these arrangements optimize charge distribution and wind protection. The statuaries settle in positions that create nodes, junctions, hubs of electrical activity. The pseudopod-slimes follow channels that become, in effect, transit networks. There is no planner, only optimization. There is no architect, only emergence. The city grew itself over millennia, or over millions of years—time moves differently here, when your fastest metabolic process might complete in a human month.

The weirdness is in the recognition. A human observer, if one could survive and observe, would feel the uncanny familiarity of the layout, the sense of walking through an abandoned district of some impossible city, streets overgrown with metallic underbrush, plazas where statuaries stand like forgotten monuments, building-composites of intergrown crystal that suggest doorways, windows, rooms within if rooms had purpose in a world without interiors. But there is no abandonment, no history of habitation by minds. The similarity is convergent evolution of spatial organization, the mathematics of optimal networks independently discovered by thermoelectric metabolism and by urban planners on worlds with water.

Sound is almost absent. The atmosphere is thin, the chemistry different, sound propagation weak. What reaches the place where ears would be is a subsonic rumble, the statistical average of crystal growth stresses, thermal cracking, the occasional catastrophic reconfiguration of a statuary. Perhaps once in a human week, a sharp crack echoes as some structural limit is exceeded, some mineral finally yields, a fragment falls from the rim wall and rolls through the coral-forest, leaving a track of crushed filaments that will heal, regrow, incorporate the mineral fragment, in a few years or decades.

The light is normal for this world, two suns small and distant in a sky the color of dried blood, their combined illumination sufficient, their combined warmth the engine of all complexity here. Shadows are soft, overlapping, doubled. The coral-trees are translucent, copper-green, filtering light into depths that shift with the slow passage of the suns. There is no day-night cycle in the human sense—the planet's rotation is too slow, the night too long, the life too dependent on stored heat. Instead there is a perpetual dusk-brightness, a warm amber gloaming in which time seems not to pass but only to deepen.

The rules of this world: structure precedes function. Growth precedes purpose. The city did not evolve to serve inhabitants; inhabitants evolved to serve the city's electrical gradients. There is no water, but there is flow. There is no death, only reconfiguration—every mineral form can be dissolved, recrystallized, incorporated, remembered in the lattice structure of what grows over its former position. There is no tool, but there is tool-like behavior: the statuaries that settle to optimize, the pseudopod-slimes that channel, the crystal-grazers that probe and test. Intelligence, if it exists here, is distributed, slow, emergent from the network properties of the whole Dryhollow rather than resident in any individual form.

And it is patient. The dynamic is glacial, change measured in generations of human lives for any single reconfiguration. Yet within this patience, there is urgency in the electrical sense, the immediate pressure of gradient seeking discharge, the NOW of current flow even while the channels that carry it shift only across centuries. The Dryhollow lives in two timescales simultaneously: the instantaneous flash of electron transfer, the eternal creep of mineral growth.

A statuary is reconfiguring now. It has been building charge for eleven years, its crystal lattice strained, its internal structure prefiguring the transformation in zones of weakness, planes of prepared fracture. The coral-trees near it have grown differently, charged differently, sensing in their electric metabolism the approaching change. When it comes, when the statuary splits and walks, the discharge will propagate through the network, a signal, a communication, a small reshaping of the urban geometry. Perhaps it will settle nearer the rim, where the temperature differential is sharper. Perhaps it will move to the center, where charge pools and gradients diffuse. The mathematics of its decision are not knowable, not predicted, only optimized.

The Dryhollow has time. The bowl is young, the rim barely weathered, the plateau beyond ancient in human terms but fresh in geological ones. The coral-trees grow, the slimes flow, the statuaries think their slow mineral thoughts. There is no water. There is no silence, only frequencies we cannot hear. There is no city, only the city that grew itself. There is no visitor, no witness, no word for what it is.

Yet it continues, warm and dense and strange and alone, a room-sized cathedral of electrical life high above the rust dust sea, patient beyond measure, complete unto itself, sufficient.
