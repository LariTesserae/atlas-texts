The city of Vervain climbs the inside of a volcano that has not yet finished erupting. This is not metaphor. The caldera wall rises in a near-perfect bowl, terraced by construction that follows the curve of cooling magma, and at the center—where a lake of fire should be—there is instead a pillar: a vertical shaft of semi-solidified rock that the inhabitants call the Wick, still channeling magma upward from depths no instrument has measured, still growing, still *becoming*.

You arrive, if you arrive, through one of the steam-gates: passages carved where geothermal vents create corridors of breathable, if sulfurous, air. The gates wheeze. They are the only sound you will hear for long stretches, that rhythmic exhalation of the mountain's lung, *hahhh, hahhh*, and then nothing but the subaudible hum of the city's machinery and the occasional crack of fresh stone cooling too fast.

---

The terraces are dense. Not crowded—there is space enough to walk, to maneuver, to live—but *dense* in the sense of layered occupation, of function stacked upon function, of the city as three-dimensional puzzle rather than sprawl. The lowest levels, nearest the Wick, are oldest: foundations sunk into basalt that was liquid when the first builders arrived, who were not the current inhabitants, whose structures remain as hollow shells, their purposes illegible, their geometries slightly wrong when viewed from certain angles. The current city grew around these, through these, over these. You will find a functioning foundry occupying what was clearly once a—chamber? a throat?—with walls that narrow to a point and then continue, impossibly, in directions the eye refuses to track.

Higher terraces are newer, cooler, more comprehensible. Here the industrial infrastructure dominates. The city runs on geothermal extraction: not steam turbines, nothing so crude, but some process of thermal gradient harvesting, of drawing energy from the difference between the Wick's radiant heat and the caldera's rim, where snow sometimes falls and instantly hisses to vapor. Factories cling to the inner wall like barnacles, their smokestacks—if they smoke at all, some vent only heat-shimmer—angled to miss the terraces above. Everything is calculated. Everything is measured. The city has been built to millimeter tolerances against a mountain that does not acknowledge millimeters.

The materials are volcanic, obviously, but transformed. There is a glass here, not obsidian but something else, something that forms when certain magmas cool in the presence of the city's extraction fields, that becomes translucent, that carries light, that the builders use for windows and for circuitry both. You will see it glowing in the walls, faint veins of amber and blood-orange, carrying information or illumination or both—the distinction may not exist here. There is a ceramic, too, fired in the ambient heat without ever seeing flame, dense as stone, that the inhabitants shape into vessels, into tools, into the masks they wear when approaching the lower levels.

---

There is no vegetation. Nothing grows. The ecology is mineral, thermal, chemical.

But there is *something like* life. The inhabitants do not speak of it directly. In the crevices where steam condenses and re-evaporates in endless cycle, you find accretions: filaments of deposited mineral that grow, that branch, that sometimes *move*, if movement is measured across weeks, across months. The inhabitants harvest these. They are not food—the people eat, you will learn, synthesized proteins, chemical rations, things that come from the factories—but they are used. Ground for pigments that shift color with temperature. Woven, somehow, into the glass-circuitry. Made into offerings that are left at the entrances to the oldest hollow structures, where the first builders dwelt.

Are the accretions alive? The question may not translate. They grow, they respond to environment, they reproduce through fragmentation. They have no cells, no metabolism as you would recognize it. They are chemistry becoming structure becoming something that, if you watched long enough, might become *more*. The city has been here long enough that some have watched that long.

---

The people. They are rare, individually. The density of the city is in its structure, its machinery, its layered occupation of space. The inhabitants number perhaps ten thousand in a volume that could hold ten million, clustered in the more stable terraces, in the zones where the heat is bearable, where the steam-gates provide breathable atmosphere. They are not numerous, but they are *present*, their works everywhere, their attention everywhere, their slow patient industry everywhere.

They do not speak much. The city is mostly quiet. Machinery hums. Steam wheezes. Stone cracks. Occasionally, from deep below, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and then silence again, long silence, the inhabitants pausing in their work to listen, to wait, to confirm that the Wick still grows, that the eruption is still *emerging*, still not arrived.

They are waiting for something. They have always been waiting. The city is not complete because the mountain is not complete, and the mountain is the city, or the city is the mountain's intention made manifest, or—

The explanations fail. The inhabitants, when pressed, offer only: *It is becoming. We are helping it become.*

---

The light is normal, which is strange in itself. The Wick glows, obviously, a constant dull red that brightens to orange in its periodic surges, that casts everything in shades of heat. But there is also, somehow, daylight. The caldera's rim is high, impossibly high, but not closed: there are gaps, there are angles, there are the glass-ceramic windows of the highest terraces that capture and diffuse and redirect. The result is illumination that shifts but never fails, that mimics something like a twenty-hour cycle, dawn and noon and dusk and dark, all in thermal tones, all in the reds and ambers and finally the deep crimson-black of the Wick's own radiance when the "night" comes.

In this light, the city's weirdness becomes visible, becomes *ordinary*, which is perhaps the weirdest thing of all. You stop noticing that the terraces curve upward in both directions, that you can see buildings above you inverted, that gravity seems to *agree* with this, that you can walk to what should be the ceiling and find it the floor, that the hollow structures of the first builders open into spaces that contain more volume than their exterior dimensions permit. You stop noticing because the inhabitants do not notice, because the city functions, because the machinery runs and the steam wheezes and the Wick grows and everything is, in its own terms, *normal*.

---

The rules of this world: causality is local. The city obeys physics, mostly, in the immediate, in the operational. But the whole, the system, the mountain-and-city-and-Wick, operates on principles that emerge from the interaction, that are not predictable from the parts. The first builders knew this, perhaps. Their structures are where the rules are weakest, where the weirdness concentrates, where the inhabitants go only when necessary, masked, leaving their mineral offerings.

The rules: time is slow. Not for individuals—people live, age, die, are recycled into the nutrient systems, their mineral components eventually joining the accretions in the steam-crevices—but for the city, for the mountain, for the *project* that both constitute. The Wick has been growing for millennia. It will grow for millennia more. The eruption, when it comes, will not be an ending but a transformation, a next phase, something the inhabitants have been preparing for since before their recorded history began.

The rules: water is precious, marginal, present only as condensation, as the steam that makes life possible, as the thin films that enable the mineral accretions to grow. There is no rain, though sometimes the rim's snow sublimates and re-condenses in strange patterns, brief illusions of weather. The city captures every drop, every vapor, recycles endlessly. Dampness is a constant at certain levels, dry heat at others. The inhabitants have adapted, or been adapted, their bodies efficient at water retention, their technology efficient at extraction.

---

What is being built here? The inhabitants would say: the city. The mountain would say nothing, the mountain is not sentient, or if it is, its sentience operates on timescales that make communication impossible. The Wick would say— the Wick would be the saying, the slow upward growth, the channeling of deep heat, the *becoming* that the inhabitants serve.

Perhaps the city is a machine for waiting. Perhaps it is a machine for transformation. Perhaps it is simply what happens when intelligent beings encounter a process too large to stop or accelerate, and choose to inhabit it, to shape it, to be shaped by it, to make their lives in the interval before the interval ends.

You can leave, if you came. The steam-gates open both ways. But something of the city stays, some mineral film deposited in the lung, some thermal rhythm established in the sleep cycle, some sense of scale, of patience, of the slow *emerging* that outlives all urgency.

The city of Vervain continues. The Wick grows. The accretions grow. The inhabitants tend their machinery, leave their offerings, wait in the mostly-quiet for the sound that will mean the next phase has begun. They have been waiting so long that waiting has become indistinguishable from living, from building, from being.

The mountain is not finished. The city is not finished. Nothing here is finished. That is the condition, and the inhabitants have made it their home.
