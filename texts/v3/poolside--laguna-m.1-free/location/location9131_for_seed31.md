The land rises in a staggered series of terraces, their edges still raw and angular, as if the stone itself had only recently split open to reveal their forms. At this elevation—high enough that the air carries a thin, sharp clarity—the world feels suspended between sky and the vast, open plateau below. The terrain is a mosaic of slick, water-slicked rock and shallow pools, their surfaces mottled with the reflections of low-hanging clouds that refuse to release their rain. Weeping rocks, porous and ancient-seeming despite the landscape’s youth, drip steadily into rivulets that carve tiny, ephemeral channels through the soil. The ground here is never truly dry; even the grasses that dot the terraces—sparse and wiry, with blades the color of tarnished bronze—seem to exhale moisture, their roots threading through a subsurface web of moss and decaying leaves.  

The settlement is a cluster of low, squat structures nestled in a crescent-shaped hollow, where the water has carved a shallow basin. Their walls are built from the local stone, roughly hewn and fitted without mortar, their thatched roofs sagging under the weight of perpetual damp. Smoke rises from a few chimneys in thin, reluctant tendrils, carrying the scent of burning peat and something earthier, like wet fur. The paths between the buildings are paved with flagstones salvaged from the surrounding rock, their surfaces worn smooth by countless footsteps, though now they glisten with a thin film of condensation. In the distance, a rudimentary aqueduct—its channels choked with algae and silt—stretches toward the town, its purpose long since eroded by the same slow, patient processes that shaped the land.  

The silence here is not empty but thick, a blanket laid over all sound. The only noises are the soft *plink* of water droplets and the occasional groan of wood contracting in the cool of evening. No birds call, no insects hum; even the wind seems muted, as though the air itself has grown tired of moving. The vegetation, what little there is, grows in stubborn patches: clumps of reedy grass near the pools, thorny shrubs with leaves like oiled leather, and the occasional stunted tree, its branches twisted into grotesque shapes by the constant moisture and cold nights. Their roots cling to cracks in the stone, drawing sustenance from the thin soil and the condensation that gathers on their leaves like dew.  

The people of the town move slowly, their lives governed by the rhythms of water and stone. They work with hand-forged tools—hoes with blades of bronze, axes with handles of seasoned wood—and their dwellings show the marks of their labor: door hinges of coiled rope, latches carved from antler, and walls patched with clay and straw. At the edge of the settlement, a communal garden of sorts thrives in a series of shallow pits lined with stones, where hardy root vegetables and leafy greens grow in the perpetual twilight beneath the dripping rocks. The people speak little, their voices carrying across the waterlogged terrain like whispers, and even their children play in hushed tones, as though afraid to disturb the fragile equilibrium of the place.  

Despite its sparseness, there is a strange vibrancy to the landscape, as if the very act of existing in such a stark, elemental place has forced life into a kind of stasis. The terraces do not erode further; the water does not drain away. The weeping rocks weep forever, the pools remain full, and the town persists, a quiet knot of human effort against the indifferent grandeur of high, wet stone. It is a place that feels both ancient and unborn, as though it has always been here and yet is still forming itself, drop by drop, breath by breath, in a silence so complete it becomes its own kind of sound.
