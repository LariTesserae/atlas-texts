**Elder Hollow**  

The landscape is a high-altitude basin, nestled in the crook of an ancient mountain range where the air carries a crisp, metallic chill. The elevation—well above the tree line—lends itself to open, wind-scoured horizons, but the hollows and crevices carved by millennia of erosion harbor a sparse, stubborn life. The terrain is a patchwork of stone and earth, shaped into terraces and shallow bowls by time’s patient hand. Here, water does not flow freely but lingers: intermittent springs seep through fissures in the bedrock, pooling into small, mirror-still ponds that dot the landscape like scattered coins. These ponds are rimmed with moisture-loving mosses and hardy sedges, their edges softened by centuries of condensation and the occasional, brief rain that passes through like a ghost.  

The built environment is a cluster of squat, low-slung structures clustered near the largest pond. Constructed from locally quarried stone and timber hauled down from higher slopes, the buildings have the haphazard geometry of a place that grows slowly, one addition at a time. Their roofs are pitched to shed snow, layered with thatch or slate, and their windows—small, deep-set—glow faintly even in daylight, as if the inhabitants prefer the dimness. The streets between them are cobbled with river stones, packed earth, and patches of gravel, worn smooth by generations of footfalls. A network of wooden aqueducts, patched and re-patched, channels water from the springs to cisterns, their surfaces green with algae and the occasional water strider.  

Vegetation here is a study in resilience. Sparse clusters of stunted trees—junipers, perhaps, or a local variant—claw at the thin soil, their roots gripping cracks in the stone. Shrubs huddle in the lee of boulders, their leaves coated in a fine layer of frost even at midday. The ground between is a patchwork of lichen-speckled rock and hard-packed dirt, where wild thyme and saxifrage bloom in brief, tenacious flashes during the short growing season. The flora feels ancient, as though the plants themselves have been worn down by time to their most elemental forms.  

The atmosphere is one of stillness. The air carries the mineral tang of wet stone and the faint, sweet decay of fallen leaves. In the early hours, mist clings to the hollows, blurring the edges of the world until the sun climbs high enough to burn it away. The temperature rarely rises above a cool, persistent discomfort, and the wind—when it comes—is sharp and carrying, whispering through the buildings’ gaps and over the water’s surface.  

Sound is sparse but distinct. The dominant noise is the drip of water into a pond, a metronomic rhythm that underscores longer silences. Wind hums through the eaves of the structures, and occasionally, the creak of old wood or the clink of a bucket against stone punctuates the quiet. Birdcalls are rare but piercing—a raven’s croak, the sharp cry of a hawk riding the thermals above the ridges.  

The fauna is nearly spectral. Tracks in the mud around the ponds suggest small mammals—voles or a stoat—while the air thrums with the buzz of flies or the skitter of beetles. A few hardy insects have made this place their domain, but larger creatures are absent, as if the harshness of the environment or some older imbalance has winnowed them away.  

The rules of this world seem rooted in stasis. The hollow feels eternal, its rhythms unchanging. The springs never run dry, the ponds never overflow, and the erosion has settled into a comfortable equilibrium, shaping the land without hurry. The inhabitants—sparse in number, like the vegetation—move through their days with a deliberate slowness, as if they, too, are carved from the same patient stone. They tend the aqueducts, mend the buildings, and harvest what little grows here, their lives a careful negotiation with the land’s unyielding character.  

At the center of the hollow, beneath a canopy of juniper branches, lies the oldest structure: a circular chamber with walls of uncut stone, its purpose forgotten but its presence commanding. Moss creeps along its base, and the air around it feels colder, as though it holds a fragment of the mountain’s ancient core. This is a place where time pools like water, where the world has narrowed itself to a few essentials: stone, water, life half-wild and half-tended, and the quiet persistence of those who dwell here.
