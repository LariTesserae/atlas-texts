**The Shattered Highlands of Vorthak**  

At the edge of the world, where the sky hangs low and perpetually overcast, the Shattered Highlands rise like the broken spine of an ancient god. Here, elevation is extreme—sheer cliffs plunge thousands of feet into mist-laden valleys, their faces scored by eons of wind and water into terraces and pinnacles that resemble the ribs of some long-dead leviathan. The erosion here is primordial, the rock itself old beyond reckoning, smoothed by countless cycles of freeze and thaw, rain and drought. The land has settled into a final, fractured form, its once-mighty peaks worn down into a maze of jagged ridges and shadowed basins that echo with the silence of deep time.  

The air is cool, tinged with the metallic bite of high altitude, and damp—a contradiction that defines this place. Intermittent moisture clings to everything: condensation beads on rusted machinery, and the rare, sudden rain comes in brief, theatrical bursts that leave behind puddles in the cracks of cobblestone streets or feed the sluggish, silt-choked rivers that carve through the valleys. These waterways are few and far between, their courses dictated by the whims of ancient glaciers that retreated long ago, leaving behind a patchwork of wetlands and dry gullies. The vegetation, though, is lush in defiance of the scarcity. Thick curtains of moss drape the stone walls of abandoned mines, while hardy shrubs with silver leaves cling to cliff faces, their roots drinking from hidden aquifers. Strange, bioluminescent fungi pulse faintly in the darker crevices, casting an eerie, greenish glow that flickers like distant lightning.  

The built environment is a testament to human stubbornness. The town of Vorthak sprawls across a high valley, its structures half-buried in the detritus of the mountains. Terraced buildings of weathered stone and corrugated metal cling to the slopes, connected by rickety bridges and funiculars that groan in the wind. The industrial heart of the settlement hums with the low thrum of steam engines and the occasional clang of metal—forges and mills that process ore from the surrounding peaks, their smokestacks belching plumes that blend seamlessly with the fog. The density here is oppressive: narrow alleys twist between overgrown courtyards, and the air is thick with the smell of coal smoke and the brine of the sea, carried inland by the mist. Yet the town is alive with quiet activity—figures in oilskin coats move like ghosts through the streets, and the rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of machinery provides a metronome to the place.  

But there is something *other* here, something that sets the teeth on edge. The rocks themselves seem to shift slightly at the edges of vision, and the mist carries whispers—not quite voices, but the suggestion of words in a language no living soul remembers. The fauna is rare, but not absent: high above, the silhouette of a *cloud-stalker*—a bird with feathers like tattered parchment—glides silently on updrafts, while in the wetter crevices, translucent salamanders with too many joints skitter between pools of water that should have frozen long ago. The soundscape is mostly quiet, save for the industrial hum and the occasional groan of the earth itself, as if the mountains are shifting in their sleep.  

This is a place where the rules of the world bend slightly, as though the weight of age has made reality porous. The industrial infrastructure is old and jury-rigged, its pipes snaking through caves that were once sacred to forgotten peoples. Some say the machinery runs on more than just coal—that the very breath of the mountains fuels it, their ancient magic slowly being siphoned off by the town’s insatiable hunger for progress. Others whisper of the "Deep Time," a force that pulses beneath the highlands, keeping the land in a state of perpetual, mournful motion.  

In the end, the Shattered Highlands are a place of contrasts: life and decay, industry and wilderness, the mundane and the inexplicable. It is a landscape that breathes slowly, deeply, and without apology, its mysteries as old as the stones themselves.
