The landscape sprawls across an ancient highland plateau, its elevation etched by epochs of wind and water into a place of patient, worn-down grandeur. The terrain here is neither mountain nor valley but something in between—a vast, undulating expanse of gently sloping hills and shallow basins, their forms softened by time until they resemble the ripples of a long-stilled sea. These hills are not of rock or soil, but of *plant-matter*: a living substrate so thoroughly colonized that the distinction between geology and biology has dissolved. The ground itself is a thick mat of interwoven roots, mosses, and fungal threads, spongy and resilient, through which water seeps continuously. Weeping rocks—if they can still be called rocks—are clusters of mineral deposits encrusted with colonies of moisture-hoarding lichens, their surfaces dripping with condensation even in still air. The atmosphere hangs heavy and warm, a humid shroud that condenses into fine, persistent mist, muting the light and lending the world a perpetual, silvery dimness.  

Vegetation here is absolute. Trees do not grow *from* the land but *are* the land, their trunks and branches fused into colossal, organic architectures that dwarf any forest of the living world. These plant-bones arch overhead, their bark etched with veins of symbiotic moss that pulse faintly with bioluminescent rhythms, casting a ghostly glow that battles the gloom. Vines drape in thick curtains, their leaves broad and waxy, channeling rainwater into hollow stems that act as natural aqueducts. Pools of standing water collect in the crooks of roots or the hollows of ancient, petrified tree-stumps, their surfaces dappled with floating mats of algae that shift and breathe like living things. The plant-life is not passive; it moves with glacial intent, tendrils slowly reaching, leaves adjusting angles to harvest light, entire groves of saplings emerging incrementally from the substrate over decades.  

The warmth is constant but mild, a baseline heat that lingers even in the absence of direct sunlight. Mist curls through the understory, carrying the scent of petrichor and something sweetly fungal. Here and there, the mist parts to reveal the remnants of older structures—crumbling stone platforms draped in liana, their edges softened by root-prisms that have grown through them like stitches. These are the only hints of the "scattered" built environment, their purpose long forgotten, their materials overgrown into the ecosystem. Technology is absent, but the land itself feels engineered, as if shaped by a slow, vegetative intelligence.  

The fauna is abundant yet elusive. Shadows flit between trunks—six-legged herbivores with carapaces like bark, their calls a subsonic hum that vibrates through the ground. In the mist, something like a jellyfish drifts, its translucent bell pulsing with captured bioluminescence, trailing stinging tentacles that prune the lower leaves of trees. The air thrums with the quiet industry of pollination: winged creatures the size of sparrows, their bodies dusted with pollen, moving between flowers that bloom only at specific intervals, their cycles spanning years.  

What is *strange* here is the sense of a world that has settled into its final form—a primordial stasis where growth and decay are indistinguishable. The mist carries spores that drift for days, some of which germinate into new life, others of which calcify into dust. Time feels thick, viscous. A fallen log may take centuries to decompose, only to reanimate as a new organism entirely. The erosion here is not destruction but transformation, a reworking of matter into ever-different configurations of life.  

Sound is rare but significant: the occasional groan of a shifting tree-trunk, the rhythmic drip of water into hidden reservoirs, the low thrumming of the jellyfish-creatures. It is a landscape of patient rhythms, where the only drama is the slow, inexorable creep of vegetation claiming what remains unclaimed, and the mist forever redepositing itself as dew.
