**The Hollow Chorus**  

Beneath a bruised, light-swallowing sky, the landscape stretches—an endless, geometric plain of artificial origin, its ground a patchwork of weathered materials: fused basalts, rusted metals, and something like concrete that has been pitted and eroded into swirling, ancient patterns. This is not a natural world. Every inch has been shaped, hammered, or grown from the detritus of an industrial age that long outlived its creators. The terrain lies low, barely rising above the level of the blackwater pools that dot it like shattered mirrors, their surfaces mottled with oil-slick rainbows and skittering with the reflections of distant smokestacks. A perpetual chill clings to the air, born not of season but of the ceaseless exhalations of the great machines—steam, condensation, and the breath of engines that never cease.  

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps from the joints of colossal pipes that snake across the land, their riveted skins corroded to a verdigris green. Elsewhere, the ground itself seems to sweat, the porous basalt exhaling mist that congeals into puddles, feeding the sparse, mutated vegetation that clings to life here. These plants are industrial hybrids—spidery metal fronds that collect dew and drip it onto crystal roots, and clusters of blackened reeds that grow in the pooled water, their leaves chiming like glass when stirred by the wind. Their growth is sparse, stunted by the chemical tang of the air, yet they persist, tenacious as rust.  

The built environment utterly dominates. There are no natural substrates left—only layers of human artifice stacked like sedimentary rock. Towering smokestacks rise in crooked ranks, their bases half-swallowed by the black basalt, exhaling plumes that merge with the gloom above. Factories, vast and windowless, loom in the distance, their walls ribbed with pipes and studded with valves that throb rhythmically, releasing pressurized steam in hissing bursts. The ground here is a mosaic of grates, conveyor belts (now motionless but still warm to the touch), and pits that glow with an eldritch light—residual energy from whatever processes once thrived here.  

The darkness is absolute, save for the industrial glow: the red eyes of warning lights, the pale flicker of electrical arcs, and the oily sheen of puddles that reflect the machinery’s eerie luminescence. Sound is the landscape’s defining feature—a ceaseless, deafening cacophony of grinding gears, rattling chains, and the deep, resonant moan of pressurized steam escaping through valves. It is a symphony of decay and persistence, as though the machines themselves are singing a dirge for a world that has moved on, yet their labor continues unabated.  

There is no animal life. Only the machines move, and even they seem to act of their own volition, cycling through operations that no longer serve any discernible purpose. Conveyor belts turn without cargo, pistons pump air into rust-furred bellows, and great wheels grind against one another in ritualistic repetition. The dynamic here is a clockwork ecosystem, where energy is expended not for survival but because the mechanisms have forgotten how to stop.  

And the weirdness—what can only be described as the *wrongness*—lurks in the details. The black reeds, when grasped, feel like wire gauze. The clouds above are not clouds but colloidal suspensions of soot and vapor, coalescing into shapes that almost resemble forgotten cities. Some of the standing pipes hum in harmonic frequencies that make the teeth ache, and the mist carries whispers—fragments of voices from the voices that once operated the machines, now trapped in the condensation like fossils in amber. The erosion has rendered the landscape into something primordial, yet every surface bears tool marks, rivets, or the patina of industrial use, as if the earth itself has been hollowed out and refilled with the bones of civilization.  

This is a world at the end of its life cycle, but not dead. It is a husk, animated by the momentum of its own making, a vast artifact where the processes of creation and destruction have fused into a single, eternal churn. The darkness, the noise, the wet and the artificiality all conspire to make it feel like a dream of the future remembered too late.
