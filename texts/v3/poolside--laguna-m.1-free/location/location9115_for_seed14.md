**The Abyssal Hollows**  

The landscape stretches as an endless, lightless trench, its walls carved by eons into smooth, concave curves that swallow sound and starlight alike. Here, the water is a roiling mass of ancient cold—brine and sediment churned by thermal vents whose pulses send shuddering ripples through the dark. The trench itself is not a scar but a *presence*, a wound in the seabed older than memory, its contours softened by millennia of relentless current. The floor is a mosaic of silt and shattered basalt, packed so tightly that rare, hardy organisms cling like barnacles to the few exposed surfaces: clusters of *iceworm moss* (a pale, filamentous growth that thrums with bioluminescent veins) and colonies of *stone coral*, their calcified branches forming a labyrinth of bone-white spires.  

The water here is not merely liquid but a living texture—dense, almost syrupy, through which movement feels both weightless and labored. It is a medium of constant flux, dragged this way and that by unseen forces: the groaning of tectonic plates, the ghostly migrations of deep-current gyres, the eruption of geothermal bubbles that hiss and pop like dying stars. The turbulence is primordial, a chaos that has raged since the world’s first seas, never settling into stillness.  

Vegetation is scarce but defiant. In the trench’s central chasm, where sunlight is but a myth, *shadow kelp* drifts in slow-motion forests, their fronds translucent and veined with silver, filtering chemosynthetic bacteria from the water. They grow in patches, sparse but deliberate, as if testing the limits of endurance. The scarcity of other life is mirrored in the silence—only the occasional creak of shifting rock or the distant thrum of a migrating leviathan (a creature so rare it is more legend than flesh) disturbs the quiet.  

The cold is merciless, a creeping numbness that seeps into bone and sinew. Ice forms and reforms in transient sculptures: stalactites of frozen brine drip from the trench’s ceiling, while the floor is mottled with patches of methane clathrate that hiss when disturbed. These formations are ancient, their surfaces etched by time into glassy smoothness, and they crowd the landscape like tombstones in a graveyard of stone and ice.  

There are no structures of man or machine here—only the raw architecture of erosion. Yet the density of the place is oppressive, as if the trench itself inhales and exhales with the rhythm of its own deep time. Pockets of warmth from volcanic activity bubble up in chaotic bursts, creating fleeting oases where extremophile microbes bloom in neon swirls. These moments of life are brief, snuffed out as quickly as they arise, leaving behind only the memory of heat and a lingering turbulence that reshapes the trench anew.  

The rules of this world are simple: nothing remains fixed. The land (or rather, the *seafloor*) is a palimpsest, written and rewritten by currents, heat, and unseen pressures. To dwell here is to be part of a slow, ceaseless churn, a witness to the final settling of a world that has forgotten the concept of permanence.  

In its heart, the trench narrows to a fissure so tight that even water struggles to pass, and here, if one listens past the static of pressure and time, there is a sound like whispering—a language older than sound, spoken by the hollows themselves.
