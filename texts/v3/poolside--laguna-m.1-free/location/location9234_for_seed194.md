**The Verdant Hollow**  

The Verdant Hollow exists in the dark. Not the total absence of light, but a slow, syrupy darkness that clings to every surface, as though the very air has soaked up illumination. Here, at the heart of an ancient limestone basin—once a seabed, now a cathedral of stone—the world has been hollowed into a vast, low-ceilinged chamber. The ceiling arches high above, its surface smooth and glassy, pocked with time-sculpted hollows where mineral-rich drips have long since ceased. The floor slopes gently, worn flat by eons of windless rain and settling dust, a topography so ancient that even the memory of upheaval has eroded away.  

Vegetation is the land here. Towering, ribbed stalks of *xerophyta* rise like the ribs of drowned leviathans, their surfaces armored in overlapping plates of crimson-brown bark. These are not trees but the skeletons of plants, their roots threading through the limestone in vast, interconnected networks, their aboveground forms twisted into the shapes of buildings: arches, spires, and walls that exhale a faint, fungal glow to push back the dark. The plants here have no need for water—they draw sustenance from the stone itself, metabolizing calcium carbonate and trace minerals through symbiotic microbes. Their leaves are thin, translucent membranes that flutter in the faint drafts funneled through fissures in the rock, producing a sound like distant wind chimes.  

The settlements of the Hollow are grown, not built. Communities dwell within the hollowed-out interiors of colossal *xerophyta* trunks, their walls lined with the plant’s fibrous pulp, which hardens into a material akin to cured leather. Tools are carved from the plant’s dense heartwood, which fractures to reveal a core of crystalline cellulose, sharp enough to slice stone. Fires are rare, fueled by the resinous sap of the *xerophyta*, burned in stone braziers to ward off the chill of the dark. The temperature here hovers just above freezing, the warmth of the settlements a fragile thing sustained by the metabolic heat of closely packed plant roots.  

Strange things move in the periphery. The *xerophyta* here have evolved to propagate not through seeds but by shedding small, root-like fragments that burrow into the stone, seeking cracks and crevices. These fragments twitch and writhe with purpose, guided by some chemical signal, forming trails that resemble slow-motion migrations. Occasionally, the plants emit a loud, resonant hum—a sound felt as much as heard—as their internal networks synchronize. This is the pulse of the Hollow, a rhythmic thrumming that reverberates through the limestone and sets the teeth of the inhabitants on edge.  

Time moves differently here. The erosion that shaped the Hollow has ceased, but the plants grow and decay in geological slow-motion, their lifespans measured in centuries. A fallen *xerophyta* stalk may lie undisturbed for decades, its decomposition feeding the next generation. The urban structures shift imperceptibly over decades: archways sag, walls bloom new growths of root and bark, and pathways become choked with mossy tendrils. The inhabitants speak of the "Great Settling," when all things will finish their work and the Hollow will achieve its final form—whatever that may be.  

Yet there are signs of tension. The *xerophyta* are not entirely benevolent. Their roots sometimes invade homes, creeping through walls, and their sap can cause hallucinations in those who handle it too long. The hum grows louder near certain fissures, where the plant networks meet something buried deep beneath the stone—a thing that twitches, too, and has begun to answer the Hollow’s pulse with its own.  

In this place of darkness and life, where the built and the biological are indistinguishable, the Verdant Hollow breathes slowly, patiently, as though waiting.
