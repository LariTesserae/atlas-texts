**The Weeping Marches of Vorthak**  

The land here is a paradox of wet and withered—a realm where water clings to existence like dew on broken glass, yet life has been scoured into something alien. The terrain undulates in low, ancient folds, erosion having ground the bedrock into a chaotic mosaic of smooth, water-smoothed basins and jagged, frost-rimed spires. These formations are not mere rock but a composite of *weeping minerals*: porous stone that exudes slow, viscous streams of brine, ammonia, and liquid mercury in shades of iridescent green and black. The ground crunches underfoot with a sound like shattered quartz, though no crystal grows here—only the calcified husks of long-dead microbial colonies, their silica skeletons forming a brittle crust that splits and reforms nightly as the mercury seeps upward.  

The air is dim, lit by a sky choked with soot-gray clouds that filter the sun into a diffuse, sourceless glow. Light bends strangely here, refracted through layers of ice and chemical vapor that hang like fog at knee height. Sound is constant and discordant: the hiss of condensing brine, the clang of submerged metal shifting in unseen depths, and the wet, rhythmic pulsing of the marches themselves—a sound like a heartbeat, or a machine struggling to breathe. Somewhere in the distance, a horn bellows, not animal nor mechanical, its tone warping as if played through a throat of liquid metal.  

Here, the rules of biology have been rewritten. The "fauna" are things like the *glasswyrms*, serpentine entities of frozen ammonia and calcium carbonate that burrow through the brine-soaked ground, their bodies leaving trails of crystallized frost. They share the landscape with *chitterclanks*, arthropod-like constructs of corroded iron and sinewy wire that scuttle in packs, harvesting minerals from the weeping stones to fuel their unknown industry. Plant life is absent, but fungal mats—if they are fungi—spread in geometric patterns across the ground, their fruiting bodies glowing with a cold, electric blue light. These growths seem to communicate through bioelectric pulses, creating a low-frequency hum that resonates in the bones.  

The industrial detritus is everywhere yet nowhere: rusted pipes jut from the earth like fossilized trees, their valves twitching open and shut on their own, releasing plumes of steam that freeze midair into fragile, dagger-like icicles. Conveyor belts of unknown purpose snake through the landscape, moving not materials but entire chunks of terrain—some of which are clearly not of this world. A chunk of red sandstone might levitate past, its surface etched with symbols that flicker between languages, while a patch of moss-covered concrete hums with the heat of a dying star trapped in its core.  

Reality here is a sieve. Gravity fluctuates in localized pockets; one step might send you skidding sideways, while the next feels like wading through tar. Time does not flow uniformly—a day might pass in minutes, or a single heartbeat might stretch into an eternity as you watch a puddle of mercury freeze, melt, and freeze again in an endless cycle. The marches themselves seem alive, reshaping their channels overnight according to a logic that might be mathematical, biological, or simply capricious.  

In the distance, the *Maw* opens—a sinkhole where the ground has collapsed into a labyrinth of metallic tunnels and caverns. From it emanates a cacophony of grinding gears and distant, guttural voices speaking in a language of clicks and whirs. Some say the Maw is the source of the chitterclanks; others believe it is the marches’ digestive tract, slowly processing the remnants of a dead world into something new.  

This is a place where endings and beginnings blur. The wetness of the land is not life-giving but clinging, desperate—a final gasp of a hydrological cycle that no longer serves any purpose. The cold is not seasonal but existential, a chill that seeps into thought itself. And the weirdness is not just present but foundational: the landscape is a palimpsest of collapsed realities, where the mundane and the impossible have been stirred together until they are indistinguishable.
