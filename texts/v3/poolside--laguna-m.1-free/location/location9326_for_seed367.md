**The Saltward Terraces of Keth-Mal**  

High above the world’s sterile lowlands, where the air is thin and the light filters through a permanent amber haze, lies a hidden realm: the Saltward Terraces. This is no vast plateau but a pocket-scale anomaly—a cluster of interlinked mesas and terraced gardens carved into the ribcage of an ancient mountain. The erosion here is so extreme, so *settled*, that the landscape feels less like stone and more like petrified time itself. The mesas are flattened by eons of wind and chemical weathering, their edges blurred into one another, as if the entire structure is slowly dissolving into the atmosphere.  

Yet *life* clings here with violent abundance. The water parameter reads 0.3, but the terraces’ lushness—scored at 1.8—is sustained by an underground aquifer of hypersaline water, wicked upward through capillary action in the porous rock. The plants are aliens here: crystalline succulents with translucent leaves that store the brine, their roots threading through mined salt veins. They cluster in terraced gardens, their forms geometric and dense, arranged in concentric rings that spiral down from the summits. The vegetation is a riot of copper and jade hues, thrumming with the soundscapes of an ecosystem pushed to its limits—buzzing, clicking, and the skittering of exoskeletons.  

The people of Keth-Mal live close here, packed into hive-like settlements carved directly into the mesa faces. Their architecture is primitive but cunning: structures of fired clay, woven salt-fibers, and salvaged minerals, packed tightly together to trap warmth in the thin air. The urban density (1.6) means no space is wasted; pathways are narrow slots between buildings, and every surface is utilized—walls are green with climbing vines, rooftops host apiaries, and subterranean chambers hum with bioluminescent fungi cultivated for light. The tech is stone-age at best, but they’ve mastered the terraces’ microclimates, channeling the saline water into aqueducts to feed their gardens.  

The fauna is overwhelming (2.7): beetle-like creatures the size of dogs tunnel through the rock, their carapaces iridescent with stored salt. Flocks of winged rodents, their fur matted with mineral dust, swarm between buildings, their shrieks adding to the din. Smaller still are the “whisper-spiders”—blind predators that navigate via echolocation, their webs strung between the terraces like tripwires. The sound level (1.5) is a constant cacophony: the buzz of wings, the grind of beetle mandibles, the creak of shifting rock, and the ever-present hiss of brine seeping from fissures.  

The light here is dim (1.0), filtered through layers of suspended dust and the translucent vegetation. Shadows stretch long and distorted across the terraces, and the air shimmers with heat rising from the salt flats below. Yet the place is *active* (2.5): seasonal migrations of the winged rodents trigger stampedes through the lower terraces; the beetles periodically breach the surface in waves, swarming over the gardens; and the rock itself shifts subtly, an ancient process of chemical erosion that groans like a living thing.  

What’s normal here is the strangeness of it all: a garden of salt and life, a city in the bones of the world, where the most mundane act—walking to the communal cistern—requires weaving through a thrum of biological noise and the watchful hunger of creatures who’ve never known water but thrive in its absence.
