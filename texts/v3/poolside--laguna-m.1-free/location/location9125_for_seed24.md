**The Saltchill Hollow**  

The air here is still, as if the world has forgotten how to move. The darkness is not absolute—there is a faint, diffuse luminescence filtering through the ground above, like the memory of starlight trapped in stone. It casts everything in shades of charcoal and pale silver, revealing a landscape that feels both ancient and abandoned. The ground slopes gently, a vast expanse of cracked earth and salt-crusted stone, arranged in irregular polygons as though the surface itself had dried and shattered eons ago. Some patches are smooth, polished by time into concave basins that collect the faintest glimmers of light; others are sharp and crumbling, their edges softened by centuries of windless erosion.  

There is no water, but the land remembers it. Skeins of crystalline deposits—halite, perhaps, or something stranger—streak the rocks in delicate, branching patterns, like frozen lightning. These formations catch the faint light and fracture it into prismatic glimmers, casting ghostly rainbow halos on the ground. The soil here is a powder of fine minerals, pale beige and cool to the touch, and it shifts whispersoft when disturbed, as if the land itself is breathing but too tired to move.  

Scattered across this expanse are the remnants of a sparse, tenacious flora. Tough, fibrous plants cling to life in cracks and crevices: stunted shrubs with waxy, gray-green leaves that exude a faint, resinous scent, and low-growing mats of moss-like organisms that drink moisture from the mineral-rich air. Their roots grip the stone in shallow, ropey networks, and they shiver slightly in the faint warmth, though there is no wind. A few clusters of fungal growths—pale, bulbous things that glow faintly amber in the dark—dot the landscape, their surfaces etched with the same geometric patterns as the salt deposits.  

The silence here is profound but not empty. It feels like the pause between heartbeats, the hush of a world holding its breath. Occasionally, there is a subtle sound: the almost inaudible creak of stone settling, or the soft rasp of mineral dust shifting as the ground adjusts to its own slow, internal rhythms. These sounds are so quiet they might be imagined, but the land does not rush to correct the mistake.  

There is no sign of animal life, but the impression of habitation lingers in the arrangement of the stones. Some are stacked in crude cairns, their placement too deliberate to be accidental, while others bear marks—deep scratches or shallow grooves—that suggest they were shaped by hands long gone. Yet the technology here is primitive in the extreme: a few chipped flint tools lie half-buried in the dust, their edges dulled by time, and a single, incomplete structure of packed earth and stone hints at a shelter that never quite became a home.  

The temperature is warm but not oppressive, a steady, mineral-heavy heat that seems to emanate from the ground itself rather than the air. It carries the scent of dry stone and something else—like ozone after a storm, or the metallic tang of old coins. This warmth keeps the environment in a state of perpetual equilibrium: the salt never melts, the plants never grow beyond their stunted forms, and the light never shifts. Time here is a concept that has lost meaning.  

Boundaries exist here, but they are not rigid. The hollow is enclosed by a ring of jagged, blackened rock that rises in a low wall to the east, while the western edge fades into a mist that neither moves nor dissipates. Beyond the mist, the land might continue indefinitely, but the eye cannot tell—nor does it strain to look. There is a sense that this place has always been and will always be, a pocket of stillness folded into a universe that has otherwise moved on.  

In the center of it all stands a single feature: a monolith of glassy obsidian, smooth and featureless, rising ten feet into the air. Its surface is etched with symbols that seem to shift when not directly observed, and at its base lies a circle of burned ash, still warm to the touch. It is unclear who—or what—placed it here, or what purpose it might serve. But it does not intrude on the silence. It simply *is*, like everything else, in its own slow, unchanging way.  

This is a place of patience, of waiting. The saltchill hollow does not invite exploration so much as observation, as though it exists to be puzzled over rather than understood. It is a reliquary of the mundane, a study in the beauty of endurance, and a reminder that even in the absence of water, life—and the marks of those who came before—can persist in the smallest, driest cracks.
