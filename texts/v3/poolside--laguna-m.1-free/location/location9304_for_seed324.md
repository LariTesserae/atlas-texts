**The Vented March of Keth-Morin**  

Deep in the high, shattered plateau of Keth-Morin, where the sky is a slate lid pressed close to the earth, lies a landscape both alien and intimate—a pocket-world of industry drowned in its own making. The elevation here is knife-edged, 2.3 grading into the jagged teeth of the plateau, but the terrain itself is *built*, a lattice of corrugated metal, poured concrete, and riveted steel that climbs like crystallized scaffolding into the mist. The entire place feels *new*, raw, with sharp angles and unfinished edges; erosion here isn’t natural but mechanical, the result of machines carving and reshaping the land to fit their purpose.  

Water is everywhere, but not as lakes or rivers. Instead, it seeps through the fractures in the industrial crust—weeping from pipes, pooling in rivulets along the seams of metal walkways, condensing in the chilled air to slick every surface. The ground is a mosaic of shallow marshes, their waters a gray-green, fed by underground aqueducts that groan under pressure. Steam hisses from fissures, mingling with the air until the entire place feels wrapped in a perpetually damp fog, visibility low, the light dim and diffused through layers of vapor and grime. The temperature is cool, biting, the breath visible in quick puffs, but the heat of the machinery keeps the marshes from freezing—an unholy compromise.  

The vegetation is sparse, a few stunted shrubs that cling stubbornly to the edges of the marsh pools, their leaves dulled by the constant moisture. These are not native plants but survivors of some earlier experiment: hydroponic weeds that grow in the acidic runoff, their roots threading through cracks in the foundations. They are the only organic matter here, and they seem out of place, like forgotten relics.  

Every inch of this landscape is constructed. There is no substrate untouched by human design. The marshes are lined with perforated membranes to regulate water flow; the walkways are suspended on rusted stilts above the muck; the "hills" are actually slag heaps and spoil from an adjacent smelting works. The scale is intimate but oppressive—hidden in the belly of a vast, half-ruined industrial complex that stretches beyond sight. You are never more than a few hundred meters from the next structure, yet the density is sparse, the spaces between machinery and pools yawning and empty, as if the place were designed to echo with sound.  

And it does. The sound here is total, a roaring that drowns thought: the thunder of water turbines in the depths, the grinding of gears in distant shafts, the hiss of steam and the metallic clang of pistons. It is not chaotic but rhythmic, a mechanical heartbeat that pulses through the ground. The noise is so constant that the occasional silence feels like a physical weight.  

Yet for all its industriousness, the place is *dynamic*. Pipes burst and spew, only to be replaced by robotic arms that weld them shut. The marsh waters churn with hidden currents, part of some larger hydraulic system. Lights—flickering, electric—strobe at irregular intervals, casting the fog in jagged shadows. There are no animals here, not even insects, as if the environment has been sterilized, made pure for its singular purpose.  

At its center looms a monolith of rust and steel, a tower that stretches into the mist like a finger pointing accusingly at the sky. Its base is surrounded by a moat of marsh, and from its summit, the deafening sound seems to originate—a vortex of machinery that drives the whole landscape’s frenetic, unfinished existence. This is a place of perpetual becoming, never completed, where the interplay of water, metal, and motion creates something both fragile and relentless.  

You get the sense that Keth-Morin is not a place meant to last. It is a temporary cathedral to industry, a hidden chamber in the plateau’s heart where humanity’s hunger for progress has carved something strange and beautiful from the stone—and where, perhaps, it will eventually drown itself in its own making.
