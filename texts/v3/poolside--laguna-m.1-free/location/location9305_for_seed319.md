**The Weeping Warrens of Keth-Mor**  

High above the world’s breath, where the air clings thin and warm, the landscape of Keth-Mor spreads like a fever dream etched into the bones of the earth. This is a place where the sky presses low, heavy with condensation that drips perpetually from stone and steel alike, turning the highlands into a labyrinth of weeping rock and whispered waterways. The terrain is a collage of contradictions: ancient erosion has carved deep, sinuous gullies into the plateau’s foundation, their walls slick with moisture and striated with rust-colored mineral deposits that bleed into the water below. These channels, known as the *Mourns*, form a network of sluggish streams and stagnant pools, their edges choked with sparse, salt-tolerant vegetation—twisted reeds, black-rooted grasses, and the occasional stunted tree that leans away from the wind like a penitent.  

The built environment here is a testament to stubborn persistence. Keth-Mor’s urban sprawl clings to the plateau’s edges and terraces, its structures an organic accretion of rusted metal, weathered stone, and corrugated iron. Factories with chimneys that belch steam instead of smoke dominate the skyline, their gears grinding in perpetual rhythm—a sound so loud it drowns out even the roar of the Mourns below. Bridges of splintered timber and iron span the gullies, their planks slick with condensation, connecting buildings that sag under the weight of decades. The streets are wide and empty, paved with stones that resonate with each footfall, amplifying the industrial cacophony. Here, the density is low—buildings are spaced far apart, as if the inhabitants feared the proximity of too many voices or the weight of too many eyes.  

The water is both curse and lifeblood. Wells and aqueducts tap into the plateau’s saturated layers, channeling water to the town’s heart, where it powers the great waterwheels and pistons of the forges. But the rocks themselves weep constantly, their surfaces streaked with moisture that gathers in hollows to form temporary pools. These pools are alive with a strange bioluminescence, casting an eerie greenish glow after dusk—a result of centuries of industrial runoff mixing with the marshy soil. The air is thick with humidity, carrying the scent of sulfur and the tang of rust.  

Ecology here is a study in adaptation. The sparse vegetation clings to cracks in the stone, while the fauna consists mainly of hardy insects, blind cave fish in the Mourns’ deeper pools, and the occasional amphibian that thrives in the hybrid environment of industry and marsh. Birds are rare, though their calls echo strangely through the gullies, distorted by the echoing architecture. The slow dynamic of the place means change is measured in generations; the rocks erode grain by grain, the machinery’s rhythms shift imperceptibly, and the town grows incrementally, as if adding pieces to a puzzle that has no final form.  

The rules of this world are dictated by the interplay of water and industry. The weeping rocks are a finite resource, and their slow depletion drives both conflict and innovation. The town’s founders built their homes to channel the moisture, creating a symbiosis between the natural and the mechanical: waterwheels power the forges, which in turn melt ores from the plateau’s depths, while the excess steam feeds the ever-present mist that blankets the lower streets. Yet there is a strangeness here, a creeping weirdness: some say the rocks hum with a low, resonant frequency, a vibration that seeps into the bones of the town and makes the machinery run smoother. Others claim the Mourns are not just waterways but veins, carrying something vital from the plateau’s core to the surface.  

At dusk, the Deafening Hour begins. The factories reach peak activity, their pistons throbbing like mechanical hearts, while the Mourns swell with runoff from the day’s condensation. The sound is overwhelming, a wall of noise that makes conversation impossible and turns the town into a hive of silent, hurried movement. Yet in the chaos, there is a rhythm, a pulse that binds the place together—a testament to life persisting in a world where water, stone, and steel have learned to coexist, however uneasily.  

This is Keth-Mor: a place where the land itself weeps, and the people have learned to listen.
