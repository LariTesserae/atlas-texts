**The Hollows of Keth-Mal**  

The terrain here is a tapestry of weathered stone and rusted metal, where the bones of an ancient industrial civilization jut from the earth like the ribs of a buried leviathan. The ground is a mosaic of cracked basalt slabs and compacted gravel, slick with intermittent moisture—damp patches that never quite dry, fed by condensation dripping from the undersides of half-collapsed structures. The air is cool and still, carrying the metallic tang of old rain and the faint, sweet rot of unseen fungal growths.  

The architecture is a chaotic agglomeration of *urban* sprawl scaled down to grove-like intimacy. Buildings cling to one another in clusters, their materials a blend of corroded steel, porous concrete, and tiles glazed in iridescent mineral deposits. Many structures lean at precarious angles, balanced by the slow, patient work of erosion and time. Vines of silvery fungi drape from their eaves, their tendrils quivering with bioluminescent pulses that cast shifting shadows. The fauna is *overwhelming* but alien—swarms of chitinous, beetle-like creatures skitter through the streets, their carapaces clicking against metal, while larger, eyeless things with too many jointed legs haul themselves through the skeletal frameworks of factories. These beings are neither plant nor animal but something in between, their biology a fusion of organic matter and crystallized mineral.  

The *sound* is constant and cacophonous: the grind of unseen machinery deep in the earth, the hiss of steam escaping fissures, and the skittering of countless legs across stone. Yet this noise exists in a state of *frozen equilibrium*—unchanging, as if the world here has settled into a perpetual moment. The *light* is dim and amber, filtering through layers of grime-coated glass and fungal membranes that diffuse the sun into a perpetual twilight.  

The ecology is built around symbiosis between the industrial remnants and the fungal-biological hybrids. The creatures here farm the fungi, which grow in the damp crevices of machinery, feeding on rust and residual energy. In turn, the fungi excrete a sticky sap that lubricates the joints of the beetle-like fauna, allowing their colonies to maintain the decaying infrastructure. The scarcity of water has sculpted the landscape into rounded, *established* forms, with channels carved by centuries of acidic condensation guiding moisture into underground cisterns.  

The *weirdness* of this place lies in its silence beneath the noise: there is no wind, no seasonal shift, no growth or decay, only the endless maintenance of a system that has forgotten why it exists. The *density* is sparse, the spaces between structures vast and echoing, filled with the hum of dormant engines and the slow drip of water into pools that never overflow. Here, the rules of the world seem to prioritize stasis over life, where industry and biology have merged into a single, eternal loop of function without purpose.  

In the distance, the skeletal remains of a cathedral-sized factory loom, its spires draped in glowing fungal veils, a monument to a civilization that once *manufactured* life itself—and now, in its absence, the land manufactures only more of the same.
