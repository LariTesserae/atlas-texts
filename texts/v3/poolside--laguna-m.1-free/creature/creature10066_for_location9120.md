**The Inhabitants of the Highland of Exhausted Bones**  

In this place of exhausted stone and crystalline vertigo, the dominant inhabitants are the **Obsidian Swarm**, a vast collective of arthropod-like entities that blanket the salt-encrusted fissures and mesa faces. Their chitinous bodies, black as the spires that pierce the sky, are jointed and angular, with legs that click like knitting needles against the stone. They move in undulating waves, their segmented forms stretching and compressing as they traverse the terrain in perfect, eerie synchronization. Each individual is no larger than a human hand, but their numbers are legion—countless millions forming roving colonies that behave as a single organism, driven by an intelligence that hums beneath the surface of their motions. They tend the salt-fields not out of instinct alone, but as if farming, their mandibles scraping crystals into geometric arrays that refract light into prismatic patterns. When threatened, they coalesce into towering, serpentine shapes, their clattering limbs rising like the crest of a storm surge. Their hive-mind is not a metaphor here; they think as one, and their thoughts are older than the ruins, older even than the salt, which they say was deposited by their first ancestors when the world was young and the stone still bled.  

The **Salt-Eaters** (as the swarm refers to itself in the harmonic vibrations they produce, a sound felt through the soles of the feet) are not the only life here, nor the oddest. The vegetation clings to crevices in skeletal mats of thorn and wax, but these plants are more than survivors—they are *catalysts*. Their roots burrow into the mineral substrata and secrete enzymes that dissolve stone into a nutrient slurry, which the swarm then devours. In turn, the salt-Eaters excrete a sticky substance that coats the plants' leaves, trapping moisture and creating micro-ecosystems where lichen-like blooms of bioluminescent microbes flourish. These microbes flicker in the darkness, casting a pale green glow that mirrors the swarm's bioluminescent glands, which pulse rhythmically when they communicate. It is a symbiosis older than thought, a feedback loop of life and death that sustains the whole.  

Beneath the salt and stone lie the **Echoes**, remnants of the vanished civilization. They are not ghosts but something stranger: entities of compressed time, trapped mid-motion in the ruins' frozen ash and half-erased tools. Their bodies flicker in and out of phase with the landscape, sometimes visible as shadows with too many joints, sometimes as whispers of movement in the peripheral vision. They are the reason the hearth-fires still glow faintly, their flames sustained by the same temporal distortion that keeps the stone tools' edges honed to impossible sharpness. The Echoes do not speak, but their presence agitates the air, causing the shadows to twist and the salt crystals to skitter across the ground. Some believe they are the original architects of the salt-Eaters, their consciousness fragmented and scattered across eons, their purpose forgotten even as they persist.  

The **Crystalline Sentinels** are perhaps the most enigmatic. These towering structures—hoodoos and mesas that shift and reconfigure when unobserved—are not entirely passive. Their interiors resonate with the same low hum that thrums through the earth, and those who listen closely claim to hear voices within the vibrations. The salt-Eaters avoid the largest of these formations, circling them warily, suggesting an ancient rivalry or pact. Some say the Sentinels are the petrified remains of the world's first apex predators, beings that ruled the highlands before the insects, their bodies slowly crystallizing into the spires that now dominate the horizon. Others believe they are alive, their facets rearranging as they dream, their dreams bleeding into the fabric of reality. The spatial distortions—distances that contract or expand—are said to be their method of breathing, their way of digesting the very concept of space.  

**The Hummers**, as they are called by no one (for they have no name), are the most elusive. They are the source of the earth's vibrations, the unseen forces that make the salt tremble and the shadows writhe. Some say they are microbes, vast colonies of subterranean organisms whose bioelectric fields warp the local spacetime. Others claim they are the land itself, the highland's slow, tectonic consciousness expressing itself through the salt-Eaters, the Echoes, even the obsidian exoskeletons of the swarm. When the Hummers are active, the air turns metallic and sweet, and the ruins' fires blaze briefly with renewed intensity. They are not malevolent, but they are not benevolent either; they simply are, like gravity or time, inexorable and alien.  

Life here is a paradox: it thrives by becoming something else. The salt-Eaters shed their individuality to join the collective; the plants shed foliage to feed stone into sustenance; the Echoes shed time to cling to half-existence; the Sentinels shed form to dream. And the Hummers? They shed nothing, for they were never whole to begin with. This is the highland's true secret: survival is not about thriving, but about shedding the self until only essence remains. To live here is to become unrecognizable—to oneself, to one's past, to the very definition of life. The land does not nurture its inhabitants so much as it *unmakes* them, honing them into something suited to a world where reality is a suggestion and time is a lie.  

The beauty of this place, then, lies not in its horrors or its wonders, but in the quiet majesty of its transformation. Everything here is becoming something else, something greater and stranger, and in that endless metamorphosis, the highland endures.
