The people of this terraced realm are a people of quiet resilience, their lives woven into the very fabric of the land’s eternal drip and stone. They are not numerous—perhaps a few hundred souls—but each carries the weight of their environment in their movements, their tools, and their unspoken understanding of the place’s fragile equilibrium.  

### Physicality and Adaptation  
Their bodies bear the marks of constant moisture: skin taut and weathered, like leather tanned by mist and cold. Hair is often cropped short or braided tightly to avoid the perpetual damp, and their garments—layers of woven grass, bark, and treated animal hide—are designed to shed water and trap warmth. Many have developed a peculiar gait, cautious and deliberate, as if navigating the slick terraces has taught them to move with the patience of the dripping rocks themselves. Their hands are broad and calloused, adapted to gripping bronze tools or shaping stone, and their eyes hold a sharp, reflective clarity, as if they’ve learned to read the land’s subtle shifts in light and water flow.  

Some among them bear other marks: faint bioluminescent patches on their forearms or necks, a byproduct of symbiotic fungi cultivated in their gardens, which they use to light their homes on the cloudiest days. Others have teeth stained green from chewing on a minty herb that grows in the mossy crevices, a practice believed to ward off the respiratory ailments that cling to the high, wet air.  

### Culture and Society  
The settlement operates on principles of communal stewardship. Decisions are made by a council of elders who claim descent from the “First Carvers”—mythic figures said to have split the terraces from the living rock with songs. These elders wear cloaks of woven reeds and carry staffs topped with crystals that chime softly in the breeze, a symbol of their role in maintaining the land’s “breath.”  

Work is divided by the rhythm of water: seasons are dictated not by sun or moon but by the flow of the weeping rocks. During the “High Drip” (when the rocks’ secretions swell into streams), the people channel water into the aqueduct, repair erosion, and harvest the algae that blooms in the pools. During the “Low Drip,” they retreat indoors to forge metal, spin fibers, or engage in the oral histories that anchor their identity.  

Their language is a series of clicks, whistles, and sign gestures, evolved to avoid disturbing the delicate acoustics of the terraces. Over centuries, they’ve learned that loud noises cause the stone to shudder—or so the stories say—and that such disruptions can lead to cracks, landslides, or worse: the rocks going silent altogether. Children are taught to “listen with their feet,” feeling vibrations through the flagstones to navigate the settlement’s maze of paths.  

### Spirituality and Belief  
The people worship the *Neth*, a pantheon of spirits believed to inhabit the weeping rocks and the pools. Offerings of woven moss, crushed minerals, and small bronze figurines are left at the base of the grandest stones. During the annual “Convergence,” when the clouds hang lowest and the rain falls in silver needles, they gather to perform the *Rite of the Unbroken Cycle*, a ritual meant to ensure the terraces do not erode and the springs do not run dry.  

They believe the land is alive, a slumbering entity whose heartbeat is the drip of water and the creak of wood. To them, the settlement is both a wound and a bandage—a scar where life persists in defiance of the stone’s indifference. This duality shapes their art, which takes the form of intricate mosaics made from colored pebbles and algae, depicting the eternal struggle between water and rock.  

### Unique Practices  
- **Moss-Weaving:** A sacred craft where moss is cultivated in geometric patterns to stabilize soil and create living insulation for their homes. Masters of this art can “read” the health of the land by the moss’s color and texture.  
- **Stone Singing:** A meditative practice where individuals press their palms to the weeping rocks and hum in low, resonant tones, believing this encourages the stones to release more water. The practice is both practical (it mimics the sound of flowing water, which the rocks seem to respond to) and spiritual (a form of communion).  
- **Root-Diving:** During the High Drip, some brave the flooded lower terraces to harvest root vegetables from the communal pits. They wear masks of woven reeds and carry torches to ward off the “shadows” that lurk in the deeper pools—undulating, translucent beings that the people believe are the ghosts of drowned travelers.  

### The Unspoken Fear  
Beneath their rituals and adaptations lies a deeper dread: the “Stillness.” It is said that if the weeping rocks ever cease their drip, the terraces will collapse into the plateau below, and the settlement will be erased. The people interpret every drought, every patch of dry stone, as an omen. Their children are named after water phenomena—“Rivulet,” “Pool,” “Dew”—as prayers for the land’s continuation.  

In this place, life is not so much sustained as *coaxed*—a daily negotiation between humanity and the indifferent grandeur of high, wet stone. The people are its midwives, its guardians, and perhaps, its first and last inhabitants.
