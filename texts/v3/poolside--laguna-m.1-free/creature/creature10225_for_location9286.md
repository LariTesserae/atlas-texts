**The Inhants of Threshold: The Resonant Folk**  

The people of Threshold are a reclusive, slow-lived species known as the *Resonant Folk*, a name derived from their unique relationship with the cavern’s acoustic properties and their role as caretakers of its rhythms. They are neither fully human nor entirely separate from the Hollows’ ecosystem; they exist as a sort of biological bridge between the living and the ancient stone, their bodies and culture shaped by millennia of co-evolution with the caverns.  

### Physiology & Adaptation  
The Resonant Folk stand at an average height of five to six feet, their forms lithe and sinewy, with joints that bend in ways both fluid and angular—like tree roots shifting through soil. Their skin is a pale, porous gray, resembling weathered limestone, and is laced with faint, lumenfern-like veins that pulse faintly in response to emotional or environmental stimuli. These veins store trace minerals from the air and water, which are then shared symbiotically with the lumenferns, creating a mutualistic relationship that sustains both species.  

Their most striking features are their eyes: large, lidless, and silver-flecked with melanin deposits that filter the dim light, granting them exceptional vision in low-light conditions. Beneath their skin, they possess specialized organs that detect seismic and vibrational cues, allowing them to "listen" to the stone itself—a sense essential for navigation in a realm where sound can disorient.  

Their voices are a haunting, layered resonance, capable of mimicking the harmonic tones of the caverns’ "stone songs." They communicate through a combination of speech, rhythmic tapping on rock surfaces, and sustained notes that can travel vast distances. Children are taught to sing in harmonics that align with the cavern’s natural frequencies, a practice that strengthens the bedrock and prevents structural collapses over time.  

### Culture & Society  
Threshold’s society is organized around the principles of *slow craftsmanship* and *acoustic stewardship*. Entire generations dedicate themselves to single projects: forging a bridge of woven roots, cultivating a grove of stonebark trees into a new dwelling, or training a chorus to harmonize with a newly discovered "stone song." Time is not measured in years but in "growth cycles"—periods defined by the slow expansion of a root structure or the migration of a stoneback herd.  

Their settlements are living things, constantly reshaped by fungal networks and root systems guided by the Resonant Folk. Homes are grown more than built: hollows in stonebark trees are expanded via controlled fungal decomposition, while lumenfern gardens are pruned to channel light into specific patterns that mark pathways or sacred spaces. Communal areas are centered around "Resonance Pits"—natural amphitheaters where the Folk gather to sing, their voices blending with the cavern’s hum to meditate on the collective memory of the stone.  

The Folk revere the spherical water pools—called *mirage pools*—as omens of change. They believe these ephemeral pools form when the caverns "dream," and their disappearance signals an upcoming shift in the geology or ecosystem. Before a pool vanishes, the Folk will perform a ritual called the *Weeping*, in which they chant in unison to "mourn the dreamer’s passing" and recalibrate their own acoustic focus to the new landscape.  

### Relationship with Fauna  
The Resonant Folk maintain a tenuous peace with the caverns’ predators, particularly the shadowstalkers. They avoid direct conflict, instead using their knowledge of echolocation to redirect these creatures away from settled areas. Shriekmoths are cultivated in vast hives near the outskirts of Threshold— their ultrasonic clicks are harvested to power "sound mirrors," reflective devices that amplify and harmonize the cavern’s natural frequencies.  

Stonebacks are treated with reverence. Their migrations are followed closely, and their molted shell fragments are collected to reinforce the cavern’s structural integrity. The Folk believe the stonebacks carry the memories of the deep aquifers within their shells, and their slow, deliberate movements are seen as a form of prayer.  

Xerophytic forests are tended like gardens. Glasscreepers are pruned to create natural light shows during ceremonial nights, and their prismatic flashes guide travelers through the labyrinthine tunnels. The hollow interiors of ancient stonebark trees are often repurposed as libraries or burial chambers, their fungal colonies carefully curated to preserve organic records etched into bark or stored in crystalline dew.  

### The Oddities They Embrace  
The Resonant Folk are unbothered by the Hollows’ anomalies. They consider the lumenferns’ shyness to be a form of consent, ensuring no human interference disrupts their growth. The spherical pools are studied obsessively; each disappearance prompts a "Reckoning," where the community collectively reevaluates their place in the caverns.  

Perhaps most mysteriously, the Folk believe they are not the original inhabitants of Threshold. They speak of the "First Chorus"—a lost civilization that once shaped the caverns through song, their voices so powerful they could liquefy stone or coax life from barren rock. The Resonant Folk see themselves as inheritors of this legacy, striving to maintain the balance until the First Chorus returns.  

In the Echoing Hollows, the Resonant Folk live as both architects and inhabitants of a living relic, their existence a quiet testament to the idea that even in decay, there is a profound, resonant beauty.
