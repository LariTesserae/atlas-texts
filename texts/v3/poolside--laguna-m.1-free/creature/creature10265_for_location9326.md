The Saltward Terraces of Keth-Mal are home to beings as strange and resilient as the land itself, shaped by millennia of salt winds and the slow, chemical decay of stone. The people of Keth-Mal are squat and sinewy, their bodies adapted to the thin, mineral-rich air. Their skin is thickened and slightly translucent, a side effect of generations spent in close proximity to the brine-storing plants, which have seeped their essence into the population through both diet and osmosis. Their eyes are large and reflective, capable of capturing every photon in the amber-filtered gloom, while their lungs are dense and efficient, extracting oxygen from the dusty haze in slow, deliberate breaths. They move with a cautious grace, having evolved to navigate the terraces’ precarious heights and the ever-shifting rock beneath their feet.  

Their society is a patchwork of communal labor and rigid ecological stewardship. Each terrace-dweller belongs to a *veth*—a kinship cluster responsible for maintaining a specific ring of the gardens. The veths rotate their tasks seasonally: some tend the crystalline succulents, pruning their geometric forms to optimize brine retention; others harvest the iridescent beetle larvae that burrow in the salt veins, their carcasses processed into the fibrous materials used for clothing and rope. The eldest of each veth serve as *thren-handlers*, interpreting the groans of the mountain itself—subtle shifts in the rock that signal which terraces are safe to inhabit or farm. These voices of the mountain are sacred; to ignore them is to risk being swallowed by the slow collapse of the mesas.  

The terraces’ soundscape shapes their culture as much as their architecture. The whisper-spiders, with their webs strung like tripwires between buildings, are both feared and revered. Children are taught to move in silence during their first migrations, lest they trigger a predator’s hunt. The beetles, when they breach the surface in their cyclical swarms, are heralded with drumbeats and chants to “shepherd” them away from crops. The winged rodents, though pests, are also a source of meat and fur, and their migrations dictate the rhythm of the year. During the “Great Skitter” in autumn, entire veths retreat to their deepest chambers, sealing entrances with salt-fiber mats while the rodents flood the lower terraces.  

Their technology, while primitive, is a testament to their ingenuity. They’ve learned to coax moisture from the brine using woven reed filters and clay pots glazed with powdered quartz. The bioluminescent fungi they cultivate in their underground chambers emit a faint blue-green light, which they believe holds the souls of their ancestors. Art is etched into the salt itself, using shards of obsidian to carve symbols that shift with the wind—a language meant to communicate with the mountain’s spirit.  

Yet perhaps the most alien among them are the *keth-mal*—a caste of individuals born with a rare condition where their cells have absorbed so much salt that their bodies exude a fine, crystalline dust. These people are both ostracized and venerated, as their presence accelerates the erosion of the terraces. They live on the outermost edges, where the rock is thin and the salt flats loom below. Some say they are the mountain’s immune system, slowly dissolving it to make way for new life. Others claim they are cursed, their existence a reminder that even Keth-Mal’s strange paradise is temporary, a flicker of green in a world destined to return to dust.  

In this place where salt and life war and waltz, the people of Keth-Mal are neither conquerors nor victims—they are collaborators in the slow unraveling of stone, their existence a hymn to survival in a world that should not support them.
