The inhabitants of Elder Hollow are a people shaped by the same unyielding patience that carved the basin itself. They are few—scarcely more than a few dozen souls—who have learned to live within the rhythm of stone and water, their lives pressed close to the earth like the hardy saxifrage that clings to cracks in the rock. They call themselves the *Thorne*, a name whispered as if it were both a relic and a secret, though its etymology has long since faded into the mists of memory.  

### **Physique and Presence**  
The Thorne are a wiry folk, their bodies lean and sinewy from years of labor in the thin air and relentless wind. Their skin is tanned to a bronze hue by the high-altitude sun, but their hands are perpetually stained with the dust of stone and moss. They move with a careful, deliberate grace, as though each step is measured against the risk of disturbing the land’s fragile equilibrium. Their eyes are pale, almost translucent in the dim light of their homes, adapted to the half-shadows they dwell within. Many bear the pale scars of frostbite or the faint, silvery tracery of veins visible against their parchment-thin skin—a mark of those who’ve lived too long in the cold.  

Their clothing is practical yet strange: layered tunics and trousers of tightly woven wool from the few hardy mountain goats they herd, dyed in muted earth tones. Over these, they wear cloaks of woven rushes and dried sedges, which rustle faintly like leaves even in still air. Their heads are often wrapped in hoods or caps of felted wool, not just for warmth but to filter the metallic tang of the air, which some say dulls the mind if breathed too freely.  

### **Culture and Custom**  
The Thorne are a people of rituals, though they would not call them that. Their lives are governed by the cycles of water and weather, and they’ve built their society around the slow, deliberate maintenance of the aqueducts and ponds. Each spring, they gather to patch the wooden channels with resin and moss, a task that takes weeks and is accompanied by low chanting—harmonic hums that vibrate in the chest, said to "speak to the stone" and ensure the springs don’t falter.  

They rarely speak above a whisper, as if the wind might carry their words away or the mountains themselves might overhear. Their language is a series of clicks, sighs, and half-sung syllables, a dialect that evolved to carry across distances without disturbing the silence. They communicate as much through gesture and expression as through sound, their faces adept at conveying nuance in the flicker of an eyelid or the tilt of a head.  

Their dwellings, too, are an extension of their ethos. The low-slung buildings are not just practical but spiritual; the Thorne believe that huddling close to the earth keeps them "rooted" in the land’s ancient ways. Windows are kept small and curtained with translucent sheets of treated hide, allowing in only the faintest light. Inside, the homes are lit by tallow candles fed by the fat of mountain birds, their flames guttering in the drafts that slip through the gaps in the stone.  

### **The Central Chamber**  
At the heart of the hollow lies the oldest structure, the forgotten circular chamber. The Thorne avoid it, yet they tend it with a reverence bordering on fear. Once a month, the eldest among them—the *Moss-Touched*, so named for the green-stained fingers they’ve grown from years of handling the chamber’s moss—crawls inside to clear debris and replace the offerings left at its threshold: small bowls of spring water, sprigs of juniper, and carved figures of animals made from pebbles.  

The chamber is cold, far colder than the surrounding air, and those who enter emerge shivering and dazed, speaking of whispers in the stone. The Thorne believe it is a remnant of an older world, a place where the mountain’s "bones" still remember the heat of creation. They say time flows differently inside, that a single hour there could be a day in the outside world—or vice versa. No one knows who built it, only that it has always been, as eternal as the peaks themselves.  

### **Relationship with the Land**  
The Thorne are not farmers but tenders, coaxing life from the cracks in the stone without upsetting the balance. They harvest the few plants that grow—wild thyme, saxifrage, and a bitter herb they call *shadowmint*—whose roots they believe draw strength from the mountain’s core. They do not hunt, relying instead on their goats and the occasional stoat or vole caught in snares. Their tools are simple: stone blades, bone needles, and wooden plows pulled by oxen whose breath mists in the air.  

They are deeply superstitious about the fauna, which they call "the Forgotten." The flies and beetles are tolerated as part of the land’s breath, but larger animals are viewed with wary respect, thought to be ghosts of a time before the hollow’s stillness. When a raven croaks or a hawk circles overhead, the Thorne fall silent, crossing their arms over their chests in a gesture of appeasement.  

### **The Quiet Persistence**  
Life here is a negotiation, a daily act of survival that has been refined over generations. The Thorne do not age as others do; their faces show the wear of years, but their bodies remain taut and capable, as if the mountain’s energy sustains them. They rarely fall ill, though when they do, they retreat to the chamber, believing the cold will "still the fever." Death is rare, but when it comes, the deceased are wrapped in rush mats and placed in crevasses high in the peaks, left to return to the stone from which they came.  

They do not write, relying instead on memory and oral tradition, passed down through a hierarchy of "Keepers" who memorize the stories of the hollow. These tales speak of a time before the ponds, when the land was a desert of stone, and of the first Thorne, who bargained with the mountain for water and moss. Whether these stories are true or not, they are all that binds the people together, a thread of identity in a place where time pools like the ponds themselves.  

In Elder Hollow, the Thorne exist as both wardens and prisoners, their lives a testament to the resilience of life in its most elemental form. They are the hollow’s memory, its breath, and its stubborn refusal to fade.
