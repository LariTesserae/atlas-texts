The inhabitants of Thorne Hollow are a people shaped by the land’s contradictions—its raw, unfinished geology and its languid, almost sentient rhythms. They are neither fully adapted nor entirely at odds with their environment, but have instead become a part of the highlands’ slow, liquid dance, moving with the tides of stone and water as if they too were elements of the mountain’s ongoing creation.  

**Physical Traits and Adaptations**  
The Hollowfolk, as they call themselves, are a wiry, resilient breed, their bodies honed by necessity. Their skin is pale but elastic, toughened by constant exposure to moisture and the mineral-rich air. The most striking feature among their young is the prevalence of webbed fingers and toes, an evolution the adults attribute to both the omnipresent water and the faint, bioluminescent properties of the moss that blankets the stones. These webbings are not mere curiosities—they confer a practical advantage in navigating the slick terrain, letting the Hollowfolk scramble along wet rock faces and wade through the pools with the surety of mountain goats.  

Their eyes are often a pale, glassy gray, adjusted to the mist and the dim, greenish light that filters through the canopy. Many adults have a faint, silvery patch of moss that grows in their hair, a symbiosis the locals consider a mark of good fortune. Their hands are calloused and stained with clay and lichen, tools of both construction and cultivation.  

**Culture and Daily Life**  
Life in Thorne Hollow moves to the pulse of the weeping rocks. The Hollowfolk are patient, methodical, and deeply attuned to the land’s slow shifts. Their settlement is a cluster of terraces and hollowed-out caves, connected by paths that wind like arteries between the stones. They build their homes to accommodate the water’s whims: walls are left partially open to allow seepage, and roofs curve like upturned shells to channel rain into cisterns. Tools are simple but effective—stone hammers and chisels for shaping the emergent rock, woven reed baskets for gathering the glowing moss, and clay pots fired in sunken hearths that mimic the warmth of the earth.  

The Hollowfolk’s relationship with the singing stone at the hollow’s heart is one of wary reverence. They avoid striking it, believing its hum to be the mountain’s heartbeat, a sound that syncs with the rumblings deep underground. Instead, they leave small offerings of white stones and bird bones at its base, hoping to appease whatever consciousness stirs within.  

Time, as experienced here, is elastic. While a day may pass in what feels like a week outside, the Hollowfolk do not mark time in hours or seasons but in “drip cycles”—the slow accumulation of water in their cisterns, the growth of a fern frond, the migration of the translucent moths. This has bred a culture of meticulous observation and long-term planning. They speak of events in terms of decades, their oral histories stretching back generations like the roots of the stunted trees.  

**Beliefs and Rituals**  
The Hollowfolk’s worldview revolves around the mountain as a living, dreaming entity. Their myths tell of the stone as an ancient being midway through a long metamorphosis, its weeping a sign of growing pains. When the great thaws come—those rare decades when meltwater reshapes the terrain—they hold the *Rite of Shifting*, a night-long vigil where the community rebuilds paths and channels while chanting low, resonant hymns meant to harmonize with the ringing tones of the singing stone.  

The phosphorescent mist is seen as a bridge between the physical and the ethereal. The Hollowfolk believe its glowing tendrils carry whispers from the “old sky,” the constellations reflected in the pools. Diviners interpret these visions to predict changes in the land’s behavior, such as where the next rockslide might occur or where new springs will burst forth.  

Children born with webbed hands are considered “of the deep,” destined to become caretakers of the underground pools where blind fish dwell. These children undergo a coming-of-age ritual at the age of ten, venturing into the caverns beneath the hollow to “marry” the water—submerging themselves in the pools for a full cycle of drips, a test of endurance and trust.  

**Encounters with the Strange**  
The Hollowfolk have learned to live with the uncanny as a matter of course. The moss’s faint glow is harvested at dusk for light, and the resonance of the singing stone is sometimes used to transmit coded messages across the hollow. When the mist turns phosphorescent, the adults don masks of woven reeds to protect against “star-sickness,” a condition they claim causes hallucinations if one stares too long at the shifting constellations in the pools.  

They trade cautiously with outsiders, who often find the Hollowfolk’s dialect—thick with clicks and whistles mimicking water’s sound—incomprehensible. Gifts are exchanged in silence: the Hollowfolk offer vials of mineral-rich water or chunks of resonant stone, while receiving cloth, salt, or stories from the lowlands.  

**Unique Practices**  
- **Stone Listening**: A ritual where the Hollowfolk press their ears to the weeping rocks to “hear the mountain’s dreams,” seeking omens in the varying tones of drips.  
- **Mistwalking**: Young adults undergo a trial where they spend a night alone in the hollow during a gust, trusting the mist to guide them home—a test of intuition and courage.  
- **The Pooling**: A communal gathering held at the central spring during the phosphorescent mist, where stories are told in hushed tones to honor the “old sky.”  

In Thorne Hollow, to be human is to be a collaborator with the land’s becoming, to read its moods in the drip of stones and the creak of trees. They are neither conquerors nor passive survivors, but custodians of a place that teaches its lessons slowly, in the language of rock and water.
