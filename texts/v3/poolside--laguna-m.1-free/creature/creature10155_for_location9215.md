**The Inhabitants of the Salt-Spires**  

**The People: Keepers of the Spires**  
The people of Keth-Morin are a stoic, weather-carved folk who have learned to exist in the liminal space between endurance and reverence. Their bodies are lean and corded with muscle, adapted to the thin air and relentless sun; their skin is tanned to a leathery bronze, often patterned with faint, silvery scars from salt-crystal burns. They move with deliberate caution, as if sharing the cliffs with the skarath—always aware of the precariousness of their stone-terrace homes. Their eyes are sharp and pale, adjusted to the glare of salt and sun, and their hair is often streaked white with mineral dust, braided into tight plaits or left to hang loose like tumbleweeds.  

Their lives revolve around the slow, sacred rhythm of the spires. Salt-core harvesting is both labor and ritual. Using bone-tipped chisels and woven nets of vine-fiber, they extract the porous heartstones from the spires’ bases, where minerals have crystallized over millennia into dense, glowing cores. These “spikeblooms” are believed to house the spirits of their ancestors—ancient souls who first learned to coax life from the barren stone. The cores are either ground into salt for trade or kept whole as talismans, their faint bioluminescence guiding the people through the perpetual chalky twilight of the Salt Sea Valley.  

The inhabitants’ dwellings are as much a part of the landscape as the spires themselves. Doorways are carved directly into cliff faces, often adorned with petroglyphs depicting the spires’ growth and the skarath’s migrations. Ladders of braided leather and rope connect terraces, swaying gently in the wind like the tails of great stone serpents. At dusk, when the succulents uncurl their leaves to drink the dew, the people emerge from their homes to tend the gardens. They whisper to the plants, a practice passed down from an age when the first settlers discovered the flora’s sensitivity to sound—certain frequencies coax the succulents to open wider, yielding more water. These gardens are not merely sources of sustenance but also sacred spaces, where the people leave offerings of spikeblooms and beetle-wing dust to ensure the plants’ continued growth.  

**The Fauna: Creatures of Crystal and Shadow**  
The skarath are the undisputed rulers of the open terrain, their six legs allowing them to navigate the spires’ jagged faces with an eerie, stilt-legged grace. Their fur is a tumbleweed-like tangle of stiff, salt-encrusted hairs that shimmer with iridescent flecks—a camouflage against both predators and the glare of the spires. Their eyes, large and mirror-bright, reflect the landscape like living prisms, a trait that unnerves outsiders but helps the skarath spot movement in the shifting light. They communicate through low-frequency rumbles that travel through the stone itself, a language the people have learned to interpret. During the warm season, their herds grow restless, their footsteps drumming a frantic rhythm as they migrate to higher altitudes, chasing the last patches of succulent growth before the blooms wither.  

Beneath the surface, the burrowing rodents—known as “stonekin” to the locals—are the unseen architects of the plateau’s ecology. Pale and blind, they navigate by echolocation, their sleek bodies weaving through tunnels lined with discarded spikebloom fragments. They aerate the soil, distributing seeds and nutrients that sustain the gardens above, while their fur carries spores and pollen between succulents. The people leave bowls of saltwater at tunnel entrances during droughts, a pact that keeps the stonekin from gnawing through the terraces’ foundations.  

The crystalline-winged insects, or “glitterflies,” form the most ephemeral yet vital layer of life. Swarming in iridescent clouds, they feed on the salt-rich dew and the nectar of midnight-blooming flowers, their wings scattering light into ghostly rainbows. Their bodies are fragile but their numbers immense; when they die, they calcify into tiny crystals that join the plateau’s mineral bedrock. The people collect their husks to grind into a shimmering powder used to line the inner walls of their homes, a practice said to ward off the “shadow-sickness” that sometimes afflicts those who dwell too long in the canyons’ depths.  

**The Flora: The Plateau’s Pulse**  
The succulents themselves are more than passive survivors—they are active participants in the ecosystem’s balance. Their stems swell and contract with the collection of dew, creating subtle vibrations that the people use to gauge the health of the gardens. Certain species, like the “weeping cactus” (whose translucent sap resembles tears), are believed to house the memories of the land. During the warm season, their blooms release intoxicating resins that attract the glitterflies and lull the skarath into a mating trance, a synchronized event that the people celebrate with drum circles and salt-core fires.  

**The Deeper Life: Unseen Threads**  
In the labyrinthine canyons, where shadows stretch like living things, there are older entities. The people speak of “deep-whiskers”—massive, worm-like creatures that tunnel through the plateau’s bedrock, their movements causing minor tremors that the inhabitants interpret as omens. These beings are rarely seen, but their presence is felt in the way the spires occasionally hum with a subsonic resonance, or how the stonekin’s tunnels sometimes collapse without warning. Whether they are predators, symbionts, or something else entirely, they remain one of the plateau’s greatest mysteries, a reminder that even in this ancient, seemingly static place, life persists in forms beyond easy comprehension.  

In Keth-Morin, all things are intertwined: the people, the spires, the skarath, and the unseen forces that shape the land itself. Here, existence is a slow dance with erosion and growth, a dialogue between ruin and renewal that has endured for eons—and shows no signs of ceasing.
