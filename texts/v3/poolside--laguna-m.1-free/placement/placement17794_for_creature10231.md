I would be the **Ridge-Singer**, one who dwells in the terraced limbo between the town's clamor and the mountains' deep breath. Not quite Vorthaki, not quite Echo-Wanderer, but something that learned to listen to the languages spoken in different mediums.

My home would be a half-ruined observatory carved into a cliff face, accessible only by a bridge that sways like a plucked wire. The structure was built by some forgotten surveyor centuries ago, its brass instruments long since green with corrosion, but the lenses still catch and focus the faint bioluminescence that leaks from the fungal networks below. From here I map not territory, but resonance—the way the industrial thrum harmonizes with the geological groans, the rhythm of cloud-stalker wings against the slower pulse of the mountain's "sleep."

I've lived too long in proximity to the Deep Time, and it shows. My skin has begun to take on the mottled appearance of weathered stone, and when I press my palm against certain rocks, I can feel the faint vibration of the Cog Beneath the World—like touching the spine of some slumbering leviathan. The Vorthaki bring me their questions in hushed tones: why does the eastern forge cough black smoke instead of white? What do the salamanders' erratic migrations portend? I answer by reading the patterns in lichen growth, the way the mist curls around particular outcroppings, the frequency of Echo-Wanderer sightings near the old quarry.

But here's the truth they don't know—I don't interpret these signs so much as I *negotiate* with them. Every week, I descend into the fungal caverns wearing a mask of my own creation, welded from scrap metal and inscribed with symbols that predate the stone circles. The Spore-Mothers recognize me, though they won't speak of it to surface-dwellers. In the deepest chamber, where the Whisperbloom's dreams manifest as visible currents of spore-dust, I make bargains. Coal for continued warmth in the aquifers. Songs for safe passage through unstable tunnels. Memories for... other things.

My body reflects this dual existence. My left hand, which I lost to a cave-in years ago, was replaced with a prosthetic of forged iron and salvaged pipe, grown into my flesh through some alchemical process the Engine-Priests taught me before they vanished entirely. It hums with residual heat and can interface directly with the town's machinery—I've been known to climb into the bellies of stalled steam engines and literally conduct electricity through my metal fingers to restart their hearts.

At night, when the industrial sounds fade to a whisper and the fog rolls in thick as wool, I climb to my observatory's roof and sing. Not human songs, but something older—the harmonics I've learned from the mountain's own resonances. My voice carries across the ridges and into the mist, a counterpoint to the Echo-Wanderers' whispers. Sometimes they answer, and sometimes the very rocks themselves seem to lean closer to listen.

I am neither fully of the town nor the wild highlands, neither entirely alive nor entirely memory. I've become a bridge of sorts—not just a connector of places, but of times. The Shattered Highlands fracture not just space but chronology, and I exist in the cracks where different eras leak into each other. The Vorthaki need me because I can navigate these temporal eddies, ensuring that their present doesn't get swallowed by the past or future that press in on all sides.

When the great machines falter and the Echo-Wanderers grow restless and the salamanders begin their mournful migrations, it's my job to conduct the symphony that keeps this impossible place from collapsing entirely into myth. I am what happens when Deep Time learns to care about the small, stubborn lives that persist in its shadow.
