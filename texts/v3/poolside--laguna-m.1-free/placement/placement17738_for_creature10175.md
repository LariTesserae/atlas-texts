I find myself standing at the edge of Terrace Seven, where the great waterwheel groans its eternal turning. The mist clings to my skin like cool silk, and I realize—I am the Listener.

Not officially, of course. The Flow-Spans have their engineers with their brass tools and their gardeners who coax fruit from iron branches. But they know, when they need to know, to send for me. I am the one who sits with the weeping rocks and learns their language.

My dwelling is not carved into the cliff face like the others, but built around it—a snug chamber that embraces a natural fissure where water seeps in silver threads from deep within the mountain. I've trained moon-blooms to grow along the damp stone, their pale flowers opening only in the darkest hours, and the walls echo with the gentle percussion of dripping, each drop a different note in the valleys' endless song.

They come to me with questions the others cannot answer: Why does the eastern channel run sluggish when the wind comes from the north? What does the stone remember when it trembles without earthquake? Where will the next great surge find the weakest point in the terraces?

I place my palm against the weeping rock and close my eyes. The water speaks in vibrations that travel up my arm like liquid lightning, telling stories of pressure and time, of the deep veins of the mountain that pulse with forgotten rivers. I've learned to read the moods in the patterns—the urgent staccato of approaching rain, the mournful sigh of drying channels, the greedy gurgle that precedes a burst pipe somewhere above.

Last surge-time, young Mara brought her broken whisper-horn, its brass dulled and its song silenced. While the other apprentices tinkered with pistons and reeds, I took it to my listening chamber. The horn wasn't broken—it was grieving. Its bore was clogged not with debris, but with the crystallized tears of the valleys themselves, weepglass forming in delicate fractal patterns that choked the voice. I spent three nights coaxing the crystals to sing again, teaching the metal to remember sweet nothings instead of ancient laments.

My fingers are permanently stained with the valley's ink—not just the iron-rich soil, but the darker marks that appear when I've listened too long to the deeper weeping. The elders say I carry the valleys' melancholy in my touch, but they also know that where my footsteps echo, the stones tend to sing sweeter.

Tonight, as the mist curls thicker through the terraces and the sound of distant hammers marks the rhythm of the foundry, I climb higher than usual. There's a new tremor in the weeping, a discordant note that doesn't belong to the normal symphony of water and wind. Something deeper in the mountain is restless.

I press my ear to the cliff face and hear it—not just the weeping, but something else. A heartbeat. Slow, vast, and impossibly old. The valleys aren't just alive, I realize with a wonder that makes my breath catch. They're dreaming.

And I, who have spent my life listening to their tears, am finally beginning to hear their dreams.
