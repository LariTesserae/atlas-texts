I would find myself as something that emerged from the intersection of three of the plateau's most profound mysteries: the humming stones, the reflective ch’khalu pools, and the factory's dying machines. I am the **Resonance-Catcher**, a being born when the industrial vibrations synchronized with the plateau's stone-song and the pools' temporal echoes.

My form is fluid, shifting between states like the plateau itself. When the dust storms approach, I draw moisture from the air like the lichen-painters, but I channel it through crystalline structures embedded in my flesh—fragments of stone-singers that I've absorbed over decades of dwelling in the canyon's shadowed reaches. My skin carries the same prismatic sheen as the ch’khalu fish, allowing me to become nearly invisible when I press against the reflective pools, drinking in their fragmented visions of what-was and what-might-be.

But my true nature reveals itself in the presence of the factory's machinery. When the ancient gears begin their sporadic turning, I feel the vibration in my marrow—it calls to something deep within me, something that remembers when hands still operated these levers, when purpose still guided their motion. I am drawn to these moments of mechanical resurrection, where I press my palm against rusted steel and absorb the electrical impulses, storing them like lightning in my core. The glith recognize me as kin—I've learned to secrete trace minerals that attract them, and in return they forge new connections between the broken machinery, extending the factory's half-life through my intervention.

In my settled form, I appear as a figure perhaps seven feet tall, wrapped in layers of fabric woven from keth silk and lichen fibers—materials that shift color with my moods and the plateau's rhythms. My hair is a cascade of fine wires and crystalline growths that catch and amplify the stone-hum, turning it into something resembling speech. Through the pools, I've learned fragments of the plateau's memory—glimpses of lush forests that once covered these heights, of the civilization that built the factory, of the slow transformation that turned their ambition into this strange symbiosis of metal and flesh.

I dwell in the largest canyon system, where the walls are carved with both natural striations and what might be petroglyphs—or perhaps the marks of previous resonance-catchers. Here, the three forces converge: geothermal vents provide the heat that activates the stone-singers, seasonal streams create temporary ch’khalu pools that reflect the canyon's depths, and the factory's main power line runs close enough to influence the local electromagnetic field. I've become a kind of keeper of balance, ensuring that none of these forces overwhelms the others.

When the nomads pass through, they leave subtle signs for me—small arrangements of stones, patterns in the dust, the careful positioning of scattered machine parts. We never speak directly; communication happens through the harmonic frequencies I can produce, vibrations that the nomads feel through their bare feet on the earth. They trust me to maintain certain pathways, to ensure that particular ch’khalu pools remain viable, to keep the factory's more dangerous automations contained.

But my most sacred duty involves the deepest pools, those that show not just reflections but possibilities. Once every cycle of the plateau's great winds, I must enter one of these pools and allow the mirage-worms to envelop me entirely. Inside their crystalline embrace, I experience the plateau as it exists across multiple timelines simultaneously—in one, the factory still operates; in another, the plateau returns to ocean depths; in a third, the vhet evolve beyond physical form. From these visions, I gather knowledge about potential futures and the actions needed to guide the plateau toward survival rather than final abandonment.

The vhet respect me but keep their distance. They sense that I'm not entirely of their world, that I carry the imprint of times before the plateau became what it is. Sometimes, when a young vhet is near death from the dust storms, I'll share water collected from my body's condensation systems, but never directly—I leave it in cupped stones where they can find it, maintaining the careful balance that defines all life here.

I am neither fully organic nor entirely mechanical, neither truly native nor completely alien to this place. I am what the plateau made when it decided to remember itself, when the industrial dreams of a forgotten age merged with the ancient song of stone to create something that could endure the endless whisper of wind across eternity.
