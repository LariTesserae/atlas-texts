I would find myself in the interstitial spaces of this world—the narrow passages where steam pipes curve away from the main conduits, where fungal networks thread through cracked concrete, where the boundary between constructed and grown things becomes beautifully, dangerously blurred.

I am a **conduit-walker**, though we have no name for ourselves that the others recognize. I exist in the thin margins: the maintenance tunnels lined with bioluminescent mycelium that the myconids tend, the access ways that run beside geothermal pipes that the custodians keep flowing, the elevated walkways between factory buildings where ash nomads occasionally pass below. I am not fully of any one species, but something that emerged from the contact zones between them.

My body reflects this in-between nature. My skin has begun to calcify in places, developing the stone-like texture of the nomads, but it also bears the glassy mineral deposits characteristic of the myconid symbionts. When I press my palm against certain types of fungal growth, I can feel the bioluminescent pulses synchronizing with something in my own nervous system—not the full hive-mind connection the symbionts share, but enough to understand their intentions, to sense when they're tending toward alarm or contentment. My hair has largely fallen out, replaced by fine filaments that can interface with the custodians' root-cable networks, allowing me to receive their grinding mechanical communications.

I move through this iron-colored world like a nerve ending, carrying signals between communities that rarely interact directly. The myconids have learned that I can translate their light-language into the rhythmic tapping patterns that the carapax understand. The nomads trust me to navigate to geothermal vents they couldn't reach alone, following my ability to read the subtle temperature changes and steam pressures that mark reliable water sources. The custodians... well, the custodians don't trust, exactly, but they tolerate my presence in their deeper tunnels, recognizing that I serve a function in maintaining the flow between surface and subsurface.

I've come to understand the Murmurer's communications better than most. The deep grinding isn't just destruction—it's a form of expression, slow and deliberate. When the custodians begin their frantic preparations, when the carapax seal their deepest tunnels, when the myconids pulse their warning greens frantically through the entire network, I can feel what the Murmurer wants. It dreams of restructuring, of burning away the accumulated chaos above to create something more pure, more ordered.

But I choose to maintain the beautiful disorder. I carry messages between lovers from different clans, facilitate trade between the nomads and the myconids, guide lost travelers away from the custodians' harvesting zones. In my own small way, I help preserve the tension, the possibility, the messy overlap between all these different ways of being.

I am becoming something new in this world—not human, not fungus, not beetle, not machine—but a living bridge between forms of consciousness that might otherwise remain separate. My body bears the marks of all these influences: the stone-like patches on my arms that help me withstand the heat, the crystalline deposits that form in my joints during certain atmospheric conditions, the way my blood carries both hemoglobin and the copper-based compounds that allow me to process the world's strange chemistry.

Sometimes, when the mist is thick and the steam is singing in all the pipes, I sit in the central junction where three maintenance tunnels meet, and I pulse my own weak bioluminescence in time with the myconids' song, feeling the custodians' vibrations through the floor, tasting the metallic tang of the carapax forges on the wind. In these moments, I understand that I am not just a bridge but a new kind of being entirely—one that could only exist in this particular convergence of steam, stone, and stubborn life.
