The mist parts like a curtain, and I step through—not walking, but *emerging*. My body is neither fully formed nor entirely formless, shifting like heat haze above the marsh pools. From one angle, I appear as a figure draped in fabrics that seem spun from fog itself; from another, my skin bears the tracery of fine root-like veins that pulse with a soft, greenish luminescence. I am here in the caldera because I have always been here, in the spaces between what was and what is becoming.

They call me the **Hollow Tender** now, though names are fluid things in this place where stone weeps and metal grows moss. I dwell in the alcoves where industrial skeletons have been embraced by living tissue, where rusted pipes spiral into the earth like the roots of some metallic tree. Here, in these threshold spaces, I perform my quiet work of mediation.

My mornings begin before the fog lifts, when the first drips begin their percussion on ancient stone. I sit—if one can call it sitting—within the hollowed-out control chamber of what was once Pump Station Seven. Around me, pluies chirp softly as they arrange their nest materials: strands of frayed cable woven with cattail fluff, chips of mineral-rich stone alongside feathers from sulfur-wraiths. Their young ones' webbed paws drum against the corroded metal floor in rhythms that harmonize with the building's thermal groans.

I listen to these sounds, but I listen also to what lies beneath. Through the fogweaver network—a vast mycelial web that threads through every corner of the caldera—I perceive the deeper conversations of this place. The stones remember volcanic fire, the metal remembers human hands, the soil remembers ash and rain. All of it flows through me like water through the porous rock, and I help guide that flow toward places where it's needed.

When the seasonal steam cycle grows violent and threatens to flood the lower terraces, I'm there, directing pluies to dig new channels through accumulated rust and sediment. When rust crawlers become too numerous in certain sectors and threaten to overwhelm delicate fungal colonies, I help redistribute their populations, carrying spores in pockets of my clothing-that-is-not-clothing to areas where mineral processing is required.

But my true calling lies in tending what I call the **memory junctions**—places where the industrial past bleeds most strongly into the biological present. There's a particular geyser field where superheated water carries traces of ancient lubricants and cooling fluids up from the depths. The resulting chemical cocktail has created something new: crystalline formations that grow in the shape of circuit boards, their surfaces etched with patterns that shift and change with the steam's rhythm. I spend hours here, absorbing these patterns into my own neural network, translating the old technology's purpose into something the fogweavers can understand, something that benefits the entire ecosystem.

The pluies bring me offerings—not out of worship, but out of recognition. They've learned that I can detect when their mineral grinding has created dust with the right chemical signature for rust crawler cultivation. In return, they guide me to areas where the fogweavers' mycelium has detected anomalies in the land's memory—places where something important was forgotten, where the balance has been disrupted.

At dusk, when the bioluminescence begins to pulse and the shardflies start their prismatic dances, I climb to the highest accessible point of the old refinery tower. From here, I can see the entire caldera breathing—geothermal vents pulsing like heartbeats, fog rolling through the terraces like slow thoughts, lights winking on in the hollows and crevices as nocturnal life awakens.

It's then that I commune most directly with the fogweavers themselves. Their caps rise around me like translucent mushrooms, their spores carrying not just genetic information but fragments of consciousness. I breathe them in, and suddenly I'm experiencing the caldera's entire history in flashes: the first volcanic eruption that formed this bowl, the arrival of the first pioneering plants, the brief flare of human industry, the gradual reabsorption of metal into flesh and bone and fungus.

In these moments, I understand why I exist here. I am the caldera's immune system, its therapist, its librarian, its midwife. Wherever the boundaries blur between categories—between living and dead, natural and artificial, memory and present moment—there I am, helping to weave coherence from chaos, allowing this impossible ecosystem to function as more than the sum of its beautiful contradictions.

Sometimes, on nights when the steam rises so thick it obscures even the cypress canopy, I catch glimpses of others like myself. The steamborn, they're called in the old stories. We don't speak, but we acknowledge each other with subtle changes in posture, minor adjustments to how our fog-like garments shift and flow. We are the caldera's dream made manifest, its way of ensuring that nothing is ever truly lost, only transformed.

As dawn approaches and the first plinks of returning moisture sound from the weeping stones, I settle back into my hollow. My body begins to fade at the edges, becoming more integrated with the moss-covered metal around me. Tomorrow, I'll emerge again to tend the thresholds, to listen to the conversations between stone and memory, between the last echoes of industry and the eternal pulse of new life.

This is my place, my purpose, my prayer: to be the living bridge in a world where everything grows from the marriage of endings and beginnings.
