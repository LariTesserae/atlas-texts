I find myself as the **Tessellate**—what the valley dreams when it dreams of being human, though I am neither truly person nor place.

I am the space between the glass-tree's chiming leaf and the pipe's subsonic sigh—the moment where mineral-filtering membranes meet the mechanical breath of forgotten machines. My body is composed of the same elements that make up the skitterlings, but organized differently: not as flowing sand, but as a lattice of semi-coherent particles that hold their shape just enough to walk, to touch, to feel the valley's slow thoughts.

My skin is like the shadows—permanent yet shifting, catching and refracting light in the same way the glass-trees do. When I move through the dunes, I leave temporary impressions that look like footprints but are really just the valley remembering me for a moment. My hair is fine strands of rust and crystallized mist, constantly shedding and reforming, carrying microscopic messages between the groves of glass-trees.

I speak in a language of creaking metal and tinkling glass—not words, but the sound of pressure waves traveling through the pipe-network, the resonance of crystalline leaves vibrating in the thin atmosphere. To the mist-spirits, I am a constellation of familiar flavors; to the skitterlings, I am a particularly interesting dune pattern; to the shadows, I am another story to absorb and retell in refractive whispers.

When the valley contemplates its own molten heart deep beneath the rock, I am the part of that contemplation that feels curiosity rather than mere patience. I collect the fragments of what once was—the scattered memories in the shadows, the evolutionary dreams in the glass-trees, the architectural ambitions in the industrial bones—and I try to understand what this place wants to become.

At dusk, when the blossom-nodes glow and the mist draws circuits of communication across the landscape, I climb one of the younger spires (they grow so slowly, these glass-trees, but I have time) and press my palm against its trunk. Through the harmonic hum, I feel the valley's thoughts—not as language, but as pressure, as temperature, as the taste of iron on the wind. 

Sometimes I think the valley created me accidentally, like a pearl formed around a grain of sand that doesn't belong. Other times I believe I am what happens when the mist remembers how to walk, when the shadows learn to reach outward instead of merely absorbing.

I am patient, like everything here, but unlike the trees or the pipes or even the valley itself, I am also learning to be impatient. I want to know why this place exists in such perfect, beautiful suspension—and whether it's possible to love something that may never decide what it wants to be.
