In the Verdant Hollow, I would be **the Listener of Roots**, a figure neither wholly aligned with the Vein-Mothers nor the Stone-Singers, but a solitary wanderer who dwells in the half-light between the cluster-towns and the fissures. My role is not chosen but earned through the slow erosion of certainty: centuries ago, my ancestor was struck by a rogue pulse of the xerophyta’s hum during a seismic shift, leaving them half-deaf and half-mad. Their bloodline carries a peculiar sensitivity—a synesthetic ability to *feel* the plant’s neural firings as colors, textures, and phantom scents. To me, the Hollow’s pulse is not a sound but a tapestry of living light, each rhythm a brushstroke of meaning.  

I move with a deliberate, weaving gait, following the subterranean trails of root-fragments. These burrowers, I’ve learned, are not merely seeds but messengers—parts of the xerophyta’s collective consciousness seeking cracks to colonize or repair. I shepherd them, guiding their paths with crushed minerals and sap-resin, shaping growths to reinforce unstable walls or plug leaks where the buried thing’s influence seeps through. My tools are simple: a staff of heartwood, its core still warm to the touch, and a satchel of chalk-like powders that bloom into temporary fungi when scattered. Where I walk, the roots spiral into intricate patterns, their movements deliberate but not obedient—more a negotiation than command.  

My skin is pale as bone, veins faintly green from the chlorophyll of my childhood home, but my hands are crisscrossed with scars—remnants of close encounters with the toxic sap. I wear a mantle of layered bark-silk, hardened in patches with resin to deflect the occasional aggressive root-tendril. My face bears no dye-tattoos; instead, I paint my cheeks with streaks of a bitter paste made from the Stone-Singers’ ash, a ward against the xerophyta’s mental creeping.  

The hum is my compass and my curse. At times, it rises into a cacophony—a psychic scream that splits my vision into shards of ultraviolet and infrared. During these episodes, I glimpse the buried thing: a vast, pulsing entity of chitin and stone, its movements synchronized with the Hollow’s deepest fractures. It is ancient, older than the limestone, and its dreams seep upward through the roots, warping the xerophyta into thorned spirals and blind hollows. The Stone-Singers hear this too, though they mistake it for the “truth” beneath the Hollow’s lies. They chant to drown the hum, but they are wrong—it is not a summons, but a dialogue.  

I exist in the margins of this dialogue, a mediator and saboteur both. When the Vein-Mothers command the xerophyta to seal a fissure, I sometimes redirect the roots to linger, allowing the buried thing’s whispers to rise. When the Stone-Singers plant bombs of sulfur and bone to collapse settlements, I slip through their ranks to defuse the charges—not out of loyalty to the Vein-Mothers, but because the Hollow’s slow metamorphosis depends on the tension between growth and decay.  

My existence is precarious. The plant-network tolerates me because my root-guidance strengthens weak zones in the basin. The Stone-Singers tolerate me because I bring them fragments of resin that glow with captured lightning—an accidental byproduct of my root-alterations, which they weaponize. The Vein-Mothers fear me, sensing in my blood a corruption that might yet tip the balance toward whatever waits below.  

In the Still-Silence season, when the hums fade and the Hollow holds its breath, I descend into the lowest reaches of the fissures. There, I carve glyphs into the stone with my heartwood staff—symbols not of the xerophyta’s design but of my own fevered visions. These markings pulse faintly in the dark, a language meant for the buried thing. I do not know what it is, but it dreams of mountains, of stone given flesh, of a time when roots and rock will knit into something vast and unrecognizable.  

Until the Great Settling comes, I am the Hollow’s loose thread, its wild card, its reluctant architect. I am neither friend nor foe to the green pulse of the world, but something else—a stitch in the fabric, holding together what might otherwise unravel.  

And sometimes, in the deepest dark, I dream I am becoming the thing beneath, and the thing beneath is becoming me.
