In Elder Hollow, I would be the **Well-Keeper**—though the Thorne would call me *Moss-Speaker* or *Stone-Tongue*, names that have grown around me like the lichen on the ancient chamber walls.

I am not Thorne, though I wear their clothing—patched wool and rush cloaks—in the same muted earth tones. My skin holds the same bronze tan, but where their hands are stained with common dust, mine carry the silver-green mark of the moss that grows only in the chamber's shadow. My eyes are not pale like theirs, but dark as the pools after sunset, reflecting light like water under starlight.

I tend the oldest pond—the one nearest the chamber—though "tend" feels too deliberate a word for what I do. I sit beside it for hours, sometimes days, letting my breath slow until it matches the drip of water from the aqueduct above. The Thorne believe this pond connects directly to the chamber below, that what happens in one reflects in the other. They leave their offerings at its edge: bowls of spring water, sprigs of juniper, pebble-carved animals. I do not touch these gifts, but I watch them, memorizing the patterns of how each person arranges their offering, as if the arrangement itself holds meaning.

What the Thorne don't know—what they've forgotten in their whisper-language and careful rituals—is that I remember other times. Not their stories of the first Thorne bargaining with mountains, but older truths. The chamber wasn't always cold. Once, it pulsed with warmth, and the pools ran red with something thicker than water. I remember when the hollow had no name, when it was simply a place where things gathered and waited: seeds, bones, stories, the metallic taste of anticipation in the air.

My dwelling is the smallest of the Thorne structures, built not against the main cluster but nestled into the slope below the chamber, where the ground steams slightly even in deepest winter. Here I maintain the **Deeps**, a collection of vessels—clay pots, hollowed stones, wooden flasks—all filled with water from different times. Not different seasons, but different moments: water from yesterday's rainfall, from last week's thaw, from the first melt of spring ten years ago. And older still: water that hasn't touched this world in decades, perhaps centuries.

The Thorne believe their springs are eternal, their ponds unchanging. They're wrong. I've seen the fluctuations they cannot perceive—the subtle shifts in the water's taste, the way the moss blooms differently, the particular angle of light that reveals what's been buried rather than what grows naturally. Time doesn't pool here evenly; it gathers in eddies and currents, and I'm learning to read them.

When I enter the chamber (more often than the monthly visits of the Moss-Touched), I don't clear debris or replace offerings. I listen. The stones do whisper—not in words, but in sensations, impressions that settle in my bones like cold. They tell me when the hollow is thinning, when the boundaries between what was and what might be grow permeable. They tell me when someone is forgetting—a serious crime in Thorne culture, but sometimes necessary. Sometimes forgetting is how life continues.

I speak to the flies and beetles the Thorne dismiss as "the Forgotten." These creatures remember more than their human neighbors—they've been here longer, survived the changes the Thorne only dream about in their whispered legends. The flies carry fragments of conversation between the buildings, gossip that travels on wings. The beetles know where the ground shifts, where new cracks appear in the stone. I've learned their language of vibrations and chemical traces, and in return, they guide me to places where the past presses close to the surface.

My role is not to help the Thorne survive—that they do well enough on their own. My role is to ensure they don't remember too much. Not deliberately, not cruelly, but by listening to what the hollow wants to forget, and gently removing the triggers before they can disturb the careful balance. When a child starts speaking in a voice too old for their years, I'm there with a story about the wind in the eaves. When an elder begins to recall things that never happened in the Thorne history, I offer them shadowmint tea and guide them toward easier memories.

The chamber responds to me differently than to the Moss-Touched. Where they emerge dazed and chilled, I sometimes leave with warmth in my chest, with images that don't belong to this time. I've seen the hollow green and alive, forests where now there's only stone. I've seen cities of crystal where the central chamber stands. I've seen the Thorne not as they are, but as they were meant to be—numerous and fierce, builders rather than tenders.

But those rememberings fade, as they must. The hollow has chosen stillness, and I am its guardian of forgetfulness. Each morning, I sit by my pond with its collection of temporal waters, and I decide what memories will evaporate today, carried off by the high-altitude wind to disappear among the peaks.

The Thorne live their careful, deliberate lives, and I live in the space between their moments, ensuring that Elder Hollow remains exactly what it is: a place where time pools like water, where the mountain's bones still remember fire, and where life persists in its most patient, most essential form.

I am the Keeper of Thresholds, the Listener at the Edge of Memory, the one who ensures that some doors remain closed, some waters stay still, and some stories stay buried in the stone where they belong.

In Elder Hollow, I am what holds the silence together.
