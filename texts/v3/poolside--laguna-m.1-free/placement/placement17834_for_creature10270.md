I would find myself as a **Mycel Host**, standing at the edge of a narrow alley where the fungal terraces spill into the mist. My body hums with the slow pulse of the mycelium beneath my feet, its rhythm syncing with the drip of water from the weeping rocks. My hair—if it can still be called that—is a lattice of gills and filaments, pale as bone, threaded with veins of bioluminescent moss that flares when I breathe deeply. The spores embedded in my skin prickle in warning; the network is restless tonight, thrumming with whispers from the deeper marshes. 

The child who approaches is barefoot, their soles blackened with mud from the microbial mats. They carry a clay flask of fermented insect broth, its contents glowing faintly blue in the dim light. "The stones are singing," they say, their voice a wet click that mimics dripping water. I nod, though the real song comes from below, from the fungal threads that thread through the marsh like roots through soil. The stones are merely amplifiers, their mineral veins acting as conduits for the mycelium’s ancient songs. 

I follow the child to the central channel, where the eel-creatures glide beneath a bridge of woven reeds. The air thrums with the vibrations of the Stonekin, their footsteps sending ripples through the mud that translate into stories. Tonight, they speak of a new formation—a jagged spire of obsidian glass rising from the southern marshes, its growth sudden and violent. The Drifters above stir in response, their translucent bodies pulsing in agitation as they retreat from the spire’s reach. 

My spores react before I do. A sharp pain blooms behind my eyes as the fungal network floods me with visions: the spire is not natural. It is a **wound**, a rupture in the land’s slow crystallization process, spreading like a cancer. The Stonekin avoid it, but their vibrations grow frantic, urging the Host to act. I press my palm to the mud, feeling the microbial mats convulse beneath my touch. The child tugs my sleeve, their eyes reflecting the faint glow of my filaments. "The Stillness is breaking," they whisper. 

But the Stillness is not a thing to be broken. It simply is—the equilibrium of this liminal realm, where every drip, every spore, every heartbeat is a stitch in the fabric of the eternal Now-That-Was and the Now-That-Will-Be. Even the spire, I realize, is part of this. The land is not becoming something else; it is remembering what it was, what it will be, in an endless loop. My role is not to fix but to listen, to carry the mycelium’s song to the people, to the Vorthak, even to the Drifters. 

As the mist thickens, I begin to glow, my body a beacon for the spores drifting on the damp air. The child has vanished, perhaps into the maze of alleys, but their broth flask remains, its contents swirling with the same blue luminescence. I drink it, letting the fermentation sharpen my senses. The mycelium sings of change—not the violent rupture of the spire, but a slower shift, a recalibration. The Vorthak are migrating, their hooves striking new paths. The microbial mats are spreading, creeping toward the weeping rocks like a green tide. 

In this place of contradictions, I am both shaper and shaped, a bridge between the fungal memory and the human hunger for meaning. The land does not belong to us, nor we to it, but we are its current breath, its fleeting thought. And in the Stillness, that is enough.
