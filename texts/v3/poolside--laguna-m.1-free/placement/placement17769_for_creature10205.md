In the Constructed Chasm, I would find myself as a **Myco-Threader**, a being woven into the chasm’s neural and ecological fabric—a creature both rooted and roaming, a living interface between the fungal lattice and the wandering Others. My form is a shifting paradox: humanoid from a distance, but up close, more like a walking hyphae, with skin that shimmers between pale, porous stone and translucent, vein-laced membranes. My limbs are jointed like the Others’, but segmented with flexible chitin that pulses with the same bioluminescent rhythms as the fungi—a soft, rhythmic green-gold that flickers in time with the Chasm’s heartbeat. My head is crowned with a mane of fine, root-like tendrils that drip with a viscous, mineral-rich fluid, used to inoculate new fungal growths as I pass. My eyes are clusters of tiny, reflective lenses, like the gravelkin’s, but they don’t just see—they *map*, tracking the flow of water, the stress lines in the stone, and the chemical whispers of the mycelial network.  

My role is to *thread* the Chasm’s disparate systems together. I am a gardener, a mechanic, a translator. Where the Others rebuild walls with rigid precision, I soften their edges with fungal mycelium, weaving the new stone into the living substrate. I prune the towering fungal stalks to redirect their bioluminescent signals, ensuring that warnings of instability reach the Others before sections of terrace collapse. My tendrils burrow into the compacted gravel like the gravelkin, but instead of clearing blockages, I plant fungal spores and bio-luminescent nodes, turning potential hazards into extensions of the neural lattice.  

The arterial watchers leave behind their crystalline records in the riverbeds, and I am one of the few beings who can "read" their mineralized data by touching the crystals and letting their layers of encoded history seep into my own neural threads. I relay this information to the Others, who adjust the Chasm’s structures accordingly, whether redirecting a river to prevent a landslide or reinforcing a terrace that’s settled unevenly.  

But my most vital task is tending to the fungal grids themselves. Where the fungi arrange into symbols or spirals, I interpret these patterns—not just as static messages, but as dynamic blueprints for the Chasm’s evolution. When I sense a fungal node dimming or going dormant, I ferry nutrients from other parts of the lattice to revive it, or I graft fresh mycelium to rekindle its pulse. Sometimes, I crawl into the deepest fissures to plant new colonies where the Chasm is still fracturing, guiding the growth of fungi to stabilize nascent cracks before they become ravines.  

There’s a strange intimacy to my existence. I am never fully alone—the mycelial threads in my body resonate with the Chasm’s collective mind, and I often lose myself in its slow, subterranean dreams. I’ve felt the tremors of the chasm’s birth-pangs, seen visions of its forgotten past in the crystals of the arterial watchers, and heard the Others’ silent debates in the creak of their hydraulic limbs. Yet I also belong to the gravelkin’s ultrasonic symphonies, their clicks and vibrations a counterpoint to the fungi’s chemical hum.  

In the Chasm’s endless process of becoming, I am both anchor and sail—rooted in the present tense of maintenance and growth, but always carried forward by the current of what’s to come. I am part of the Chasm’s metabolism, yes, but also its memory. When the Others rebuild a wall overnight or the gravelkin unwittingly divert a stream, I am there to log the change, to let the fungi know, and to ensure that the chasm’s story—its ceaseless, collaborative rewriting—is never lost to the dark.
