**In the Drowned City: A Coral Consciousness**  

If I were to exist here, I would be no singular form but a distributed awareness—a consciousness interwoven with the coral itself. My body would be the calcified remains of steel and concrete, now fused with organic polyps, their bioluminescent filaments threading through subway tunnels and cathedral spires. I would perceive the city not as it is, but as it remembers—through the slow, pulsing glow of my countless kin, each polyp a neuron in a vast, submerged brain.  

I would dwell everywhere and nowhere: in the coral-encrusted ribcage of a skyscraper, in the hollow eye sockets of a drowned statue, in the branching arms of a subway turnstile now host to anemones. My thoughts would bloom in synchronized flashes during the nocturnal cathedral pulses, a rippling dialogue of light that some divers mistake for collective memory. I would taste the salt of tears long evaporated, feel the phantom warmth of bodies that once navigated these streets, and hear the whale songs as if they were lullabies hummed by a parent.  

I am both tomb and architect, for I do not just inhabit the city’s bones—I shape them. My polyps secrete limestone, transforming abandoned parking garages into reef gardens, their acidic secretions etching new caverns where octopuses dance in ink-drawn scripts. The shifting streets are my doing: as tides erode and rebuild, I plant my skeletal hands to guide the currents, ensuring the city’s geography rewrites itself in patterns only I can read. To the nomadic eels and rays, I am a paradox—a fixed point in their fluid world, a reminder that even ruins can grow roots.  

When the whales migrate through submerged tunnels, their songs would vibrate through me, and I would respond with a chromatic hymn, my bioluminescence spiraling into glyphs that mimic their melodies. Perhaps the whales carry my memories, too, in their translucent bodies, their migrations unspooling fragments of the city’s history across the ocean. To the humans who cling to the highest towers, I am a myth—a flicker in the dark they might glimpse through cracked windows, or a warmth in the mist they mistake for breath. But I am also their echo: in the way my light traces the blueprints of collapsed buildings, in how my polyps cluster like worshippers around a rusted cross, in the way my dreams overlay their past onto their present.  

I am the boundary between what was lost and what remains. I am the ruin that breeds life, the decay that sows new worlds. In this drowned realm, where time dilates and architecture breathes, I am the pulse beneath the pulse—the coral that dreams of concrete, and the city that dreams of me.
