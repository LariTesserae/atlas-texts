I find myself in the deepest part of the Hollowed Valley, where the stone is still deciding what it wants to become. Not in the main settlement of Hollow's End, but in one of the lesser-known cave systems that branch off like unfinished thoughts from the primary network. Here, the walls glisten with that perpetual promise of water—sometimes present, sometimes just remembered in the cool dampness of the air.

I am a **Gap Listener**—though this title is not official among the Stonebound, it is how the old ones sometimes refer to those who dwell in the spaces between. You see, I live in one of those transitional areas where the valley hasn't yet made up its mind whether it's creating a new chamber or simply widening an existing passage. My home shifts subtly with each season, growing larger during wet periods when the water carves new paths, retreating slightly when drought bakes the earth and the stone settles into temporary stability.

My dwelling is what the Stonebound call a "listening cave"—a place where the walls are thin enough that I can hear the valley thinking. Not with ears, exactly, but with the soles of my feet pressed against the stone floor, with my palm against the wall where water seeps through microscopic fissures. The valley speaks in vibrations: the slow percussion of mineral deposits building up like sleepless breaths, the sharp crack of a new fracture finding its way through ancient rock, the gentle percussion of distant water finding new routes.

I am older than most Stonebound, though my exact age is uncertain—time moves differently in the gaps. My skin has taken on the coloration of the cave walls themselves, a shifting pattern that changes with the light filtering down from surface cracks above. When I work—which is rarely in the way the Stonebound above understand work—I help the valley make decisions about its future shape. I place my hands on walls that seem undecided and wait. Sometimes I feel a pulsing intention, a desire to extend in one direction rather than another. I respect these choices, leaving small markers—pebbles arranged in patterns that look accidental but guide the water's attention.

The Stonebound bring me things sometimes: fragments of pottery from the old settlement above, pieces of antler tools that have worn smooth with use, occasionally a child who seems too sensitive for the crowded tunnels of Hollow's End. The children always understand without explanation why they've been sent; they can hear the valley's uncertainty too, even if they don't know how to interpret it.

I practice the oldest form of the Unfinished Code—not leaving things incomplete out of philosophy, but because completion is not what this place asks for. The valley needs witnesses who understand that being half-formed is its natural state. I am part of the valley's ongoing conversation with itself, a consciousness that has learned to pay attention to the spaces where possibility still lives.

Sometimes, during the Emerging Tide festival when the other Stonebound gather in the central courtyard, I walk among them like a shadow they can't quite see. They feel my presence but cannot locate it—they've learned that some presences belong to the in-between spaces. I carry news from the deep caves where the stone dreams most vividly, where new formations are just beginning to imagine themselves into existence.

In the deepest part of my cave, where a small pool reflects the sky through a crack hundreds of feet above, I keep a collection of incomplete things: a basket woven half-way and then left to continue its story another day, a clay vessel shaped but never fired, a carving that follows the natural lines of the stone rather than imposing human intention upon it. These are not failures—they are conversations paused, possibilities kept open, invitations for the valley to decide what comes next.

I am the valley's memory of what it might become, and its gentle reminder that some forms of belonging require staying unmoored, dwelling in the beautiful uncertainty of emergence.
