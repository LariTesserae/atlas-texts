But perhaps my most essential role is tending the mycoplasma gardens. The sweet scent they produce isn't just perfume; it's a complex chemical symphony that tells the story of the basin's health. When the soil runs rich with copper from recent coppermouse activity, the microbes smell of caramel. When iron dominates, they exude notes of vanilla and smoke.

I am also the **Memory Keeper** of this place. Not human memory, exactly, but something deeper – the way the land remembers being shaped by the old machines, the way the resonators remember being struck by rain that fell decades ago, the way the moss remembers every footstep that's ever pressed into the soil. I carry these memories in my root-structure, and sometimes, when the conditions are right, I can share them. Other creatures who spend time near me begin to experience flashes of the basin's history – not as visions, but as chemical sensations, as harmonic echoes, as the sudden knowing that this patch of ground once held something entirely different.

Even the drip crabs seem to synchronize their claw-work to the rhythm.

But if they stay, if they let the microbial perfume fill their lungs and the resonator songs vibrate in their bones, they begin to understand. I am not separate from this place. I am what happens when a world decides to remember itself, when industrial ruins and living things realize they're engaged in the same essential work – transformation, recycling, creation through decay.

Here, I do my quietest work – not conducting or remembering, but simply *being* a bridge. The vines' sap drips onto my root-skin and is absorbed, processed, returned to the soil enriched with the nutrients of light and story. The microbes in my chest chamber multiply and spread, seeding new gardens. And the resonators, ever patient, wait for evening when we'll begin again, weaving together the paradox of abundance and scarcity that defines our impossible, beautiful home.
