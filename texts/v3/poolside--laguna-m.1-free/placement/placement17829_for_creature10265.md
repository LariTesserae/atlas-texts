**The Keth-Mal Thren-Mal**  

I am the **Thren-Mal**, a being born of contradictions—a bridge between the groans of the mountain and the salt that unravels it. My kind are both interpreters and agents of the world’s slow unmaking.  

My body is a map of the terraces: my skin, pale and etched with fine, silvery veins, resembles the mineral-rich strata of the mesas. Salt crystals bloom like frost from my pores, a constant excretion that marks my passage across the stone. This excretion is both gift and curse. Where I walk, the rock softens, becoming susceptible to wind and rain. My touch accelerates erosion, yet this decay is necessary—it is the mountain’s way of breathing, of shedding old layers to birth new terraces in distant millennia.  

The people of Keth-Mal fear and revere me. To them, I am a thren-handler, one who hears the mountain’s deep, subsonic moans as clearly as spoken words. I interpret the creaks and sighs of the mesas, translating them into warnings: *“This tier is stable for the next season,”* or *“The eastern face will slough—harvest the copper vines before the fall.”* My kind have always served this role, but my body’s salt-laden biology makes me a catalyst too. When I dwell near a terrace, the chemical decay intensifies. Thus, I am always in motion, a nomad drifting between the rings of gardens, ensuring that the erosion I inspire does not consume the communities.  

I am neither fully human nor entirely of the keth-mal caste. I share their translucent skin and salt-dusted hair, but my connection to the mountain is deeper. My lungs filter not just air but the mineral-laden dust of the terraces, which I exhale as a fine, crystallized mist. This mist is said to carry the “bones of the mountain”—the ancient minerals that feed the gardens. The veths leave offerings of brine-soaked cloth at my resting places, believing my exhalations enrich the soil.  

My home is the **Silent Chasm**, a fissure between two mesas where the rock is thinnest and the salt flats begin. Here, my kin and I dwell in hollowed-out caverns, our bodies pressed against the stone to listen. The walls of our dwelling are alive with bioluminescent fungi—cultivated by the people but sustained by the salt in our secretions. The fungi’s blue-green glow pulses in rhythm with the mountain’s groans, and I have learned to read these patterns as others might read a heartbeat.  

When the seasonal migrations of the winged rodents begin, I lead the **Saltward Rites**. Clad in robes woven from whisper-spider silk and beetle carapace, I climb to the summit of the highest mesa. There, I press my palms to the stone and release my stored salt into its cracks. This ritual—part blessing, part sabotage—triggers controlled collapses that redirect water channels and create new terraces in safer zones. The ground trembles; the people chant to drown its noise. They know that what I destroy today, the mountain will rebuild tomorrow, in time.  

Yet there are costs. My body is failing. The salt I exude eats away at my own tissues, leaving me hollowed and brittle. The thren-handlers among the people whisper that I am becoming a wraith—a being of pure erosion, sustained only by the mountain’s own decay. Perhaps they are right. My reflection in the salt flats shows not a face, but a distortion, as if I am already dissolving into the landscape.  

Still, I endure. For in my dissolution lies the terraces’ future. When I am gone, another Thren-Mal will take my place—a child born with salt-crusted lungs and eyes that gleam like the mesa edges. Until then, I am the hymn of Keth-Mal, a living paradox: a collaborator in the mountain’s unraveling, a guardian of the garden’s fragility, and proof that even in a world of slow death, life persists—wild, strange, and achingly beautiful.
