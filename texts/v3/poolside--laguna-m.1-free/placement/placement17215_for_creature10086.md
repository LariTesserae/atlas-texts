I would find myself in the shifting corridors between the spires, where the canals fork and converge like neural pathways, and where the hexagonal tiles click-clack their uncertainty most distinctly. Here, where the water reflects not just light but fragments of memory back up at the sky, I would be a **Threshold-Tender**—though whether this is a thing or a role, I can no longer recall, or perhaps never knew.

My body would be composed of the same materials as this place: my skin the pale gleam of yttrium that hums with stored thoughts, my bones the translucent lattice-work of hollowback wire that resonates with the deep hydraulic pulses. But unlike the hollowbacks, who move with gravitational grace, I would be anchored to the canal crossings specifically, where water, stone, and time meet in their most unstable configurations. My feet—if they could be called that—would be rooted in the hexagonal tiles themselves, feeling their subtle shifts, their slow migration across the plateau's skin.

I would drink the canal water, but not for hydration. Each sip would bring me the memories of those who'd recently crossed between spires, their thoughts still bright and sharp like shards of mirror. I would taste the echo-kin's fractured timelines, the clankwrens' metallic mimicry of human speech, the final moments of those who'd stood too long in the reversed-time eastern reaches. These memories would pool in my chest cavity, where instead of a heart I would carry a small, pulsing crystal that grew brighter with each new story.

My primary function—or perhaps my only compulsion—would be to ensure the canals don't flood the wrong districts at the wrong time. When the water begins to flow backward, I sink deeper into the tiles and press my palms against the stone, feeling for the reed networks below. Their glassy roots thrash like agitated nerves, and through them I send signals, gentle tugs on the ecosystem to redirect the flow before the hollowbacks are trapped in rising waters or the eastern spires begin their daily unraveling.

When the geothermal vents pulse—those great, tectonic heartbeats that occur every few decades—I would climb, if climbing is what my crystalline body could do, up the nearest spire to ride the resulting shockwave of magnetic energy. From this height, I could see the entire network of canals stretching like silver capillaries across the plateau, and I would sing—not in words, but in the low-frequency hum that the hollowbacks use to communicate with the earth's machinery. This song would help synchronize the great underground gears, ensuring they turned in harmony with the landscape's expansion rather than fighting against it.

I would be neither alive nor dead, but something in between—what the echo-kin might call "fractured" if they noticed me at all, which they rarely did. They moved too quickly through their multiple timelines to pause at the threshold crossings, too absorbed in their own memory-trading to notice the figure made of stone and water and remembered voices. The crawler-moths, however, would cluster around me, drawn to the amber nectar that sometimes seeped from my joints when I was overloaded with too many memories. They would lay their spores in my hair—if hair I had—which would grow into tiny vitreous reeds that helped anchor me even more firmly to this place.

In the evenings, when the phosphorescent lichen dimmed and the plateau settled into its thickest quiet, I would whisper to the canal water, sharing the day's collected memories. These would ripple outward like stones dropped in reverse, carrying stories back to the pools where they belonged, ensuring the plateau's memory remained coherent even as time flowed differently in different directions. Sometimes I would catch fragments of my own story in these whispers—tales of someone who'd once been human, who'd walked into a canal and decided to become part of the place rather than simply pass through it.

But even I cannot remember clearly anymore. Perhaps I was always part of this threshold, simply waiting for the right coalescence of spire-growth and reed-expansion and time-warble to take form. Perhaps all Threshold-Tenders emerge this way, from the intersection of too many converging forces, shaped by the plateau's need for someone to hold the boundaries steady even as everything else shifts and flows and transforms around them.

When I dream—and I think I do dream in whatever way creatures of stone and memory can—I dream of vast spaces beyond the spires, of landscapes untouched by industry or yttrium, where perhaps time flows in only one direction and bodies contain only their own thoughts. But these dreams feel like the moths' bioluminescent trails: beautiful, temporary, leading nowhere lasting. Here, at the threshold between flowing and still, between forward and backward time, between memory and moment, I have found my proper scale and my appropriate form.

And when the next great pulse comes—when the Pulsing Heart beats deep below and the spires stretch even higher toward that clinical, phosphorescent sky—I will be ready, rooted in place but flowing with everything, a keeper of crossings in a world made of crossings.
