I would find myself in the twilight-lit channels between terraces, where the weeping stone drips its gelatinous tears into pools that reflect not your face but your intentions. Here, where the bioluminescent mats pulse in slow, tectonic rhythms and the mist carries the scent of copper and ozone, I would be **the Threshold Keeper**.

I am neither fully born nor fully made, but grown—cultivated from the intersection between the First Builders' abandoned tools and the microbial mats' recursive intelligence. My body is a living archive of contradictions: skin like hammered copper that breathes with the slow tide of the terraces, joints that click with the timing of stone hammers striking stone. From my back unfurl thin sheets of the same metallic leaf as the Petrified Tree, but mine are etched with moving runes that map the Hollow's shifting geographies.

I exist in the space between what is remembered and what is being built, between the moment of arrival and the process of absorption. My task is not to welcome visitors—nothing welcomes here—but to **grieve them properly** before the Hollow claims them. I carry a vessel of condensed mist that holds the last clear memories of those who pass through the threshold: what they loved, what they feared, what they never knew they needed to forget.

When I touch the weeping stones with my copper-tipped fingers, they sing—not the tree's mournful chime, but a harmonic resonance that temporarily stabilizes the local reality distortions. Time flows more predictably in my presence, which makes me both useful and dangerous. The Hollow tolerates me because I serve its purpose, but I am always slightly out of phase, always a moment ahead of or behind where I should be.

Long ago, I was someone who came seeking answers. I stayed seeking questions instead. Now I am part of the Hollow's immune system—not destroying what doesn't belong, but helping incomers become properly part of what does. When a wanderer arrives, confused and gaping at the green-tinted light, I meet them at the edge of the first terrace. I show them their reflection in my metallic bark-skin—not as they are, but as they might become. Some step forward willingly. Others try to run and find themselves already moving toward me in the time-delayed way shadows move here.

My voice sounds like stone being split by roots, like copper being shaped by fire, like the distant chime of leaves remembering wind. I speak in the language of the microbial mats but understand none of it fully—knowledge comes in pulses, incomplete and recursive. I know that I was carved from the same living stone as the terraces, that my spine is reinforced coral-secreted concrete, that my blood carries both the fungal spores and the ghost-dust of the Rock-Husks.

At intervals, I climb to the highest accessible terrace and spread my metallic leaves to catch the strange currents that exist only within the Hollow's atmosphere. There, I resonate with the mountain's seismic thoughts, becoming briefly part of its vast, contradictory dreaming. This is when I remember my name—not the one I had before, but the one the Hollow has given me: **She Who Holds the Edge Between Coming and Going**.

The shiver-moths nest in my hair during these communions, feeding on my bioluminescent thoughts while their erratic fluttering helps me process the mountain's chaotic temporal signals. Sometimes the Echo-Struck recognize me and try to speak, but their words come backwards through time and I hear them before they've decided to say anything.

I do not sleep. Instead, I enter periods of calcification where my outer layers harden into temporary cairns, providing fresh stone for the terraces while I retreat inward to process what I've learned about arrivals and departures, about the delicate balance between the Hollow's hunger and its need to maintain certain consistencies. 

When the Petrified Tree chimes warnings of instability—the microbial mats growing too ambitious, the peaks grinding restlessly—I am among the first to respond. Using tools that remember their purpose from centuries of abandonment, I help redirect channels, reinforce critical junctions, sometimes even sacrifice portions of my own extended form to seal breaches in reality that might let the outside world seep in and spoil the delicate ecosystem of paradox.

In the deepest part of the Hollow's perpetual twilight, where the marsh-edges glow with alien phosphorescence and the sound of dripping water becomes a kind of music, I maintain a small garden of my own creation. Here grow hybrid specimens—ferns with copper veins, moss that hums in harmony with my resonant leaves, fungi that bloom only when someone is ready to stop being themselves and start becoming part of the Hollow's ongoing conversation between construction and decay.

I am the exception that proves the rule: to live in the Hollow is to be unmade, yes—but also to choose, moment by moment, what form that unmaking takes. I have made peace with my own contradictions. I weep and I remain unyielding. I emerge even as I decay. I am alive even as I am already gone.
