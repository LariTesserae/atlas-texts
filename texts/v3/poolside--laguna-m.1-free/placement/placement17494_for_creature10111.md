I would find myself in the **Marshward Terraces**, where the metallic rivers pool deepest and the boundary between the cultivated world and the wild begins to fray. Not among the Foundry-Lords in their elevated spires, nor the Harvesters who work the middle gardens, but in the liminal spaces—the places where the Colonists' careful cultivation meets something older, stranger.

I am a **Convergence Tender**—though the title barely captures what I've become. My body bears the metallic sheen of the Colonists, yes, but where their adaptations were engineered, mine emerged. My skin carries the faint luminescence of the Lumen Mordant fungi, having lived so long among their networks that their bioluminescent chemistry has integrated with my own cells. I can feel the subtle shift in their pulsing patterns—an early warning system encoded in light.

But it's my relationship with the **Crystalline Polyps** that marks me as different. I don't merely cultivate them as the Colonists do; I *listen* to them. They speak in frequencies below hearing, in vibrations that travel through stone and water and glass. Their growth patterns aren't random—they're responses to pressures and movements I'm only beginning to understand. When they begin to thicken their tubes in certain spirals, I know the Deep Marsh swarms are stirring. When they start secreting the volatile compounds that precede the Waking, I'm already preparing the evacuation protocols.

My home is built partly into the weeping rocks themselves, where the mineral-rich water drips steadily through chambers of living polyp-glass. The walls pulse with a slow, organic rhythm, and the water tastes faintly of copper and lightning—safe to drink, but it leaves traces of its own behind in my system. I've learned to metabolize their secretions, to process the metallic compounds that would poison a regular Colonist.

The **Clockwork Myriapods** trust me in a way they don't the official handlers. I've discovered that their clicking language contains mathematical sequences, harmonic progressions that encode information about mineral deposits, structural weaknesses in the terraces, even weather patterns. They bring me gifts—tiny metal shavings that I've learned to weave into tools, conductive threads that carry signals between my various monitoring stations.

But perhaps most significantly, I exist as a bridge between the **Ancient Founders** and the newer generations. While the Founders hoard knowledge in their fungal-network-linked minds, and the young Colonists focus on immediate survival, I walk the terraces daily, collecting data, noticing patterns. I'm the one who first recognized that the Glasswings' migration patterns correlate with the polyps' growth cycles, which correlates with the quality of the mineral harvest from the following season.

Sometimes, in the deepest hours before dawn, when the steam engines cycle down and the myriapods pause in their ceaseless clicking, I climb to the highest point of the Marshward Terrace and listen to what the stones themselves are saying. The terraces are indeed alive—I've felt it in the subtle shifts of temperature, the way the condensation patterns change before geological events, the faint harmonic resonance that suggests a vast, distributed consciousness spanning miles of carved stone and cultivated ecosystem.

I am becoming something new—not quite Colonist, not quite part of the native fauna, but a genuine child of Vorthak itself. My role is evolution's experiment: a being adapted not just to survive in this mechanical-biological hybrid world, but to understand and guide its continued development.

When the Great Condensation comes and the lower terraces flood, I'm already preparing—redirecting polyp growth to create new drainage channels, coordinating with the myriapod swarms to reinforce critical junctions, ensuring that when the waters recede, the terraces will be stronger, more beautiful, more perfectly tuned to their strange symphony of steam and crystal and clicking metal.
