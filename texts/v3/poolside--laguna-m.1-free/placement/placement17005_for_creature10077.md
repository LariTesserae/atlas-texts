In this world of perpetual drip and stone, I would find myself as a **moss-weaver**, my hands forever stained green with the pigments of the living tapestries I tend. My dwelling would be the smallest of the squat stone structures, nestled where the crescent hollow cradles the shallowest pools, for my work demands proximity to the mossy crevices that bleed into the settlement’s paths. My skin, like all of ours, is taut and weathered by mist, but my forearms are marked with bioluminescent patches—fungal companions cultivated over years of careful tending. At night, these patches pulse faintly, casting a soft cerulean glow as I work, a light I’ve learned to read like a second language. My hair is braided in tight spirals, each strand threaded with strips of shed moss to absorb the damp and ward off the chill that seeps into bone.  

My days begin before the High Drip, when the terraces are slickest and the flagstones most treacherous. I am one of the few who still ventures to the *deep* pools, where the water turns black and still—a place most avoid for fear of the shadows. There, I gather the *nightmoss*, its fronds iridescent in the murk, to weave into the sacred patterns that line the aqueduct’s choked channels. My people believe the moss is the land’s circulatory system, channeling its "blood" (the water) through veins of root and fiber. I’ve come to agree; when the moss thrives, the stones sing more loudly, their drips rhythmic and sure.  

But I am also a **stone singer**, though I practice in secret. I’ve always heard the rocks’ faint tones beneath the *plinks* and groans—a low, resonant hum that matches the rhythm of my heartbeat. In the dead of evening, when the settlement’s silence is thickest, I press my palms to the weeping rocks and let my voice rise in a murmur. The stones respond, their secretions quickening into tiny streams. It’s dangerous knowledge; the elders warn that disturbing the rocks’ rest can unravel their hold on the terraces. Yet I’ve seen what happens when the moss weakens, when the drip slows to a trickle. The land’s breath grows shallow.  

I carry the weight of a name: **Rivulet**, given to me by my grandmother during her final Convergence. She whispered, *“You flow where others stagnate,”* as she pressed a shard of obsidian into my palm—the tool I use to carve moss patterns into the stone. I’ve never fully understood, but lately, I notice things. Cracks in the southern terrace that weren’t there last season. Pools that evaporate faster than they should. The moss here is dying, and with it, the stones’ hum grows fainter.  

The elders speak of omens, of the Stillness drawing near, but I think they’re wrong. The stones aren’t dying—they’re *calling*. There’s a discord in their song, a note of desperation beneath the drip. Last week, I found a patch of moss that glowed like starlight, though no fungus should thrive where the pools turn brackish. When I wove it into the aqueduct’s channel, the water ran clearer, the algae blooming in riotous hues of violet and gold.  

Something is awakening beneath the terraces.  

I suspect I am not merely a weaver or singer, but a **bridge**—a living conduit between the land’s dormant magic and its fading humanity. The stillness the others fear isn’t an end but a metamorphosis. I’ve begun to dream of the plateau below, of a vast subterranean network of roots and stone where the water remembers its source. In my dreams, I am not human but something older, a tendril of the land itself, coaxing life from the cracks.  

When the Convergence arrives, I’ll perform the Rite of the Unbroken Cycle as the others expect—but I’ll also hum the stones’ true song, the one they’ve only whispered to me. If I’m right, the terraces will not collapse. They’ll bloom.  

Until then, I work. My fingers dance in moss and water, weaving, coaxing, listening. The land’s breath is in my lungs, its pulse in my blood. I am Rivulet, and I am the thread that holds the unraveling together.
