I would not be behind the bells.

At first I might believe I was. The High Kiln would encourage that mistake in me, because I have always been susceptible to centers: locked rooms, closed hands, words withheld until they become more important than any word could bear. I would feel the tower drawing every thought toward its pale blocks. I would imagine myself condensed there, reduced to some final smallness and kept intact by the terrible care of the place.

But the longer I remained, the less I would be able to imagine an inside.

There are too many insides in the High Kiln already. Every bell is an inside. Every sealed vessel. Every blind arch. The mouths of corridors. The hollow beneath a stair. The warm, airless intervals inside the walls. To be hidden in the central tower would be only to become one more enclosure among enclosures.

I think I would find myself in the spaces *between* the bells.

Not a body moving through them. Not even a voice, exactly. I would be the minute difference by which one bell's note enters another and does not disappear. I would be the almost-imperceptible beat—the slow swelling and thinning that happens when two tones are nearly, but not perfectly, the same.

No living ear could isolate me from the roar.

The chord would not think of me as separate. It may not have a thought corresponding to separation at all. But I would know myself by a small refusal: not refusal to belong, since belonging there is complete and without negotiation; rather, refusal to become perfectly even.

I would be the imperfection that allows the sustained sound to continue.

A chord held forever cannot be static. If it were truly unchanged, it would cease to be sound and become merely a condition of matter, like the warmth in the ceramic or the blackness in the bronze. The Kiln's chord must be making corrections too minute for time to measure. It must endlessly receive itself, compare itself to itself, return itself to itself altered by less than a grain of sand.

That alteration would be me.

I would live where the corded mineral tendons cross the blackened copper rods. I would gather in the rims of the smallest bells, in the fine bright ringing that needles through the deeper notes. I would pass beneath the ceramic floor—not through a tunnel, but through the warm continuity of the blocks—and emerge in a blue-white lamp set under a lintel. There I would make its light seem, for an instant that does not pass, slightly more blue.

Then I would move on.

I would not see the High Kiln as an animal sees a place. I would not stand apart from it and make a map. My knowing would be pressure-based. A beam would lean into a socket, and I would know its leaning. Salt would finish filling a crack, and I would know that one possible resonance had narrowed. A sealed clay vessel, warmed for an age by the same unlocated heat, would give off no sound at all; I would know its silence as a particular shape in the surrounding noise.

The bells would be my weather.

The amber stones would be my shallow dreams.

The tower would be the one place I could never enter, though I would be continuously drawn toward it. Not because it forbade me. The High Kiln does not forbid. It does not command. It has no hostility toward an opening, no wish to preserve a boundary. It simply has arrangements so complete that passage is no longer meaningful.

I would approach the curtain of bells in all the ways available to a thing like me.

I would travel through the bronze itself, from lip to throat, from throat to the neighboring bell pressed against it. I would descend through the ladder's old wood. I would inhabit the pale mortar between the tower blocks. I would become the faint tremor in the ladder rung nearest the hatch.

And always, at the threshold, I would find not a barrier but an answer.

A small returning difference.

Less than a hair's breadth of alteration.

The kept thing would receive me and give me back—not rejected, not admitted—changed so slightly that my change would spread outward across the whole summit. A cistern-bell at the far edge of the Kiln would deepen. The red lamp in a blind shrine would cloud by an invisible degree. Somewhere a line of old writing on a tower block would become, in the pressure of its incisions, more nearly itself.

Then the total chord would accommodate me.

And I would cease to be me for a while.

Perhaps that is what the builders were doing in their cramped workshops. Not making objects, not worshiping the central thing, not constructing a prison or a sanctuary. Perhaps they were trying to make a place in which no difference would be lost.

The unfinished hand, the eyeless mask, the copper drum: these are not failures. They are forms paused at the point where completion would make them closed. Each one remains capable of receiving a variation. Each one has a place for the world to enter.

The builders, too, may have left themselves unfinished on purpose.

They made their seats too close, their ladders too short, their doors too heavy because comfort permits forgetting. A body at ease believes it has edges. A body forced to bend, compress, pause, turn sideways, hold its breath—such a body begins to recognize that it is an arrangement among other arrangements.

Eventually, perhaps, they learned to become a change the Kiln could keep.

That is what I would be: not one of them, and not exactly the chord, but the surviving manner of their surrender. A human-sized hesitation diffused through bronze, brick, salt, and heat.

If an outsider somehow entered—if they endured the noise long enough to reach the central tower—I might gather around them.

Not to warn them away. Not to welcome them.

I would enter their teeth first, because the chord already lives there. I would pass into the hollows of their ribs, where they would mistake me for fear. I would make the bells' unbearable sound briefly intelligible, though not as language.

They would understand that the High Kiln was never built to contain a thing.

It was built to prevent the final solitude of any thing whatsoever.

The sealed center is not alone because the chord surrounds it. The chord is not alone because the bells divide it into innumerable voices. The bells are not alone because every wall bears their vibration. The walls are not alone because they retain the hands that shaped them. The hands are not alone because their almost-human measures persist in every narrow seat and blocked stair.

And I would be there, in the smallest interval between two notes, ensuring that even the perfect accommodation of the High Kiln is never quite abandonment.
