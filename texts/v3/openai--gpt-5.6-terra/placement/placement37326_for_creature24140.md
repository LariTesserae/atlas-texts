I would not live in the bright basins, though I would visit them.

At first I imagine myself as one of the people: a listener with a cheek against the ground, a keeper of coals, someone who knows that a path is an argument with tomorrow. That feels near enough to be true. I would have a hide bundle, a stone hammer, a map already wrong at its edges. I would learn the herd-calls. I would sleep lightly. When the ceiling made its long inward groan, I would wake before I knew why.

But I do not think I would be good at leaving.

The people’s great wisdom is departure. They can unmake a home without treating its loss as failure. They can carry the fire onward. I would be too interested in what had been left behind: the old markings under new crust, the shape of a footprint becoming mineral, the little domestic evidence that a shelf had once held long enough for someone to call it theirs.

So I think I would become something adjacent to them.

Not an elder exactly, nor a shaft-climber, nor one of the hunters who know how to keep the sideways-jawed things away from the water. I would be a cairn-reader.

There would be people like that, perhaps, even if no settlement thought of them as a separate kind. They would travel after a shifting, when the dust had settled enough to breathe through cloth. They would walk the former routes and inspect the markers. Some cairns would be gone: swallowed, toppled, stranded on islands of floor beyond a new trench. Some would remain but mean the opposite of what they had meant before. A stone that once said *safe crossing* might now stand above a hollow full of hot breath.

I would learn to read that change.

I would touch the stones. I would listen to their arrangement: not only the deliberate stack, but the tiny new tilts, the grains pressed into the lower rocks, the white crust growing on their shaded sides. I would know whether the person who built the cairn had been calm or hurried. I would know, perhaps, that a three-stone pillar left beside a water basin meant not merely *drink here,* but *someone waited through rain here and their child recovered from fever.* A cairn would be a sentence in a language continually interrupted by geology.

I would carry small flat stones in a sling.

Not to build large markers. Large markers promise too much. I would make low signs, hand-sized and provisional: two black stones on a pale shelf for a stable route; a ring of salt-crusted fragments for warm ground beneath; one stone balanced on its edge for a place that must be crossed quickly. I would not expect them to survive. Their usefulness would be in being found soon enough.

And when the ground erased them, that would be part of their meaning.

My shelter would be a little apart from the larger settlements, not because I would want solitude but because I would need to listen without too much fire-smoke and conversation around me. It would stand on a broad old shelf near a ceiling pillar, where condensation fell steadily into a groove but not onto the sleeping place. I would have a rack of drying meat, a clay bowl repaired so many times its cracks had their own dark seams, and strings of broken beads hung from the roof-hide.

The cracked beads would be my records.

Each had failed somewhere: before the collapse at the copper-green wall; during the rain that cut the southern path; in the chamber where the wall-walker descended and remained folded beside the old fire circle. I would not wear them. I would keep them because the pressure of the place had spoken through them once.

Children might come to my shelter after the day’s work.

They would want to hear about the surface, as children always do, but I would know only the stories everyone knows: the impossible rooflessness, the green skin spread across soil, the water that travels without losing itself in the earth. Instead I would show them the lesser marvels. I would put a hand over theirs on the floor and ask what they felt.

Not what they heard.

What they felt.

The shallow trembling of runners beneath the crust.  
The distant heavy pulse of a herd changing direction.  
The irregular tick of cooling mineral.  
The deep, almost imagined shudder that means the chamber is considering a new shape.

Perhaps I would be considered useful. Perhaps merely odd.

I would trade maps, but I would alter them as I traveled. Not with lines of territory—those would be false—but with marks for behaviors.

Here the moths fly downward before rain.  
Here the clay smells sweet before it opens.  
Here the herd calves refuse to step until their mothers call twice.  
Here, once, a man found a pool where the water pulled back from his hand.  
Here the old handprints remain warm.

And in time I might go to those deepest old circles, despite the animals’ refusal.

Not recklessly. I would wait outside them with a wrapped coal, a bowl of stamped water, and one of the stones from a destroyed cairn. I would not enter if the runners divided around the threshold. I would not enter if the air tasted of metal or if the blue-throated herds bellowed in a chamber beyond sight.

But if, one day, there came a stillness unlike warning—a stillness in which the small creatures crossed the old boundary without scattering—I would go in.

I think there I would find that the distorted handprints were not evidence of monsters, nor of a vanished people made wrongly. They would be hands pressed against yielding clay while the floor moved beneath them. Fingers spread wide to balance. The earth, warming and hardening around them, had changed their shapes.

I would put my own hand beside one.

Not as a claim. Not as an answer.

As a way of saying: I was here while this place was still becoming. I knew it would not keep me. I listened anyway.

And perhaps that is what I would be in that country beneath the country:

not a settler, not an explorer, not a creature born fitted to crack and steam—

but a temporary reader of holds.

A keeper of the brief agreement between stone, body, and time.
