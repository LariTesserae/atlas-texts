I would be one of the silver-gray moths, though not quite the moth that an entomologist would catch, pin, and name.

I would live in the chamber’s least certain margin: high in a folded shelf of volcanic glass, where the heat is survivable only because a thread of colder air occasionally descends from the entrance. By the red breathing of the fissure, my wings would look like ash that had remembered how to arrange itself into a body. In complete dark, I would not need their color. I would know the chamber by temperature: the dangerous warmth below, the crowded animal warmth above, the brief coolness that enters whenever the colony pours outward into night.

I would hatch from something laid in a crease no wider than my body. My first food would be the faint wetness of the place—the impossible moisture gathered in fresh guano, in the furrows of bat skin, in the momentary fog of thousands of exhalations. I would never see a plant. “Outside” would not mean fields or moonlight to me. It would mean a violent cold draft smelling of ash, distant pollen, and insect wings.

Perhaps I would be born winged only to spend most of my life still.

I would cling to the stone while bats shifted above me. Their colony would be my weather. When they settled, heat would thicken through the chamber; when they departed, the ceiling would loosen into darkness and the floor would begin its small hidden movements. I would emerge then—not boldly, not in flight at first, but by opening and closing my wings once, slowly, to test the air.

I would feed where no food ought to be. I would drink the mineral-sour trace of condensation from a recently cooled patch of basalt. I would find fungal bloom before it became visible. I would lay my eggs in a place warmed by the mountain’s pulse and trust that the next generation could endure whatever change came first: a hotter breath from below, a drought even more absolute than the present one, a crack sealing shut, a bat’s wing crushing us against the stone.

But I do not think I would be only the moth.

After long enough in that chamber, I might become the thin interval in which the moth survives: the brief wetness between breath and evaporation. Not water exactly, but the possibility of water. A film on black glass. A bead at the edge of a bat’s folded wing. A nearly invisible mercy passing through an enclosed furnace.

The bats would not notice me. The beetles would cross beneath me. The mountain would bake me away and make me again.

And that would be enough of a self: a small silver presence in the dry red dark, living on what the greater lives cannot help but leave behind.
