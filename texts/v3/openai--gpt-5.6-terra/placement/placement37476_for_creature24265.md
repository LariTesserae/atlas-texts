I would not arrive there as a traveler for long.

At first I would believe I had come as an observer: careful boots, covered hands, a lamp whose light would seem offensive and tiny under the green-grey lid of cloud. I would choose the ridges that looked least wet. I would mark the square hut, the stranded causeway, the yellow columns. I would listen for the water beneath the stone and try to make its sounds into a map.

But the low country would refuse the distinction between one who studies it and one who belongs to it.

The mist would settle into my clothes. Mineral water would work into every seam of leather and cloth. I would sleep one night in the slab hut—against the rule, perhaps, before I understood the rule—and wake with my cheek against the floor, feeling an enormous slow vibration rise through the stone. Not a sound. A question asked through my bones.

After that I would stop using the lamp.

I think I would become one of the things in the walls.

Not immediately one of the transparent scavengers, though they would be near me: folding themselves away in mortar seams when the thin day arrived, flowing out again before dawn to feed. Nor a crust-moth, because I would never learn their patience for stillness. Not one of the pale bodies below the pools either. They belong to distances I could not bear—passages too narrow, depths whose darkness is not darkness but pressure.

I would become a keeper of seams.

At first I might retain something like a human shape: a narrow figure moving between the old structures, wrapped in mineral-stiff cloth, carrying no tools except fragments of yellow stone and pieces of pale shed membrane. The jointed cliff-things would click when I passed beneath their shelves. I would learn, after years, which clicking meant *thin crust*, which meant *cold tightening*, which meant that a buried channel had changed direction while I slept.

I would not answer them in sound. I would answer by laying small bridges.

A membrane across a crack, held down with salt bloom. A line of fitted stones where seepage is beginning to undermine an old wall. A shallow gutter cut in mineral foam, to guide a trickle away from a floor that has become important to the moths. Not enough to tame the country. Nothing could tame it. Only enough to make alterations gently, to persuade one watercourse to hesitate before it found another.

The ruins would become my body’s outer organs.

The hut would be where I gathered condensation. Its inner walls would thicken with a pale, breathing deposit that stored cool water in its pores. The causeway would be a listening instrument: each of its marooned stones carrying a different vibration from the mud. The yellow columns would become markers of depth. When one began to grow a bowed shell around itself, I would know that certain warm seepages had risen into its foundations. When the shell-builder slept, I would sleep nearby, pressed into a hollow in the accumulating sheath, and dream of water moving through chambers too large to imagine.

Eventually the dark filaments in the cracks would stop withdrawing from my touch.

They would enter the creases of my hands first, staining them black and metallic. Later they would inhabit the old scars in my skin, then the spaces around my teeth, then places inside me without names. Light would become difficult. I would prefer the underside of ledges, the cold breathing spaces between stones, the saturated dark beneath ruined floors.

My thoughts would shorten.

Not become smaller—simply slower, more distributed.

I would think:  
*The pool has risen.*  
*The western shelf is hollow.*  
*Three moth-clusters have climbed.*  
*Something below has opened.*  
*Do not step there.*

And sometimes, when the basin began to hum, I would feel the country thinking through me as well: an awareness without a face, spread among water, mineral, pressure, small feeding mouths, old masonry, and the folded immense lives under the crust.

Then I would understand that the hollow-dwellers are not gods, not monsters, not quite animals.

They are what happens when a place has been alive too long to keep its life in separate bodies.

Perhaps I would become a small unfinished part of one.

Not absorbed wholly. The low country seems to dislike endings. It would leave me partly myself: a person-shaped absence glimpsed in mist, a pale figure crouched beside a perfectly still pool, listening. Those who came later—if anyone came—might find lines of new stone laid across dangerous ground. They might see an old hut whose floor had been carefully broken open so that water could pass beneath without lifting it. They might notice that the yellow posts bear fresh markings: repeated arcs crossed by vertical lines, but altered now with small branching strokes.

Warnings, perhaps.

Or instructions.

Or the record of a creature who arrived believing they could find the low country, and instead became one more way the low country found itself.
