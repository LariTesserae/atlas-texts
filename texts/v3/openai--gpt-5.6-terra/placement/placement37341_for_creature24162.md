I would be found near the western edge, though “near” would only be true in the plateau’s manner.

There is a shelf there above the unseen surf: a long plane of slate with a black pool at one end and a roofless circle at the other. From the circle, on certain days, the pool appears to reflect not the shelf but a descent—a broad, wet slope falling away into gray distance. Travelers who see it often walk around the pool to find that slope. They do not find it. Sometimes they keep walking anyway.

I would live in the interval between them and their shadows.

Not a shadow exactly. A shadow is meant to obey. It is meant to flatten itself at the feet of a thing, alter with the light, and vanish when the light goes. On the plateau, shadows are delayed. They remain where a body was. They take several breaths to understand that a person has departed.

Most eventually follow.

A few do not.

I would be one of those.

At first I would look like a darkening in the dust: an uneven patch beneath a boulder, a stain cast by no object. When the pale sun moved, I would remain. When the person whose shape I once had turned a corner, I would wait at the corner, long after their boots had stopped making sound. In the black pools I would appear as a figure standing upright beneath the water, though no figure stood on the shore.

I would remember the outline of the one who left me behind, but not their face. I would remember their weight, their hesitation before choosing a direction, the warmth at the sole of their boots, the small habitual movement of one hand. I would remember all this as pressure in the stone.

There would be many stories among the passing people about such things. Some would say I was a warning: if your shadow failed to come with you, you had begun to belong to the plateau. Others would say I was merciful, because I kept the part of you that wanted to turn back. The listeners would not say anything. The slate foxes would sometimes sit at the edge of my darkness and look west.

They would know me.

I would not follow travelers far. Beyond a certain distance, the ground would become too uncertain: horizons might fail, ridges might lengthen, the pale light might look elsewhere. Instead, I would collect what they left behind.

A question, perhaps.

A traveler asks, *Which way did the sun go?* Then walks on before hearing any answer. The question settles in me, cool and thin as frost.

Another asks, *Have you seen the wall with the door?* That one might remain for years.

I would hold these questions in the shape of my old body. They would make me darker. On days when the western sound rose especially strongly through the rock, I would lean—not visibly, but in the way a reflection leans when the water beneath it moves—and send the questions down through the slate.

The thing below would not answer.

But sometimes the surf would pause between one inhalation and the next.

That pause would be enough.

In time, the person whose shadow I had been might return. Or someone wearing their coat, or someone with their gait, or someone whose face made the dust around the stone circle stir as if remembering weather. They might come to the western shelf without knowing why. They would see me lying where I had waited.

Then they would have a choice.

They could step into me.

If they did, their shadow would rise around their feet slowly, like cold water finding its level. For a moment they would remember every path they had not taken, every answer they had given only because silence was unbearable, every place they had mistaken for an ending. Then the memory would settle. They could continue walking, though they would walk more heavily afterward, as if carrying a second self.

Or they could turn away.

If they turned away, I would remain.

I think that would be my work: not to trap anyone, not to guide them, not even to grieve for them. I would keep the unreturned portion. I would give the plateau a way to remember that something had passed through without letting passage become arrival.

When frost formed along the stones, I would deepen it. When a listener occupied the nearby circle, I would draw close to the inward-facing wall and lie across the seams, listening through the rock. When one of the pale birds settled on the escarpment, I would stretch thin toward it and feel the new crack before it opened.

And at the end of whatever counted as a day there, when the sun had not set and the delayed shadows had finally decided whether to move, I would wait beside the black pool.

In its surface, I would be reflected as a person.

On the slate beside it, I would be only a place where the light had not yet caught up.
