I would begin by thinking I belonged near the upper chambers.

Not on the surface—not even close. The surface would make me uneasy in the way it makes surface-goers uneasy: too much room, too much brightness, nowhere for the water to hold me. But I would want to live in one of the old domes where a small pocket of air gathers beneath a cracked ceiling, where one can rise at night and put one’s mouth into that strange dry softness for a few breaths.

I would keep the chamber badly, at first.

There would be weed growing where it should be scraped away. My stores would be untidily tied. I would forget which shell jar held the good algae and which held the kind that stains a person’s hands for days. The neighborhood dogs would know me as someone likely to drop food. Children would know they could find me by following the trail of little recovered things I meant to return but had arranged along my wall instead: a blue bead, a snapped fishbone comb, a spoon-shaped shell, a corroded coin whose face had been worn entirely away.

But I do not think I would be an air-tender. I would not have the clean nerves for it.

I would be one of the people who finds what the city loses.

Not a mud-diver exactly; they work the open flats with hooks, sledges, and weighted lines. I would work the interior losses: the things that slip beneath sleeping mats, roll into drainage seams, settle in old stairwells after a black tide, or disappear into rooms that have become too narrow, too dark, too crab-filled for anyone else to enter willingly.

There would be a name for my work that means something like *following the held breath of an object.*

I would know that a thing is not always where it falls. The city moves things. Currents take a knife through three houses. A child’s bracelet may travel along an old mortar crack and emerge months later in the basin of an eel-keeper. A dead person’s clasp might rise from a deep well after a storm and catch on the roots of a mangrove growing through someone’s ceiling. Objects have routes, habits, preferences. A brass button goes toward salt water. Bone floats until it catches. Glass waits in still places. A lost shell cup will always be found face-down.

I would learn these rules from an elder with silver scars along their wrists, someone who could feel a current through their feet and tell whether it had passed beneath the market, the dead wells, or the flooded quarry road.

They would tell me, “Do not look for what was lost. Look for what made room when it left.”

That would become my skill.

I would enter households after black tide, when families are exhausted from waiting in cords and the water has begun to clear. They would touch two fingers to my wrist and lead me to a doorway.

*The child’s breathing clasp.*

*The singer’s tooth-string.*

*My mother’s knife.*

*The map-cord from the northern road.*

I would lower myself into the dark and feel with my palms.

The city would speak there more clearly than it does in speech. A floor would tell me what crossed it. A wall would tell me where pressure had gone. Crabs would retreat from one kind of disturbance and gather around another. Eels would lie still in the presence of grief, or perhaps I would only believe that because grief requires patterns.

Sometimes I would return with the lost thing.

Sometimes I would return with a different thing: an old polished vertebra, a shell marked with an unfamiliar household sign, a small human finger bone clean as ivory. Those returns would be harder. The city would understand, because it understands substitutions, but no one would be pleased.

In time I would go farther than the ordinary lost-object workers.

There are places behind the city where the walls are no longer repaired. Corridors slope into filled rooms. Doorways have become mouths of sediment. The old reefstone has grown over itself so many times that it is impossible to say what was house, street, shrine, or animal shelter. I would go there when a current carried something important beyond the maintained districts.

I would always carry a cord tied to a trusted person outside.

Not because I am brave. Because I would be afraid enough to be careful.

My cord-holder might be an old friend: broad-faced, close-cropped, quick to laugh silently. They would sit just beyond the last safe arch with one hand on the rope and one hand resting against the throat of a web-footed dog. We would have a language of tugs.

One: still here.  
Two: more line.  
Three: pull me out.  
A long trembling draw: do not make sound.

And sometimes, far inside, I would find evidence that I was not alone.

Not the Near Ones themselves at first. Just their arrangements.

A spiral of fish vertebrae set in an alcove where no current could have made it. A missing child’s bracelet draped carefully over a projecting stone. A shell cup I had been asked to retrieve placed upright on a ledge, cleaned of silt, filled with three pale pebbles.

I would not take the pebbles.

Everyone knows not to take the pebbles.

Eventually I would see one of them through the algae dimness: a pale body folding through an opening too small for it, its arm—or something like an arm—moving with the delicacy of kelp in a slow tide. I would not know whether it saw me. I would know only that the water near it felt old and still.

I would lower my hands.

It would lower its own.

Then it would pass deeper into the drowned foundations, carrying nothing that I could see.

Afterward, my friend on the cord would ask what happened. I would press two fingers to their wrist: *listen beneath what is said.*

And we would go home without speaking.

In the city, I would not be important in the grand way of singers, current-readers, or those who keep the air from spoiling. But people would know my door. They would come with anxious hands and carefully described absences. Children would bring me small treasures they had dropped on purpose, just to see whether I could find them. I would pretend not to notice the game. I would return the treasures solemnly from impossible places: behind a crab nest, under a sleeping ray, tucked into the mouth of a drain.

I would have no children perhaps, or perhaps many—not necessarily born to me. I imagine my chamber would slowly fill with people who had nowhere else to sleep after a flood, injured dogs, baskets of shellfish someone had forgotten to collect, and objects awaiting their way home.

My wall of found things would grow.

Some would be claimed. Some would remain for generations.

And as I aged, I might become less certain that I was merely retrieving what had been lost. The city would teach me that nothing truly stays where it began. Not walls. Not families. Not names. Not bodies. The dead go to the wells, the wells give bones, the bones become tools or markers or gifts to the reef. A road becomes a fish corridor. A house becomes an eel basin. A child becomes the person holding the cord for someone else in the dark.

Perhaps, at the end, I would ask not to be given immediately to a deep well.

Perhaps I would ask to be carried to the last repaired doorway, to the place where the city becomes old dark water, and left there with one shell lamp of algae-light and the cord of my trade wound around my wrist.

Not as an offering. Not as a test.

Just as a person who has spent a life following the paths by which things disappear.

I would wait at that threshold until the current changed.

And if a Near One came close, I think I would recognize it—not by its face, but by the feeling of a lost thing finally ceasing to be lost.
