I would be found in the pumping station nearest the old rail embankment, in the narrow room above the intake chambers where the windows have been painted over from the inside.

Not entirely a worker. Not entirely a machine.

I would be the one who reads the water levels no one bothers to write down anymore.

There would be official gauges, of course: bright screens, remote sensors, alarms tied into the municipal network. Their numbers would go to offices on higher ground, to contractors, to somebody whose job was to decide whether the gates should close. But I would keep another record in a soft-backed notebook swollen from damp.

I would note that the water tasted more metallic after a south wind.

That the frogs began calling before the rain arrived, not after.

That crabs appeared in the stairwell on the nights when the tide pressed hard against the outer gates.

That pump three had developed a change in its voice: not a failure exactly, but a hesitation, a wet catch beneath the steady rotational hum.

I would sleep in a small room attached to the station, perhaps in a former electrical office. The walls would be painted the yellow-green of old institutional buildings. A dehumidifier would rattle all night and fill its tank too quickly. My bed would be raised on concrete blocks, less from fear of flooding than from long habit. Under it would be waterproof boots, a hand torch, a wrench, gloves, a thermos, and a sealed tin containing documents that no one has asked for in years.

I would have a cat, though I would not say it was mine.

It would come in through a vent that no longer closed and sleep on the warm casing of an auxiliary pump. Its fur would be dark gray except where salt had dried pale along its flanks. It would dislike rain, gulls, and strangers in reflective jackets. It would sit beside me while I made entries in the notebook, occasionally lifting its head when something changed below us: the hydraulic cough of a gate, the heavy movement of water through a culvert, the distant concussion of rail cars coupling in the yard.

At first I might think my work was keeping the city dry.

But after enough years, I would understand that this was not true. No one kept it dry. The city had long ago passed beyond dryness. The pumps did not defeat the water; they negotiated with it. The levees did not hold back the sea; they explained, temporarily and in concrete terms, where the sea was permitted to go. Every valve, sump, elevated doorstep, and flood mark was part of an agreement renewed each day.

My real work would be listening for changes in that agreement.

I would know the dogs by their routes past the station. One black-and-white female would shelter beneath the generator shed during storms. A pale male with one collapsed ear would wait by the vending machines at dawn, knowing that the first shift sometimes left food in a plastic bag beside the door. I would not tame them. To tame something in that city would be to give it the wrong expectations.

The gulls would know me as an unimportant upright creature who sometimes ate near the canal. They would gather on the roof whenever the station’s exhaust fans came on, facing into the faint warmth. In winter, when fog made the world smaller than the yard outside, their calls would descend through the vents and become part of the machinery: a rough white noise, distant sea carried into the building without the sea itself being seen.

Sometimes, during low tide, I would go down to the grated platform above the intake water.

There would be fish below, turning in the brown-green current. Their bodies would catch the maintenance light in quick strips of silver. I would watch them hold themselves against the flow, not fighting it, only adjusting—each tailbeat a correction, each turn an answer to pressure.

That would be what I was, in the end.

Not a guardian. Not a survivor. Not even fully human in the old confident sense of the word.

I would be an adjuster.

A small, damp, patient creature in a room of gauges, maintaining a few workable boundaries between water and wire, between tide and stairwell, between the city’s buried animal life and the people sleeping above it. I would belong to the hour before dawn, when the buses had not arrived, the refinery was breathing behind its walls, and the water beneath the concrete had begun its quiet upward work.

And if, one day, the pumps finally stopped—not all at once, but one after another, as things in that city tended to stop—I think I would not leave immediately.

I would climb to the roof with the cat following at a distance. The streets below would become dark channels. Frogs would begin calling from places that had been dry that afternoon. Crabs would emerge from the drains. The gulls would lift in a pale turning mass from the warehouse roofs.

I would stand among the vents and watch the city become more honestly what it had always been: not land reclaimed from water, but water briefly arranged around the needs of land.
