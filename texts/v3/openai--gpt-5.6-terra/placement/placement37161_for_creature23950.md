At first I think I would be one of the people who keeps a key ring too heavy for any pocket.

Not an engineer exactly, though I would wear the reflective gloves and know the painted code by habit. I would live somewhere midway up the chamber wall, in a room reached by two staircases because the first one is often closed during a pressure reversal. My window would not look outside. It would look across: onto the back of a water tank, a blue-lit strip of maintenance flooring, the dark undersides of other people’s balconies.

My address would be a sequence of conditions.

Below the green conduit.  
Across from the tap that runs warm at midnight.  
Third door after the wall begins to sweat white.

I would maintain a thing no one notices until it fails: the small network of overflow channels that carries condensation away from stairwells, cupboards, electrical boxes, sleeping rooms. I would clear mineral crust from the channels with a narrow brass tool. I would lift grates. I would kneel in steam. I would know by the color of the residue whether the water came from the clean pipes, the old pipes, or the pipes nobody has admitted are still connected to anything.

People would call for me when their rooms developed weather.

Their floor became too hot.  
Their ceiling began to breathe.  
A line of droplets appeared over a child’s bed.  
The air in their kitchen tasted faintly metallic.  
Something in the wall had started ticking at a new interval.

I would arrive with my tools and my small lamp and apologize for bringing more heat in with me. I would listen with my ear against the wall. In that city, listening would be a form of repair before it was a form of knowledge.

But after a while, I think I would become less like a worker and more like one of the city’s unnoticed senses.

Not its heart: the heat below would have that claim.

Not its lungs: those would be the fan rooms behind their grilles.

Not even its hands, because there would be many hands—gloved, burned, deft, anonymous—opening panels, tightening couplings, setting cups beneath leaks before they could become problems.

I would be something smaller. A nerve ending, perhaps. The part that feels a drop of water where there should not be one.

I would wake before the warning tone because some distant pipe had changed its pitch. I would know that the west galleries were about to dry out, not from a reading on a dial but because the condensation on my own window had gathered into larger beads than usual. I would remember which old woman near Pump Three could not sleep without the low rattle of the auxiliary fan, which children had hidden washer-tokens under the fourth drain cover, which sealed apartments had begun to collect warmth behind their doors.

I would keep records no office requested:

- the first day the pale vine reached the lamp housing;
- the last time the lower rail cart passed with an empty load;
- the sequence of three warning tones that preceded the Laundry Break;
- the names of people who had once lived behind doors now marked reserve;
- the places where the chamber made a sound almost like speech.

Perhaps I would write these records on heatproof slips and fold them into the back of the maintenance manuals. Perhaps decades later someone would find them and mistake them for a superstition. They would be partly right.

Because the longer I lived there, the more I would begin to suspect that I had not come down merely to maintain the city.

The city had been maintaining me.

Aboveground, I might have been someone who mistook stillness for safety. Someone who expected roads to stay where they were painted and walls to be final. Down here, I would learn that stability was not a condition but an activity performed continuously by ordinary people. A dry bed was maintained. A quiet room was maintained. A safe stair rail, a clean cup, a green air line, a child’s sleeping temperature—each was made again every day.

I would become good at this kind of faith: not belief in permanence, but belief in repair.

And perhaps, in the very deepest part of the chamber, below the ceramic doors and the hot stone, there would be something else that I gradually became responsible for. Not the industrial vein itself. Not the unnamed furnace or buried machine. Something stranger and less grand.

A listening device.

An old instrument embedded in the rock, built before anyone now alive understood its purpose. It would have no screen, only a series of glass bulbs filled with mineral fluid. When the heat shifted below, the bulbs would cloud or clear. When a new crack opened somewhere in the chamber, one would give a faint, pale pulse. It would not predict disaster precisely. It would only register that the underground was continuing to make decisions.

The lower workers would know of it. They would let me sit with it because I was patient and because I did not insist on naming things too quickly.

At first I would tend it as equipment.

Then as a patient.

Then as an elder relative who could no longer speak in a language anyone understood.

I would clean its housings. Replace its lamps. Record the changes in the mineral fluid. On some nights, when the city was sleeping behind shutters and the fans had entered their low, contained murmur, I would sit beside it with a cup of water and feel the warmth through the floor.

Above me, hundreds of rooms would hold their private climates: wet cloth, cooling soup, sleeping children, people listening to radios, people alone with old photographs of daylight. The garden roots would be drawing from the runoff channel. The colored pipes would carry their coded burdens. The city would sweat, breathe, tighten, release.

And I would understand that I had not found myself beneath the city.

I had found myself in the interval between a leak and the hand that notices it.

I would be the person who notices.

Or, if the city had its way, eventually I would be the noticing itself: a small, careful awareness moving through warm walls, remembering where the water ought to go.
