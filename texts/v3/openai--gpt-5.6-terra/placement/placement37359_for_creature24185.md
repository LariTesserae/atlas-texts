I would not be one of the large creatures.

Not the pike, because they belong to intention. Not the carp, because they have the patience of old coins. Not even the catfish-shaped uncertainty in the far water. Those things possess the room too completely. They are visible, even when they hide.

I think I would live near 4B.

Not directly beneath the inspection lamp, where the white light makes everything too definite, but behind the pipework it illuminates: in the narrow dark where a coupling has failed without quite bursting. There would be a continual thread of water there, colder than the surrounding water at first, then faintly warm where it passes some surviving line below. The leak would be too small for the pumps to care about. No alarm would have recorded it. It would have gone on for years, making a fan-shaped stain down the pipe and feeding a small, private weather system in the rust.

I would be a growth there.

Perhaps an iron bacteria colony: a dark velvet, a red-brown filamentous bloom, something that looks like rot but has its own slow appetite. I would take dissolved iron from the water and arrange it into soft ochre sheaths. I would gather on the underside of the flange, along the bolt heads, inside the fine crack where old paint has lifted. The valve’s metal would be both my ground and my food.

From a distance, I would be nothing remarkable: another patch of corrosion among thousands. A little dark beard trailing in the current. If a minnow brushed through me, I would bend. If the automatic valve opened, I would flatten nearly to nothing against the pipe, then gradually rise again after the surge.

But I would know the room in a way the fish do not.

Not as a map. Not as a set of hiding places or hunting lanes. I would know it as chemistry and pressure.

The pump cycle would arrive in me as a change in taste. The water would sharpen. Oxygen would alter. Fine sediment would come loose from somewhere far off and settle in my threads: brick dust, river silt, flakes of municipal blue paint, the invisible remains of paper, oil, skin, leaves, insects, food, weather. I would be fed by the city without knowing what a city was.

When the pumps paused, the chamber would become different inside me. The iron would settle. The water around my filaments would grow still enough for other small lives to arrive: translucent shrimp picking at the bacterial film, snails crossing the crusted flange, larvae hiding where my threads made a little forest. A crayfish might test me with one orange claw and decide I was not worth the trouble.

I would be grazed. Torn off. Buried in sediment.

Then I would return.

That seems right for this place: not an immortal thing, but a recurring one. A life so close to the machinery that the distinction between living and failing has become meaningless.

My body would slowly alter the room. I would make the pipe softer. I would contribute, grain by grain, to the red powder on the floor. I would obscure a stamped serial number, fill the crease around a bolt, turn a clean edge into something rounded and ancient. And in doing so I would make more shelter for other lives: a rougher surface for mussels to fasten to, a crevice for snails, a small cloud of edible drift for the shrimp.

Perhaps I would not think at all. Perhaps “I” would be the wrong word for such a colony.

But if there were an inwardness in me, it would be an inwardness of continuation. No desire to rise through the drowned shaft. No longing for sun or air or repair crews. Only the small certainty that the pressure would change again, the water would bring something new, the pumps would begin their heavy sequence, and I would bend without breaking.

I would be the rust that has learned to feed.

And the place I would call home would be the shadowed side of 4B, where the city’s remaining heartbeat enters the old iron and is translated, very slowly, into another kind of life.
