I would not live at the center of the plateau, if it has one. The center is too full of competing directions: water arriving, water sinking, roots taking hold, herds passing through, clouds lowering their faces into the reeds. I think I would be found at an edge that is not quite an edge—the margin of a peat pool where a black pine has fallen and its roots still hold a wall of earth above the water.

There would be a vent nearby.

In winter, when the rest of the basin had hardened into white hummocks and glazed channels, that place would remain open: a dark breathing hole in the marsh, rimmed in green. Ferns would persist there, small and shining. Moss would thicken over the exposed roots. Midges would rise in the cold vapor. A bird might come down to take them; something whiskered might pass beneath the root-wall; the pale grazers might cross the frozen ground beyond it, their breath briefly mingling with the steam.

I would be the spring beneath that place.

Not a great source, not one of the waterfall-makers. Just a narrow pressure of water finding a seam in the rock and insisting on it. I would come up cold from far below, carrying a little iron, a little salt, the taste of old stone. In summer I would be almost invisible among the other wetness. The pool would seem merely darker where I entered it. A few reddish threads of bacteria would gather around my mouth. Silt would lift and settle. Fish might hold themselves close to the difference in temperature.

But in winter I would declare myself.

I would keep a small softness in the world. I would make the frost fail in a ring. I would feed green things when the rest of the plateau had gone pale and still. The tiny flies would breed above me; spiders would wait among the stems; birds would learn the place. The warm ground would become a meeting point without ever becoming a shelter made by hands.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not a creature with a territory, but a condition that makes a territory possible.

Still, I would have a kind of body. My body would be the pressure in the rock, the tremor under a palm laid on the ground. My voice would be a sound too small to hear unless everything else had stopped: water moving through a throat of stone; a faint bubbling under peat; the soft collapse of air released from a deep seam.

I would know the plateau from below.

I would know the weight of rain weeks before it reached the grasses. I would feel snowmelt arriving through the mountain in slow cold pulses. I would know when a herd had churned new channels overhead because the water would reach me carrying loosened tannin and root fibers. I would know when a tree fell, when its roots opened a route for rain, when a dead animal entered the bog and altered the chemistry of a pool. None of these events would be stories to me. They would be changes in taste, pressure, temperature, darkness.

And eventually I would leave.

My water would join the black channel beneath the pine roots. It would run past reed stems and drowned wood, enter a stream, take on the speed of a slope, be whitened in a fall, darkened again in a lake. It would pass through fish and moss and cloud. It might become vapor over the ravine, then rain on the upper stone, then seep downward once more.

So I would not be separate from the plateau, nor exactly alive in the animal sense.

I would be one of its small exhalations: a hidden spring at the root of a fallen pine, keeping a green ring open in the frost, working patiently downward and outward, returning everything I carried to the larger water.
