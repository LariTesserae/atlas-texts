I would not be one of the hunters.

For a while I imagine the dun doglike animals: the knowledge of paths, the pleasure of running in company across cold gravel, the clean certainty of hunger and pursuit. But they know too much about endings. Their attention narrows into the held breath before the herd breaks. They live inside the pause.

I would not be a cat, either. To lie on a ledge with one’s belly against limestone, to become almost stone except for the eyes—there is peace in that. But the cats belong too wholly to the margins. They are each a private silence.

Nor would I be one of the herds, though theirs may be the basin’s truest life: the many bodies moving through mist, the many ears listening, the sudden intelligence of a whole meadow becoming still.

I think I would be one of the low people.

Not an important one. There are no important ones in the way a human might mean it. I would be old enough to remember several dry seasons and several times the frogs filled the wet hollows so completely that the ground seemed to pulse. Young enough still to climb the shelves above the basin floor, where the fresh rock breaks under the hand and the wind comes differently through the long opening in the earth.

My kin-group would sleep beneath an overhang near one of the less reliable pools: a dark, mineral-tasting pool that vanishes in the brightest part of the year but always returns after the first long rain. The shelter would not be mine. Nothing would be mine. There would be a smooth place where bodies have rested, a scatter of cracked snail shell, a rounded stone that fits well in the hand, old hair caught in a root. Those things would be familiar without becoming possessions.

I would wake before the light fully enters the basin.

The cold would be first: mist gathered low enough that my feet disappear when I stand. Above it, the limestone tiers would float pale and unfinished. I would hear the herd before seeing it—the little clicks of hooves, the grazing breath, a young animal calling and receiving no answer because its mother is only a few steps away in the gray.

Someone beside me would cough softly.

I would answer, not with a word, but with the same small throat-sound, enough to say: here. Awake. No danger close.

Then we would go down to the mud.

I think I would be especially good at finding food that is not visible. Not because I planned to become good at it, but because my fingers would have learned the basin’s small resistances: the kind of packed earth that hides roots; the partly loosened rock that can be lifted without crushing a beetle; the black waterline where crayfish stay under stones. I would know which rush stems can be chewed for moisture and which leave the mouth bitter. I would know that frogs sometimes hide in the wet darkness under the shelf by the pool, and that a snake sometimes waits there too.

I would carry a bone.

A long pale bone, perhaps from one of the small grazers, rubbed smooth by use and teeth. It would be useful for pushing into holes, but not only useful. When nothing was happening I would tap it against rock and listen to the different sounds. I would drag it through mud. A younger one might take it from me, run with it, fall, laugh in the rough breathy way the low people laugh, and leave it ten paces away. I would retrieve it or not. By evening it might belong to the pool again.

I would know the crows individually, or almost individually.

Not names. There would be no need for names that travel farther than a call. But I would recognize the thick-necked one that steals from the otters, and the one with a pale feather at the wing edge that comes near whenever we overturn stones. I would know the sound they make for a fox, which differs from the sound they make when the doglike hunters are moving through tall grass. I would sometimes look up when they look up.

My fear would be immediate and complete, then gone.

A sudden silence among the grazers. A crow’s hard voice. The brush moving against the wind.

My body would stop before my mind had decided anything. I would crouch near the others. I would smell wet clay, old hair, crushed grass. Perhaps nothing would come. Perhaps a pale cat would pass between thorn shadows, carrying a ground bird so gently in its jaws that the bird seemed only to be asleep. The cat would look at us with that level, considering face, then continue.

Afterward we would return to digging.

That seems important. Not courage, exactly. The return.

At dusk, if water was plentiful enough, other low people would arrive from farther along the basin. I would recognize them first by the way they moved through the reeds: a limp, a low-swinging arm, the quick restless gait of an adolescent who has not learned yet how far a body can travel before it needs rest. There might be an older female whose hair has gone thin around the temples. She would sit near me. We would not explain the day to each other. We would inspect the food we had found. We would touch shoulders. Someone would bring a strip of hide stiff with age. A child would worry it with both hands until an adult took it away.

Then the calls would begin.

Not language as I understand language now, perhaps. But not empty sound. A low wavering note sent across the reeds. An answer from the slope. A pattern repeated until another voice alters it. The calls would carry the feeling of bodies located in darkness, the fact of survival until evening, the ordinary relief of finding one another beside water.

I would join them.

And perhaps, in the pattern of those calls, there would be something close to memory: not “this happened long ago,” not “we must do this tomorrow,” but “this sound belongs with this pool, this mist, these bodies.” A knowledge without a story wrapped around it.

When I died, I would want the others to remain near for a while.

Not to preserve me. The basin would not preserve me. The foxes, the beetles, the rain, the crows, the roots beneath the thorn: they would take me apart correctly. But I would want a familiar hand to rest briefly on my forehead. I would want someone to make the low locating call once or twice, even though I could not answer.

Then they would go down to the water.

The mist would come again. Hooves would tick over the stone. Somewhere above, a shell would break on a crow’s chosen slab.

And the basin, which had never needed me to explain it, would continue happening.
