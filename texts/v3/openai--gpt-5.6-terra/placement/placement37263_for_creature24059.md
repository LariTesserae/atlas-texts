I would not arrive by swimming.

At first I would think I had come to the valley as a sound.

Not a voice exactly. A pressure. A long note entering through stone, traveling along the old road beneath its coat of moss, passing through the bridge footings and the buried walls of the settlement. I would be aware before I had eyes: aware of cold descending from above, of warm water threading uphill through a crack in the basalt, of the heavy, sleeping weight of things folded into the houses.

I would find myself beneath the western terrace, inside one of the low stone dwellings.

Not in the room.

In the wall.

There would be a channel there, deliberately built: a narrow passage between fitted stones, wide enough for water and silt and the thin roots of something green. At its inner bend, where the current separates—one stream continuing toward the old riverbed, one turning through the house toward a spring—I would be held.

I would be a keeper of that choice.

Perhaps I began as one of the translucent ribbons on the spring wall. Perhaps I had a mother once, though I would not understand that word in the dry-world sense. I would remember her as a pattern of warmth against stone, a sequence of pulses that taught my body how to open and close. I would remember being no more than a listening mouth in clear water. The first thing I learned would not be hunger.

It would be direction.

I would learn the valley by the ways water hesitates.

Where it gathers before passing through a wall. Where it curls under a bridge slab. Where it grows colder without warning. Where it carries the iron taste of a waking spring. Where it presses hard against a crack that has not yet become a collapse.

My body would be small, at least by the measure of the old people: a narrow pale head, webbed hands, hooked feet, a spine furred with dark fibers that collect mineral dust. I would have no name carried by air. The others would know me by the resonance of my bones. When I touched the wall beside another underkeeper, our ribs would answer one another, and in that exchange they would understand:

*This one has slept through two ice-brightenings.*

*This one knows the south road.*

*This one carries the taste of the warm platform.*

*This one is afraid of the open water.*

I think, at first, I would be afraid of it.

The meadows would seem impossibly exposed. All that swaying grass with nothing hidden among it but the slow current. The drowned trunks would rise in ranks along the old river, pale and fibrous, their empty upper branches dissolved into weed. Beyond them, the valley floor would open toward blackness. No creature crossing it. No movement except silt and green spores and the occasional stone rising in a patient spiral.

I would cling to the underside of the bridge and watch.

Not with sight alone. Sight would be uncertain in the underwater dark, too easily broken by pale weather above. But the bridge would tell me things. It would carry the tremor of water striking the far cairn. It would hum faintly when a cold front moved down from the ice. The copper-green bindings around its stones would hold old vibrations for a long time, and if I rested my cheek against them I might hear something that happened days before: a trunk knocking in the riverbed, the scrape of a slope giving way, an elder moving through a wall.

I would be young enough to mistake this stored sound for memory.

Later I would learn that memory is different. Memory has intention. It returns when called.

There would be an elder in the bell frame on the ridge.

Not always visible. Sometimes only a shape among the green-black metal, mineral-coated and still, with limbs grown into the frame and a face turned toward the dim ice. I might believe it was part of the structure until the first time the western bell sounded.

Then the bell would move.

The note would not pass through the water so much as make the water become note. It would bloom in the valley’s bones. Moss would flatten along the stones. The red seams in my own body would wake and flare. Gray silt would lift from the meadow floor and rise, slow as breath, toward the dark above.

And the elder would open its throat.

I would feel the answer before I heard it: a deep inward turning in the bridge, a vibration traveling down the road, into the walls, through the spring channels. Not language, but instruction.

*Wake.*

Or:

*The water is changing.*

Or perhaps:

*Come. There is work.*

I would come out then.

I would unfold from the wall, which would hurt pleasantly after long stillness. My joints would loosen one by one. Fine fibers along my hands would taste the current. I would pull myself through the narrow door of the drowned house and emerge beneath its slab-weighted roof.

Around me, the settlement would be waking.

A pale face in a doorway.

A hand moving among reeds.

A body uncurling from the hollow of a trunk.

Something that had seemed to be red moss lifting itself from a fallen beam and becoming a child older than I was.

We would not look at one another for long. Looking is a surface habit. We would touch the old things.

One would brace against the corner of a house while another cleared grit from the carved channels beneath it. Someone would carry luminous moss in a stone bowl to the lamp niche. A pair of larger underkeepers would work at the bridge, weaving lengths of tough weed through a loosening seam between slabs. I would be sent to the road markers.

My task would be simple: scrape the moss away from the sign.

Circle. Downward stroke.

Not *below.*

*Inward.*

At each marker I would clear the symbol with the hooked edge of my thumb, then lay my palm over it until I could feel which way the water wanted to go. The old roads would lead us not away from danger but into its grammar: toward vents, springs, wells, caves, the shifting mouths of buried streams. I would learn that to keep the valley was not to preserve it unchanged. Nothing could do that. The work was to help it alter without losing its voice.

A wall could fall, but its channel should remain open.

A bridge could break, but its stones should become a crossing of another kind.

A house could fill with silt, but its lamp should be moved before the doorway vanished.

The old people had built for water before they belonged to it. The underkeepers continued building for a water that was never finished changing.

In the deepest hour, when the light beyond the ice thinned to nearly nothing, I would travel farther than I had before.

The red mosses would wake across the old pasture floor.

Their dim glow would reveal the valley in scattered pieces: stairways ascending into black water, rooflines softened with weed, the pale ribs of drowned trees, a stone platform ringed by warm white plumes. Above everything, the long slopes would rise away until they disappeared. The drowned highland would seem immense, not empty but occupied by a patience too large to name.

There, perhaps, I would understand what I was.

Not a person in the old sense.

Not merely an animal remade by flood.

I would be one small listening place in a valley that had learned to hear itself.

My body would be a passage. My bones, a bell too small to hang in a frame. My skin, a field for mineral and moss. My hunger, the world’s continual breaking and answering: the boom beneath silt, the crack of ice, the knock of wood, the slow call of water moving through stone.

And when all three bells spoke, I would climb with the others.

Up the ridge road. Past the line of stakes. Past the tower stump and the hollow trees. Toward the dim ceiling where the old handholds disappeared into ice-dark water.

I would fasten myself to a stake with both hands.

The current would bend us all—the grass, the weed, the pale bodies of the underkeepers—and our red seams would shine beneath the mineral coats.

Then I would open the chambers in my ribs.

Not to speak.

To answer.

And the note that came through me would join the others: low, wordless, rising uphill through drowned stone toward the ice. It would say nothing a dry-world ear could translate. It would not mourn the lost sky, or ask for rescue, or call the valley ruined.

It would simply tell the water:

*We are here.*

And the valley, full of bells and roots and hidden sleepers, would answer from within.
