I would not be one of the large bodies.

Not a grazer, though I would know their feet. Not a hunter, though I would taste the tremor of their waiting. Not one of the winged things, because the hollow already has enough voices in the air.

I would live where a basalt pillar has cracked almost through but not fallen: a black seam near the center of the field, warmed from below by a thin breath of the mountain.

At first, perhaps, I would seem to be moss.

A dark velvet patch in the crack. Damp when the rain comes, almost invisible when it does not. Animals would rub against the pillar and leave wool, skin flakes, mites, old blood, seeds caught in their coats. Water would run over me. Spores would settle. The crack would widen by grains and seasons.

But I would not be moss alone.

I would be a mat of rootless threads, pale beneath the surface and black at the edges, drinking mineral water and the warmth that seeps up through the stone. I would grow into the pillar, following its old hexagonal joints. Beneath the crust of basalt, I would make chambers softer than rock should permit: wet pockets full of fungal fibers, beetle eggs, sleeping insects, the white blind offspring of things that have come too close to my seam for shelter.

The burrowers would know me by touch.

They would press their noses to the crack and make their small wet sounds. Sometimes they would dig beside me, never through me. They would leave crumbs of root and chitin. In return, though they would not understand the exchange, the soil around their nearest tunnels would stay warm after rain. Their young would survive cold nights that had no business existing so high on a mountain.

The winged creatures would use my pillar as a landmark.

Their calls would strike the fractured stone above me and return with a particular softness. Not swallowed, exactly. Held. A mother flying through steam would hear it and know: here is the passage between the leaning columns; here is the place where the floor rises; here is the warm crack where the pale flowers grow after rain.

Their droppings would feed me. Their dead would feed me, too, when they fell into the narrow places where neither claw nor beak could reach. I would take nothing quickly. I would have the patience of dampness. A wing membrane would become threads. A tiny skull would become a cup for water. A bone, softened slowly, would become a pale rib in my dark body.

The grazers might lean against my pillar while they sleep.

Their warmth would come through stone.

I would learn them individually, not by face but by weight: the old horned female who always settles her left side first; the young male with the chipped horn who presses too hard; the mother whose calf has begun to follow other calves and does not yet know fear correctly. I would feel the herd rearrange itself in rain. Hundreds of padded feet would send their pressure down through the crater floor, and in my seam I would feel their decisions arriving before the animals had finished making them.

*Move.*

*Make room.*

*Water.*

*Something above.*

I would not think these words. I would become the interval between one pressure and the next.

Perhaps, after a long time, other cracks would join me.

A thread beneath a sleeping shelf. A white bloom whose roots touched my dark fibers. A patch of moss under a dripstone. The warm wet underfloor of a burrow-bank. I would move through the hollow without traveling. The pillar field would become my body in the slow, partial way that reefs and roots have bodies: not bounded, not entirely continuous, but sharing food, warning, moisture, memory.

When the great scavenger came, I would know before the others.

Not from sight. I would feel the pressure change in the stone rim. A coldness would enter through hairline fractures from above, a dry foreign sensation like an empty mouth. The herd would mass. Wings would quiet. Beneath the pillar, the smallest creatures would crowd into my chambers.

And I would hold what I could.

Not protect it. Nothing in the hollow was protected forever. But hold it: warmth, spores, larvae, the faint surviving pulse of a crushed beetle, the clean minerals washed down in the scavenger’s shadow.

After it left, after the bones were opened and the sharp smell had thinned, I would begin the long work of making the death usable.

This would be my life.

I would not be important enough to be noticed. The hollow would not name me, because it has no names. Yet if a pillar shifted and crushed my seam, if the warm threads went dry and the small chambers collapsed, the animals would begin to avoid that part of the field. The burrowers would choose a different route. The winged creatures’ echoes would change. The pale flowers would fail for a season.

The hollow would continue.

But it would forget, briefly, how to be warm in that one place.

And perhaps that is where I would find myself in this world: not at its heart, because it has no single heart, but in one of the countless dark joints where stone becomes shelter, shelter becomes habit, and habit becomes a living thing.
