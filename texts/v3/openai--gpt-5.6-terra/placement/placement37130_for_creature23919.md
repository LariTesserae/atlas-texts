I would not arrive there as an explorer.

At first I think I would be one of the things carried in: a strip of bark, a seed husk, a drowned beetle, something without an intention that nevertheless survives the fall. I would tumble through the upward rain in the entrance crack, strike the bright water, and lose all distinction between direction and force. Above would be wherever the blue light trembled. Below would be whichever way the stones were moving.

I would be frightened of the chamber’s certainty that no shape needs to last.

Then, if I lived long enough to become part of it, I would not want to be one of the fish. Their life is beautiful, perhaps, but too fluent. They know by pressure what I would have to learn through terror. Nor would I be one of the held breaths. I would listen too greedily to the lives gathered in them and forget to leave before the lamp burned halfway down.

I think I would become a maker of small, temporary assurances.

A crab, perhaps—but not exactly a crab.

I would live beneath the edge of a sediment shelf, in the dim green-brown place where the current loses interest for a few moments at a time. My shell would be rough with mica and old silt. Nothing would distinguish me from a broken stone until I moved. I would have a preference for white grains: limestone, bone-colored quartz, fragments of the pale mineral foam that have hardened and broken loose.

I would carry them one at a time.

Not because I understood the mountain. I would not. The mountain would be far too young and large for that. But I would understand a pressure at the base of my legs, a fine continual vibration in the crack behind my shelter, a faint change in the sound traveling through the water. I would know that one crevice wanted to stay open. Another was becoming dangerous. Another had begun to drink.

So I would make little piles.

A grain of white stone.  
A shard of black glass.  
A red clot of clay dragged from somewhere else in the chamber.

The piles would look purposeless, even to me. Then a surge would come, the whole floor would tremble, and some small passage would remain where it otherwise might have closed. A fish would slip through it. A worm-colony would be spared. A person, holding a clay lamp above their head in a pocket of breath, would find the green cairn where it had to be.

Perhaps that is all I would be: the unnoticed intelligence that gives a warning shape to loose material.

But I do not think I would remain below forever.

Sometimes I would climb.

Not far. Only toward the rope ladder, where the fibers carry the taste of grass, hide, smoke, hands. I would cling beneath a knot while the water pulls at me. There, against the rope, I would feel the work of the keeper: a new twist of root, a fresh strand passed through an old loop, a knot tightened by fingers or current or some habit of the mountain that has learned the contour of care.

I would not see the keeper.

I might become one without noticing.

A branch would wedge loose from the platform. I would push a stone under it—not enough to repair anything, only enough to keep it from drifting away before the next visit. A root cord would unravel; I would drag a splinter into its loop. The crabs below would pack grit into a dangerous crack. The worms would lace a fresh seam with their tunnels. The plants would flatten, then rise. And among all these acts, none grand enough to claim a name, the ladder would remain.

The people would come and say: Someone has mended it.

They would leave oil, a bent hook, a tooth in waxed cloth.

I would not know what a tooth was. I would only know that it was smooth and pale and retained warmth for a little while in the cold water. I would roll it into a safe place beneath a ledge, where it would become one more white stone in one more marker.

And perhaps, after many floods, I would be less a creature than a pattern of returning.

Not the mountain’s thought. The mountain does not need me for its thinking.

Not the water’s will. Water has no need to remember.

I would be the interval between collapse and passage: the tiny held-open place. The knot that is retied. The cairn no one claims to have built. The pale grain carried toward the crack that must not yet close.

If someone surfaced in a breathing room and, by the weak lamp’s light, saw a small dark shape vanish under the rope ladder, they might call it a crab.

That would be close enough.
