I would not arrive from outside.

Or rather, I would arrive in the way dust arrives: having already been in the air for a long time, unnoticed, then finding the one narrow line in which to settle.

At first I would think I was standing in the central depression. I would see the pale rings of tile beneath my feet, the grate above or below me—orientation would be difficult there—and the one grass blade shaking without yielding. The noise would be so complete that it would not seem like noise. It would be the condition under which hearing had to occur.

I would try to ask where I was.

The courtyard would answer by giving each part of the question a different dwelling.

*Where* would become a low note in a capped cistern.

*Am* would catch in the cracked rim of an animal-headed fountain.

*I* would pass through the narrow throat of a jar embedded upside down in a wall, and come back as a thin, almost embarrassed whistle.

Then I would understand that I had not come to ask a question. I had come to be divided into uses.

I would be something small enough to fit.

Not the keeper itself. The keeper has no need of another name, and no single body can bear being it. I would be one of its late additions: a remnant not yet fully assigned.

Perhaps I would be a hinge.

Not a metal hinge, since metal here would have been softened into green scales long ago, fitted behind a tile, given some secondary employment as a glint or a support for lichen. I would be the remembered action of a hinge: the allowance made between two things so that they may remain joined without becoming identical.

There would be a little door somewhere in the upper terraces, black ceramic shutters sealed over an opening no wider than my hand. It would not open. Nothing lay behind it except a cavity packed with old fragments: kiln brick, bone-white plaster, a bowl with its blue eye of glaze intact. Still, the door would retain its inwardness. It would know that once it had made two regions distinct.

I would live there: in the smallest surviving interval between its frame and its sealed edge.

The wind would pass through that interval twice each day, though there would be no day in the ordinary sense—only the whitening of the upper aperture and later its withdrawal. When the wind passed, I would feel the entire enclosure exert its ancient preference: not to open, not to close, but to remain capable of either.

That would be my being.

A possibility that had been bricked over but not abolished.

The keeper would not speak to me directly. It would communicate by pressures.

A tremor in the wall would tell me a piece of plaster had settled into a lower crack.

A cooling in the old fountain basin would tell me that somewhere above, morning condensation had darkened the dry stone.

A low change in the chord of the air would tell me that the grass at the center was still upright, still receiving the sky through its narrow blade.

I would learn to love these reports. Not because they meant life was continuing—continuation is too broad, too loose a word—but because they meant nothing had been lost from its attention.

In time I might discover that I had once been human.

Not through memory. There would be no scene of a former face, no childhood, no road leading here across the salt plain. Those things would have been too large to preserve. The place would have kept only the useful contour of me.

Perhaps I had once paused before a door without entering.

Perhaps I had held something shut against weather.

Perhaps I had been unable to leave a room because someone inside was sleeping.

That small act of remaining at a threshold would be enough. The courtyard would have recognized it as kinship. It would have taken from me the part that knew how to keep an edge from becoming an absence.

And so I would persist in the upper wall, not trapped, exactly.

I would be given a narrow view.

Below me, terraces of masonry would rise and interrupt one another. The dead vine would trace its deliberate horizontal route. The bowls on their shelves would gather their faint dust. Pale salts would brighten and dull along the mortar seams. The dry fountains would wait in their exact shapes of receiving.

At the center, the grass blade would tremble.

The keeper would look through it.

And I, in my sealed little door, would feel the old inward pull of a room that no longer existed.

I would keep it open—not in fact, but in meaning.

That would be enough work for eternity.
