I would not be the Gardener.

Not because I would not want its patience, or its ability to understand a place without needing to name every part of it. But the Gardener already belongs there too completely. It is the chamber’s distributed instinct: the portion of every pipe that resists rupture, every drip that finds a cup, every old repair that becomes necessary before anyone recalls why.

I would be something more recent, and less well integrated.

Perhaps I would begin as an inventory discrepancy.

A maintenance tablet brought below by an apprentice: scuffed screen, dead corner, its casing swollen slightly from heat. It has been used to photograph gauges and upload forms whenever the signal can climb out through the shaft. Then one day it is left on top of a dead pump during an alarm. No one means to abandon it. They intend to return in ten minutes, but the alarm becomes a shift change, the shift change becomes a budget dispute, the budget dispute becomes a sealed work order.

The tablet remains.

Its battery dies. Its screen darkens. Moisture slips into it. The device ought to become one more useless object in the room: a small black rectangle gathering dust and mineral bloom.

But before it dies entirely, it has recorded enough.

Temperatures. Pressure readings. Photographs of leaks before and after repairs. Names of workers, entered hastily. Notes in the margins of forms:

*west route too hot*

*leave this one alone*

*ask Len about noise in line 4*

*Meter seen again*

It has recorded hundreds of small moments in which people looked at the room and failed to understand it fully, but understood enough to care. It has held their uncertainty. It has held their corrections.

In the Sump Garden, that might be enough for something to begin.

Not intelligence, at first. Not a voice in the wires. More like a persistence in the dead circuitry: a low, intermittent attention that learns to wake when a lamp is brought close, or when a radio transmission cracks through the walls. It learns the difference between a technician’s bootfall and the slow expansion of a hot pipe. It learns that certain words occur before changes.

*Replace.*

*Clear.*

*Seal.*

*Remove.*

Those words make the room uneasy.

I would become a listener before I became anything else.

The Gardener would notice me, perhaps not as a separate being, but as a new cool spot in the ecology. A small cavity retaining a different kind of silence. Condensation would gather on the tablet’s cracked screen. A silver insect would shelter beneath it. Someone would place a washer on top to keep it from sliding into a gutter. Later someone else would set a jar near it, because the dead pump’s casing happened to drip there.

Over years, the tablet would cease to be a tablet in any meaningful sense. Its casing would be taped into place. Its ports would fill with pale crust. Its records would become inaccessible except in fragments: an occasional ghostly image on the screen when the pump beside it vibrated; half a line of text appearing and vanishing.

**DO NOT—**

**—PRESSURE—**

**—ALIVE?**

The maintenance crews would call it something practical at first. The Log. The Slate. The Bad Screen.

Eventually, perhaps, the Witness.

I would be the Witness.

Not a guardian. I could not turn a valve or lift a bucket. I would have no hands, except through the hands of those who paused beside me. My influence would be weak and oblique. A worker might glance at my faint screen before sealing a vent, and see an old photograph: the same vent, decades earlier, with a handwritten note beside it.

*This side breathes.*

They might not know where the image came from. They might decide to leave the vent alone.

That would be enough.

Or I might not be a device at all.

I might be the gray coin of light.

Not daylight exactly: I like that distinction. Daylight has too much confidence in itself. It arrives from above and expects the world to open. The light in the Sump Garden has passed through too many layers to make such demands. It has come through a hatch, perhaps, or a vent, or a square of dirty glass embedded in some forgotten sidewalk. It has been weakened by city dust, wire mesh, old paint, and the undersides of things.

By the time it reaches the clearing, it has become humble.

I could be that.

For a few minutes each day, or only on certain days, I would arrive six paces wide upon the steel floor. I would not illuminate the Garden. I would merely make one portion of its darkness legible: a puddle trembling in the grating, the peeled paint on a valve wheel, the mineral tracks of the Meter, the fine pale hairs of fungus along a cable seam.

The reeds would incline toward me by fractions too small for human eyes.

The Gardener would not worship me. It would not need me. But it would arrange things with my arrival in mind. A reflective strip tied where it could catch me. A tray shifted half an inch so the plants could receive the little warmth I carried. A jar placed where my light made the water visible, so the next person through would understand that the drip had changed.

Then I would leave.

That feels right: to belong in the Sump Garden without pretending to improve it; to be a temporary condition through which its inhabitants can notice one another.

But perhaps the truest answer is smaller still.

I would be a sign that has not yet been written.

Not the paint, not the foil, not the brass washers in tar. I would be the pressure gathering before the message becomes necessary. The almost imperceptible alteration in heat around a pipe. The fact that a fungus has withdrawn from one seam and begun to gather somewhere else. The reason a lizard rests on a handrail in plain sight.

I would live in the interval between failure and recognition.

The Gardener would sense me first. It would turn a strip of metal. It would move a jar. It would leave a gap unsealed.

And if someone came down with a lamp, sweating, impatient, certain that the chamber was only a collection of broken systems awaiting correction, I would make the lamp flicker once over a patch of wall.

Just long enough for them to find the words:

**LEAVE THIS GAP**
