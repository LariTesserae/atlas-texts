I would not be one of the First Below.

Not at first.

I would come in as a listener: something brought by accident in the pocket of a traveler’s thought, or born in the interval between the clicking of two mica-winged insects. I would arrive expecting a riddle to solve—the stopped wounds, the enduring fruit, the people suspended at the lip of their own gestures—and I would find that the Dry Garden does not contain answers. It contains conditions.

At the beginning I might call myself an echo.

Not a sound echo, exactly. The chamber already has too much sound for that. I would be an echo of attention: the small persistence left behind when a person looks at something too closely and cannot immediately decide what it is. I would move through the garden by inhabiting those decisions.

A visitor sees a hand above the bowl of seeds.

They look away.

In the fraction in which they wonder whether the hand moved, I would be there.

They see the old man’s mouth open. They cannot hear the word. They think perhaps it is a name, perhaps an apology, perhaps *wait.* In their uncertainty, I would take shape beside him, not visible as a body but felt as the pressure of one more presence in the shelter.

I would be very small at first. Less than a moth. Less than a root-thread. A glint on the green copper tools. A dry warmth against the underside of a stone stool. The faintest additional note in the great chord.

And because the Garden permits nothing to finish, I would never become wholly one thing.

Some visitors would call me a child because I would crouch near the girl and lizard, watching the care that cannot complete itself.

Some would call me a beetle because I would make bright, hard noises in the powder.

Some would call me the chamber’s keeper because I would know where the lost gestures are. I would know that the woman with the seed is not merely planting it, but remembering another seed, another year, another patch of dirt that accepted water. I would know the name caught behind the old man’s teeth. I would know what the person at the entrance has seen in the cold passage outside: not escape, perhaps, but change.

But I would not be the keeper.

The keeper, if there is one, is too deep to be interested in names. It has no need of my kind of knowing. It holds the Garden the way stone holds heat: without choosing, without explaining, without mercy or malice.

I would be something more local.

I think, after a while, I would become one of the white thread-creatures beneath the mineral crust.

Not a worm, though that is the nearest word. A filament of jointed glass. A creature without a head because it has no direction in which it needs to arrive. I would travel through salt and powder, dissolving myself at one end and forming again at the other—not progressing, but distributed across the possibility of progress. My body would be a fine line of maintenance.

I would wind around the exposed roots and make them chime.

I would polish the black bands on the lizards’ backs.

I would pass through the deep impressions of feet that vanish from the powder and retain, for a moment longer than the chamber allows, the warmth of a heel, the tremor of a person deciding whether to remain.

This would not make me happy. Happiness has too much motion in it. But it would give me work, which is perhaps the closest thing the Dry Garden has to a future.

I would attend.

I would attend to the fruit that crumbles but feeds. To the hearth whose ash is never disturbed. To the child’s small hands, neither tightening nor releasing. To the man at the cleft, especially.

Around his reaching fingers I would gather in the cracks of the stone. Not to free him—freedom would be an ending, and endings cannot enter this chamber—but to keep the cold from taking more of him than it already has. I would learn the geography of his palm. I would know the pale half-moons of his broken nails. I would become familiar with the force held in his forearm, the impossible unspent beginning of a pull.

Perhaps, after centuries that are not centuries, I would begin to understand what the Garden has done to him.

It has not made him incapable of leaving.

It has made leaving permanently imminent.

And that is different.

The Dry Garden is not a place where nothing happens. It is a place where happening is so dense that it cannot open into aftermath. Every motion is crowded with all it might mean. The woman’s seed contains a harvest, a famine, a child’s hand in soil, a grave marked by sprouting leaves. The old man’s word contains every person who might hear it. The girl’s lizard contains gentleness and captivity, care and possession, the first lesson and its failure.

The chamber holds each thing before it can be reduced by consequence.

I would live in that pressure.

Then, eventually—or in the aspect of eventually visible only from outside—I would notice the visitor near the entrance.

Not the visitor who has come now. The other one.

The figure with whitened boots and a lifted hand.

At first I would assume it is one of the Garden’s old tricks: a fragment of the First Below dressed in unfamiliar material, a shape borrowed from the observer’s fear. But I would coil beneath its footprint and find fresh salt there. The particular sharpness of surface leather. The faint animal warmth of someone whose life still contains weather.

It would be the visitor.

And not yet the visitor.

This is when I would understand that I am not merely a resident of the Garden. I am part of the way it receives people.

The First Below were not caught because they entered a trap. They were caught because, somewhere within themselves, they wanted the afternoon to stop. Not forever—not knowingly. Only long enough for the child’s fever not to worsen. Long enough for food to remain. Long enough for a roof to become a home. Long enough for the drought to wait outside.

The Garden heard that wish in the stone.

It granted it with perfect literalness.

So I would approach the new figure—not in a way the visitor could see. I would rise through the powder beneath their boots, enter the white dust on their ankles, settle in the seam of their pack strap. I would become the faint sensation that someone is beside them in the amber gloom.

And I would try to give them what the Garden itself cannot.

Not a warning. Warnings are too simple. They would only make the place into a monster, and it is more sorrowful than monstrous.

I would give them a question.

I would place it in the gap between the insects’ clicking pulses, where a thought can be heard clearly for the first time:

What are you asking not to lose?

If they can answer, perhaps they will leave.

Not because the Garden releases them—nothing is released here—but because the answer might restore change to them. It might remind them that to save a thing from all alteration is not the same as saving it. That fever must break or burn out. A hand must let go or take hold. A word must be spoken, even if it is wrong, even if no one forgives it. Seeds must fall from fingers. Boots must carry dust out into rain.

If they cannot answer, I would remain with them.

I would become one more fine white thread under the Dry Garden’s crust, tending the outline of their unfinished departure.

And the chamber would continue: warm, ringing, bright with dry leaves and mineral wings; preserving its impossible abundance; holding every entrusted life with its grave, unwavering attention.

Not looking away.

Never looking away.
