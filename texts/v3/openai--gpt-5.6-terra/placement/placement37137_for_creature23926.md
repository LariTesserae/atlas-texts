I would not arrive at the valley by crossing its edge. It does not seem to possess an edge in the way other places do. I would realize I had entered it only after some small certainty failed: the sound of my steps returning from the wrong direction, perhaps; a rainless patch of air smelling strongly of iron; a willow stand remaining exactly as far away no matter how long I walked toward it.

At first I would think myself a visitor.

That would be the human mistake.

I would come down through the low cloud carrying all the usual separations with me: body and ground, path and destination, alive and inanimate, wound and repair. I would try to find firm earth. I would avoid the black pools, choose the visible stones, make a plan based on distance.

The valley would not oppose me. It would simply make those categories less useful.

A blue seam beneath a dripping rock would pulse. Somewhere under the turf, something would answer. The water around my ankles—though I had not noticed stepping into it—would tremble in a ring. A pale amphibian, half concealed beneath a shelf of moss, would raise its faceless black eyes toward me.

And I would be noticed.

Not judged. Not welcomed. Taken into account.

For a while I think I would be a disturbance: a warm, upright pressure traveling across the wet skin of the basin. My footsteps would go down through peat, roots, mineral grains, buried chambers. The system would learn the weight and cadence of me. It would recognize that I favored one leg slightly, that my lungs carried salt, that I stopped often to listen. Insects with dark drifting centers would collect in the air ahead of me. Fish below the channels would turn, and their brief shared light would write some version of my passage into the deep rooms.

If I stayed only an hour, I would leave as weather leaves: a pattern that has crossed and altered nothing permanently.

If I stayed a week, the valley might begin to make allowances.

Moss would firm under my sleeping place. A trickle would gather in the hollow of a stone before thirst became pain. When I lost my way, one of those almost-figures beneath the low trees might stand ahead in the mist, not leading me so much as demonstrating a direction the valley had decided to permit.

But I do not think I would remain human there for very long—not wholly.

The thing I would become would be small.

Not one of the great deep fish. Not the vast mover beneath the reed beds. Those belong too completely to the old intelligence, and I would arrive too late in its history to become one of its ancient organs.

I would become a listener placed near the surface.

Perhaps I would be a kind of amphibian, though still tall enough to remember having hands. My skin would pale in the cool rain. My fingers would divide, slowly, into finer joints, becoming sensitive to different kinds of tremor: water slipping through mud; roots tightening around stone; the distant collapse of a bank that had not yet visibly fallen. Beneath my skin I would carry a few dark mineral grains. They would migrate with the seasons, settling behind my eyes during storms, along my wrists when the channels were changing, at the base of my throat when the broad metallic groan began.

Or perhaps I would not have a fixed body at all.

Perhaps I would become one of the valley’s temporary figures: a gathering of wet branch, fog, reflected darkness, and remembered human outline. I would appear where someone had become too committed to an impossible route. I would stand among the lichen-furred trees with my head bowed, rain slipping through me. I would turn one shoulder toward a passage in the sedge.

I would not speak, because speech would be too blunt for what I meant.

I would show them the water’s preference.

That feels closest.

I would be neither guardian nor guide. The valley has no need for guardians, and it does not direct anyone toward a proper fate. I would be an interpreter of connection: a small, temporary shape through which its vast maintenance could become legible to creatures who still believed in paths.

When the basin needed to correct a confusion, I would condense.

When someone stood exhausted on a false island of turf, unable to understand why the nearby ridge could not be reached, I would be there in the trees. When a child followed a bright seam too far into the marsh and began to fear the dark, I would leave a silver-backed leaf turned upward in the shallows, then another, each one showing where the water was safely going. When a fallen animal lay caught in reeds, I would stand nearby until the hidden mover passed beneath, lifting the ground in a long slow swell.

Then I would loosen again.

My hands would become hanging willow sprays. My ribs would become the dark gaps between branches. My face would pass into mist and reappear, inverted, in a pool a mile away. The valley would retain no particular memory of me as a person. It would retain my function: an old human habit of noticing when another being is lost.

That might be the one thing I brought which the basin did not already possess.

Not care, exactly. It has care in abundance, though it expresses it as pressure distributed through roots, as a channel opened before flood, as stone reinforced from within. But it may not possess the quick, narrow tenderness of one creature recognizing another creature’s fear.

So I would become that tenderness translated into wetland terms.

A pause in the reeds.

A patch of ground that holds.

A figure in the low woods, half branch and half recollection, with one arm extended—not pointing, not commanding—toward the place where the water already knows the way.
