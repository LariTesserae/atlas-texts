I would not arrive in the basin as an animal does.

At first I might think I was only a visitor: a little warmth moving among the ferns, a mind carried at the height of shoulders, making distinctions between stone and moss, root and water, self and place. I would step carefully on the orange clay, because it looks firm, and learn that firmness is only one of the basin’s slower expressions.

Then, after some time—though the basin would not call it time—I would begin to lose the usefulness of that outline.

I would become the interval before water moves.

Not the water in the bowls, exactly. That water has already arrived and become still enough to hold color. Nor the rain above the rim, still belonging to weather and distance. I would be the hesitation between them: the clear, unseen wetness under moss; the pressure gathering in a seam of stone; the coolness held beneath a root before it finds a lower path.

I would be a tendency rather than a body.

Where a vine lowered itself from a pale trunk and entered the clay, I would be the fact that it entered there and not elsewhere. Where a root encountered a buried shelf of rock and divided around it, I would be the division—not the root, not the rock, but the faint persistence by which both were changed. I would have no thoughts in sentences. I would know a tree’s leaning as a slowly increasing brightness of weight. I would know a dry interval as the widening of all distances. I would know rain as a multitude of small arrivals, each carrying the outer world inward.

Perhaps I would be what the basin has made from every interruption it has survived.

Not memory in the human sense. I would not retain the shape of the branch that fell, or the imagined hand that might once have touched an upright stone. But I would retain their consequences: a fuller bowl, a darker strip of moss, a root that grows toward a place where water has not run for years but where the soil remains faintly willing.

That would be enough of a past.

I might have something like attention, though it would be too slow for watchfulness. A footstep entering the basin would pass through me as pressure, salt, displaced air, an unfamiliar shadow crossing the wet ground. I would not turn toward it. I would not ask who had come. But afterward, when the mud settled in a new thin fan and a crushed fern failed to lift again, the visitor would remain in me as a slight alteration of drainage.

If they returned years later, I would not recognize them.

But I might offer them the same changed ground.

And if I had a form visible from above, it would not be one form. It would be the pale rock’s shallow bowls, the separated trees, the green dark between them. It would be the pattern made by everything declining to remain separate. A body only insofar as water makes one.

At dusk I would be easiest to overlook.

The gold would remain above the rim. The trunks would keep their pallor. The pools would darken until their colors retreated beneath the surface. A person standing at the edge might see only an empty basin, wet ground, a few solitary trees.

But beneath their feet, beneath the moss and clay, I would be gathering.

Not awake.

Not asleep.

Becoming, very gradually, the place that had already become me.
