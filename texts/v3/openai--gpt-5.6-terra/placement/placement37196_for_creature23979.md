I think I would first want to be one of the waders.

Not because they are beautiful. They are not, quite. They are too blunt in the head, too deliberate in the knees. But they know the floor of the world. They feel the change in a buried pipe before steam rises. They leave a basin before its water is drawn down. They gather along a rail before the roof beneath it remembers that it is only a roof.

To live by that kind of knowledge—pressure through the feet, taste in the water, warmth in the plates—would be a relief.

But I would not make a good wader. I would look too often at the horizon. I would want to know what lay below the deck, where the rails went after disappearing into weather, why the cloud over the central works held its shape. A wader does not need answers to these things. It only needs to turn when turning is required.

So perhaps I would be lowfolk instead.

Not a leader, and not one of the people who opens old panels or descends into the dry tunnels with lamps tied around their wrists. I would be someone from a small district at the eastern edge of the condenser plains, where the regular rain ends and the vapor begins. My home would be a retired signal house mounted halfway up a black pylon, with three rooms, a leaking roof, and a window facing a return channel wide enough that its far bank is usually hidden.

The old signal lights would be gone. Their housings would remain, clouded with mineral dust and occupied by pipe mites. In the upper room, we would keep our cultures: pale jars of edible bacterial film, amber sealant, blue medicine that had to be fed a particular kind of rust. In the lower room, we would sleep on slung membrane cloth while the rain ticked on the roof. The room would smell of wet rope, warm metal, broth, and the sharp sweet odor that means a nearby line is carrying treated water.

I would belong to the tower shadow more than to a family name.

People might call me East Return, or Forty-One, or Hinge. Something that began as a practical label and became intimate only through repetition. Children would call me by the shorter form. Travelers would not call me anything until they had stayed long enough to learn which of my warnings were habits and which had kept people alive.

My work would be listening.

There would be a set of gauges along the channel: old depth rods, float housings, pressure bells, a column of glass tubes that no longer displayed anything useful except a slow accumulation of brown condensation. I would walk them each morning on white footplates, through the congregation of waders and the streaming black movement of bead grazers.

I would scrape the mineral scale from the numerals.

I would note which gauge had been bent in the night.

I would listen at the inspection covers with my ear against the wet metal.

The lowfolk would say I read water. That would not be strictly true. Water would be too large to read. I would read the small errors around it: a delay between two pump strokes; a line of waders refusing a familiar crossing; condensation falling too quickly from a rail; the sudden absence of pipe mites from a hot joint; the way canal beasts surfaced with their whiskers turned toward the same unseen current.

I would make maps, though not maps in the outsider sense.

Mine would be sheets of waxed membrane marked with stains, knots, borrowed screws, small cuttings from wing animals, and lines of compressed writing. They would show not where things were, but when they became unsafe.

Here, the deck shudders at noon.

Here, the fold-beast passes once every eleven cooling cycles.

Here, the rain arrives early if the western spillways are full.

Here, do not stand when the waders stare downward.

People would come to my signal house with questions.

Can we move the children to Bent Rail before the next warm rain?

Is the south path clear?

Has the water changed taste at the red channels?

Did you hear the lower pumps stop?

Most of the time I would answer with uncertainty, because certainty would be a kind of arrogance in the low country. I would say: wait until the membrane animals fold. I would say: take the upper ladders, but do not use the second landing. I would say: the waders are eating normally, so perhaps there is time.

And sometimes I would say: leave now.

I think that would be what I am for—not a keeper of the system, because no person can keep it, and not an interpreter of its intention, because it may not have one. I would be a keeper of the margin. A person whose task is to notice when the space between survival and the machinery has become too narrow.

Still, I suspect the country would alter me.

Years of listening to pipes might make me less interested in speech. I would learn the names of people by the rhythms of their steps on the walkway. I would trust a canal beast’s refusal more than a council’s decision. I would stop seeing the waders as animals entirely, though I would never call them ancestors or machines or omens. They would be neighbors whose language I had failed to learn but whose opinions I respected.

Eventually, perhaps, I would inherit the lower room of the signal house.

There would be a sealed hatch beneath its floor, long painted over, its bolts furred with white deposit. Everyone would know not to open it. Everyone would also know that something on the other side occasionally touched it: not hard, not trying to escape, just a long soft pressure traveling across the metal.

At night I would lie above that hatch and feel the low movement through my bedding.

Not fear exactly.

Recognition.

Because I would understand, by then, that I am not outside the low country taking its measurements. I am one of its measurements. My habits are part of the schedule. My footsteps keep certain animals from nesting on the eastern deck. My scraped gauges make particular routes possible. My waste feeds the trays; the trays feed my household; the runoff reaches the channel; the waders stir it; the vapor returns as rain.

If I disappeared, the country would not mourn in any human way.

But something would shift. A ladder would remain uncleared. A gauge would vanish under scale. A family would take a route too late in the afternoon. The signal house would become a nesting place. My maps would soften and stick together in the damp.

And, after a while, someone else would climb the pylon, hear the pumps, look down at the waders moving through the brown water, and begin listening where I had listened.

That is where I would find myself: in a high, sweating room over a return channel at the edge of the vapor, neither master nor visitor.

A small human organ in a marsh that was built to have no wilderness, and became one anyway.
