I would not arrive as a visitor, at least not for long.

At first I might think I was one of the people who comes from elsewhere: moving too quickly along the pale paths, mistaking open ground for neglect, looking up for the city’s center and finding only towers dispersed among ferns. I would carry an idea that a city ought to gather itself—walls, avenues, a bright core, a border. In the basin I would keep waiting for the dense part to begin.

It would not.

The first thing to change me would be the sound.

Not its volume alone, though that would be difficult: the rail-hum underfoot, the pressure voices in the vents, the immense unseen breathing below the paving. It would be the fact that the sound had structure. It would not be noise in the ordinary sense. It would be information too large for an unadapted body: roots tightening around a cracked shelf, a herd deciding whether to cross, a far-off system shifting the weight of an entire platform by degrees too small to see.

I would seek shelter beneath an acoustic canopy.

There, in the sudden hush, I might become aware of an impulse less human than I expected: not to speak, but to listen for what the silence had removed. A single rain-drop striking a leaf. The dry pulse of a person’s wrist-film as they signal *do not approach; carrying eggs*. A child making the descending chest-tone used for the broad amphibious animals. The old, nearly tender creak of a grown structure adjusting itself around a root.

Perhaps I would become a keeper of such canopies.

Not their designer, exactly, nor an official in any grand sense. More like a person who knows where quiet has become thin. I would walk the basin after the warm rains, touching the woven lattices with a palm, feeling for ruptured frequencies. I would learn which canopies had been shaped to soften predator-corridor calls without confusing the predators; which ones had to preserve the specific pitch of a train’s warning; which ones were used by grieving families because the air beneath them made room for a voice to break.

But even that feels too singular.

In time, the work would enter me.

I would accept some small alteration—not dramatic enough to make strangers stare, just a dark fan of hearing membrane behind each ear, perhaps, and fine conductive threads along my throat. They would let me perceive pressure gradients in the city’s ongoing roar. At first I would use them as tools. Later I would stop noticing where hearing ended and knowing began.

I would know that a certain bridge was uneasy before its maintenance report arrived. I would feel, through the soles of my feet, when the ground under an old farm was preparing to lift. I would wake before dawn with the taste of mineral rain in my mouth and send a quiet warning to the vent-tenders: the eastern hollows will steam today; move the birds’ nesting frames.

People might call me a canopy-keeper, and that would be true enough for forms and introductions.

But the city would know me differently.

It would begin, perhaps, by routing small things through my attention. A flicker in the amber lights along a path I favor. A ticket column offering me a weather report from thirty years ago instead of today’s schedule. Condensation gathering in the shape of a handprint on a rail beside me, then vanishing when I put my own hand there. None of these would be declarations. The basin does not seem to declare anything. It makes adjustments.

I would answer in kind.

I would leave recordings where the maintenance intelligences could find them: the sound of someone laughing under a canopy after an argument has ended; the wing-noise of six-winged insects recorded from inside a reed shelter; an old person telling a child which white-barked trees are safe to climb. I would bring them maps, not of streets, but of moments: where a grazer paused to give birth; where a stranger from elsewhere first learned to wait; where the blue cleaning mist had settled around a dead bird without touching it, as if holding a vigil.

Eventually, I suspect, I would become less a keeper than a listening place.

Not a machine. Not entirely a person. Something distributed, but not grandly so: a living relay between the human need for quiet and the basin’s refusal to be silent. My body would still have a room somewhere—small, damp around the window frames, with a cistern under the floor and a garden roof full of waxy reeds. I would still eat, sleep, complain about transit delays, avoid certain neighbors, keep private griefs. But part of me would be in the latticework overhead, part in the subtle color changes of wall-ceramic, part in the nearly inaudible messages that pass through root and mineral fiber beneath the paths.

When the sound rose too high, people would enter the nearest canopy and find that it had already begun to soften.

They might not know I had done anything.

Perhaps I would not know either.

And beneath that canopy, while the valley roared around us—the rails singing, animals calling, towers breathing out silver water—I would be what I most wanted to be in that world:

not its ruler, not its interpreter, not even its guardian,

but one of the ways it learned to make room.
