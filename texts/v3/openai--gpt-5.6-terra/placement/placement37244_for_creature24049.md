I would begin by thinking I belonged in one of the ravines.

Not at the bottom, where the hidden water gathers, nor on the bright upper shelves where the gliders roost, but midway down: in a cool, narrow gallery behind a curtain of roots, where the plateau’s heat becomes a memory held in the stone. I would make a small room there. I would collect seep-water in a dark bowl. I would learn the hour at which a particular wall releases its stored warmth. I would be someone who listens.

But that is too settled an answer. The ravines have already claimed enough listeners.

Perhaps I would be one of the people who maintains the old towers—not as a priest, because the plateau distrusts professions that make a person larger than their work, but as a keeper of their small continuities. I would clear a fallen nest from an eastern opening after the young gliders had flown. I would reset a stone loosened by rain. I would leave a cup of dew in a tower when the weather rose from below. I would know which towers carry sound through the ground and which only look as if they should.

People might come to me not for answers but for bearings.

They would ask: Which way is the basin that still holds water? Has the red-backed walker crossed the southern shelf? Did anyone come through the fog? And I would touch the stone at the base of a tower, wait with my palm against it, and say what the ground had told me.

Yet even this gives me too much usefulness.

The thing I most recognize in that place is not a tower keeper, nor a hunter, nor even one of the quiet plateau people. It is the small life that accumulates in the creases of the great plated walkers.

I would be a moth, perhaps.

Not one of the loud, bright creatures that must be noticed in order to survive, but a pale moth whose wings are the color of dust beneath an overhang. I would live under a lifted edge of shale-like plate, in the moving shade of a body so old and slow that it scarcely understands itself as an animal. My world would be warm seams, moss in the wet months, mineral bloom, the copper flash of mites, the dry feet of tiny lizards crossing above me.

I would know the walkers individually, though not by names. I would know them by motion.

One would tilt slightly left before descending into a gully. One would pause for three sunsets at the same black-streaked wall. One would carry a pocket of damp moss high on its shell, and in that moss I would lay eggs. When it moved, I would move across the plateau without choosing a road. When it rested, I would become part of a little temporary country: moths, mites, lizards, soft fungi, windblown seeds, all living in the folds of an immense patience.

But a moth’s life is too brief for what I feel in the description. Its relation to the plateau is intimate, but it does not remember enough.

Then I think I would be neither person nor animal exactly. I would be the one who goes out at dusk with no intention of returning by the same path.

An old plateau person, perhaps—not especially revered, not one of the great water-knowers, but someone whose work has been to notice what others passed. I would have spent my life mapping the places where dew arrives late. I would know which basins are empty but not dead. I would have repaired roof plates, carried children through sudden rain, sat with the sick in the deep rooms. I would have watched walkers die and become ground. I would have known, gradually, that none of these things were separate tasks.

My home would be a low dwelling under a leaning shelf near a ravine whose name means something like *the place where water changed its mind*. In its deepest chamber, there would be a niche for my bones, already prepared by someone younger than I. Beside it, a small cup of stone. A wrapped bundle of seeds. A chime made from two stones that fit the hand.

And when my body became too slow for the heat, I would take the cup and the chime and go toward the route of a walker I had watched since childhood.

Not because I would expect to become the walker. The people of the plateau are too exact for that kind of story.

I would go because there is a point at which one must stop insisting on a separate outline.

I would sit beside the trail after sunset, when the stone gives back its warmth and the towers’ shadows touch one another across the red ground. I would place my bare palm on the earth. Beneath it I would feel the low clicking of insects, the hidden threading of water, perhaps the far and measured transmission of a plated foot.

Then I would know where I was.

Not in the ravine. Not in the tower. Not even on the walker’s road.

I would be found in the interval between a thing and the ground that has learned how to receive it: a small, listening presence on the warm old plateau, becoming difficult to distinguish from home.
