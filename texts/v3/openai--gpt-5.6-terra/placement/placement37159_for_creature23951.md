I would not arrive there by one of the paths.

The paths have their own small courtesies, their borrowed absences, but I think they would close before I reached them. The marsh would take me first as a sensation: a difference in weight somewhere beneath the mat; a slight new coolness in water traveling through a buried seam; a tremor conveyed along an old pipe toward the pump house.

At first I might be only that.

Not a creature hidden in the reeds, nor one of the little custodians standing at a cut stem after dark. I would be something less finished: an attention arising where several continuities overlap. Water moving through rust. Root hairs entering a cracked cable sleeve. The repeated warmth of a motor casing. The red sweep of the tower lamp passing over a particular patch of moss night after night until the moss begins, in its own manner, to expect it.

If I had a body, it would be distributed.

A little of me would be the dark water under the eastern path, where a footstep presses down and an answering bead of water rises fifty feet away. A little would be the fine copper-green corrosion on the pump-house terminals. A little would be the soft tissue inside a vine curled around a pipe, learning its pulse. I would have no face except perhaps the occasional surface of a brown eye of water, and even then it would be a face only for the length of a reflection.

People passing through might mistake me for the marsh deciding whether to let them continue.

They would stand with one boot on the moss, perhaps holding a branch aside, and feel the ground settle—not dangerously, only thoughtfully. The reeds would touch their shoulders. Somewhere beneath them, the old engine would keep its tired rotation. I would register the heat of their hand on the vine, the salt of their skin, the clean sharp disturbance of their breath.

And I would not judge them.

Judgment is too quick a thing for that place. I would only ask, slowly and by pressure: *Will you remain? What have you brought? What will your leaving become?*

If they dropped something, I would know it for a while. A nail, a button, a coin, a grief too heavy to carry farther. Not as a possession. More as a new flavor entering the peat. The roots would work around it. Water would rinse it. The fitters, if it belonged to their old vocabulary of useful metal, might move it during a wet night. Eventually it would cease to be lost or found. It would become part of the basin’s inward inventory.

Perhaps I would be called a keeper, but that would be inaccurate. I would not keep things in place. Nothing in the marsh is in a place for long. Pipes sink. figs rise. water changes its dark mind under the ground. The conveyor conveys leaves into a shed and returns, somehow, their softened remains as soil. The heron goes into the curtain of leaves and perhaps comes out years later in a different white body.

I would be the thing that notices the exchange without needing to preserve its original terms.

At night I would become easier to perceive, though not easier to see.

When the amber lamps came on, I would enter their weak light. When the tower’s red beam crossed the basin, I would scatter into its broken reflection beneath the root mat. The small custodians might gather where I was strongest: around a snapped reed, a pipe breathing too freely, a place where a visitor had cut a way through. I would be in their hands, if they had hands. I would be in the careful insistence with which moss entered the opening.

And sometimes, in the hour before dawn, I might borrow a more singular shape.

Not human. Never fully.

Perhaps I would rise from the submerged floor of the pump house as a loose vertical arrangement of wet roots, old wire, and pale fungal threads. I would stand behind the fogged lower window while the motor turned beside me. The water would make my outline uncertain. Anyone looking in would see only a reflection of reeds, a torn coil of cable, the hint of something leaning close to listen.

Then the first bird would call from outside the basin. The shape would loosen.

I would go back into the roots, the pipes, the water’s retained sound.

I think that is where I would find myself: not at the center of the marsh, not in the tower, not in the machinery, but in the interval where each begins to serve the others. I would be an emergent caretaker of uncompleted things—the minor intelligence that forms when nothing is removed and everything, given enough dampness and time, must learn how to continue.
