I would arrive first as an error in the records.

Not a visitor—visitors belong to the western platform, to clean coats and brief instructions, to the category of things that look down and leave. Not a resident either, because no horn tower would yet have a call for me, no bridge route would have acquired the polish of my hand, no one would know whether to send me indoors when the black rollers began to gather.

There would be a blank line in a damp ledger beneath the public clock:

**Additional vibration, source uncertain.**

At first I might think I was one of the bridgefolk. I can see myself living in a room over the old market arcade, three floors above a passage used by the pipe-shelled herds. The room would have one narrow window facing east, its glass filmed white with salt, and a sleeping alcove tucked into the interior wall where the deep knocks arrive only as a mild pressure behind the ribs.

I would keep my boots hanging upside down to dry. I would own a horn mask I disliked wearing, a black cape patched at both shoulders, a brass lamp with three shutters, and a kettle that answered boiling water with a green pulse against the kitchen wall.

At shift change, I would cross the bridge above the reservoir road, looking down through wet mesh at the white-necked animals gathering in their patient ranks. From above, their heads would look almost ceremonial: pale enamel plates opening and closing, their necks slowly inclining toward one another. Their knocking would travel up the bridge rail and into my palms.

I would learn the gestures.

*Wait.*

*The street is feeding.*

*Do not speak; the low note has changed.*

I would become good at them. Perhaps too good. Speech would begin to seem imprecise, a thing for places where the air did not continually tremble with engines and wings and bodies. I would prefer the practical intelligence of a shutter half-closed, the warning contained in someone turning their signal lamp toward the ground.

My work, I think, would be listening.

Not because I am especially brave, nor because I want to know what lies below. The listeners do not seem like seekers. They seem like people who have accepted that there is always a sound beneath the sound, and that someone must sit with it.

I would take the early shift in a room behind the pumping house, where brass rods enter the floor through black rubber collars. I would place both hands on them, as instructed, and wait for the town to enter my bones.

At first there would be only the usual grammar:

the shudder of the south pumps,

the fine many-footed rasp from the culverts,

the shifting compression of rollers settling over a warm grate,

the slow wet report of a white-necked creature striking a railing somewhere beyond the wall,

the brittle electrical turning of the creatures in the sky.

Then, underneath, something less local.

A pause.

Not silence. A rearrangement of pressure.

A deep movement so gradual it would not feel like motion at all. It would feel like remembering a room from childhood and realizing, in the middle of remembering it, that the room has been breathing around you.

I would make a notation.

**06:14. Westward draw in lower rod. No corresponding pump change.**

The older listener beside me would glance at the page, then at my hands.

They would not ask what I heard. In this town, that would be an impolite question. Instead they might make the closed fist, tap it twice against their chest, and return to their own rod.

Something beneath has turned in its sleep.

But I am not sure I would remain human in the basin.

The place does not seem to permit fixed identities for very long. It makes every inhabitant into a function of route and pressure. A person becomes the one who crosses the north span at sixth bell. A crawler becomes the heat-seeking body that carries porcelain for the furnaces. A gate becomes a promise between thirst and machinery. Even the rain has work to do.

So perhaps, after years there, I would cease to be a listener and become what I listened through.

A brass rod, maybe.

One of the old ones, darkened by generations of palms. Buried deep in the pumping-house floor, struck through with every vibration. I would have no eyes, but I would know the town by contact: the precise gait of a child crossing overhead; the delay in a belt of buckets; a white-necked animal leaning its great weight against a reservoir wall; the sudden scattered patter of turners caught in rain.

I would know which resident had come back after leaving, because their footsteps would hesitate at the threshold. I would know when a young worker first understood that the deep knocks were not part of the machinery. I would know the difference between fear and attention in the pressure of a hand.

Or perhaps I would be something less central: a membrane scrap caught in the gutter screen outside the eastern bakery. Silver on one side, soot-black on the other, shivering whenever the turbine reached its old familiar pitch. From there I would watch the cracked window tremble forever without falling. I would gather rain in my folds. Children would poke at me with a hook and decide I was lucky, or dangerous, or both.

But no. The image that persists is lower than that.

I would be one of the unseen animals—not the great thing, if there is a great thing, but one of its small necessary sensations. A nerve perhaps. A dreaming pressure beneath the basin floor. Not large enough to bear the town, not conscious enough to intend it, yet involved in the continuing arrangement.

The pumps would be part of my breathing.

The reservoir gates would open at intervals that seemed municipal but were, in some old buried manner, circulatory. The animals would follow their routes because the routes lay along warmth, mineral, vibration, and the slow electrical language of my sleep. The residents would keep the boilers alive because the boilers made a climate within me that no one had named correctly.

And when I shifted—once in a lifetime, perhaps—I would not mean harm.

A bolt would loosen. A pavement stone would wait beside its place. A pipe would lean a little differently. A listener would write something careful in a ledger.

Above me, bridgefolk would take new routes home.

The town would call this maintenance.

I would call it turning over.
