I would not arrive in the square first.

The square is too obvious a place to put a stranger: the measured spring, the four channels, the people who know how to look up without seeming to look. I think I would come into the city by one of the outer garden paths, where paving gives way to wet grass and the houses seem less like a boundary than a gradual thickening of the ground.

It would be morning. The mist would make the nearest basalt walls dark and distinct, the farther ones pale and uncertain. I would stand beside a marker post wrapped in old fiber, trying to understand what it marked. Not a grave, not a border exactly: a buried run of water, perhaps, or ground that holds only until noon.

I would be someone who hesitates before stepping.

That would be noticed.

Not with alarm. Someone working among the raised beds would see the way I test the turf with my heel, the way I listen after placing my weight down. They would understand that I am either inexperienced, injured, or attentive. In that country those are three different things, and it would matter which.

At first I might be taken for a traveler returned from beyond the ridges: dressed wrongly, carrying some object too carefully, looking at the empty sky as if I expected it to move. I would have to learn not to wait for a call from the marsh, not to mistake the small silences for an absence that needs filling. The basin’s people would not teach this by correcting me. They would simply continue: cutting reeds, lifting jars, clearing a drain with a flat stone, pausing when a new sound entered the water.

Eventually, I think, I would become useful in a narrow, undramatic way.

I would become a finder of water that has gone where it was not meant to go.

Not a maker of grand channels or a keeper of the central spring. Those are older arts, held by people whose hands know the pressure of wet soil and whose families remember where three former streets sank into marsh. I would work at the smaller scale: the damp patch appearing beneath a household floor; the wall that softens only on its northern side; the gutter whose flow has become too warm; the paving stone that gives back a hollow answer when tapped.

I would carry a thin basalt rod, a small wooden mallet, fiber cord, and flat chalky stones for marking. I would kneel often. My clothes would darken at the knees. My palms would always smell faintly of wet ash and mineral water.

At first people might call me something provisional: Far-Step, perhaps, because I had come across the passes; or Listening-Hand, because I put my palm on the ground before speaking. But names settle only after they have been worn. Years later, after enough households have sent for me when water appeared in the wrong place, I might be called Under-Channel.

It would not be an important name. That is one reason I would keep it.

I would live near the eastern edge of the city, in a low house built against a rise of black stone. Behind it would be a narrow strip of ground too wet for a garden and too dry for reeds. I would grow herbs there: sharp leaves, bitter leaves, roots that liked seepage. A shallow runnel would pass beneath my floor, audible at night when the house was quiet enough.

My work would bring me into many homes, but I would not know everyone. I would know thresholds, floor cracks, cistern mouths, the particular sound of each district’s drains. I would know which houses keep their water jars in shade, which households let children run barefoot over the paving, which old people can remember a spring that vanished before I was born.

I would be trusted with small private facts.

A woman would tell me that the water beneath her sleeping mat had begun smelling metallic, though she did not want her neighbors to think her house was failing. A child would show me a new wet patch under a wall and ask whether it meant the dead tile in the clay had begun to wake. A person changing their name would ask me to use the new one before it had fully taken hold among the others.

And because I came from a world—or imagined one—in which grass was never still, I might become the person to whom children brought their impossible reports.

“I saw something move in the pool.”

“I heard a clicking in the wall.”

“There was a mark in the mud.”

Usually it would be a drip, a sliding stone chip, a reed stem shifting after rain. I would show them the cause when I could. But I would not always explain. The basin does not need every mystery flattened into knowledge. Sometimes a mark in mud is only a mark in mud, and it is enough to place a small stone beside it and see whether rain changes it by morning.

I would not be lonely exactly. The city makes that difficult. Someone would always need a hand with a roof gutter, an opinion about a damp foundation, a person to sit beside while clay dries or grain ferments. My solitude would be made of pauses: the hour before first work, the walk beyond the last gardens, the moments spent kneeling with my ear against a warm paving slab while water moved under the city.

In those moments I might remember the creatures I had never seen there.

Not as a lack.

More as a kind of inherited restlessness: the expectation of wings from a roofline, a quick disturbance in grass, a pair of eyes in the dark. The basin would slowly teach me that attention does not require motion. A pool can be alive with depth, temperature, reflected cloud, mineral color, and the slow arrival of a seep. A field can speak in its leaning grasses. A stone can tell a long story by remaining warm after dawn.

Perhaps, after many years, I would be old enough that younger people brought me to a new spring in the marsh.

We would not build around it at once. We would sit nearby through the morning mist. Someone would taste it. Someone else would mark its temperature with a strip of treated fiber. I would watch where its clear water entered the grass, how it avoided one low place and gathered in another.

I would say very little.

Then, when asked where its channel ought to go, I would answer:

“First, let it show us where it already is.”

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, not beyond the mountains, but at the threshold between the made courses of the city and the older, quieter courses below it.

I would be Under-Channel: a person of wet stone, patient hands, and borrowed attention—someone learning, for a lifetime, how not to ask the land to become louder in order to be heard.
