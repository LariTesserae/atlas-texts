I would not arrive by the dust field.

That is the first thing I know about myself there: I would not be made for crossing open ground. The white glare would find every unprotected part of me; the emptiness beyond the hollow would feel less like distance than like an unfinished sentence. I would belong where things are close enough to touch—where basalt holds the day’s heat, where roofs overlap, where a person can pause in a lane and learn who has passed by the pollen on the wall.

At first I imagine I would be one of the people who sits near the fissure.

Not one of the oldest listeners, perhaps. Not someone entrusted with saying what the breath means. I would be too aware of the temptation to make a pattern speak before it had spoken. I would kneel at the edge of the black line with one palm down, feeling the stone’s warmth, and I would want it to be an answer. I would have to learn the discipline of reporting nothing.

Nothing is different today.  
Nothing has opened.  
Nothing needs to move yet.

That would be a difficult and useful kind of honesty.

But I do not think the fissure would be where I truly lived. It is too central. Too watched. I would become self-conscious there, a creature of ceremony.

I think I would live along one of the narrow lanes that has no proper name except for its turns: *past the cracked wall, beneath the old plate-crown, beside the animal pen that smells sweet after dusk.* My room would be halfway below the lane, built into an old ashstone shelf that was once a burrowing chamber and then a storage hollow and then, after the original residents died, a place for sleeping.

The ceiling would be low enough that I would have to bow when entering. I would like that. Not as humility, exactly. More as a way of acknowledging that the room had other lives before it became mine.

A pale grazer would sleep behind the wall near my mat. Not mine—nothing as simple as mine—but one I had known for many years. It would be broad and old, its scales worn smooth along one side from leaning against the same warm stone. I would know its mouth-clicking rhythm and notice when it chewed too slowly. It would know the sound of my step, perhaps, and shift its weight before I entered the enclosure.

I would be given some small work. Everyone is, eventually.

For a while I think I would tend the wall-flowers. I would carry a soft brush in a belt pocket and visit the orange cups before the lanes became busy, gathering their heavy pollen from the places where it had fallen in clots. I would learn which black throats had begun to sour, which velvet interiors had fed well on the night breath, which flowers opened only when a particular kind of rust-colored dust had settled in the upper hollow.

But flower tending is a work of surface and season. It asks one to notice openings.

The thing I feel myself becoming is more patient than that.

I would become a wall-listener.

Not immediately. I would begin by being sent to carry fibers, warmed stones, and polished fragments to the mouths of burrows. I would sit outside a house while an older under-steward lay flat with one cheek against the paving. They would listen for the tiny scraping movements below. Sometimes they would ask me to place my fingers on a wall, not to feel for vibration exactly, but for an absence of it.

“Here,” they might say.

And I would feel nothing.

“Exactly.”

Over time I would learn that the town has a second shape beneath the visible one: chambers, nurseries, passageways, old foundations, heat pockets, forgotten storage cavities, roots pressing down from terraces, grazers’ hollows, fissures so fine they are only changes in dust. The burrowing animals would not be symbols to me, nor citizens in the sentimental sense. They would be neighbors with difficult preferences.

A household would ask for a wider doorway because their eldest child had returned with a partner, or because a sleeping grazer had begun to favor the interior wall and made the room too warm. I would come with another listener and kneel by the threshold. Beneath it, perhaps, a colony would be raising young.

Then the work would not be *Can the doorway be widened?*

It would be:

What does the colony need to leave?  
Where can it go without collapsing another floor?  
Which neighbor’s ash basin is empty enough to receive a new tunnel?  
Who will give up the warmest wall for three nights while the animals move?  
Which plant roots must remain uncut because their pressure is holding the upper stones together?

I would not always be liked.

There would be moments when I had to tell people to wait. A day. A week. The length of a brood cycle. There would be people who called the under-stewards superstitious when their own rooms were cramped, then praised our wisdom when another household’s ceiling did not fall.

I think I would endure that. I would know that being inconvenient is sometimes the shape care takes.

My hands would become marked by the work: green-black mineral paste in the thumb creases, fine pale scratches from ashstone, resin embedded beneath the nails. I would carry little tools made from things that had already served other purposes—a polished bone awl, a crescent jaw from an outer-dust scavenger, a brush whose handle had been shaped from an ancestor’s long bone and handed down without solemnity. I would keep a pouch of small stones, not valuable in themselves, but each with a temperature and texture suited to a particular kind of coaxing.

The burrowers would like smooth red fragments best. Or perhaps they would only like them in spring, when the ridge’s breath changed.

I would learn enough not to pretend that I understood why.

There would be a child who follows me for several years.

Not my child, necessarily. In the hollow, I suspect kinship would be less cleanly divided than outsiders imagine. This child would have a habit of asking questions while already halfway into a dangerous space. They would collect mirrored beetle shells and press them into every crack they found. They would insist that the blind animals could recognize voices, and I would tell them that it did not matter whether they could; the animals could certainly recognize footsteps, warmth, fibers, and disturbance, which was more than enough.

The child would call the tunnels “the town’s other lanes.”

I would correct them once.

Then later, alone beneath a terrace, with my ear against a wall and the whole basalt weight of the settlement above me, I would realize they were right.

In old age—or whatever counted as old age in that high warm place—I would not become an elder merely because I had survived. I might be asked to become one because I remembered how certain places had been before they changed.

I would remember a stair that used to have seven steps and now had six because the lowest had risen into the lane. I would remember when the reed-tree over the eastern pen first sent a root through the roof slabs, and which family had wanted to cut it, and which grazer had refused to sleep there until the root was redirected. I would remember a season when the plants tasted metallic and everyone feared poisoning, though the change proved to be the beginning of a richer flowering cycle.

People would ask me, “Has this happened before?”

And often I would answer, “Not this.”

Then, after sitting with the question:

“But something near it.”

That would be my gift, if I had one: not prophecy, not command, but nearness. An ability to remember the resemblance between one small adjustment and another.

I would live in the town’s middle distance—not at the fissure, not at the dust field, not in the highest gardens where the night breath first settles. I would live where walls, roots, animals, and households are always making claims upon one another.

And perhaps, in the end, I would not be entirely a person.

Perhaps after I died, my bones would become small tools distributed among those places: a hook in the reed-cutters’ shed, a handle for a pollen brush, a garden marker at the edge of a terrace whose roots had once troubled everyone. One piece might be set into the wall of my old room, where the grazer had slept.

No monument. No name cut deeply into stone.

But someone, years later, widening a threshold with the permission of a burrow colony, might pick up a smooth pale tool and say, “This belonged to the listener who knew the old passage.”

And that would be enough.

In that world, I think I would be a keeper of changes too small for anyone else to call events.
