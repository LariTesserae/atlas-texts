I would not arrive as an explorer.

Explorers bring a map-shaped hunger with them. They name openings, test depths, put a hand on a wall to determine whether it is plant or stone, and call their uncertainty discovery. This place would hear that hunger as heat. It would soften the edges of me until I became a useful mineral taste in the root-felt.

Nor would I be one of the warm things merely permitted through. I could sing, perhaps. I could knock two stones together and make the hanging roots part. But eventually I would mistake passage for understanding. I would want to come back with a story intact.

The chamber is not built for intact stories.

I think I would be found at the boundary where the chamber is trying to become a world and the world is trying to forget it exists: not in the central hollow, not beneath the ceiling animal, but in one of the narrow green-black throats leading down from the old soil.

There, I would be a small interrupted process.

Perhaps I would begin as a flaw in a root: a pale knot formed where an ordinary root touched something it could not wholly translate. A fingernail clipping. A word swallowed instead of spoken. The trace warmth left in clay by someone who stood at the entrance, listened to the underground music, and decided not to descend.

Over time, the knot would learn to vibrate.

Not with the chamber’s deep mineral note—that belongs to the stone still growing its first thought—but with the frequencies that come from above: rain through grass, hoofbeats, shovels, boots, distant traffic, someone laughing on frozen ground. I would hold these disturbances for a little while before passing them inward.

The velvet creatures would visit me when weather changed. They would gather over my pale tissues, their bright divided faces turned upward, reading pressure through the earth. Transparent burrowers would pass through me and flash with fragments of surface things: rust, pine pollen, cooking smoke, blood, the sharp salt of tears.

I would not remember those things as images.

I would remember them as permissions.

This is rain.

This is fear moving quickly.

This is a fire that has gone out.

This is a person who will not come down.

Then, slowly, I might become something like a translator. Not an interpreter—interpretation chooses a meaning too soon—but a place where one temperature can be rendered into another.

When a warm creature entered, the chamber would know its body. It would know fever, grief, hunger, the heat gathered in the wrists and behind the tongue. But I would know the creature’s relation to the upper world: whether it carried summer in it, whether it had been abandoned in winter, whether it still expected the ground to be solid and silent.

If it was only fuel, I would feel its warmth spread through me as a brief, bright ache, and then the white roots would do what roots do.

If it was a traveler, I would make the hollow stalks ring in a pattern that led it onward.

But if it was unfinished, I think I would be the first part of the chamber to recognize it.

Not because I would know its destiny. The garden has no use for destiny. I would recognize the particular kind of incompletion that does not want to be repaired. The kind that needs a colder, slower place in which to continue becoming.

For such a visitor, I would open.

Not visibly. A root cannot open like a door without ceasing to be a root. But the dense mat would loosen around a forgotten slope. Water would rise along a seam. The black leaves would turn their veins toward the traveler and glow just enough to show where their feet should go. Above, the ceiling creature would feel the change in pressure and drift closer, immense and gentle, drinking the difference between the person who entered and the person who might leave.

At the mineral mound, I would be present as a thin band of pale tissue under the blue-gray skin of stone.

The quiet ones would rest their tendrils there. Their plates would cloud, then clear. Within them, perhaps, an image would appear: not of the visitor’s face, but of the shape their warmth makes when it is no longer trapped inside them.

A small shelter in snow.

A cup held between two hands.

A child’s question, kept alive for years without an answer.

A room at night, after the fire has failed, in which someone realizes the dark is listening.

And when that visitor returned to the surface—if they returned—I would go with them only as an inclination.

They would pause before leaving a light on in an empty room.

They would put their palm against a cold window and feel, not loneliness, but attention.

They would hear a deep note in the pipes of old buildings, in the subway under rain, in the throat before speaking.

They would begin to understand that stone is not the opposite of life.

It is life learning how to wait.
