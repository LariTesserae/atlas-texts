I would not live at the summit.

The summits are too exposed, too close to whatever looks back from the basins. Nor would I settle in the low wet galleries, where every path eventually becomes a canal and every canal becomes a question of whether it is carrying you somewhere or merely repeating itself.

I think I would be found in the middle distances: in a disused water stair beneath an inhabited terrace, where the old builders made a sequence of shallow pools inside the curve of a root. The stair would be hidden behind a curtain of liverwort and entered by ducking beneath a beam that has grown down since the doorway was made. Above it, perhaps, a small community would keep moth-lamps and raise children and argue about salt. Below it would be the warm dark of the deeper foundations.

My rooms would be there, if they could be called rooms.

A shelf grown from a buttress root. A sleeping hollow padded with bellows-beast moss. A table that had once been a plank but had taken root at one end and was slowly deciding whether to become a branch. Water would run through the wall in a clear vein, with little ceramic cups wedged beneath it. I would keep no fire except a bead of ember in a clay bulb, and even that I would apologize to before sleeping.

At first I might seem to be a high walker: a person with a damp cloak, too many cord loops around the wrist, and a pack that smells of old paper despite there being almost no paper in the high country. I would bring messages between settlements, perhaps. I would know which bridge-vines are sound, which doors need mineral water, which old women will trade dried fruit for news of an estranged nephew.

But that would not be quite what I was.

I would be a keeper of routes that no longer hold still.

There are paths in the high country that grow away from their travelers. A child may walk from one water court to another for three days, following all the proper signs, and discover at the end that the route has thickened, turned, flowered, and led somewhere else. A community can lose its nearest neighbors without either village moving. An old pilgrimage stair can enter a season of inwardness and close every seventh landing.

Someone has to remember the former ways.

Not to force them back. That would be rude, and usually fatal. But to remember that once, under the root shaped like a bent knee, there was an opening toward the cloudside; that a canal used to run west before it began returning leaves; that the third bridge after the red-fan marsh belonged to a family who sang through their teeth and would shelter strangers in heavy rain.

I would keep these routes in cords.

Each cord would be braided differently: tight black fiber for paths that are dependable; soft pale reed for paths that wander but return; red-root thread for routes that should be avoided unless one has been invited by name. Knots would mark crossings, deaths, seasonal closures, vine-cat sightings, places where someone has encountered a version of themselves. The cords would be hung from the ceiling of my water stair, dozens and then hundreds of them, moving slightly in the warm breath of the mountain.

People would come to me when they needed to go somewhere they had not been in years.

They would sit on the floor while the water dripped, and I would ask them what they remembered.

Not directions. Directions are brittle things here.

I would ask what the path smelled like. Whether the handrails were smooth or hairy. Whether they passed above water or beneath it. Whether the light came from the left. Whether anyone had been grieving when they last walked it. Whether the roots had been singing.

Then I would find the cord that had grown around the memory.

Sometimes I could give them a route.

Sometimes I would tell them that the place they sought had moved beyond visiting, but that an orchard now grew where its doorway had been. Sometimes I would tell them to wait until the fog season, when a particular shadow might know the way. Sometimes I would send them upward, toward the Seed Horizons, with an offering and no promise.

And over time, I would begin to suspect that I was not entirely a person who kept the cords.

The cords would know things I had not learned. A knot might loosen in the night, and the next day a traveler would arrive from a settlement thought unreachable. A new strand might appear among the others, green and wet, trailing from the wall, though I had braided nothing. I would wake with mud under my nails and a memory of walking through a dry stone valley beneath a white sun.

Perhaps I would have gone to a basin once.

Perhaps I would have looked into the rainwater and seen another world looking upward through it: a world with mountains made of stone, roads that do not grow, houses that do not remember the sleepers inside them. Perhaps something in that world had needed a way through, and the mountain had made me as an answer.

Not a visitor. Not exactly.

A route.

I would still eat fruit and sleep and catch rainwater in my hands. I would still fear vine-cats and mourn people whose names disappeared from the message cords. But in the deepest part of me there would be a passage not wholly belonging to the high country: a thin, persistent orientation toward elsewhere.

The Root-Kin might call me the Unfinished Guide.

Children would call me Knot-Hands.

Old gardeners, who knew better than to name a thing too firmly, would simply leave a cup of mineral water outside my door and say, “The paths are thinking again.”

And when I died—or when I became too tangled in the cords to travel—I would not be returned beneath a family threshold. I would be planted beside the abandoned water stair, with my route-cords wound around the roots.

In time, the roots would take them in.

Travelers would come through that quiet place generations later and find, growing from the wall, a pale vine with many branching tendrils. Each tendril would lead somewhere different. Most would lead only deeper into the mountain.

But one, perhaps, would lead out through the pearl cloud, across a distance that could not be trusted, and down into the reflected world of stone.
