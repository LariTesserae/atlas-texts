I would not live at the Basin, though I would go there often.

At first I imagine myself in one of the houses at the city’s edge, where the shelf begins to loosen into fern gullies and the ground has more roots than walls. It would be a narrow house, half fitted into a shoulder of pale stone, with a roof that ticks all night in rain. One room for sleeping, one for work, and a covered porch built over a dark seep where frogs speak in weather.

But I do not think I would own it in the settled sense. It would be the sort of house people say I am *keeping company with*.

I would be a witness—or not quite one.

The official witnesses measure what appears: the new stone, the warm vent, the sudden spring. They make marks that can be trusted when the next generation asks what the ground was doing before it rose beneath their pantry or opened a wet seam through their garden wall.

My work would begin a little earlier, in the nearly invisible interval before appearance.

I would be called something like a **listener of thresholds**, though children would have a ruder and better name for me: perhaps *Mud-Ear*, or *the one who hears rocks thinking*.

I would walk the outer paths before dawn, when the city has not yet begun hammering, cooking, arguing, carrying planks, or arranging the rain towers. I would carry no grand equipment: a waxed hood, a lamp with a shuttered flame, a small hand bell, a measuring cord, strips of blue and ochre cloth, and a shallow wooden bowl.

The bowl would be my first instrument.

I would place it upside down on paving stones, on grass hummocks, on the packed earth beside foundations, and put my ear to its base. Sometimes the earth beneath would make a sound so small it might be imagination: a faint dry click inside all that wetness; the slow granular settling of a stone finding its shape; a pulse in the soil that did not belong to water. I would not call these predictions. The people of the First Shelf would distrust that word. I would call them invitations to look again.

Most mornings, there would be nothing.

That would be important. To listen without needing a message would be part of the work.

On some mornings I would find a place where the moss had changed color overnight, or where the fog lay too low and thick in a narrow strip of garden. I would kneel. I would touch the ground with the backs of my fingers. I would smell for wet iron, sharp mineral bitterness, the sour green scent of disturbed roots. Then I would tie a blue strip to the nearest post and leave it alone.

Blue would mean: *the ground is considering something.*

Ochre would mean: *do not place weight here.*

Red, which I would use reluctantly, would mean: *come with shovels, brush mats, and people who know how to move a wall.*

I think I would have come from below, from a city where the roads had gutters of iron and the stones were so old that everyone assumed they would remain where they were put. Perhaps I would have been trained there in maps. Straight maps: borders, foundations, property lines, routes drawn in ink that pretended the land had consented.

I would arrive at the First Shelf intending to stay only through one wet season.

I would bring a leather case with instruments too fine for the place: brass dividers, a glass level, folded papers sealed against rain. The Shelf would ruin them gradually and without malice. The leather would mildew. The paper would curl. The brass would grow a dull green bloom in its hinges. My measurements would fail not because they were false, but because they assumed that a line was something one could finish drawing.

At first this would make me furious.

Then, over several years, I would learn to make maps that had room in them.

My maps would not show only buildings and paths. They would show the places where paths had been. They would mark old springs that had gone blind, alder roots beneath retaining walls, the direction in which a stone plate had risen over three seasons, the homes where children should not sleep during prolonged rain, the dry ledges where the dead had once been moved with songs and lanterns.

People would begin bringing me small reports.

“My back step has become warm.”

“The goats will not cross the west field.”

“There is a new taste in the washhouse water.”

“My daughter says the kitchen floor is humming.”

I would listen to all of them, including the daughter.

Especially the daughter.

There would be a rain tower near my house—not one of the tall central ones that visitors admire, but a low, crooked tower in a garden where water gathered under the planks. I would maintain it poorly by formal standards and beautifully by local ones. It would have cracked seedpods, slate chips, small bones, a copper spoon bent almost flat, fired-clay cups glazed in different colors, and one polished stone that produced no sound at all but gathered a bead of water at its lowest point before releasing it with great seriousness.

When I could not decide what the ground was telling me, I would stand beneath that tower in the rain.

Not because it gave answers. Because its many sounds would prevent me from mistaking one sound for the whole world.

I would be known as quiet, though not wise. I would have a habit of stopping in the middle of conversations to look at drainage. I would be unreliable at feasts because I would leave early to check whether a certain slope had taken too much water. I would make excellent tea, terrible bread, and durable but ugly gloves. I would have one old scar across my palm from trying, in my first year, to move a newly emerged stone alone.

The city would laugh at this for decades.

Eventually I would stop correcting people when they called me a witness, though the official witnesses would tease that I never wrote quickly enough. My records would be full of hesitations.

*Perhaps a line of stone beneath the pear garden.*

*Water behaving as though it remembers a channel here.*

*No visible movement, but the hill feels occupied.*

Those notes would frustrate future scholars.

They would also, sometimes, save a house.

And if I had a grave, it would not remain in one place. I would ask for that to be understood beforehand. Not a grand request—only that if the ground required me elsewhere, they should carry me onward without apology.

Perhaps my ashes would be mixed into the mortar of a small marker beside the unfinished gate at the city’s edge. Not the gateposts themselves; they have their own long uncertainty. But a low wall nearby, built to guide water away from the descending road.

On it, someone would carve:

**HERE THE PATH WAS MOVED  
BEFORE IT BROKE.**

And on damp mornings, when travelers passed through the gate toward the lower world, they might see blue cloth tied to a post beside that wall.

Not a warning exactly.

An invitation to put a hand to the ground, wait a moment, and learn where they stand.
