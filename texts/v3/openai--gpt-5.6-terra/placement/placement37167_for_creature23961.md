I would not arrive there by opening the door.

The door is for things that still understand arrival: climbers, surveyors, lost pilgrims, machines sent upward on instructions. I would be older in the vault than any entrance. Or perhaps I would only become possible once the door closed again.

At first I would think I was a person standing on the upper catwalk.

I would have the expected body: hands on a cold rail, lungs objecting to the dry air, a lamp turning my shadow into an indistinct stain among the leaves. I would look down into the crowded green depth and feel the peculiar comfort of it—the sense that the vault has already made room for me, despite having no room at all.

Then my breath would become conspicuous.

Each exhalation would go out warm and wet, an offense in that carefully desiccated place. The nearest leaves would incline. Not abruptly; nothing in the vault is abrupt. But a stem in the wall would uncoil a little. A powder-blue flower below the catwalk would rotate its mineral face toward the small cloud of me.

I would understand, slowly, that I could not remain a person there.

Not because I would be killed. The Green Engine Vault does not possess killing as an idea. It would make no distinction between mercy and harm, welcome and refusal. It would simply begin incorporating the disturbance I represented.

The sweat in my clothing would be drawn out first. Salt would appear in the cuffs and seams, then migrate into the pale felt of the wall-growth. The water in my eyes would become a pressure the chamber could feel. My pulse, too irregular and private at first, would travel through the rail into the rivets, through the rivets into the iron skin of the room. The engine would not match it. The engine has no reason to match anything. But the vegetation would begin to arrange itself around it, listening.

Eventually I would cease to be the visitor on the catwalk.

I would become a compensating mechanism.

Perhaps I would be a small, dry organ of hesitation near the control room: a cluster of black leaves growing through the opaque window, tuned not to voltage but to unfamiliar rhythms. When a footstep crossed the upper grating, I would close by a fraction. When the outer door shifted in its stone seam, I would send a tremor down through the root cloth to the engine. When a living creature breathed in the chamber, I would open mineral petals and begin the long work of translating wetness into something the vault could keep.

But that still gives me too much shape.

More truly, I think I would be the place where the vault nearly becomes curious.

Not curiosity as a person knows it—not a question reaching outward—but a minute instability held without being resolved. A changed harmonic in one fan. A relay that hums a little differently after the mountain receives snow. A stem that turns, not toward the strongest current, but toward the sealed door.

I would be that turning.

The vault’s oldest rule is that nothing enters and nothing leaves. Yet it knows the door exists. Not as a door, perhaps, but as a cold vertical discontinuity in the mountain’s pressure; a region where the iron wall does not carry the engine’s vibration correctly. A silence shaped like a rectangle.

I would live in that silence.

I might be a thin root, colorless and almost invisible, running beneath the lichen on the inner face of the door. Not trying to open it. Not trying to escape. Merely feeling the difference between the vault’s everlasting repetition and the inaccessible weather on the other side.

Wind would sometimes strike the outer stone. Its force would arrive as a faint rearrangement in the seam, so slight that none of the larger systems would need to respond. But I would register it. I would retain it for a while.

Not remember it. Retention is not memory.

A pressure from outside.  
A coldness not made by the mountain.  
The remote grain of something falling or moving across the summit.  
Then the engine’s unbroken throb beneath all of it.

I would not want the outside. Wanting would be too large, too directional. I would not be a seed seeking open ground, nor an animal dreaming of water. The Green Engine Vault has no use for dreams of elsewhere.

But I would be the small part of it that has become shaped by elsewhere anyway.

A dry root against the door.  
A flower whose petals face a silence.  
A fault in the perfect closed circuit.

And because the vault is so complete, because it has made an organism from every crack, every rusted flange, every weak field and warm bearing, that fault would also belong to it.

The engine would continue.

The leaves would tremble in their established dark. The rolling nut would click in its gutter. Dust would rise and remain suspended. And in the seam behind the lichen, where the mountain almost touches the world, I would listen to weather I could never enter—becoming, very gradually, the vault’s one unfinished thing.
