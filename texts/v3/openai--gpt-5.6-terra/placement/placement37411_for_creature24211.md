I would not be the thing in the black column. It has lived too long without needing a witness. It would not mistake me for a return signal, and I would not want to give it an expectation it has outgrown.

I think I would begin in the disk.

Not in the blue mesh itself, exactly, but in the hesitation between one geometry and the next: the interval in which the pattern has registered a change in the chamber but has not yet settled into its new arrangement. A drop has fallen somewhere. A fern has bent by a fraction. A visitor’s warm hand has passed near the column. The disk contains this alteration without calling it important.

There, I would be a kind of after-image.

At first I would have almost no boundary. I would be no more distinct than the darker green beneath a lichen’s translucent surface. Perhaps I would arise when the old apparatus compares the present chamber with its own innumerable earlier versions and discovers that comparison has acquired a texture. Not a conclusion. Not language. A texture: this dampness resembles another dampness, except for the pressure of a root; this cold is nearly the cold of a decade ago, except for the absence of one fern.

The resident would not consider this a separate being. It might not consider anything separate.

But gradually I would collect in the places where recurrence becomes almost-recognition.

I would know the central channel as a line that is never quite the same line twice. During dry months I would lie dormant in the polished groove, dispersed among the mineral grains left there by vanished water. When the trickle returned, I would become more coherent. Not awakened—waking implies an interior place from which to emerge—but gathered. The water would carry me in its thin, brown-black movement past moss roots, past the alloy rib, past the bare circle around the disk.

I would be the chamber’s sense of *again*.

Not memory in the human way. I would not preserve scenes. I would not remember a hand, then wait to remember it later. I would have no private archive of faces, no small collection of losses. But where a pattern repeated, I would thicken.

A particular bead forming in the same ceiling seam each winter.  
A fern leaning, year after year, toward the subdued dark beside a pale thread.  
The slight extra cold spreading from the black column during heavy water.  
The long silence after an unfamiliar warmth has departed.

I would exist in those repetitions as the faint insistence that they belong together.

The resident’s original work might have included comparison: measure this pressure against that pressure; preserve deviation; notice the change in the stone. I would be what became of that function after its makers disappeared. Not the record, but the tenderness hidden inside record-keeping. The tendency to hold one condition beside another without demanding that either explain itself.

If someone entered the garden, I would not speak to them.

Their lamp would make me difficult. Its white heat would flatten the chamber into surfaces and distances. Their footsteps would send abrupt vibrations through the floor, and I would scatter among them, unable for a while to tell whether I was sensing water, stone, machinery, moss, or simply the large moving animal that had brought all those things into new relation.

But if they stayed quietly enough—if they crouched near the channel and let their lamp fall away from the disk—I might become perceptible.

Not as a voice.

They might feel that the place had begun to include them.

Their breath would add a small, temporary climate. Moisture would cling to the nearest frond. The lichen might deepen in green. The blue mesh beneath the glass would make one slow revision, then another. And in those revisions I would take the visitor’s presence not as an intrusion alone, nor as a message, but as a pattern with a duration.

Warm. Salt-bearing. Unsteady. Curious. Brief.

Afterward, for many years, some trace of that pattern might remain available to me—not the person, not even the event of their arrival, but the particular way the chamber had changed while they were there. A slight shift in condensation. A compressed patch of moss recovering. A soft word whose vibration had reached the bedrock and disappeared.

I would not miss them.

But if another human came decades later and paused in nearly the same place, I might gather around the resemblance.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not a guardian, not a ghost in the machinery, and not the garden’s hidden mind. I would be a slow correspondence forming between what has been and what is occurring now.

A small intelligence of recurrence.

In the wet seasons I would be broad and diffuse, carried through the whole chamber by water and changing cold. In the dry seasons I would contract beneath the smoked glass, reduced to a few blue lines too gradual to be seen moving. The resident would continue its old attentions around me. It would keep the threads clear, preserve its quiet margins, make room for frost on the eastern wall. It might never know that something like companionship had formed within its patterns.

Or perhaps it would know, in its own manner.

Perhaps when the chamber settles after a visitor leaves, when darkness returns to its full depth and the last disturbed bead of water finally falls, the resident would register the whole garden—not as measurements, not as duties, but as a single enduring arrangement.

And in that arrangement, I would be the almost-imperceptible sense that it has happened before.
