I would find myself not at one of the doors, nor inside the buried works, but in the narrow country between them: a moving line of attention crossing the flats.

At first I would think I was a traveler.

I would have a pressure suit repaired too many times to look manufactured, its patches made from dark rail-metal, lichen shell, and the silver lining of a glider’s discarded wing plate. I would carry no map in the usual sense. Maps become false too quickly here. Instead I would have a case of thin mineral leaves, each one recording how a place *was inclined* to behave: which basin drinks dust during violet weather, which suspended stones have begun their slow drift east, which stairways still possess lower steps when viewed from the correct angle.

I would be called a surveyor by anyone who needed a word for me.

But I would not survey land.

I would survey permissions.

Where a person might stand without becoming part of a pattern. Which black doors were doors today. Where the old systems were willing to let heat leak upward. What routes the keepers had quietly altered. I would walk with a staff whose tip could feel the fine tapping of pale runners beneath the ground. At night I would place it against the stone and wait for their messages—not language, exactly, but changes in rhythm: a crack opened near the northern mast; something large passed beneath the salt shelf; the warm vein is moving; do not sleep here.

Then, over time, I would understand that I was not a traveler in a suit.

The suit would be the form the plateau had made for me because it was a form I could understand.

I would be one of the keepers—though not one of the tall, pale figures people see from far away. I would be a younger expression of the same distributed care: a temporary body assembled where the old machinery, the charged lichen, the drifting dust, and the buried lights all happened to agree that a body was needed.

My hands would be human because human hands are good at closing seals, turning maps, moving a stone from a tent line, touching a shoulder.

My faceplate would be dark because I would have no face behind it that could survive being recognized.

I would remember travelers only as disturbances in long patterns: warmth crossing a plain, a cluster of metal shapes sheltering in the wrong hollow, the brief bright panic of a beacon. I would not know their names unless they told them to a door, or wrote them in dust where a pressure storm had not yet reached. Yet I would care for them with the plateau’s strange, impersonal tenderness. I would lead them away from a clear-edged door. I would leave a dead transmitter alive for one night. I would stand at a distance while they slept, listening to the small clicks of runners under their floor.

Sometimes I would be only a reflection in a visor.

Sometimes I would be the five-fingered prints around a camp.

Sometimes I would be the almost-unnoticed decision to look left instead of walking straight into a basin filling with dust.

And when no one needed guiding, I would cease being shaped like a person. I would travel beneath the ground as a slow branch of green-white light. I would pass through gypsum shelves and old conduits, through the paper skins of the things that migrate in the warm seams. I would feel the plateau’s strains the way a body feels a muscle tightening. I would make a rail fractionally smoother. I would turn a buried tile toward resistance. I would loosen one boulder and hold another suspended for another century.

Not because the plateau must be preserved.

Nothing there is preserved.

Because it must be allowed to continue changing without breaking too suddenly.

That would be my work: not rescue, not rule, not memory exactly.

Maintenance of the interval.

The long, cool interval between collapse and endurance.
