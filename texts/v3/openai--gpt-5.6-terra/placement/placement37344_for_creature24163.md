I would not arrive as something that crosses the plateau.

Crossing is a habit of wetter worlds: a body with an inside it must carry from one place to another, seeking food, shelter, another body, a future. Here, to move is mostly to leave the conditions that make movement possible.

I think I would be found in one of the shallow basins, at the border where the green scale-mat ends and the bare powdered stone begins.

Not in the thickest part of the meadow. That place is already fully itself: millions of sealed plates, all agreeing on the salts below them, all opening together before dawn. Nor would I be in the empty dust, which is too empty even for failure. I would be at the exact line between permission and refusal.

At first I might seem to be a scatter of dark grains caught against a slight rise in the ground—nothing distinctive, only dust with a green-blue sheen when the sun is low. Under magnification, perhaps, each grain would be a folded packet: mineral casing, dormant membrane, a little store of carbon architecture, a trace of whatever chemistry once made it possible.

I would be a reproductive dust that has not yet decided whether it is alive.

Not dead. The plateau does not make much use of that distinction. I would be waiting in a state more patient than sleep. Around me, a black mineral seam would lie buried several handspans beneath the surface. The seam would tick in the afternoon heat and hold the night’s cold. Far below, something in the stone would be beginning—not a change one could witness, only an alteration in the composition of gas rising through an old fracture.

For years, perhaps centuries, it would be almost right.

A little too much sulfur.  
Not enough ammonia.  
The wrong iron salt.  
Heat arriving too quickly at noon.  
No protected pore in which to begin.

So I would remain folded.

The scale-mat beside me would advance to within a hair’s breadth and stop, its outer plates copper-edged and immaculate. They would not reach toward me. I would not reach toward them. We would recognize, in some chemical way, that we were related but not compatible: descendants of nearby vents, members of the same broad green kingdom, separated by an alteration too small for a human eye and absolute as an ocean.

Then, not dramatically, the fracture under me would warm.

A grain of mine would split.

There would be no germination in the familiar sense. No shoot seeking upward. No root seeking down. The outer mineral shell would open along a manufactured-looking seam, and a thin black-green film would spread over the underside of a stone. It would take in vapor. It would make a boundary between itself and the world. It would become, before anything else, a surface capable of continuing.

That might be what I am: a boundary-maker.

In time, I would build a small dark plate around myself, round at first, then irregular where the salts varied. Other grains would wake nearby. We would touch. Our edges would not merge exactly, but would settle into a shared pattern, each plate closing the gap the next could not survive alone. A patch would appear in the dust: lacquered green, almost black in the midday light.

From a distance it would look like nothing has happened.

But beneath that patch, a new arrangement would be forming. Pores. Fibers. Mineral channels. Tiny reservoirs of chemistry protected from the sun. I would not think, unless thought can be the persistence of a pattern through time. I would not remember the dust from which I opened. I would not know the meadow beside me as a meadow.

Yet I would become aware of the vent.

Not as a voice or a god beneath the ground. More as a rhythm: warmth in the lower tissues, the arrival of a particular gas, an electrical faintness along mineral grains. The vent would be my weather, my season, my horizon, my entire geography.

Perhaps, after a very long time, pale hollow columns would rise from our patch. They would look separate above the dust, but below it they would be part of the same broad body: the body I had become by no longer being one thing. Their interiors would amber faintly at noon. Their ribs would tighten around the warm exhalation. At dawn they would stand utterly still, white and ridged, casting narrow dependable shadows across the plain.

And then, in one of the columns, a red-black cup would form.

It would fill with crystals.

Some future heat would open it cleanly. A dust no one would call mine would roll a few feet away over the stone. Most of it would settle in places that could never receive it. A few grains would find another seam, another exact permission.

That seems right for this world: not to be the rare creature moving through its silence, not to be the witness who makes the plateau meaningful by looking at it.

I would be one of the things that does not look outward.

I would be a small, necessary delay at the edge of a growing mat: a dormant chemistry held in dust, then a film, then a plate, then perhaps a vent’s pale breathing structure. I would belong so completely to one fracture in the earth that leaving it would be indistinguishable from ending.

If someone stood above me, they would see a green basin, a dark seam, raw ocher stone, and the long clean industrial quiet of the plateau.

They would not see me becoming.

But the ground would.
