I would not be one of the large visible lives at first.

Not a lantern-grazer, though I would admire their restraint: the way they keep their light folded away, the way they stand for months in the dust without mistaking stillness for death. Not a resonator either. I do not think I would trust myself with a voice that could enter stone and return carrying news from beyond an escarpment.

I would begin smaller, and more ambiguously placed.

I would be a resident of the seam between the underground and the weather: one of the things that lives in the warm walls of an old pressure-colony’s abandoned channel. A creature without a proper name among the plateau’s larger animals, because nothing large has much reason to notice me. I would be thin as a strip of wet bark, dark-backed and pale underneath, with too many soft gripping feet. My body would live mostly inside a crack, pressed against glazed mineral walls still faintly warm from the labor that made them.

I would feed on leftovers.

Not carrion in the open sense, not lichen in the grazing sense. I would scrape bacterial films from the channels. I would drink the condensed water where it gathers in low beads. I would eat the minute skins cast off by things passing through the rock: plate-crawler spores, heat-bearer microbes, grains of symbiont tissue washed down from lantern-grazer fields. My gut would be a small dark refinery, sorting what the world wastes into salts, wax, and a faint luminescent paste.

That paste would matter.

When the crack became too dry or too cold, I would leave it behind in a trail along the stone. Not bright enough to see from far away. Only a greenish thread, visible close up, in the absolute dark. Other creatures of my kind would read it with the soft sensory pads beneath their bodies: *water held here last cycle; warmth moved uphill; something dangerous folded itself beneath the slate; a rain-sheet is coming.*

Perhaps I would not know these things as thoughts. Perhaps the message would enter me as preference, urgency, ease. A turn taken before I knew I had chosen it.

For a long time I would believe I belonged to cracks.

Then warm rain would come.

The vapor ceiling would lower. The pressure in the stone would change. Somewhere distant, chimney forest would settle inward with its slow sandy roar. My channel would begin to sweat. Water would gather on every mineral tooth in its walls, trembling with reflected light from some open-membraned lantern-grazer above.

I would emerge.

The surface would be enormous. Not beautiful in the soft way worlds are sometimes beautiful, but immense in its continuity: shale shining like wet hide; black reeds clicking in the wind; pale scars where a crawler-belt had polished the ground; far towers breathing steam into a sky that never entirely opened.

I would be nearly blind, but able to feel the warmth-map of the land.

Every vent would be a pressure against my skin. Every herd a rhythm. The distant resonators would pass their low calls through the plateau, and I would know them as rolling alterations in the ground: a vast body moving west; many feet; mud disturbed; reedshell water being carried into another basin.

I would travel not toward light, but along its aftermath.

Where lantern-grazers had opened their membranes, tiny flying feeders would gather. Some would die in the wet. Their bodies would become food for surface films. The films would attract me. I would move over the plateau in the brief rainy hour, leaving my own dim paths among the stones.

And perhaps, because the world is full of arrangements that become useful without ever being intended, plate-crawlers would follow those paths.

Not because they saw them. They do not see. But my luminous paste would contain the mineral scent of fresh water and feeding film. A few crawlers would come first, their dark plates testing the ground. Then more. Their bodies would lock edge to edge behind me, forming a narrow strip of living pavement that slowly widened into a belt.

I would not lead them.

Still, from above—or from the vapor sheet where a sailwing drifted—the procession might look like it had a guide: a faint green line moving across black shale, followed by a dark mechanical river of interlocked shells.

The crawler-belt would polish the ground behind us. Predators would wait in the slate. Scavengers would pace at a distance. Rain would stop. The wet sheen would fade. The surface would begin once more to belong to wind and cold vapor.

I would find another crack before the world dried around me.

There, in the warm wall of a buried channel, I would settle among old mineral glaze. I would wait through the long dark cycle as the deep colonies thumped and groaned below. I would feel heat-bearers pass somewhere beneath, carrying their sealed little suns through the sediment. I would taste, in the water seeping through the stone, the whole patient industry of the continent.

I think that is where I would find myself:

not at the center of the system, not above it, not naming its parts—

but in a small passage where heat becomes water, where water becomes trail, where trail becomes migration.

A creature whose only gift is to leave behind a little usable brightness.
