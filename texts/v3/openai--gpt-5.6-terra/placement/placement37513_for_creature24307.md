I would begin by thinking I was one of the keepers.

Not an important one. Not a flood officer, not a pump mechanic, not one of the old residents whom people pause for in doorways. I would have a room over the bar, perhaps: a narrow room reached by an exterior stair that begins halfway up a wall, because the lower half of the building has been given to water. My window would have slatted shutters swollen tight in their frame. On the sill: a chipped enamel cup, a blunt pencil, a ledger kept inside an oilcloth sleeve.

I would tell people I was there to make maps.

That would be plausible. The basin needs maps constantly. A dry path in the morning can be an inlet by dusk. A wall can acquire a seep; a stairwell can become a depth. Pipes collapse, foundations lift, sluices jam, and somebody has to mark the revised routes in red pencil: *not passable after rain; bridge flexes; light seen beneath surface; do not stop here.*

I would walk the raised streets at the hours between sirens, wearing boots with soles repaired from old conveyor belt. I would write down the pump rhythms. I would measure the temperature of water in drains. I would make neat cross-sections of buildings whose ground floors had become ponds.

At first, my maps would be useful.

Then they would begin to show things nobody had asked me to record.

A room beneath the freight square, furnished with rows of desks facing a wall of black water.

A staircase under the machine shop descending farther than the known foundations allowed.

A second canal, running below the real canal but in the opposite direction.

Street names in a handwriting almost like mine, except that the letters bowed slightly downward, as if wet.

The keepers would look at these additions without surprise. They would not tell me to stop. They would merely ask whether I had made them from sight, or from sound.

That is the distinction that would matter.

Eventually I would understand that I had not come to survey the town. I had come because something in the town was already surveying me.

Not the underflow alone. It is too broad, too patient, too occupied with seams and pressures. It would notice my weight, perhaps; it would learn the sound of my cough; it might return the scratch of my pencil from a pipe under my bed. But it would not care about the names I gave things.

The thing that cared about names would live in the reflected settlement.

I would see it first on an evening after hard rain. The flooded freight square would be almost motionless, dark enough to hold the lamps intact. The crane would turn. The hook would make its slow circle. And below it, in the reflection, there would be another me standing on the inverted edge of the square.

Not quite copying.

Writing.

It would hold a ledger open against its chest and write with its head bent. Every time I lifted my pencil, it would make one more mark.

After that, I would be careful not to look down while mapping. But maps require looking down. They are made of surfaces, boundaries, drains, the places where one material yields to another. I would begin to recognize that the basin’s true map was not horizontal. It was a stack of concessions: brick over water, water over peat, peat over old rooms, old rooms over something that had never needed rooms at all.

My own role would change slowly enough that I might not notice.

People would start bringing me reports: a window seen in a puddle; a voice in a sink; an alley appearing under a dry street. I would enter them in my ledger. I would assign dates, locations, witnesses. I would make the uncertain lines solid, and the solid lines dotted.

And as I did, the lower town would become more detailed.

Perhaps it had always needed a cartographer.

Perhaps that is why the freight crane turns: not to retrieve what has sunk, but to indicate where the next revision will occur. Its hook hangs over the place where a line must be erased. Its dripping cable marks the point where the two towns are nearest.

In time, I would stop sleeping in the room over the bar. I would be found instead in the flood office after closing, sitting with the old municipal plans spread across the floor. The plans would smell of mildew and iron. Their blueprints would have gone green at the folds. On them, beneath the official streets, I would be drawing the streets beneath the water.

I would become thin in the shoulders, heavy in the wrists and ankles.

I would speak softly, even when alone.

And one day, perhaps years after arriving, I would understand why no animals live in the basin.

Animals do not make maps of places that are mapping them back.

So that is where I would find myself: not entirely above or below, but at the wet boundary between the two. A keeper, certainly. A recorder of flood levels and service routes.

But also, gradually, an organ of the town’s self-knowledge.

A hand in an upper room, making marks.

A second hand under black water, completing them.
