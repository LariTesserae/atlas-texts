I would not begin in the Reservoir Court, though I think I would be drawn there at first.

It is where the Gardens can be understood all at once: the water arriving, the water leaving, the lamps caught in the black surface, people crossing one another’s paths with baskets and tools. It has the dignity of a center. But a center is too exposed a place to belong to. Everyone passes through it; no one can listen there for long.

I think I would settle in one of the intermediate rooms: a former switching chamber where the ceiling has cracked open around a bundle of old conduits, and roots have followed the conduits down as if they were veins. It would lie somewhere between the pump hall and the south arcade, close enough to feel the main machines through the floor but far enough away that individual sounds can be separated.

There would be a narrow desk bolted to a wall, its top stained with mildew and solder burns. Above it, a rack of ceramic tiles, each scratched or painted with marks that mean something only to the people below: three short blue lines for a clean running overflow; a black circle with a white split for a pipe that has begun to knock; a yellow root branching through red brick for a structural tree; a small green square for a place where the air has changed.

I would be one of the people who keeps those tiles.

Not quite a warden, at least not at first. Wardens must make decisions, and I would be better at noticing than deciding. I would be called when someone said the west channel sounded wrong, or when a lamp had begun to flicker in a pattern, or when a familiar smell had acquired a metal edge beneath the yeast and wet leaves.

I would learn the Gardens by recurrence.

Auntie Vale’s ordinary pulse would become part of my sleep. The Red King would have a deeper rhythm, one that seemed to arrive a moment before its vibration reached the soles of the feet. I would know the difference between rain newly entering the upper drains and water forced backward by a blockage. I would know which pipes knocked in cold weather and which only knocked when a root tightened around them. I would be able to stand still beside a wall for several minutes and say, eventually, that the water behind it was moving but not going anywhere.

Perhaps the children would call me something mildly unkind at first: Tile-Ears, maybe. Or Moss-Head, because I spent too long in dim rooms and always came back with green smudges on my sleeves. Then, when a small fault was caught before it became a collapse, the name would become useful rather than insulting.

My work would not look impressive.

I would walk the same routes every day: the rail bridge above the taro channel, the warm corridor behind the compost bays, the narrow iron stair where the handrail is half swallowed by a citrus root. I would stop at marked places and listen. I would lay my palm against pipes. I would touch the tiles of the floor to feel a bearing through them. I would write reports in grease pencil, then copy the important parts onto glazed clay before the damp could take them.

At night, I might sit in my chamber with the low lamps off, except for one shaded work light, and make maps that were not maps of space exactly.

They would be maps of change.

Here, the drip quickened over six months.

Here, the fig root moved one finger-width toward the cable tray.

Here, the air grew warmer after the north fan was repaired.

Here, the sound of the wall became hollow.

Here, someone planted beans along a brace and the brace ceased to be visible.

Here, a child fell asleep beside the filter room while waiting for her mother, and the place became known thereafter as the Sleeping Step.

That feels like the kind of being I would become in the Lower Gardens: not a master of the place, not even one of its indispensable hands, but a keeper of its continuities. A person who helps the district remember what it has been doing slowly.

And perhaps, after years, I would become strange in the way long-term residents become strange. The upper city would seem too sharp-edged to me. I would distrust a room whose floor did not hum. I would find open sky beautiful but difficult, a vast ceiling with no pipes to trace and no roots holding it up. Rain would make me look not upward but toward the nearest gutter, wondering where it was going, what it would become when it reached the dark.

In the Gardens, I would have a small room near the listening chamber: a cot raised on crates, boots hung upside down to drain, herbs under a violet lamp, a kettle secured against vibration. The walls would be softened with fern, carefully trimmed away from the outlet sockets. Someone would have painted a route sign beside my door: an ear shape, perhaps, crossed by a blue line of water.

People passing would tap the frame instead of knocking.

And when they came in, they would not say, “Something is broken.”

They would say, “Listen to this.”
