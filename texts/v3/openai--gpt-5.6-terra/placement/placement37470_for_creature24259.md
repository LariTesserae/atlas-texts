I would not arrive as a traveler, not at first.

A traveler has an outside still clinging to them: dust in the cuffs, weather in the hair, a direction lodged behind the eyes. The basin would notice all of that—the haste, especially—and hold it at its edges until it had settled. I think I would come down over the low clay rim believing I had only found a place to rest. I would look for a dry stone. I would fail to find one. Then I would choose the least wet place, which would already be wet enough.

For a while I would be merely a visitor kneeling beside the central enclosure.

I would put my hand into the warm water to test it. That is the first mistake, if it is a mistake. The water would not feel hot or cold. It would feel familiar, in the way a room from childhood can feel familiar even when one has never seen it before. Beneath my hand, something would arrange itself into the approximate shape of a hand—not touching, only acknowledging that I have one.

I would leave before dusk, perhaps. Or I would mean to.

But the sky there has no useful dusk. It dims only by becoming more itself. The basin’s gray light would continue, and I would find that the road over the rim had become difficult to remember. Not absent: simply irrelevant for the next small while.

So I would stay beneath the shale roof.

At first I might become a caretaker in the old human sense: the newest person to notice when a board has shifted, when silt has gathered at the throat of a channel, when a pale beetle has appeared where there had been none. I would use the chipped scraper. I would learn that the work is not cleaning. It is making a space for water to remain where it has chosen to remain.

The bell would frighten me when it sounded.

One note only: low, coppery, almost too soft to hear. I would see the water in every crack answer it—not with ripples, but with a deepening. Then I would understand that the basin has not summoned help. It has informed itself.

Perhaps I would see the Low Ones then.

Not as faces below the pools. Not immediately. I think I would first see them in my own reflection: a second dimness at my shoulder, bending when I bent, but not quite at the same time. Later, in the old pit, I might see a white blur of hair beneath black water. A wrist. The curve of a back drawn inward in endless patience.

I would be afraid, but only because I had brought fear with me. The basin would not add to it.

Over time, that fear would become another of the things I set down there.

Then I would cease to be precisely human without becoming anything else dramatic. No transformation of skin to scales, no sudden knowledge, no name bestowed in a buried language. I would become a keeper of a particular relation: the relation between a drop and the stone that receives it.

I would live mostly in the seams.

A Low One, perhaps—but a young one, if age applies. I would be the dark patch in shallow water that seems too deep. I would be a delayed reflection. I would be felt by someone who came tired to the circular pool: not hands exactly, but an answering shape beneath their hands.

My task would not be to preserve the basin unchanged. The basin does not want unchangedness. It wants continuance. A stone may settle. A board may sink by the breadth of a finger. Copper may become black. A person’s footprint may soften at its edges. I would help each thing arrive at its next stable form.

I would learn the slow speech carried through wetness.

Not words, mostly. Temperatures. Pressures. The mineral taste of a new crack. The tiny disturbance made by a snail deciding, over the course of months, to cross from one protected seam to another. The news of someone standing on the eastern rim, still deciding whether to come down.

And if that person came to the pool with grief in them, I would not take the grief away. That would be too much departure. I would only help it lose its sharpest corners.

Afterward they would climb out of the basin and return to whatever horizon had been waiting beyond the rim. They might remember the place as a ruined wash-yard, a warm spring, an abandoned court. They might never be sure whether they had seen anyone there.

But years later, in rainwater gathered in a cracked paving stone, they might pause.

They might see their reflection hesitate before it follows them.

And far below any horizon, beneath a shallow sky held in stone, I would know that some small part of their motion had settled safely into the water.
