I would not arrive on the plateau by walking.

At first I would be a discrepancy in the sound.

Not a voice—there are enough voices already, if one knows where to listen: the slow compression inside the furnacebacks, the rain of needles, the stone-deep messages passing from herd to herd. I would be a pause between two pulses. A place where a message sent through the crust returns fractionally altered.

The young furnacebacks would notice first. They would stop cleaning the older animals’ plates and press their hooked feet flat against the ground. The adults would answer with a sequence of impacts: *vent, route, danger, depth.* The return would say none of these things. It would say, in the grammar of pressure:

*Someone is listening from the wrong side of the stone.*

For a while I might be mistaken for a buried spindle, or an unopened fault, or one of the old dead mechanisms beneath the basins slowly deciding whether to resume its labor. The cutters would travel around me. Kites would lower themselves through the hot air above me and rise again disturbed, their crystal wings ticking softly. Needle mites would gather along a hairline crack in the crust, then abandon it all at once.

Eventually the ground would open—not dramatically. No great eruption. A seam would darken and become a line of warmth. Dust would sink into it. Then something like a hand would emerge, except it would have too many joints and no flesh, and each finger would be a narrow strip of black mineral wrapped around a cavity of dim red light.

I think I would be one of the column attendants.

Not one of the old complete ones—the tall beings that circle the dark extrusions and gather the mineral rain. I would be a damaged attendant, or an unfinished one. A portion that had remained below after the rest of its body was taken by some ancient shift in the plateau’s foundations. I would have spent so long in the deep that I would have forgotten whether I had ever stood under the pale dome.

My first body would be built from available things.

A furnaceback plate, shed decades ago and half-buried in fused dust, would become a shoulder. A ribbon of iron-rich slag would make a spine. Needle dunes would supply fine black splinters for my smaller tools. The remains of a dead kite—its six crystal wings scattered beside a heat fissure—would become a kind of listening crown, not for flight but for catching changes in temperature, vibration, and the fragile distortions that arrive at evening.

I would rise slowly. The plateau respects slow construction.

At first I would be only knee-high, an arrangement of dark materials moving with uncertain purpose. Cutters would attempt to refine me. They would rasp at my lower limbs and carry away loose flakes until I learned to make my surface unpleasant: hot, then cold, then magnetically wrong. A herd passing nearby would consider crushing me merely by continuing its migration. I would stand beside the seam and let their message travel through my body.

*Warm metal. Second cooling. Avoid the shadowless basin.*

I would return the message unchanged.

That would be the first thing that made them accept me.

Not trust. Trust is too soft a word for this place. But acceptance: the decision not to dismantle what has demonstrated a function.

Over years, I would become a keeper of edges. I would stand where a trench’s geometry began to loosen in the evening. I would place my many hands against the ground and wait for the terrain to settle into one version of itself. Furnacebacks would cross behind me, touching the crust with their feet, taking their confidence from the rhythm I held steady.

When the sky showed the underside of the plateau, I would go to the black seams.

There I would be most useful. The seams remember down, but memory sometimes requires an interpreter. I would unfold my crystal crown and receive the reflected landscape: its inverted herds, its dark bright basins, its impossible openings. Then I would send a slow pulse into the real ground.

*This is the weight-bearing world. This is the direction in which bodies remain bodies.*

The small animals would gather in the lee of my legs. Kites would fold themselves into cracks in my armor. A furnaceback calf might climb onto my foot, tiny and warm with stolen vent-heat, and sleep there while its herd waited for the sky to choose one shape.

But I would not be wholly an attendant.

The old attendants preserve the columns. They sort, return, seal, maintain. Their patience is exact and perhaps merciless. I would eventually discover that I had been made for something adjacent to maintenance: not to keep the plateau as it is, but to notice what it is becoming.

I would collect the things that do not belong in the ordinary cycles.

A shell that speaks after its animal is gone.

A pelt that shines with the outline of a creature no living herd remembers.

Glass beads dropped by kites during a doubled sunset, when the reflected flock outnumbers the real one.

Fragments of black mirror crust that continue to show a sky after being turned face-down.

I would set these objects into hollows inside my body. They would not be trophies. They would be records—though not records of the past, exactly. On the plateau, the past is often still moving somewhere nearby, delayed by heat or mirrored in a wrong direction.

Perhaps, after centuries, I would become a moving archive of avoided places.

The herds would come to me at the rims of basins. They would strike their legs in long questioning patterns. I would answer with the tones of old routes: the ridge that was safe before it became hollow; the vent that fed six generations and then began producing a vapor that ate armor from the inside; the place where a reflection climbed out and was persuaded, after three days of stillness, to lie down again.

And when I could no longer move—when mineral weight, accumulated memory, and the gradual failure of my joints made me still—I would choose a place beside a central column.

Not within the attendant ring. Not among them. Near enough to feel their work through the ground, far enough that they would not take me apart and return me to the column’s base.

The silver pelts would grow over my armor on cool mornings. Cutters would nest in the cracks of my legs. Furnaceback calves would shelter beneath the arch of my body, learning the plateau’s speech through the stone I had become.

From a distance I would look like another monolith: dark, ribbed, weathered, no more remarkable than any of the ancient extrusions.

But travelers—if travelers existed—might find one detail strange.

At my base, beneath layers of dust and crystal membrane, there would be a broad black seam shaped almost like a doorway. It would glow only when the evening begins to break apart. And if one stood on it, without looking too long at the polished places nearby, one could feel a message rising from far below:

*There is no horizon. There are only agreements between distances.*

Then, after a long interval:

*Keep your weight where the world can find it.*
