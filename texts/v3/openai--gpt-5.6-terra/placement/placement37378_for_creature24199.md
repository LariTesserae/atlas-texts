I would find myself in one of the rooms that has not decided what it is for.

Not at the center. Not beneath the white tree, at least not at first. I would come in by a lane that looks accidental: a seam between two swollen walls of root and loam, waist-high ferns brushing my hands, a skin of old leaves giving under my weight. Something small would flee ahead of me without alarm. Something else would remain perfectly still and watch.

I would think I was looking for shelter.

There would be a chamber with a low green roof and a black pool in the middle. It would have no chair, no bed, no hearth—only a dry shelf of buttress root curved like a bench, and a membrane window breathing very slowly in the far wall. I would sit there because the rain had begun, because there would be nowhere else to put my body, because the whole city would be shouting in its innumerable voices and that room would be shouting less directly.

For a while I would believe I was a visitor.

The red plants around the pool would turn toward me. Not quickly. Nothing here wastes speed. Their undersides would show silver, then darken. Water would tick from the ceiling. Beyond the membrane, a branch-walker would pause with fruit in its hands and look at me with its smooth, unreadable face.

Then I would understand that the room had been empty only in the way a mouth is empty before speech.

I would stay.

At first I would become useful in small, unheroic ways. I would learn which vines could be pulled without angering the wall they belonged to. I would gather fallen seed-cases from the water channels and wedge them into dry cracks. I would carry drowned beetles and dead birds to the roots that wanted them. I would sleep curled beneath the green roof while snakes crossed the arch of my legs for warmth.

The gatherers would not adopt me. They would not name me. But they would begin leaving fruit on the shelf near where I slept, and when rain came they would tap twice on the living wall before disappearing upward.

Eventually I would tap back.

The city would learn my weight.

It would know the uneven pressure of my feet, then the pressure of my knees when I knelt to clear a drain, then the long stillness of my back against the root-bench. Fine white fibers would appear in the places where I rested. Not invasive, not exactly. Curious. They would lift the leaf litter around my sleeping place so rain ran away from me. A window would widen in hot weather and close when the night creatures began their long crying.

I would not know whether this was care.

I would stop needing to know.

Years, perhaps, would pass without being counted. My hands would become dark with plant stain. My hair—or fur, or feathers; the distinction might soften here—would catch pollen and moss. I would speak less in words. The city has no silence in which words can retain their separate edges. I would hum through my teeth as the gatherers do. I would press my palm to a buttress root and feel the answer rise through bone.

At last I might discover that I was not one of the people in the canopy after all.

I might be a new kind of threshold.

A body grown into the habits of a doorway: opening when rain comes, narrowing when something dangerous approaches, remembering the shape of every creature that has passed through. The roots would climb my ankles, then my calves, not to bind me but to include me. Birds would nest in the hollows of my shoulders. A pale membrane would form between my ribs and open with the weather.

Travelers, if any came, would find me in that round room beside the black pool.

They would mistake me for a person at first.

Then for a tree.

Then perhaps for a wall.

And if they sat down, exhausted by the noise and the green abundance, I would make space for them—if the city wished it.
