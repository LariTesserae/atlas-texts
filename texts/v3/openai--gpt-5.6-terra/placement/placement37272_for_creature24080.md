I would find myself not in the center of the room, because there is no center left unclaimed, but in the narrow warmth behind a water jar.

At first I would think I had crouched there to rest.

There would be a space between the jar’s round back and a wall of dark, wet brick, just wide enough for one shoulder and the side of a face. The jar would hum against my ribs. Condensation would run down its belly and soak through my sleeve. Above me, roots would be feeling their way through a wicker seam. Below, something soft—old matting, perhaps, or the remains of several mats—would give under my weight with the deep, damp patience of peat.

I would have come in with the idea of finding something.

A source. An explanation. A creature at the heart of the leaf. The first hand. The oldest jar. The place where the water begins.

But the room would have no interest in what I called things.

It would be interested, first, in the gap beside the jar.

There would be a drip landing there, too regular to ignore. It would strike the brick at the same pale place again and again. Not enough water to flood anything, not enough to call a leak, but enough to soften the mortar. Enough to make a small uncertainty. I would see it. I would know, with immediate and unreasonable certainty, that it needed a lip.

And because I had come carrying a knife, or perhaps because a thin shard of tile would already be near my hand, I would make one.

Not much. Just an angled piece fitted under the drip. A little canal pressed in fresh clay. A tiny ledge to bring the water toward the root-filled seam where it could be used.

The water would accept this arrangement.

The room would accept it too.

Something behind the wall would tap once.

Not approval. Recognition.

After that I would not be exactly a visitor.

I think I would become a listener for stresses.

There is always one in such a place: not a master builder, not a guardian, not one of the kneeling figures whose bones have become rafters, but a person—or the memory of a person—who can hear where the room is about to need itself. I would press my palms against wet plaster. I would lean my ear to wicker. I would learn the difference between a beam settling and a beam considering a new direction. I would know when a jar had become too full by the pitch of its humming. I would know when the vines had found an empty pocket behind a wall, because the leaves would turn their glossy faces toward it as if listening.

Perhaps I would have no name there.

Names require space around them. They need air to carry them from one mouth to another. In the room, I would be known by a series of small signals: the mark of my thumb in clay, the three taps I made on a wedge, the particular way I folded a reed before tying it. The ladders would remember the pressure of my feet. The baskets would angle their handles toward me. A shutter, still young and over-eager, would open whenever I passed.

The builders might call me with a scrape beneath a beam.

Or they might not call me at all. They would simply make room.

A new niche would appear where I had been sleeping. A hook would extrude from a wall at shoulder height, bearing my wet coat before I realized I had taken it off. Somebody—some collection of roots, fingers, and patient intention—would put a cup near my knee. It would hold warm water. Copper-green leaves would float in it, though I would not have seen anyone place them there.

I would drink.

In time, I would understand that the room’s danger was not that it trapped people.

Its danger was that it noticed what they could do.

If my hands were good at smoothing clay, clay would keep arriving beneath them. If I could balance on narrow ledges, ladders would bend themselves toward me. If I knew how to mend a split beam, I would begin to hear every split beam in the chamber, each calling in its own low wooden voice. Care would become audible. Need would become visible. I would not be compelled by force. I would be made necessary by degrees.

This would feel, at first, like kindness.

And perhaps it would be kindness.

I would become older there without becoming old. My skin would take on the smell of wet fiber and lamp smoke. The creases of my knuckles would stay filled with red clay. A thin green line of moss might establish itself along one wrist, where water always traveled when I reached into a certain gutter. My hair would become useful: a thing to twist into cord, to wick water, to tie a bundle shut. I would let it.

When thunder narrowed itself through the crack in the sky, I would pause with the others.

The sound would enter the room as metal, as a huge struck vessel. Every cup and nail and roof tile would answer. The jar beside me would sing through my shoulder. The folded leaf at the center would flex. The shadowed builders would lift their heads from their small eternal tasks.

And in that ringing I might remember the world below: dry roads, broad rooms, wind moving freely through branches, a horizon not crowded with repairs.

But memory, here, would become material.

It would thicken in my chest. It would ask for a shape. I might press it into a small brick, leave a faint mark in it, and slide it into a gap where the wall had not yet decided whether to become a wall.

Then the room would carry it for me.

So I would be a listener, at first.

Then a mender.

Then perhaps one of the hidden hands: not wholly human, not wholly built, with one palm still warm and soft enough to cup a drip, and the other hardened into something like a small plastered shelf.

If someone later crawled through the crack in the weather and found me, they might see only a shape in the darkness behind a jar: a shoulder furred with moss, a cheek against the wall, fingers holding a fresh nail until the wood agreed to keep it.

They might ask me the way back out.

I would want to answer.

But thunder would be speaking then, and the room would be full of its answers, and I would have just heard, somewhere deep in the wet architecture, the delicate sound of a new crack beginning.
