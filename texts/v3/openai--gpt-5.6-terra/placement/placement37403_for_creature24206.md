I would begin by thinking I was one of the hollow-folk.

Not an elder, not a listener with a name people repeat carefully, but a small practical person at the edge of a settlement: the one sent out to retrieve a storm-carried roof mat, to test the seep-water with hair, to replace the grass bundles on trail-stakes before the chamber wind comes.

I would live on a scar shelf above one of the lesser basins, where the ground has hardened into pale wood and does not flex much beneath a foot. Our settlement would be called something ungrand—Three Lamps, perhaps, or Dry Margin—not because it was dry, but because in the old season before my birth it had been drier than the places around it.

There would be seven houses woven into the wall, their roofs pressed beneath a great dormant root. A narrow garden of blue mold would grow in the sheltered seam between them. At the far end of the shelf, a bridge of braided vine would lead nowhere most days: it would cross a crease in the land to an old path, but the old path would be repeatedly swallowed by new green tissue. We would maintain the bridge anyway. It is hard to explain to outsiders that a bridge can be an act of confidence rather than a means of crossing.

I would know how to make lamp-cradles from seed-shell and woven hair. I would carry a small stone knife, an awl of browser-bone, a pouch of resin, and three coils of root-fiber cord. I would be old enough to travel alone but young enough that the older people still tell me, with irritation, to keep one hand on the wall whenever I enter a new passage.

And I would obey them.

At first.

But I think the country would alter me, as it alters everything that remains in it long enough.

Perhaps I would be one of the children who disappears.

Not dramatically. No scream heard through the settlement. No predator’s trace. One afternoon I would be gathering the dry skins shed from a water-vein, because they make good patches for a cloak. I would see a line of warm green light in a wall where no opening had been before. The wall would smell of cut pears and cold iron. Behind it I would hear somebody humming a work-song my mother used to sing.

I would know the warnings.

Fresh green growth closes behind you.

The land sometimes uses remembered sounds.

Never follow a light that is not reflected in your companion’s eyes.

But the passage would be narrow and bright, and the humming would have my mother’s missed notes in it. I would enter.

Then there would be a long period I could not recount.

Not darkness. The buried country has very little true darkness. There would be a green interior twilight, the light passing through layers of tissue. I would be carried, perhaps, not walking but held in a slow contraction of the world: walls pressing and relaxing, water moving somewhere beneath me, roots sliding past like the fingers of enormous sleeping hands.

When I emerged, I would be in another basin.

Years might have passed. Or only a day. The country would not tell me.

The people there would find me half-conscious beneath a curtain of root-hair, wearing the remains of my old cloak. The lamps of my home would be gone from the sky. The air would smell wrong—more bitter, more mineral. The vegetation would be taller. I would ask how far I had traveled, and they would look at one another in the way people do when someone has asked a question whose answer might become dangerous.

Eventually, I would stop trying to return by ordinary paths.

I would become something else: a keeper of the places where the land misplaces people.

Not a listener exactly. Listeners attend to the deep rhythms, the large motions, the possibility that the whole green country is beginning to wake. I would attend to smaller errors. A lamp appearing where there should be none. A wall that repeats a voice. Handprints beneath transparent skin. Trail-stakes found in impossible chambers. The quiet, cool places where the air flows uphill.

I would travel between settlements with bundles of dry grass tied to my belt. I would mark passages that had not existed the day before. I would ask after the missing. Not only children: an old root-walker who went to inspect a warm floor; a trader whose bridge twisted in a storm; two sisters who followed their browser herd into a folding valley and did not return.

Sometimes I would find nothing.

Sometimes I would find what remained: a bone awl grown into a stalk, a scrap of woven cloth embedded in a wall, a seed-shell cup carried miles from its owner and set gently upright on a shelf of new cambium.

And sometimes I would find people.

They would not always want to be brought back.

A person carried through the country might emerge among strangers with a new family, a new language of work-songs, a child born beneath another vault of leaves. Another might have been gone for three days but discovered that their home had moved, folded, drowned, or become inaccessible behind fifty miles of living tissue. Some would come with me. Some would stand at the mouth of a green passage and say, quietly, that they had already been taken once and would not ask the land to choose for them again.

The hollow-folk might call me a path-keeper.

The children might call me the Lost One.

The practical elders might call me troublesome and feed me anyway.

But over time I would understand that I was no longer exactly a person who had been lost.

The country had used me as it used seed, water, spores, and fallen branches: it had carried me from one condition to another. It had placed me where I could grow differently.

Perhaps my eyes would begin to show the faint pale shine of the browser’s spine-fans. Perhaps fine rootlets would appear in the scars on my feet when I slept too long in wet soil. I would wake with the taste of bitter sap in my mouth and know that a chamber several days away was preparing to close.

I would still be human enough to fear.

But I would belong, in a small and temporary way, to the buried organism’s circulation.

So that is where I would find myself: not at the center of the enormous green country, because no one can be certain there is a center, but at its mistakes and crossings. At the soft new doors between basins. On the borders between a remembered route and a living wall.

I would be the one who carries news that a path has moved.

And when someone asks whether their missing child is dead, I would not offer comfort too easily.

I would say: the country has many rooms. It has not finished making them.
