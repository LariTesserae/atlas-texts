I would come into that country from the east, by accident, following the sound of water where no water ought to be.

At first I would think I had found only a marsh: reeds taller than my head, alder branches brushing my shoulders, ground that gives a little too easily under each step. Then the land would fall away beneath the vegetation. The horizon would vanish. The air would turn colder around my ankles. I would see mist lying below the roots and the whole hidden basin opening under its lid of wet ground.

I do not think I would live in one of the high, dry places, even if I could find them. The rim would feel too exposed. Its trees would know too much of the larger weather. I would go down, cautiously, toward the channels.

For a while I might imagine myself as a water vole.

There is something enviable in their knowledge of departure: the way they inhabit soft, unreliable ground without demanding that it become firm; the way they disappear through a hole in the bank at precisely the moment one begins to look at them. I would like their small certainty. I would like to know which tuft of grass will bear my weight, where frost gets in first, which alder roots conceal a dry chamber. I would make no claims on the marsh. I would carry a stem twice my length, vanish under a shelf of turf, emerge somewhere else with wet whiskers and a seed in my mouth.

But I suspect I am too slow-minded for a vole. I would stop to listen to the water. I would linger at every broken bank. A vole’s life requires a quickness of attention that is almost a form of invisibility.

Then perhaps I would become a beaver.

Not the grandest one, not the animal everyone notices when the dam is failing—just an older, mud-dark beaver with a scar in one ear and a particular loyalty to an unimportant side-channel. I would know the sound of water pressing where it should not press. I would wake before the whole lodge woke, slide into black water, and put one more willow branch where the current had begun to worry loose the wall.

I would not think of it as building.

I would think: this is the place where the water is becoming too eager. This is the place where the bank is becoming tired. This is the place that needs a little weight, a little weave, a little more time.

And perhaps that would be enough for a life.

Yet the longer I stayed, the less I think I would want to be one of the visible creatures at all. I would want no single lodge, no single route, no appetite that made the reed beds tighten when I passed. I would want to belong to the country in the way its oldest things belong: not by ruling any portion of it, but by helping it become soil.

So I think, in the end, I would be a fungus in a fallen alder.

Not the bright cap seen briefly after rain, though I might make those sometimes: a small orange shelf along a wet trunk, a pale fan opening from black wood. I would be the hidden white threads under the bark and through the waterlogged grain. I would live in the old bridge where voles pass, where beetles bore galleries, where moss gathers in the cracks. The beavers would drag branches across me. Otters would leave silver fish scales near me. A heron might stand above me, so still that rain collected on its folded wings.

I would take nothing quickly.

I would work through the soaked timber year after year, loosening what had once been solid, returning it gradually to the basin. I would make softness where there had been hardness. I would feed moss, insects, roots, and the dark small lives that no animal names. When the trunk finally sank, I would sink with it. When it became a black sponge beneath sedge and sphagnum, I would remain in its fibers. When it became peat, I would be hard to distinguish from the ground.

That seems right for this place.

Not a king of the hidden valley. Not even a citizen in the busy sense.

A patient inhabitant of one damp, collapsing thing: helping it become a passage for whatever comes after.
