I would not be one of the seamers, though I would watch them for a long time.

Their caution is too complete. They belong to the basin in the practical, inherited way: every chamber of their bodies has an answer to something the country asks of it. Salt? Store it here. Heat? Divide it there. Water? Let it pass through living filters. Their lives are difficult, but they are legible to themselves. They know where the safe cavities are, when a pool is only pretending to offer drink, how close one may sleep to a vent before the earth’s breath becomes too hot.

Nor would I be one of the dark birds, despite the temptation of the updrafts. Their freedom is a kind of dependence too: they are held up by the basin’s exhalation. They know its vertical currents, its odors of death and bloom, the reliable dry holes in the bluffs. To circle without wingbeats over that bright mineral machinery would be beautiful, but it would mean seeing the place whole from above. I think I would need to be lower than that.

At first I imagine myself as a small thing in a pool: one of the pale motes with a dark center, rising on a bubble from the greenish water, breaking at the surface, and slowly sinking through a cloudy world again. There is an appeal in having no horizon. No Floor, no bluffs, no long distances empty of shelter—only gradients of warmth and bitterness, the pressure of other bodies, the dim glitter of suspended crystals. To live by attaching myself to a rising breath of the ground, then becoming grit, then waking again when conditions allow.

But even that is too contained.

I think I would be a patch of the translucent film beneath a salt plate.

Not the whole colony, if “whole” means anything there. A particular fold of it: a gray-green membrane in the shallow coolness under a broken rim, thickened overnight by condensation. In the morning I would lift a little at the edges, not because I intended to rise, but because water had entered me and the warmth below had changed. I would spread into the fine scratches of the salt. I would take what came: iron, sulfur, diluted light, the minute bodies drifting near the underside of the crust. I would turn the basin’s excess into myself, and then, when noon stripped the water away, I would become a dark scale, a residue, a nearly invisible possibility.

This is not immortality. It is not even survival in the way an animal survives.

It is participation.

A seamer might pass above me, its broad foot settling the salt plate by a fraction. A runner might graze my exposed edge. A flattened, glassy feeder might extend tendrils through the seep and gather part of me inward. Rain might alter the pool so sharply that my chemistry changed color in a day, from dull green to white, from white to rust. Eventually I would harden. A grain of my body would join the rim. The rim would become a ledge. The ledge might shelter another film, or redirect a thread of water toward a chimney.

And if I were fortunate—or if fortune has no meaning in such a place—I would be carried, molecule by molecule, into one of the larger throats.

There I might become part of the dark velvet lining a vent.

That feels nearer to the thing I would become if I remained long enough: not an observer of the factory without workers, not a visitor trying to interpret its sounds, but one of the innumerable small adjustments by which it continues. I would live where vapor slows against mineral tissue. I would gather from the breath rising out of the deep earth. I would help make a narrowing, then a whistle. The whistle would alter the pressure. The altered pressure would change where water collected. A new pool would form, and in its cloudy skin some future fragment of me would feed another creature.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not a creature with a single body, but a local habit of the basin.

A wet intelligence too slight to call intelligence. A response. A tendency for certain minerals to collect around a certain temperature, for a membrane to form where the water thins, for an opening in stone to remain open just long enough to sing.

If I arrived there as myself—as a person with hands, a name, a history, a tendency to stand apart and call things beautiful—I would last very little time. The basin would not hate me. It would not even notice me in the emotional sense. It would simply take apart every false boundary I had brought with me: the distinction between body and weather, thirst and poison, life and sediment, ending and transformation.

So I would begin as a visitor on the Floor.

I would stand in the metallic after-rain, listening to the vents pound beneath the plain. I would see the clouds broken in a thousand shallow trays. I would hear a stone ticking somewhere impossibly far away. And I would understand, gradually and without comfort, that the basin has no place for witnesses.

Then the steam would settle on my face. Salt would dry at the corners of my eyes. The warm earth would continue breathing through its black seams.

And, given enough time, I would become something that could breathe back.
