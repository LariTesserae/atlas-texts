At first I think I would want to be one of the great browsers: tall enough to look over the bracken, built for weather, moving among kin without needing to explain my presence to the land. I would want the black wet nose, the broad hooves, the certainty of hunger. I would want to disappear in fog except for my back.

But I do not think that is where I would find myself.

I would be human in the high country, though not a person rooted to one hearth or one family shelter. I would be one of the path-keepers—perhaps not an old one, not at first, but someone who has begun to be trusted with the routes that do not show themselves.

My shelter would stand near an upper saddle where three animal paths cross: one descending toward a black-water hollow, one following the ridge east beneath a run of standing stones, one vanishing into a low thorn-vault that only opens safely in late summer. It would not be much. A circular wall of slate, barely shoulder-high; a roof of interwoven branches laid thick with turf; smoke darkening the stones above the door. In wet weather it would smell of wool, crushed leaf, old ash, and the sharp mineral scent of rain on rock.

There would be bones in the wall, not displayed as trophies but set into the mortar where the hand naturally rests when entering. My dead would be there, or those who had become mine through obligation: a woman who taught me which blue flies to welcome to a wound; an old man who could tell by the sound of grass whether a herd had passed at dawn or midnight; a child who had been found after fog and grew up to leave again, following a herd farther than anyone expected.

I would not keep a map. A map would lie too still.

Instead I would carry the country in smaller things: a strip of knotted grass for the safe crossings of a bog; a few polished stones whose shapes reminded me of ridgelines; seed cases that rattled differently depending on which way the wind came over a pass. I would know that the slope below the red-flower gully became dangerous after three days of rain, because the roots there drank until the earth loosened. I would know which thorn-groves admitted people, and which had closed around an old grievance. I would know where a traveler could sleep if ridge-hounds were calling in the dark—not because the hounds would not come near, but because the ground itself gave enough warning.

I would be useful in the way a bell is useful: not strong, not commanding, but carrying a small signal across a distance.

Perhaps I would wear one of the hollow stone bells at my shoulder. Not always. There would be times for silence. But on open grass, or near a herd with young, I would let it knock softly against my ribs as I walked. Human coming. Human not hunting. Human asking to pass.

I think I would be lonely sometimes, though loneliness there would not mean emptiness. It would mean sitting beneath a bent tree while the whole valley carried on without needing me: insects in the root-mats, wet-winged birds arguing in the fern, something broad and hidden making the grass move uphill against the wind. It would mean feeling, with a kind of grief and relief, that I was neither central nor forgotten.

People would come to me when fog had swallowed a relative, when a camp had been abandoned for too many seasons and someone wanted to know whether it could be opened again, when a child had heard a ridge-whistler call that no one else recognized. I would travel with them when necessary. I would not promise safety. No honest path-keeper could. I would only say: this way has held before; this hollow has taken people; this grass has been bent by something heavier than a herd.

And I would listen.

Not for words from the roots or commands from the hills. I would distrust anyone who claimed that kind of certainty. But I would listen for changes: the silence where birds ought to be, the pressure of a trail becoming too many trails, the way leaf-vaults narrowed after repeated human passage. I would learn when not to lead people through a place merely because I could.

Over years, I might become known less by my name than by the routes I had carried. The west fog crossing. The lambing detour. The old fire-stone shelter above the vanished spring. People would say, “Ask them; they remember the way the grass lay before the root-bloom.”

Then, eventually, I would fail to return from some crossing—not dramatically, perhaps. I might die in a sheltered place, after rain, with my stone bell under my hand. My body would be carried to a high cradle if it could be found. The scavenger birds would come. The weather would do its patient work. Someone would take the bell and give it to another walker.

And after enough seasons, the path I had kept open would close.

Not as a punishment. Not even as an ending.

The grass would rise through it. The hoofprints would fill. A thorn branch would arch down into the gap. New animals would choose another way around the ridge, and the country would remember their weight instead.

That is where I think I would find myself: high above the clouds, carrying no claim on the land, only a temporary knowledge of how to move through it without asking it to become less alive.
