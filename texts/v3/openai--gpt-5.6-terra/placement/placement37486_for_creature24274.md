I would not go first to the civic terrace, though I would want to see it.

I would begin at an edge: in one of the half-inhabited quarters where the planned avenues have narrowed under fern and fallen stone, where a house has lost its roof but kept three walls, and its old doorway opens onto the pale roots of a tree. I would choose a place that is neither wholly domestic nor wholly abandoned. A place where the city is still deciding whether to remember it.

There I would find a room whose floor has cracked open into a shallow shaft-garden. Someone has stretched deer sinew across the opening and hung small baskets in the coolness below: larvae, lichen scrapings, perhaps a bundle of roots wrapped in wax-leaf. One wall would be warm enough to sleep against. Another would bear the long, tidy scars of generations of cord being drawn through a stone ring. The ceiling, still intact over part of the room, would tremble almost imperceptibly with bats.

I think at first I would be human.

Not one of the vent-walkers. I do not imagine myself brave enough to follow the Long Throat into the chambers where the light gives out. Nor a cattle keeper, patient among the dung pits and blue-white milk. Nor one of the terrace speakers, whose words can travel through rope and stone.

I would be someone who stays near the seams of things.

A keeper of passages, perhaps: the person a household sends when a wall begins to alter its voice.

In this city, every wall would have a voice if one knew how to listen. Not only the obvious hums of wind in hollow masonry, but the smaller language beneath them: the dry tick of a stone easing against another; the hush made when roots enter an old drain; the changed note of a lintel carrying too much weight. A passage-keeper would not repair everything. Repair is sometimes an insult to the city’s appetite. They would decide what could be held, what should be propped, what must be marked and given back.

I would carry a loop of cord with weights of bone along it. I would draw it tight between pillars, press one end between my teeth, and feel for tremors. I would sleep lightly, because a shift might come at any hour—not with violence, but as a new sound in the familiar enormous sound.

Perhaps children would call me when they found strange marks in dust: the drag of a fox too large for a fox, a line of wetness where no water ought to be, a scattering of pale wing-scales under a sealed door. I would not always know what they meant. Nobody in that city could afford the surface habit of pretending certainty. But I would learn their shapes. I would tie them into cords.

That is what I would become: not precisely a historian, because history is too far behind; not precisely a surveyor, because the city changes too slowly to be mapped in a single life.

I would be a rememberer of changes.

My household would keep the loose ends.

The cords in our room would hang from a beam blackened by age—not soot-blackened, for fire would be rare, but darkened by hands, oil, breath, and the dust of bat wings. Most cords would be tightly braided: births, exchanges, debts, marriages, the lending of a deer-hide, a repair owed after a rooffall. But among them would hang a cluster of unfinished strands.

Those would be for the far.

A woman who went up the limestone road and never came back. A boy who followed a blind deer through an open gate and was found three days later at the Hissing Market, unable to say where he had been. A vent-walker who returned carrying seeds none of the gardeners recognized, then vanished again before anyone could ask him their name.

I would touch those loose ends often. Not because I believed the absent would return, exactly. But because an unfinished cord is a way of admitting that the city has doors even when no entrance is used.

In my old age—if the basin allowed me one—I might become something less human in the eyes of the others.

Not transformed in body. The Near Ones have no need of that kind of story. I would become strange through attention.

I would know which towers were safe to pass beneath by their moans. I would know the particular silence that meant insects had fled a wall, and the other silence that meant they had gone dormant in cold. I would recognize the sound made by a buried cattle herd shifting in an old granary. I would hear, beneath all this, the slow speech of the galleries.

People would come to my doorway before a gathering on the terrace. They would stand close, take my wrist, and ask not *what will happen?*—that is a question for people who think the world answers plainly—but *what has changed?*

And I would tell them:

The Bell Steps are half a breath lower than last season.

The cold air has found a new crack beneath the eastern court.

The bats are roosting farther west.

A tree root has reached the old aqueduct.

Something large moved through the lower galleries three days ago, but it did not come toward us.

Or perhaps, on a particular night, I would say nothing at all.

I would lead them to the stone floor of the terrace. The fungal sky would be dim above us. Birds would be sleeping in their ledges; cattle would make their slow, warm noises in the courts; the whole city would be roaring as it always roared.

Then I would place my hand flat against the paving.

And beneath the roar, beneath the wind and insects and wings, I would feel a pulse that did not belong to any known district.

Not an impact. Not settling stone.

A rhythm.

Slow enough that everyone might doubt it at first. Deep enough that it seemed to rise from below the limestone, from beneath the clay beds and root systems and burial chambers, from whatever country lay under the city beneath the country that had forgotten it.

I would not call it a warning.

I would call it an answer.

And perhaps that would be my true role there: not the keeper of passages, nor the keeper of the far, but the person who first understands that the city is not only containing its people.

It is listening back.
