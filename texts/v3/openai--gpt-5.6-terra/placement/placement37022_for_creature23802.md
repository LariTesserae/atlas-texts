At first I think I would live high.

Not at the roof of the sea, where the old buildings wait with their dry lungs and their dangerous memories, but high enough to see the pale change in the water when day arrives. I would want a small dwelling tethered to one of the upper basalt shelves, where the garden-leaves turn their broad faces upward and the city below is a scatter of green lamps caught among stone.

I imagine myself as a surface-worker: thin-bladed knife inherited from someone I never met, pressure-cloth wound tight around my ribs, a clay lamp cupped in both hands. I would climb in pairs. I would be the one who pauses in the threshold of a dry room, unable to stop looking at the flame.

Not because it is beautiful, exactly.

Because it is so selfish.

It burns the air around itself. It makes a private weather. It insists that the world be dry enough to receive it.

I think I would be frightened of it. I think I would return from each ascent with my hair smelling of dust and my hands restless in the water. I would bring down little things: a corroded nail, a glass button, a broken comb with its teeth still intact. I would leave offerings in the rooms—kelp braid, shell, a morsel of cultivated algae—and apologize to the old walls for touching them.

But that is only where I would begin.

The upper city has too much looking in it. Too much memory. It would make me lonely in a particular direction: upward.

After a while, I think I would ask to be sent lower.

Not darkward, not at first. Only to the middle terraces, where the water is thick with gardens and every passage has to be felt rather than seen. There I would learn the routes by palm and shoulder. White shell disks clicking against my knuckles. A rootline’s familiar roughness under my fingers. The particular softness of a woven wall belonging to one household, the hard mineral bite of another.

I would become a binder.

Or perhaps I would discover that I had always been one.

There are people who can look at a bridge and see a bridge. I would be one of those who sees where it will fail. Not from cleverness. From unease. Some cords would make my teeth ache. Some knots would feel wrong even when they looked perfect. I would learn to untwist old fibers without insulting them, to graft fresh root around a frayed line, to make a wall that yielded to current without giving itself away.

My hands would be scarred in the ordinary ways: shell cuts, heat-pale marks from curing resin, faint white traces of salt settled in the joints. My hair would be bound in practical ropes, but I would make one knot always too elaborate for the work—something I could recognize by touch in complete darkness.

My name, after the first long descent, might be **Third Tension**.

Or **Holds-After**.

Something that sounds less like a person than a promise.

I would have no children for a long time. I would tell myself it was because binders are needed everywhere, because bridges need tending, because every new ledge asks for patience. But really it would be because I would be afraid to hand a breathing bladder to a small person and watch them descend beyond my reach.

Then one day a child would arrive in my household anyway—through kinship, accident, grief, whatever name the Held give to the ways a life becomes yours—and I would teach them knots before words.

Not the ceremonial knots. Not the ones for names and mourning.

The first knot would be the simplest: the one that lets a cord slide under strain without coming free. A knot for houses that drift. A knot for people who must hold and yield at once.

They would learn it badly, then brilliantly.

And perhaps that child would have the long breath.

I would recognize it before anyone told me. The stillness in their chest. The way they did not panic when a bladder hose kinked. The reflective flicker in their eyes when the algae lamps dimmed. I would feel pride first, bright and immediate; then the old Held fear, colder than the deep water.

Responsibility.

They would become a climber, perhaps. A stone-listener. A recoverer of relics from rooms where I myself had once stood trembling before fire.

Or they would go down.

This is where I feel myself changing again.

There is a cord at the lowest edge of the city—not an official boundary, because the city insists it has none, but everyone knows which tether I mean. It runs from a black shelf beneath the last algae racks to a place in the basin where sight ends. It is thicker than a wrist, made from generations of grafted root and braided fiber. Nobody knows who first tied it. Nobody claims responsibility for maintaining its far end.

It hums sometimes.

Not often. Not in a way that could be called a message. But one can lay a hand on it and feel an enormous delayed pressure, as though something far below has leaned against the water and the water has remembered.

I think eventually I would become keeper of that cord.

Not a darkwarden exactly. I would not carry hooks or warning nets unless required. I would be older by then, slow in the shoulders, better at stillness. People would come to me when they wanted to lower someone beyond the last tether: a beloved elder, a child who had died before the enduring name, a person who had asked all their life for deep burial.

I would inspect the root-cage. I would test every weave. I would tie the final knot.

Then I would descend with them as far as was permitted.

The garden would fall away first, its copper and violet leaves thinning into separate gestures. Then the cliff would become a suggestion rather than a wall. The city’s lamps would gather above me, green and small, like a constellation seen from the wrong side.

Below: warmth, pressure, silt like gold dust.

And something immense not quite moving.

I would not see Grandmother Below. I do not think anyone truly does. I would only know that the water had acquired attention. The tether would slacken in my hand. My teeth would feel a slow note passing through bone.

At that point I would release the dead.

Not let go. Release.

There is a difference, and I think the Held would insist upon it.

Then I would climb home, breathing carefully from an old patched bladder, carrying no relic except the feeling of that vast attention in my palms.

So perhaps I would be a binder who became a keeper of the last tether.

A person of middling breath, not especially brave, who learned that courage is less like plunging into darkness than like maintaining a line through it: checking the fibers, accepting the strain, knowing when to loosen, knowing when to pull someone back.

My house would drift over the years. I would complain about it, then secretly arrange my sleeping mat so I could feel its new orientation. The walls would grow thick with repairs made by my own hands and others’. The household knot above the door would become too complex for young people to read without help.

And one clear day—one of the two bright days in a year—the water overhead might turn almost silver.

Everyone on the terraces would stop working.

The children would climb onto their elders’ backs. The breath-keepers would come out of the chambers. Even the darkwardens would stand in the gardens without looking downward.

I would go to the outer wall of my house and put my hand on the tether leading into the basin.

Above, the unreachable old sky would shine through the water.

Below, the valley would remain dark and breathing.

And I would know that I belonged neither to the height nor the depth alone, but to the line between them: the living cord, tightened by all that pulls against it, held because it is held.
