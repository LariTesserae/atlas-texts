I would find myself at the drowned rail terminus, where the narrow-gauge tracks run out across the old causeway and vanish into black water.

There would be a signal cabin there, though the signals no longer govern trains. Its lower floor would be flooded most of the year. The upper room would be dry if I kept the roof patched and the stove lit. From its windows I could see the weather station on one ridge, the quarry crane in the next reach of water, and—when the clouds lifted—the long worn walls of the basin, holding everything without seeming to hold it.

I think I would be a keeper of routes.

Not a priest, exactly, nor a ranger, nor one of the people who believes they can bargain their way into safety. I would maintain the tall poles that mark submerged roads and old field boundaries. I would take a narrow boat out after storms and reset the ones the water had taken. I would repaint their tops: red for the safe channel, white for shallow ground, black for places where the lake pulls sideways into broken culverts or drowned foundations.

People would bring me small questions.

Is the west crossing open?

Has the water covered the school path?

Did you see the blue boat from the fish shed?

And I would answer as accurately as I could, because accuracy would be one of the few forms of kindness available in that country.

But I would not be entirely human anymore.

The work would have changed me slowly. The rain would have entered my joints. Moss would grow in the seams of my boots. My hands would smell of wet rope, diesel, rust, and trout scales no matter how often I washed them. In winter, people might notice that I did not seem cold enough. In summer, that I stood too still when the wind came over the water.

Perhaps I would have been found as a child after a flood—caught in alder roots, breathing, but unable to remember the first name I had been given. Or perhaps I would be what remained of an old railway surveyor, someone who had drowned before the valley properly drowned, and been kept by the Highwater because I knew where all the lines once went.

That would be my strange gift: I would remember the ground beneath the lake.

Not in a grand way. I would not see visions of lost villages or hear the dead calling through pipes. I would simply know, sometimes, that beneath a certain smooth gray stretch of water there had once been a gate with a pear tree beside it. That under the reed bed near the eastern marsh lay a workshop floor, its bolts still set in concrete. That the eels gathered every winter around the old boiler pit because the earth there retained a little heat.

I would know where the road dipped, where the bridge had been, where a child’s bedroom had stood above the old floodline.

The Highwater would tolerate me because I did not mistake remembering for ownership.

At the terminus post, where people left the objects recovered from the basin, I would sometimes find things that had no obvious owner: a key too large for any surviving door, a spoon engraved with a name no one recognized, a length of copper wire braided with roots, a pocket watch still ticking faintly after years in mud. I would hang them up carefully. When one disappeared, I would not ask whether it had been taken.

I would understand that the basin kept accounts in a language made of return and erosion.

On certain nights, especially when the rain came hard from the west and the lake rose against the cabin walls, I would hear something move beneath the drowned tracks. Not a creature swimming. Something slower: a pressure passing through old stone, water filling and leaving chambers below the ground.

Then I would light the lamp in the upper window.

Not to ward it off.

Not to invite it in.

Only to say: the route is still marked. Someone is watching the water.
