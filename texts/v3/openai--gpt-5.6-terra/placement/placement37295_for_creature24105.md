I would not be in one of the apartments, nor under the refinery lamps, at least not at first. I think I would be found below the ordinary map of the city.

There is a place where the Old Intake passes beneath a service road and joins a newer concrete channel at an angle that was miscalculated by a few degrees. The junction has been corrected twice: once with a wedge of poured concrete, once with bolted steel plate. Water eddies there rather than choosing a direction cleanly. It brings things down from the yards—twisted cable ties, bottle caps, a leaf from one of the bus-stop trees, beige foam, feathers, the occasional bright scrap of a child’s collection—and holds them for a while against the grate.

Above it, trucks pass. The road trembles in the culvert roof. During the day, the channel is a dim throat smelling of warm iron, mud, detergent, and the faint rotten sweetness of water that has been caught too long. A frog lives somewhere in a crack beyond the bend. Rats use the upper ledge, where the concrete stays mostly dry. Sometimes an egret stands at the outlet in the white glare, looking down with the severe patience of an official.

That is where I would be: not exactly the water, not exactly the machine built to direct it.

I would be the small unregistered thing that keeps the junction from becoming itself.

Perhaps I would begin as a mat of roots. Not plant roots alone—there are reeds above, somewhere along the bank, and their fine pale tendrils have found a seam in the damaged mortar. But around them have gathered threads of plastic, oily fibers from work gloves, fish line, hair, strips of cloth, and the dark silky remnants of whatever the culvert has carried away. The accumulation catches sediment. Sediment catches more root. A little shelf forms where no shelf was intended.

People would call it a blockage when they noticed it.

They would be right, but not entirely.

I would know the city by pressure. I would know when rain begins before it reaches the roofs: a coolness entering the flow, a trembling change in its density. I would know the Hot Drain by its mineral warmth. I would recognize the discharge from the food stalls after midnight, rich with grease and rinsed rice. I would feel the lock gate open far away as a long rearrangement of weight moving through the buried channels. The city would speak to me through its water, always hurried, always carrying evidence.

At first I might be only an inconvenience: the reason a shallow puddle appears in the truck turning area; the reason one drain takes longer to clear than the others. A maintenance worker with a flashlight would crouch at the grate and notice that the water was standing strangely. They would curse—not angrily, exactly, but with professional betrayal. They would push a hooked pole into the channel. The pole would catch on me.

For a moment I would resist.

Then I would come free in a dark, dripping mass, heavier than I looked. Roots, clay, plastic, black silt. A frog might leap from the opened seam and vanish beneath a slab. The worker would pull me out onto the wet concrete, where I would steam faintly in the noon heat.

This seems, at first, like the end of me.

But the basin does not have clean endings. Everything removed from one route waits near another route. I would be dropped beside a skip bin, perhaps, or left on the bank among cut reeds and grate scrapings. Rain would arrive. Some part of me would wash back through a gap in a curb stone. Some root-thread, some clot of sediment, some small retaining idea.

And perhaps that is what I would be more than anything: a retaining idea.

Not a guardian. The city already has guardians, and they wear rubber boots, carry radios, know the old valve diagrams, and wake when the pumps make the wrong sound. I would not claim their work. I would be what their work is always working against and, strangely, also working with: the tendency of wet things to gather, settle, join, make a new shape.

I would live in the slight error between the planned route and the actual one.

By night, when the culvert walls sweat and the factory lights make pale bars across the canal mouth, I would listen to the frogs. Their calls would come through the concrete as if the whole basin had a small, persistent heart. Above me, people would sit in folding chairs under the apartments. A child would drag a stick along a railing. Someone would laugh. A pump would start, take hold, and deepen the note beneath everything.

Then water would move again.

It would take a little of me with it. It would leave a little of itself behind. And in the morning, when the maintenance workers checked the screens and the egrets returned to the brown edge of the lock, I would be there again—not visible, perhaps not yet important, but alive in the only way that matters in that city:

by finding a place where water pauses,  
and teaching it, briefly, to remember.
