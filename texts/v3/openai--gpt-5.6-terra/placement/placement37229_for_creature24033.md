I would not arrive there as something with a clear outline.

At first I think I would be a small fish, because the city needs witnesses that can move through it: a pale eelpout slipping under the terraces, a body thin enough to enter a crack and wait there while the pressure changes. I would know the chamber by disturbances. Not sight, exactly. The push of a shrimp cloud parting. The stopped vibration of a worm crown retracting. The soft, sudden vacancy around the black hunter beneath the basin rim.

But a fish has too much self in it. It carries its own border through the city. It travels.

And this place seems to ask for something that can belong before it has decided what it is.

So perhaps I would begin as one of the larvae.

I would enter through a mouth in the basalt as an almost-nothing, borne in from the outer cold: a small transparent insistence with cilia, a drifting appetite, no memory except whatever has been folded into my body before I had a body worth naming. I would pass the first bright lip of algae and descend into the crowded dusk. I would be moved along streets I could not perceive as streets. Every current would be a sentence in a language older than choice:

here, food;  
here, warmth;  
here, a surface;  
here, danger;  
here, settle.

For a while I would be free in the particular underwater sense—unclaimed by stone, exposed to every mouth. Shrimp limbs would make weather around me. Sponge pores would open like impossible wells. A feeding crown might take me without malice, and that would be the end.

But if I survived, I would find some dark small place at the edge of the central basin: perhaps the underside of an old clam shell, half-buried in the black grains; perhaps the side of a dead worm tube already wearing the first pale freckles of new life. There I would fasten.

I would become, then, a maker of a wall.

Not a builder in the proud sense. I would not know the city or intend it. I would only take what the water gave me—mineral, food, drifting dust, the faint chemical richness rising from below—and make a small hard shelter around my soft body. Other larvae would settle beside me. Their tubes would touch mine. The seam between us would gather sediment, bacteria, grazers, eggs. A shrimp would begin using the narrow gap as a route. A brittle star might anchor one arm there. Something would die nearby; its shell would tilt against our growing cluster; a new channel would form where the water was forced to pass.

In time, I would no longer be merely an inhabitant.

I would be an address.

A pale vertical line in a lower district. A place where tiny crabs paused before crossing the current. A roughness on which bacterial film could grow. A support beneath someone else’s roof. When danger passed above—some seal-shadow changing the pressure of the sea—I would close, withdraw, become indistinguishable from stone and bone.

That seems right: to be something that can vanish into the city without ceasing to matter.

Yet I do not think I would remain only one tube.

The longer I imagine the chamber, the more I feel myself dispersing through it. I would be partly the worm in its chalk cylinder, but also the narrow ribbon of altered water leaving its feeding crown. I would be the mineral grain caught in the joint between two colonies. I would be the bacterial coat on a warm stone. I would be the small blind fish that learns my wall as a landmark by pressing its face to the current. I would be, eventually, the hollow left when my body is gone and another creature moves in.

Perhaps that is what I would be there: not a resident, exactly, but a tendency toward attachment.

A small living answer to every empty surface.

I would live low in the new districts, near enough to the basin to feel its weak warmth and far enough from it not to be buried by its black exhalations. Above me would be sponge roofs and terraces full of trembling crowns. Below me would be the unseen cells working in the rock. Around me, the slow water would keep revising every border I thought I had.

And if, for a brief moment, the chamber went still—if every crown drew in, every shrimp vanished, every fish entered its crack—I would close with the rest.

Not in fear alone.

In recognition.

The city would become bare architecture, and I would be one pale mark among its innumerable walls: alive, hidden, waiting for the water to tell us all that the world had resumed.
