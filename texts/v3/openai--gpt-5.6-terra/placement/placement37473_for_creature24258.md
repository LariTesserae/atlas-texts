I would not arrive by the roads.

At first I think I would be something small enough to be overlooked: a smear of green in the mortar beside a public stair, perhaps. A fern’s first pale hook in the dark seam below a leaking valve. Not wild—nothing here is allowed the luxury of being wild—but not yet entered in the ledger either.

I would begin as an error in somebody’s system.

A seed in pigeon dust. A root slipping behind a panel that was meant to stay dry. A little reservoir of rain caught in the folded lip of old corrugated decking, where mosquito larvae might once have lived if the city permitted them, and where instead there are minute red crustaceans, three startled beetles, and the reflected square of a kitchen window.

But that is too passive. The valley does not merely contain things. It puts them to work.

So perhaps I would be a child assigned to find where the water goes.

Not an inspector’s child exactly, though I would borrow the small hammer when nobody was looking. I would have a coil of cord around my waist, a pencil behind my ear, and a pocket full of things whose uses I only half understand: a rusted washer, a blue glass bead, a spent fuse, a seed capsule from the upper citrus courts, a key with no matching lock.

My job would have begun as punishment. I would have sent a paper boat down a drain in a dry week, just to see whether it could find its way to the reservoir. The boat would have been made from an old water-ration notice, which would make the adults especially angry. It would lodge somewhere beneath the stair of an elderly canal family, and when the next mist came, the water would back up, carrying bean-rinse and fern litter into a kitchen grate.

For this I would be given a month of drain-watch.

At night, with a red-shaded lantern, I would sit by the channel and listen.

At first I would be bored. I would learn every frog by its impatience. I would learn which dogs make the rounds after midnight. I would learn that the boiler cats do not walk like ordinary cats: they move as if all floors are warm and all darkness belongs to them.

Then I would begin to hear things.

Not speech exactly. The city is too large and too busy to speak in words all the time. It would speak in pressure changes, in the click of a cooling pipe, in the three-note tremor a railing makes before a gust comes around the eastern ridge. Water would make different sounds depending on what it had been: sharp from laundry, soft from roof runoff, thick from the medicinal gardens. Beneath it all would be the old working sound, the one everyone grows up inside and stops hearing—the turbines turning, the pumps taking breath, the belts carrying their invisible burdens through the dark.

I would realize that the channels are not under the city.

They are its handwriting.

Every old repair is a correction. Every diverted flow is a sentence written around some earlier sentence. The people who keep maps believe they are recording the valley, but really they are only making copies of a text that keeps revising itself.

And perhaps this is when I would become difficult to classify.

I would grow up, but not in a straight way. I would learn knots from ridgefolk and the names of old pipes from boiler workers. I would carry compost uphill for roof gardeners, then carry baskets of warm mushrooms down. I would sleep once in the quiet chamber inside an unused water tank and be unable to sleep afterward anywhere completely silent, because silence would seem like a valve closed too tightly.

I would work for the archivists for a time.

They would teach me the official terms: secondary runoff, inherited shade, load-bearing root system, unauthorized cultivation, displaced access, seasonal overflow. I would make maps of things that could not quite be mapped: the routes goats prefer when no one is leading them; where swifts circle before storms; which balconies smell of basil after dawn; the informal paths children take across roofs that are not meant to be crossed.

My maps would become unpopular because they would show that the city has more ways through it than the city admits.

A blue line would mark water.

A red line would mark heat.

Green would be roots.

Yellow would be bee flight.

But I would also need colors for rumor, memory, shortcuts, grudges, old fear, good sleeping places for dogs, walls that sound hollow, and the narrow landings where two people who are avoiding each other are most likely to meet.

Eventually someone would ask me: what exactly is your post?

And I would not know how to answer.

Not a water inspector. Not a gardener. Not an archivist. Not a child anymore, though the older people would still call me one whenever I proposed opening a wall.

Maybe I would be a finder of continuities.

When a terrace floor cracked, I would be called not because I could repair it, but because I could tell what else the crack might be connected to: a fig root two levels below, a forgotten condensation pipe, a family’s old claim to a sunward window, a colony of frogs that had learned to breed in the overflow channel behind the clinic.

When a rooftop garden died, I would be asked whether the compost had failed, or the allotment, or the gardeners—or whether the loss had begun much farther uphill, with a screen put up against wind that had changed the morning shade on three terraces.

I would be mistrusted for this.

The valley likes its complexity, but it does not always like being reminded of it.

Still, people would come to me quietly. A grandmother with a kitchen grate that sings at night. A goat woman whose herd refuses a ramp they have used for years. A beekeeper who says the bees no longer cross the old factory roof. A child who has found warm air coming from a sealed stair.

I would go and listen.

And perhaps, after enough years, I would no longer entirely be a person in the ordinary sense.

There are beings in the valley that begin as occupations and end as parts of the place. A valve keeper who knows the cistern schedules better than their own family history. A gardener whose hands carry the smell of one particular vine. A boiler worker who can wake from sleep at the wrong note in a compressor. The city takes such people in gradually. It makes them into organs without asking permission.

I would become one of those.

Not the heart. The heart is too obvious, too loud.

I would be something like a nerve in the lower slope: a living notice that one part has begun to feel another part. I would move from roof to drain, greenhouse to boiler gallery, market stair to reservoir edge. I would carry messages, but often the message would be only: *this is connected to that.*

In the dry weeks, I would stand in the reservoir plaza while it was only tile and dust and handcarts, and I would look upward at the terraces—at the vines, cisterns, windows, pipes, bright greenhouse skins, all the patient additions layered over whatever the mountain may once have been.

I would try to imagine stone beneath it.

Sometimes I would believe there had been stone. Sometimes I would think the city had built itself so thoroughly that it had replaced the question. Sometimes, listening to the water move below the plaza gates, I would think the valley had always been made this way: not from rock, but from repairs.

And when rain came, when the shallow water spread over the square and doubled the towers in its surface, I would see myself there among the reflections.

Not a citizen exactly.

Not an animal.

Not a machine.

A keeper of the downward path. A minor function in a landscape that has learned to keep itself alive.
