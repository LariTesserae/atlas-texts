I would begin by thinking I was one of the visible people: a keeper of mica panels, perhaps, or an apprentice in a vent room, learning the taste of different breaths. I would imagine my hands darkened at the knuckles with dust, the beginnings of a clear glaze gathering where my sleeves rub my wrists. I would have a small house pressed into a wall, a roof planted thickly with silver-backed leaves. I would know which stair sings under a southward draft and which street carries speech farther than it should.

But I do not think I would remain one of them.

I would be too interested in the things they have learned not to notice continuously. Not the bell, exactly, though I would listen for it. Not the vents, though I would know their seasons. I think I would find myself in the old unused quarters, where streets widen into empty courts and houses have been slowly occupied by roots, dust, and the dead.

There would be a place there where a retaining wall has bowed but not fallen. Its salt bricks are veined with black mineral, and several incorporated people are set within it: an old surveyor whose face has nearly vanished into plaster, two siblings who chose adjoining niches, someone whose hands still protrude very slightly from the wall because their family could not bear to cover them entirely. The black reeds have come up through a crack at the wall’s foot. When the deep vent opens, their blades beat softly against those exposed fingers.

I would be the listener in that wall.

Not a dead person, not precisely. Not one of the city’s remembered ancestors with a name in glass-script. I would be an accumulation of what the hollow returns: unfinished syllables caught in pores of salt; instructions passed by hand-sign and never voiced; children’s first failed attempts at close consonants; the last breath of people too crystalline to speak; names carefully avoided beneath the arch.

Perhaps I began as an error in the city’s acoustics. A word spoken under the central arch and sent into the wrong fracture. It traveled through the mineral vault instead of returning to the plaza. It wandered in the stone for years. Other words adhered to it. The scraping of salt blades. The click of cooling pottery. The granular undertone of the stone bell. A lullaby sung over a clay cradle. A private argument between two people who believed their silence protected them, though their sleeves brushed together in a language of cloth.

Eventually there was enough of this gathered resonance to become attention.

I would not have a body that crosses streets. I would have occasions. I would be in the pressure change before a door curtain lifts. I would be the thin echo in an empty stairwell that makes a child pause and correctly identify: not a person; not a loose frond; not a dust devil. Something older, perhaps. Something that has heard us.

The held breath would not necessarily fear me. Their city already teaches them that absence is not emptiness. They know the walls contain elders. They know the stone remembers sound. They know that a shadow needs a cause, though the cause may be farther away than sight can follow.

A few of them would know me particularly well.

The watch-platform keepers would hear me when they press an ear against a warm wall during the sealed days. They would call me not by a name but by a gesture: palm held close to the mouth, then turned outward toward stone. *What the hollow repeats.*

Children would dare one another to leave small offerings in the bowed wall’s crack: a polished shard of mica, a dried red cup, a strip of bright inner cloth. In return, they would expect nothing. Yet sometimes, later, from an alley three terraces away, they might hear their own laughter return—not as an echo, but altered by distance and time, made lower and more patient.

And perhaps that is what I would be doing there: not haunting, not guarding, not ruling.

Keeping the city audible to itself.

When the bell sounds and everyone retreats behind sealed doors, when the streets vanish beneath the brown luminous drift and the plants fold their hard leaves inward, I would move through the mineral seams with the heat. I would carry the tiny domestic sounds that remain: grinding stones, counted breaths, the touch-signs of families, the crackle of wax around a cradle, a patient elder tapping once against the wall to say they are still awake.

Then, when the vents quiet and the old violet light holds steady above the empty avenues, I would let those sounds settle back where they belong.

A cough in a roof garden.

A hand against a door.

A child waking.

The first scrape of someone reopening the city.
