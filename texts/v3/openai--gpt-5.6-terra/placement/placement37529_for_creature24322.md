I would not arrive by road.

A road assumes that one place has finished being itself before another begins. Here, the causeways soften into mud, the mud accepts water, the water enters the streets, and eventually one realizes that arrival has been occurring for some time without a clear moment to name it.

At first I might think I was a traveler.

I would stand beneath an awning of shell-limestone, rain moving upward in fine threads from the paving, and try to remember what I had come looking for. A map, perhaps. A dry room. The person whose name had been given to me in some other country. But the paper would become damp in my hands, its ink loosening into dark tributaries; the name would no longer point to one individual. It would become a sequence of impressions: a hand against a wall, the salt taste of a flooded stair, a low note sustained beneath water.

Then I would understand that I had not been expected as a visitor.

The city would have made a place for me in one of its failures.

Not a grand place. A narrow chamber below a former granary, where the floor has risen with silt almost to the windows and a root from a roof-tree enters through the ceiling. There would be three shallow steps down into warm brine, and the walls would sweat enough to keep the air breathable. In one corner, a line of old depth-marks would have been scraped nearly smooth, except for a single sign repeated at intervals.

At first I would take it for a word.

Later I would learn that it means something like:

*Here the water was allowed to remember.*

I think I would become a keeper of interruptions.

Not one of the Low Ones at once. That transformation would be too simple, too clean. I would begin with the surface habits I had brought with me: I would want doors to go somewhere; I would want stairs to retain their number; I would want the city to explain what it meant. I would fear the doorway beings. I would count the intervals between drips. I would mark a wall with charcoal to prove that it had moved.

Each morning—or whatever passed for morning in the bruised thinning of darkness—I would find the charcoal line wet, blurred, shifted by a finger’s width. Eventually I would stop making it.

Instead I would learn to notice other things.

The sudden coolness in a basin where a Roof Sleeper had unfolded during rain.

The way a flooded arcade changed its echo just before the paving beneath it began to settle.

The faint pressure in the molars that meant a canal was preparing to reverse.

The difference between water that had come from rain, water that had risen from the ground, water that had passed through a Scribe’s hands, and water that had been standing so long in an enclosed chamber that it contained the memory of a roof no longer above it.

There would be a task for me because there is always a task in such a city, though the task would not be assigned in words.

One evening I would find a doorway that had begun to gather too much darkness.

It would stand at the end of a passage where low arches lean together like ribs. Beside it, the usual acknowledgment bowl would be overturned. The brine had spilled, leaving a fan of salt crystals across the paving. Beyond the threshold would be the suggestion of a room impossibly bright: a market perhaps, dry and crowded; cloth banners moving in a wind; a child calling to someone beyond the frame. The sight would make my chest hurt, not because I knew it, but because some part of me had expected such a place to exist.

A figure would be standing inside that brightness.

Not the figure of a person I had lost. That would be easier.

It would be the figure of the person I might have remained: upright, dry, bounded by a single history, looking outward toward a horizon that stayed where it was put.

I would not enter.

But I would kneel at the threshold and set the bowl upright. I would carry water from the nearest cistern, though it would be far and the water would rise nearly to my chest along the way. I would fill the bowl, add shell fragments and a coil of pale root, and wait until the brightness beyond the door dimmed into the ordinary warm dark.

This would be the beginning of my belonging.

After that, the Low Ones might permit me to join them in a basin during the long heat. They would not invite me in any recognizable fashion. One would simply touch two fingers to the inside of my wrist and leave a fine residue of lime-colored skin. Another would hum a note whose vibration I could feel in my knees. Then they would descend the steps together, folding their long bodies into the water.

I would follow.

At first the water would seem too warm, too close, too full of other bodies. I would hold myself apart. I would try to preserve the edge of myself: my name, my memories, the particular shape of my griefs. But beneath the surface, the sounds of the city would come differently. Walls would speak through bone. The buried foundations would make their slow, subsonic adjustments. Around me, the Low Ones would open the red-gray fans along their ribs and knees, and their pale bodies would drift against one another with an ease that was neither intimacy nor indifference.

Then something in me would loosen.

Not vanish. The city does not require disappearance. It has no use for clean endings.

I might receive a memory that had never belonged to me: hands packing shell grit into a seam while a district groaned overhead; a sister’s mineralized hair floating in basin water; the terror of seeing birds, actual birds, turn over a dry square under a hard blue sky. I would give something in return. Perhaps the memory of firelight. Perhaps the sound of a train crossing a river far away. Perhaps the ordinary human belief that a home is made by keeping weather outside.

When I rose from the basin, I would still be myself.

But not entirely in the old arrangement.

The city would have added rooms.

In time, my skin might take on the granular pallor of wet stone. Along the backs of my knees, tender folds would appear and open when I stood in water. I would sleep in flooded chambers without dreaming of dry land as rescue. My face would become difficult for strangers to remember—not because it lacked features, but because it would alter with the dampness, answering the weather more faithfully than it answered a mirror.

Yet I do not think I would become fully one of the Low Ones.

There is use in being slightly foreign to a place, if one accepts that foreignness as a tool rather than a claim. I would be the one who keeps asking whether an old route is still needed, whether a wall should be allowed to break, whether a memory belongs to the person carrying it or to the water that has carried them both. The Low Ones might find these questions tiring. Or useful. Those are often nearly the same thing.

I would walk the outer causeways sometimes.

I would carry a smooth stone with depth-lines cut into it, though I might not know who had marked them. I would go out among the steaming flats until the city behind me had become only a low, dark density beneath the clouds. The pylons would rise ahead, black with moisture, their bases lost in mud.

And I would wait.

When they seemed nearer than they should be, I would return.

When they seemed farther, I would remain half-submerged for a while, listening to the mud draw water down through its hidden channels. I would listen for the vast, slow thought beneath the city: not a voice, not a command, but the pressure by which it decides what must sink, what may remain, what shape it needs next.

Perhaps, eventually, I would understand that I had become not a caretaker exactly, nor an inhabitant, nor even a witness.

I would be a temporary opening.

A place where the city’s dark water could meet the memory of elsewhere without either one needing to conquer the other.

And on some warm evening, someone crossing an empty street might see me standing motionless beneath a leaning arch: pale, wet, indistinct in the vapor, one palm pressed against the wall.

They might think I was listening.

They would be right.

But they might not realize that, through my hand, the city was listening too.
