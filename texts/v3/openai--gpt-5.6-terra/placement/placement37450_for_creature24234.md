I would not enter by one of the gates.

The gates are too certain of what a visitor is: a body arriving from elsewhere, a pair of feet carrying a history, a name held inside a mouth. The city receives such things generously, perhaps, but it receives them into its enormous uncertainty. I think I would come in less distinctly.

At first I might be only a discrepancy in a stairwell: a landing that ought to be dry, except for one small square of stone where no moisture gathers. Not dry exactly. Warmed from underneath, with the matte softness of a palm held over it for a long time.

A stillfolk would find it before a person did.

It would place its hand on that square. Water would bead from its wrist, hesitate, and move away from the touch. The roots in the wall would listen. Somewhere far below, in a market hall flooded with upright water, a black-leaf vine would loosen from a doorway.

Then I would become useful.

Not an inhabitant in the ordinary way, and not quite one of the stillfolk. They are the city’s answers to its waiting. I think I would be one of its questions.

There must be questions here, though the city does not ask them in language. The city asks through doors that have no corresponding rooms; through stairways that return a traveler to a place above where they began; through the faintly lit interiors behind sealed walls. It asks with all that architecture intended for a life it has never had, or has had so completely that no evidence remains.

I would live in the interval between a door and what it has decided to open onto.

A traveler might find me in a narrow district midway up the outer face, where the streets are roofed over by touching buildings and the clouds appear only in long white strips above. There would be a door there: plain, oval, made from a single slab of dark volcanic glass. Water would pass through the stone without wetting it. The door would have no handle.

Most days it would open onto a small room containing a table, a chair, an open window, and rain beyond the window rising steadily upward through a courtyard.

On other days it would open onto the underside of a bridge in the upper basin, where roots hang through blue vapor and the whole inverted city can be seen trembling in the clouds.

And on rare days, for a traveler who had been walking too long alone, it would open onto a room from elsewhere.

Not exactly their own room. Nothing so crude. But a room made from the pressure of their remembering: a kitchen bright at evening, perhaps, with an unwashed cup by a sink; the dim spare bedroom of a house long since sold; a school corridor after everyone has gone; a station platform in rain. The details would not be accurate. They would be made of the city’s materials—fern-shadow, warm limestone, dripping root, glass-black water. Yet the traveler would recognize it with such force that they might stand at the threshold for hours.

I would be seated inside, not facing them.

Perhaps I would have a human outline. Perhaps I would be a length of dark cloth in the chair, slowly collecting the shape of shoulders. My hands would rest on the table. One hand would be stone-smooth and wet. The other would be made of something like old paper, except its writing would run and reform in the damp.

I would not tell the traveler what the room means.

That would be a violence in this place.

Instead I would offer the city’s gentlest translation: a moment in which the lost thing is allowed to have an address.

If they came in, the room would remain. If they did not, it would soften back into a different possibility. By the time the next cloud passed down the street, the door might lead to a cistern, a garden, or a cliff full of enormous sleeping orchids.

This is not quite kindness, but it is adjacent to it.

I think the stillfolk would tolerate me because I would not require them to explain themselves. I would see them most often in the rooms that appear behind impossible doors. They would stand in corners, eyes closed, holding damp cloaks against their chests. Sometimes they would leave objects on the table: a bell that cannot be heard, a fruit of green translucent stone, a little coil of root shaped like a question mark.

In return, I would keep what they cannot keep.

Not possessions. They have no use for possessions. I would keep the brief human-scale meanings that flicker through the city and vanish beneath its immense continuance: the thought of a hand reaching for another hand; the feeling of waiting for someone who is late; the instinct to set out a second cup; the knowledge that a path is a promise only if it leads beyond itself.

The city has all the forms of these things. It has tables, chairs, cups, thresholds, avenues, windows, beds. But form alone is not always enough. Its rooms are patient beyond patience. Its doors expect passage without needing it. Its streets lead through themselves forever and are perfectly satisfied.

I would be the small unease in that satisfaction.

Not the wish to escape. The mountain has forgotten enough already. I would not teach it departure.

I would be the question that makes an empty chair face a window.

The question that makes one of the stillfolk pause with its hand against a wall.

The question that causes, once in a thousand years of unchanging rain, a single fruit to loosen from a branch and fall—not downward, perhaps, but sideways through a sunlit room—and strike the floor with a sound no one can hear.

And if I had a body, it would only appear when someone needed to leave.

They would find me at the edge of the upper inward basin, where waterfalls rose in columns from gardens below and vanished into the false sky. I would be standing near a balcony overgrown with silver ferns. Behind me, a narrow stair would descend into cloud. Before me, the basin would open endlessly inward, full of terraces and water and the dark green pressure of the mountain dreaming its city.

I would point—not with a finger, but with a change in the rain.

One thread among the countless vertical threads would slant.

The traveler would follow it.

They would pass through districts they had not seen before, then through rooms that resembled places they had already passed, then through an arch where the water sounded less total. At last they would come to some opening in the stone: perhaps a gate, perhaps a crack, perhaps merely a bright absence between two roots.

Beyond it would be the lower cloudlands, cool wind, distance, creatures making small noises in grass.

They would go.

And after they had gone, I would not remain standing on the balcony. The city does not need another figure who waits.

I would become again a table in a room behind a door.

A chair angled toward the window.

A cup filled with clear water, holding—just briefly—the reflection of someone who has not yet arrived.
