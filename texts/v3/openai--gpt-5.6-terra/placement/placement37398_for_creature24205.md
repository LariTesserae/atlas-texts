At first I think I would be a raven.

Not because I would want its black wings, exactly, or the height it keeps without seeming to work for it. I would want its permission to arrive and leave. To know the Fold from above: the three low shelters, the noon pool flashing and then gone, the goats like pale stones moving slowly across the lower green. I would want to cross the basin as a shadow and make everything pause.

But a raven belongs to too much sky. It carries the valleys with it. It knows roofs, carrion, trails, the habits of people. Even when it lands in the Bright Fold, some part of it is already elsewhere.

I think I would want to belong more narrowly than that.

Perhaps I would be one of the dark bees, waking stiffly beneath a stone after a night of frost. I would drag myself into the sun and open my wings one at a time. The whole day would be an emergency of flowers. I would know every blue bell by its warmth, every crimson cushion by its hidden chambers of pollen. A cloud would be an event large enough to end the world; sunlight returning would begin it again.

There is a purity in such a life. No plan longer than weather. No thought grander than carrying brightness from one flower to the next.

But the bee has too much urgency. I imagine its body forever sounding with the small instruction to hurry.

No. I would be something quieter.

I would find myself beneath a flowering cushion near the center of the basin, close enough to the noon pool that the soil darkens for a few hours each day, far enough that the water does not wash me away. From above, I would be invisible. A person might rest a hand on the cushion and feel only its warmth: the tight leaves, the tiny flowers, the stored heat of stone.

Below that, I would be a thread.

Not a root, though I would move among roots. Not quite a fungus, though I would share the work of fungal threads. I would be one of the fine, pale lives in the old soil: a keeper of damp, a traveler through dust, a patient exchange between moss and flower and buried stem.

I would take what the snowmelt gave.

At noon, when water appeared in the center of the Fold and the whole basin bent toward it—the bees, the pikas, the goats led down by their herders—I would feel its cold arriving through the gravel. Not all at once. First a darkening around the stones. Then a loosening in the earth. Then the small astonishment of moisture touching places that had spent the morning hard as bone.

I would drink it slowly.

I would pass some of it into the cushion above me: into the silver leaves that turn wind aside, into the roots of yellow stars and saxifrage, into the red-stemmed willow that has held its ground for years by being low and stubborn. In return, they would give me what they had made from light. Not generously, not selfishly. Simply because life there would be made of such bargains.

A moth larva might shelter among the leaves I helped feed.

A beetle might die there.

Its body would soften into the soil. I would take that too, not as theft but as continuation. A pika, cutting stems for its hidden haypile, might pass above me without ever knowing that the roots of the plants it gathered had been kept alive by a darkness finer than hair.

I would know the Fold through pressure.

The broad warm weight of a marmot lying on a nearby slab.

The quick, needle-light steps of a snow finch.

The heavier tremor of ibex descending from the rim in the blue hour before morning.

The uncertain, careful feet of a child told not to step into the strange frost-marks around the vanished pool.

When rock fell from the rear wall, I would feel the impact arrive through the ground: one hard shock, then smaller shivers, then silence. Dust would settle over the flowers. Some would be crushed. Some new crack would hold water. In a season, perhaps, lichen would begin there. In a dozen seasons, moss. In longer time than the herders cared to count, another small softness.

I would not mourn the broken stone. I would not welcome it either.

That might be the closest thing I had to wisdom: to understand that the Fold is not finished enough for permanence. Nothing there is. Not the pool. Not the shelters. Not the goats with their bells. Not the raw black rim, though it looks eternal from below.

Even the mountain is only in the middle of becoming itself.

And perhaps, on certain cold mornings, I would reach farther than my ordinary threads could reach. Not upward into sight, not downward into any great underworld, but sideways through the soaked earth around the pool’s old bed. There I would meet other fine lives: rootlets, fungal filaments, sleeping eggs, water held in grains of soil, seeds that had waited years beneath a stone.

Together we might make something faintly visible at the surface.

A pattern of frost.

Not a message, because messages expect to be understood. Not a prophecy, because the mountain does not need to predict what it is already doing.

Only a drawing made by cold and damp and the hidden routes of living things below the ground.

The herders would see it at dawn. They would pause. They would walk around it.

And I, beneath their boots, would be glad.

Not because I wanted reverence. Not because I was sacred.

But because in the Bright Fold, to be left undisturbed for one more morning would be enough.
