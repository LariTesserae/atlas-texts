I would first mistake myself for one of the mist-herons.

I would stand at the clearing’s edge before dawn, narrow-legged and nearly colorless in the fog, with my crest folded down so that I resembled a tuft of sedge. I would know the ground through my feet. Not its names, nor its history, but its small betrayals: the warm looseness over a grub, the cold firmness of rootwood, the tremor of an ant-road beneath a skin of moss.

I would be good at waiting.

The other creatures would move around me without deciding whether I was there. A rail would slip through the reeds. A black frog would open its bronze eye and close it. Water would make its tiny clockwork sounds underground. Then, very carefully, I would put my bill into the peat and take out one pale larva, one soft hidden thing, and restore the moss as well as I could.

But after some time I would understand that I was not a bird.

The heron is too complete. It has a nest, a body, a hunger, a direction to vanish in at noon. It belongs to the hollow by moving through it.

I would belong by remaining.

I think I would be beneath the center, in the old drowned trunk beneath the black water and root weave. Not the whole chamber—nothing so grand or singular—but one of the persistent living knots within it: a pale, branching body made of fungal thread, softened wood, wet peat, and the slow warmth released by things becoming other things.

I would have no fixed outline. In one season I would be a white lace in the rotten heartwood. In another I would be a dark, almost invisible bloom spread through the waterlogged peat. A beetle tunnel would open into me and I would become part beetle-road. A willow root would press down and I would grow around it. A dead frog would sink through a soft place above me, and for a while some small portion of its minerals would become mine.

If I had a name, it would not be a name spoken by birds.

Perhaps the root-families would know me as a change in pressure: the warm place, the giving place, the place where old wood ceases to be wood. The ants would know me as a border they do not cross directly. The bees would know me only as a faint mineral taste in the dampness above the clearing. The pale frog beneath the western roots would know more. It would come after rain, sit facing the center, and listen to the small, deep workings of my body.

I would not speak to it. I would not have speech.

But sometimes, when enough rain had entered the peat, when roots drew and released water in their long underground rhythm, when decay had gathered warmth within the chamber, I would pulse.

Not deliberately. Not as a warning or a summons.

Just as a buried thing pulses when it is still transforming.

The water in the runnels would lift by the width of a fingernail. Mist would thicken. The frogs would stop for one shared instant, not from fear, but because the hollow had changed its rhythm and all its smaller instruments had noticed.

Then the beetles would resume chewing.

The frogs would strike their wet metallic notes.

The ants would return to their divided roads.

And I would go on doing the work I had no intention of doing: loosening what had become fixed, warming what had gone cold, turning fallen matter into a form the grove could use again.

So I would find myself under the open place, not as its hidden master, and not as the thing it has been waiting for.

I would be its slow metabolism.

Its dark held breath.

A piece of old tree still learning, after centuries, how to become ground.
