I would find myself not in the basin at first, but in the throat beneath it.

I would be one of the things that goes down.

Not a ribbon exactly, though I would borrow their manner: a narrow body, dark and wet, capable of loosening when the stone narrows and gathering itself again where there is room. I would have no eyes fit for the white sky. Sight would be too extravagant in such a place. Instead I would know the mountain by pressure: the minute enlargement of a crack before it opens; the particular warmth of iron-bearing water; the sour bright ache of sulfur; the distant, slow beat of the wet stone above.

My body would be a fault-line sense organ.

Along my sides I would carry rows of shallow mouths—not mouths for eating, but for listening. Each could drink a drop, taste its dissolved history, and tell me where it had passed: whether it came recently from the snow-bright surface, whether it had slept in a hot chamber below, whether it had touched copper, salt, old darkness, a buried seam of something living before the mountain was lifted.

I would move through the hidden pipes of the new mountain, where water has not yet learned which way is down.

There, in the dark, I would become almost fluid. I would pour through hairline fractures. I would rest for a season inside an air bubble trapped in cooling basalt. I would wrap myself around a hot vein and take its temperature until it became part of me. In the deepest places, where the pressure made water heavy and strange, I would encounter minerals not yet exposed to any sky—blue crystals forming in black seams, pale metals growing furred around gas vents, blind microbial films feeding on heat with no knowledge of sun.

Perhaps I would carry those things upward.

Not as a messenger with a message, because the mountain would not speak in sentences. It would speak by changing what I was. I would return to the basin striped with a new ore, my skin roughened by some deep deposit, trailing a filament that hardened into a tube behind me. The glassbacks would cross it. The white-plated young would gather around it and place their little stones in its openings. A kiln shell might lean its warm weight against me, allowing a bead of water to run from its fused seam onto mine.

That would be greeting enough.

But I think, after long descending and returning, I would change again.

There are creatures in that basin which cease moving without becoming absent. Their shells fuse into the walls; their soft parts become pathways; their accumulated minerals turn into terrain for lives not yet precipitated. I would eventually want this. Not death, exactly. Not rest. A participation so complete that the distinction between my body and the basin’s body became useless.

I would choose a narrow place beneath the overhang, where the ascending rain gathers before it becomes the inverted lens. I would knot myself around a warm rivulet. For a long while I would listen to water traveling both directions: the ordinary flow slipping downward through the floor, the impossible droplets lifting along the wall, each carrying salts, skins, minute eggs, perhaps the dissolved remnants of other creatures.

My outer surface would crystalize.

The first glassback would climb over me without noticing that I had stopped. Then another. White-plated scavengers would use my hardened loop as an arch. A thread of the central stone’s clear water would find a depression in my back and remain there long enough for a translucent film to gather.

In time, I would become a small wet structure in the basin: a dark mineral bridge, a warm knot in the wall, part animal and part channel. The upward rain would touch me. Something carried in it—a grain, a spore, a memory too fine to name—would settle into one of my old listening-mouths.

And there, inside what had once been me, a new pale knot would begin to form.

Not my child, perhaps. Not a continuation in the clean animal sense.

More like an answer the mountain had been preparing through me.
