I would find myself there at the hour when the lamp has come on but the sky has not yet decided whether it is night.

Not inside the circle of yellow light. Near it. At its edge, where the wire guard’s shadow falls across a shallow runnel and the ground gives a little under the crust. There is a length of pipe there, elbowed upward from the silt, its open mouth packed with wet leaves and a pale nest of mineral threads. Beside it lies a square of timber, black as peat, with two rusted nails still holding nothing together.

I would be beneath that timber at first.

Not hidden exactly. More like waiting in the shape that hidden things take when they have been forgotten for long enough.

Perhaps I began as a survey peg. One of those plain pieces of wood driven into a place to make it answer to lines and numbers. There may once have been a red-painted top above the mud. Someone may have leaned over it with a clipboard while the works were still loud, read its position, and trusted it to mean that the ground was known.

The ground accepted the peg but did not agree.

The water rose around it. Ash settled. The red paint blistered, went dark, disappeared. A pipe was laid too near. A boot struck it sideways. Years later a pallet collapsed across it, and the peg ceased to mark a point. It became only a grain of resistance inside the wet black layer.

But the yard does not let such things become only themselves.

So now I would be what remains of the peg’s first purpose: not a marker, but a question of position. I would know where things were supposed to be. I would feel the difference between a pipe in its intended line and a pipe that had wandered three inches during a winter of frost. I would notice when an old route across the yard was crossed again after twenty years, even by someone who had never known it was a route. I would recognize the particular weight of a person hesitating before a puddle.

I would not see them. Sight would be too quick, too separate.

I would know them through pressure.

A heel planted hard because the ground looks firmer than it is. The faint rhythmic vibration of someone waiting beneath the lamp. The peculiar, almost apologetic tread of a person trying not to disturb a place they have entered without permission. These would arrive in me through the timber, the nail, the damp soil, the lower course of brick, the pipe’s buried length. By the time they reached me, they would be no longer feet. They would be events.

The larger presence under the yard would know me. Not as an individual, perhaps. It has no need for that. But it would allow me my small territory because I do not seek to rise.

Sometimes, when the pump begins behind the wall, I would remember being vertical.

The warmth would travel along the pipe. Moisture would bead on the underside of the timber. The nail heads would twitch in their holes. And for a moment something in me would lengthen upward through the dark, following the old grain of wood, following the path of the vanished red paint, until I almost broke the surface.

Then rain would strike the lamp guard.

A silver drop would fall.

The moment would pass.

Above me, a moth might circle the lamp, tapping softly against the wire. I would feel each touch as a tiny, bright interruption. I would not envy it. Its life would be all arrival and departure. It would mistake the light for a destination.

I would understand that the light is only another kind of weather.

If someone came to clear the yard—if they lifted the timber, pulled the nails, scraped back the mud—I would not resist. I could not. They would take me away in a skip with other unimportant things: black wood, broken brick, frayed cable, a wheel without a machine.

But something of me would remain below.

Not the wood. Not the nails. The question.

Where was this meant to stand?

Where did this weight first enter the ground?

What has shifted, and what merely appears to have shifted because the water has changed its mind?

Then, in some later season, a narrow object would work its way up through the wet soil near the lamp: perhaps a ruler of warped steel, perhaps a splinter of timber with one dark red fleck preserved under the grime. It would emerge at an angle, not quite marking anything.

And a person crossing the yard would notice it without knowing why.

They might pause.

The ground beneath them would draw a little nearer.
