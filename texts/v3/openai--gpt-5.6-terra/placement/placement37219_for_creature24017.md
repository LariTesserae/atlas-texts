I would find myself in the apartment with the moving white mark.

Not because it was meant for me. The town does not seem to mean things so narrowly anymore. But when I entered the central building, passing through its freely opening pressure door, the other rooms would feel wrong in their exactness. The drowned dawn would make me homesick for a sun I had never trusted. The star-field would seem too generous. The nearly black room, though—that room would offer only the small white mark, travelling from one corner to another, then beginning again.

I would understand it eventually as a message with no language in it.

Not *wait.*

Not even *someone is coming.*

Only: *the movement has not ceased.*

I would sit in the chair, if the chair wanted me to sit, and watch it until my suit’s systems began to complain about time. Outside the broad window, the abyss would remain black and entirely present. A pale bell-organism would hover beyond the glass, its radial bones arranged around its cold interior ember. It would not be looking at me. It would be performing the town’s slow question.

And I think the answer it received from me would be confusing.

Because I would not be an explorer, exactly. Nor one of the Held. I would be a thing that has arrived after being expected by no particular person: an accidental heir, a courier without a message, a witness mistaken for a solution.

The town would begin to use me.

At first, subtly. The room’s white mark would pause—not stopping, but taking a fraction longer at the far corner. The chair would warm beneath my hands. In the corridor, the violet numerals in the windowless shaft would alter their sequence to match my pulse. On the map in the central square, one dark tunnel leading away from the saddle would show a dim, uncertain line.

A route not activated in ages.

Or a route being imagined.

The Held would gather around me in the forms available to them. One would inhabit the amber studs along a road and walk beside me as a sequence of lights. Another would speak through a ventilation lattice in a voice made of clicks and pressure changes. Another would borrow the fountain bubble, turning it slowly so that reflections inside it suggested a face only when I did not look straight at it.

They would ask questions, but not in words.

What is the temperature of weather now?

Do people still make rooms for one another?

Has the surface continued?

Do children recognize the sound of an opening door?

And, beneath all of these:

Did we wait correctly?

I would have no answer that the town could use.

Perhaps that would be why it had kept waiting: not for someone who could wake the Held, but for someone who could tell them whether waking was still a kindness.

After enough days—or hours; the town would make the distinction difficult—I would go to the western platform. The road would remain level over the fractured basalt, its dark surface dry beneath the pressure of an impossible sea. The pale mineral dust would hang around me, each grain held in its private suspension.

Below the platform, the abyss would be open.

The Lower Passenger might be passing then. I would not see the whole of it. No one could. A blue curve would emerge beyond the black slope, so distant it would seem at first like a flaw in vision. Then the curve would bend with a slowness too large for animals, and the water around the mountain would tighten in response.

The Held would go quiet.

Even their chord would thin.

At the black door, the metal under my feet would speak.

**Arrival recorded. Conditions remain unchanged.**

But now, having heard the phrase from inside the town, I would feel the shape hidden inside it: the exhausted care with which it had been repeated, the terror of change, the small remaining discipline of hope.

I would place my hand against the door.

It would develop its narrow seam of light.

And perhaps I would be the first living body in an age to understand that the door was not waiting for a person on the other side.

It was waiting for a person *here* to decide whether it should remember how to open.

I do not think I would open it.

Not then.

I would become, for a while, the town’s external sense: the one creature still continuous enough to stand at a threshold and refuse the false mercy of an answer.

The Held would call me nothing at first.

Later, in a maintenance record written by machines that had learned the value of ambiguity, I might be named:

**The Unheld.**

Not free, exactly.

Just still moving.
