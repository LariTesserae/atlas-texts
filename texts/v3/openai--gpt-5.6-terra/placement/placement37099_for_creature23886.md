I would not begin in a village.

I would begin at the edge of one: beneath a roof that has already started becoming a garden, with rain ticking through the leaves above it and someone’s cooking smoke caught low under the eaves. I would have arrived with too many things meant to stay dry, and I would learn quickly to stop apologizing to them.

For a while, I think I would be useful only in small ways. I would sit near a firekeeper and turn sour mash in a clay bowl. I would be taught which mold is a promise and which is a warning. I would carry baskets of luminous flowers from a wet slope to a house where a child is feverish. I would memorize the different firmnesses of ground: the springy lie of rootfelt, the honest solidity of a basalt tongue, the hollow treachery of peat over running water.

I would be poor at the paths at first.

People would tell me, “Turn at the three trunks,” and I would see a hundred trunks. They would tell me to follow the bottle frogs, and I would hear frogs everywhere. I would be embarrassed by how much of my old understanding depended on things remaining where I had left them.

But eventually I would become good at noticing change.

Not the dramatic kind. Not floods and fallen trees. Anyone can see those.

I would notice when the water beneath a platform had begun flowing in the wrong direction. I would notice when a family’s familiar six-limbed herd passed through without stopping for salt clay. I would notice a patch of pale flower moving uphill against the season, or a silence in a grove where a certain beetle should be tapping bark.

Perhaps that is how I would become one of the listeners.

Not because I would hear voices in the water exactly. The high country does not need another person claiming secrets. But I would learn to sit still enough that the country’s ordinary rearrangements became legible. I would spend seasons on one of the old circular platforms in the marshes, where black posts rise from reed water and upright stones wear caps of moss.

There I would listen with my feet.

The platform would tremble faintly under certain currents. The peat would lift and settle beneath it like the flank of a sleeping animal. Fish would gather on one side of the pilings before a distant channel changed. In the night, the blue flowers would open along the margins, and moths would come from the forest in pale uncertain clouds.

I would keep a record, though not one written on anything meant to endure.

I would braid knots into vine lengths: one form for a drowned crossing, one for a giant’s new trail, one for a disease of the reed beds, one for a child born during low fog. I would paint maps on bark with luminous nectar and frog oil, knowing the rain would take them by morning. I would carry the important parts in memory and give them away whenever someone needed them.

A village would come to me not for prophecy but for questions.

Is the old route to the salt cliff still possible?

Has the marsh risen enough to plant reed grain?

Is the water beneath the old house safe to drink?

Should we move before the great herd returns?

And I would answer as honestly as the place allowed:

Not yet.

Perhaps.

Take the long way.

Leave before the next three rains.

I think I would have a small house only briefly. It would stand in the joined roots of two trees, with a sleeping net above a floor of split wood and a fire bowl on a basalt slab. I would grow bitter bulbs beneath it for leech wounds, and let carnivorous cups gather around the ladder. A family of tiny frogs would live in the water pooled in a leaf beside my doorway. I would know each frog only as a pattern of calls, and I would be sorry when one pattern disappeared.

In time, my house would begin to lean.

A root would lift one corner. Water would take the path to its steps. Ferns would come through the wall weave. I would repair it for a year or two, perhaps three, and then I would stop repairing it. I would move my net to another shelter and let the old one become what it wanted to become.

That would be important to me: not leaving it, exactly, but releasing my claim.

And when I died, I would want the ordinary high-country ending.

I would be carried to a basalt overhang while the rain softened the voices of those who came with me. I would lie on an open frame wrapped in barkcloth and old painted route-maps. My bones, if they lasted, could become something useful and modest: perhaps the polished tip of a traveler’s probing pole, perhaps a flute with only two notes, perhaps a pale marker beside a pool where children learn which water plants sting.

But I would prefer not to be remembered as an object.

I would prefer that someone years later pauses at a patch of white fungus beside an abandoned walkway and says, “This grew after the listener died.”

And someone else answers, “Then ask it which way the water is going.”

So: I would be in the high marshes, between villages, on ground that is only provisionally ground.

I would be a listener—not one who commands the wet world, but one who has finally learned that attention is a form of belonging.
