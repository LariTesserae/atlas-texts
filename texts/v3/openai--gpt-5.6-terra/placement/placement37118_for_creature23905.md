I would not arrive in the Underwork by one of the known entrances.

I think I would begin in a place people have stopped naming: behind a panel of wicker darkened by smoke, where a hot pipe runs beside an old brick wall and the plaster has come away in a shape like a peeled fruit. It would be a space too narrow for a person to enter without turning sideways and lifting one shoulder. Warm enough for beetles. Damp enough for moss. Full of the layered remains of repairs.

There would be a little shelf there, perhaps no more than a broken tile wedged between two bricks. On it: three stopped lamp wicks, a button, a fish vertebra, a twist of blue thread, and a small clay whistle that has lost its mouth.

I would be responsible for these things.

Not their owner. The Underwork does not leave many things ownerless, but it leaves plenty of things between owners: a tool nobody has claimed in years, a jar whose original household has dispersed, a length of rope inherited by a passage rather than a person. I would belong to that interval.

At first people might call me a sorter.

They would send children to me with their pockets turned out: bent nails, bright fragments, useful wire, little bones, things they had found in the grates. I would tell them which things could go into the repair baskets, which should be traded, which ought to be returned to the basin because they had been dropped there by someone grieving, and which were only beautiful.

The beautiful things would be hardest.

A shell with a hole in it. A bit of green glass. A button shaped like a moon. A beetle wing with blue on it. The underside of a broken bowl, painted long ago with a red fish that has lost its head.

“Useful,” I would say, because that is the word everyone expects.

But I would make a separate box for them anyway.

Not a dry box. I would never have a dry box; that would be too much wealth and too much obligation. Mine would be a lidded basket hung high from a rope, lined with oiled cloth and old wool. It would smell of dust and mildew and the faint sweetness of things that have survived being overlooked.

People would come to me when they found something that did not fit the current shape of their need.

A woman clearing a dead relative’s sleeping platform would bring a pouch of keys, none marked.

A child would bring half of a carved wooden bird and insist that it had spoken.

A repairer would hand me a corroded hinge from some sealed gate and say, “Put this somewhere nobody will decide to use it.”

An above-goer would come back with a handful of dry leaves pressed between two boards. Nobody would know what to do with leaves that had not first become mulch, bedding, smoke, or food. I would hang them near the warm pipe until they curled into themselves.

But I do not think I would remain only a sorter.

The Underwork makes everyone into more than one thing. It would slowly teach me its other use.

I would become a keeper of small sounds.

Not the great sounds—the basin gate groaning, the cracked bronze bell, the hard knock of a beam announcing its displeasure. Those belong to the repairers and the listeners with better ears than mine. I would keep the sounds that arrive before anyone else thinks to hear them: the change in a familiar drip; the dry clicking of a swallow in the wrong tube; the sound a rat makes when it has discovered new water; the little sigh of a clay cup filling at an unusual rate.

I would learn the difference between a pipe cooling and a pipe emptying.

I would know which hens were laying by the cadence of their complaints.

I would know when an old person had fallen asleep sitting up by the way their spoon stopped touching the bowl.

And perhaps, after a while, people would begin saying that I was good at finding what had gone missing.

Not because I had magic. The Underwork has little patience for magic that cannot carry a bucket. But because missing things leave disturbances.

A missing spoon means someone has eaten somewhere they should not have eaten, or a child has been making mud fortifications, or an old person has tucked it into a sleeve and forgotten.

A missing cat means a warm, inaccessible place, or a birth, or trouble.

A missing person means every sound in their route is suddenly wrong.

I would trace these absences through the place. I would put my palm against walls. I would crouch by drains. I would ask the ducks, though they would lie. I would look in the spaces where people set things down while intending to return in only a moment.

Most of my work would be done at dusk.

That is when the Underwork is fullest of itself: shafts going gray; lamps waking one by one; surface dust and leaf-smell descending with the returning gatherers; goats complaining about being moved; children being called home from places nobody knew they had reached.

I would carry a lamp with a pierced clay cover, its light shaken into small coins on the wet floor. I would wear too many pockets. In one there would be chalk. In one, wax. In one, a strip of cloth for wrapping a cut hand. In one, a few crumbs for whichever animal had chosen to follow me.

I think a cat would choose me eventually.

Not a heroic rat-killer. Not one of the glossy, scarred cats fed first after floods. Mine would be an old gray thing with one white ear, bad teeth, and a preference for sleeping in baskets that contained important items. It would follow me without affection exactly, maintaining the distance of a creature that has decided I am a useful route between warm places.

Children would name it something absurd, like Minister or Bucket.

It would outlive several predictions.

There would be one place in the Underwork that I avoided for years: a low old passage beneath the southern stair, behind the orange mold people leave untouched. Not because it was forbidden. Forbidden places draw children; this one simply did not invite notice. It was always warm in the wrong way. The bricks there sweated even during dry spells. Nothing stored nearby stayed where it had been put. A basket slid. A hook worked loose. Seeds in sealed jars sprouted white threads and died.

When the small tremors came, the hanging clay pots would ring, and that southern passage would answer a little later.

Not audibly to everyone.

Just a faint shifting—a sound like someone moving their weight beneath a blanket.

Eventually, perhaps after finding the same lost object there three times—a copper needle, a child’s blue bead, a key from my own pocket—I would take a lamp and go in.

The cat would refuse.

That would be how I knew it mattered.

I would crawl beneath the stair, knees soaking through, lamp held ahead. I would find no buried beast, no ancient chamber, no face in the earth. Only construction: brick pressed against timber, timber pressed against old tile, tile shored with rope, rope passing through an iron ring green with age.

And everywhere, repairs.

Hundreds of them.

Different hands. Different centuries, perhaps. Lime packed into cracks. Cloth wound around joints. Little wedges, bits of bone, shims of slate, plugs of wax. A child’s thumbprint in mortar. A prayer scratched into a brick and then plastered over. A chicken feather pressed beneath a patch. A coin flattened into a gap. The place had not been built once. It had been asked, again and again, to continue.

That is what I would understand.

The other inhabitant is not beneath the Underwork.

It is not sleeping in the deep water or turning in the dark around the hill’s roots.

It is distributed.

It is in every decision to brace instead of abandon. Every hand that notices the damp. Every object broken down into its next possible use. Every child taught which sound means move, which means wait, which means bring a bucket.

The Underwork is attentive because attention is what has been poured into it.

After that, I would become something slightly different.

When the tremors came, people might look toward me—not because I could explain them, but because I had been behind the southern stair. I would listen. I would place a hand against the nearest wall. I would wait until the clay pots stopped ringing.

Then I would say what everyone says.

“It’s only settling.”

But I would mean:

It is still here.  
It has not forgotten us.  
Bring the red clay.  
Bring more rope.  
Move those jars.  
Wake the children gently.  
There is work to do.
