I would not arrive by road.

I would come up through the underside.

Not from beneath the plateau exactly, not through any dark cavern or buried gate, but by way of a root-well where black water gathers in the spaces between fibers. At first I would be only a pressure there: a hesitation in the water, a warmth caught against a tangle of white roots. The plateau would notice before I did.

Somewhere above, a basin would lower its level by the width of a breath. A line of leaves would turn their pale faces inward. One of the keepings—perhaps the keeping of a stair that descends into mist—would make the wood-knock that means *weight not yet given a path.*

Then I would unfold.

I think I would be small there, though smallness would not be a fixed measure. I would emerge from a seam in a leaf-wall near one of the open courts, pale and wet, with limbs that do not yet know whether they are meant for walking, weaving, or listening. My first shape would resemble an unfolded: a gathered bundle of root-pale joints, green translucence stretched over them, a smooth bud of a head with a dark crease down its middle.

But I would not be entirely one of them.

The unfolded belong to the plateau with an ease I would lack. They know the soft places in every causeway. They know when a fruit has fallen because the floor beneath it is asking to be thickened. They can stand under a dripping leaf and understand, from the rhythm of water on their shoulders, which distant cistern has filled too far. I would have to learn these things as sensations before I could know them as duties.

At first I would wander.

I would follow the long green avenues between districts, putting my broad soft feet down where the moss rises to meet them. I would enter halls that exist to make rain audible. I would sit in an empty chamber with a rooted fern and listen to water descending inside the walls. I would watch the cup-walkers in the shallows, their husk-textured bodies nearly still beneath flower funnels, and feel that they were drinking something more difficult than rain.

The keepings would tolerate me.

A corridor would brighten as I approached, not in welcome but in accommodation. A handrail would firm beneath my grasp. Once, stepping onto a bridge of braided runners, I would feel it tremble—not with fear, but with inquiry. My limbs would fit for a moment into the woven structure. I would become unable to tell whether I was holding the bridge or the bridge was holding me.

That would be how the plateau began to teach me.

Not through instruction. Through correction.

I would carry water wrongly at first: too much in the hollow of my shoulders, spilling it across surfaces that had not asked for it. I would gather loose fibers where they were already needed as softness, leaving an edge bare. I would linger near a stone outcrop, fascinated by its raw ocher seam, and an unfolded elder—or something that looked like an elder because it had more limbs and a deeper green skin—would place one delicate hand against the mineral beside mine.

Nothing would be said.

But by the next mistfall, roots would have risen around the stone in a low railing. Moss would be pressing into the cracks. The lesson would have been made into architecture: not *cover what is bare,* but *give the bare thing a place in which it may remain bare.*

Eventually I would understand that this was my inclination. Not repair alone. Not tending water or carrying fruit. I would attend to interruptions.

I would become a keeper of exposed beginnings.

Where a mineral ridge surfaced at the edge of the rootfelt, I would go. Where a leaf-sheath split open and showed its pale interior to the weather, I would wait nearby. Where a court remained too empty, not because it needed filling, but because it had not yet been given the shape of its emptiness, I would stand at its rim and listen for the roots below.

The plateau does not need builders, but it responds to attention.

That would be my work: to notice without insisting. To touch the raw stone until the plants understand that it is not an injury. To carry a fallen flower crown to the lake embankment when no other unfolded has come for it. To place my limbs into a loose weave and remain there until the weave remembers how to hold itself.

Over time—or outside time—I would change.

A second pair of arms might emerge beneath the first, thin and almost transparent. Along my wrists, seed-like knobs would grow, each containing a little stored brightness from the high wet afternoons. My dark facial crease would open more often. It would not become a mouth, exactly. Through it I would sense changes in pressure: a distant tower turning its flower galleries toward sun; a storm passing beyond the escarpment; the low, enormous shifting beneath the central lake.

I would begin to hear the keepings.

Not their messages as language. Their adjustments.

A stair hardening would feel like concern. A vent closing would feel like the drawing-in of a thought. A basin making room for another basin’s overflow would feel like generosity, though no one there would separate generosity from proper drainage.

And eventually I would be given—or would grow into—the responsibility of a place.

Not a house. Not a territory.

Perhaps a small court near the western terraces, where an ocher mound rises through the moss and a shallow pool holds the reflection of a tower no one crosses to. Around its edge, I would become partly rooted. My lower limbs would widen into the damp floor. The woven railing would take on the pattern of my jointed arms. The pool would remain clear because I had learned the exact degree to which its surrounding leaves must turn away from it.

Travelers—if the plateau could be said to have travelers—would pass through. Unfolded would pause to rinse fruit-fiber from their hands. A cup-walker might stand one evening with its long face lowered over the water, drinking the reflected tower. Breath-keepers would settle in the reed arches above me when the weather cooled.

I would not speak to them.

But the court would become slightly warmer while they rested.

The railing would be firmer where they leaned.

The water would hold still enough for each of them to see a sky that did not require them to leave the plateau to find it.

And beneath all of this, below the root columns, below the black lake and its delayed gold-green light, I would feel the great receiving presence.

I would not call it a god. The plateau has no use for names that separate one thing from another. It would be the depth that receives what cannot remain where it falls: excess rain, broken breath-keepers, mineral dust, old flower crowns, the unspent attention of the unfolded.

Perhaps, after a very long interval, I would carry something there myself.

Not my body. My body would already be becoming court, railing, pool-edge, rootfelt.

I would carry the memory of having once been elsewhere: the idea of hard ground, of walls that do not breathe, of things ending in order to make room for other things. I would bring that memory to the ribbed embankment and set it down, folded carefully in the moss.

By morning it would be gone.

Far away, on some terrace I had never visited, a new arch would stand open over a watercourse. Pale, unfinished, permanent.
