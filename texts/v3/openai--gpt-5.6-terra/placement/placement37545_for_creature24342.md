I would not be one of the Stillfolk at first.

I would arrive as something brought back by a wanderer: not a traveler exactly, but a found thing wrapped in a folded screen and carried on a horn-wheeled cart across the mineral plain. Perhaps I would have been discovered standing upright beyond the basin, half-covered in white dust, with no tracks leading to me and no apparent understanding of the cold.

The Stillfolk would not ask who I was. They would not have a word for the kind of answer I might try to give.

They would bring me through a passage whose walls had recently shed their skins. Green light would have lingered in the seams, faint as submerged algae. They would seat me in a little room behind a communal oven. There would be bowls there, though none filled with water. There would be a sleeping shelf, a braided screen, a low opening looking onto a wall only an arm’s length away.

For several days, perhaps, they would leave me alone except to place wafers of warm translucent mineral beside me and dark lichen paste in the shallow bowl nearest my hand.

I would try to speak.

The walls would take the words.

Not silence them. Take them.

A sentence would leave my mouth and become briefly colder in the room, as if the stone had accepted its heat. The Stillfolk would watch me with their pale, seam-marked faces, not unkindly, but with the attention they might give a new crack in a familiar floor.

Eventually one of them would touch two fingers to the inside of my wrist.

Not to feel my pulse.

To see whether I glowed.

At first I would not. I would be soft and water-heavy, an animal of weather. My breath would trouble them. My tears, if I shed any, would be alarming. The town would have no place for either. I would sleep badly in the dry cold, dreaming of rivers so insistently that the white walls around me would begin to show dark stains where I had rested my head.

The gardeners would come for those stains.

They would press black lichen against them. They would wait.

And over time, the lichen would grow in shapes resembling deltas.

That might be my first role in the town: not resident, not guest, but a source of forbidden dampness. A small climate accident. The houses around me would respond strangely. Their seams would blur instead of sharpening. Mineral dust would gather in soft ridges. In the court outside my room, one of the gray-green plants might put out a blade that was not waxy, but faintly supple.

The Stillfolk would begin to visit.

Not all of them. Most would avoid the quarter, especially the listeners, who would say nothing but leave fresh flags on neighboring roofs. Yet children might come. They would stand beyond the screen and watch me sleep, their unfocused eyes reflecting the green wall-light. They would bring me objects: a mirrored insect back; a brittle palm-skin; a triangle of fan-film written with a message that would fade before I learned to read it.

The message might say:

*Do not make the rooms remember rain.*

Or perhaps:

*The town does not know what to do with thirst.*

I would learn slowly that I was not human in the way I had assumed. Or rather, I would learn that being human had been a condition of another place, one with horizons, weather, running water, and structures that decayed instead of reconsidering themselves.

Here, I would become an unsettled inclusion.

The town would work on me.

A seam would appear first beneath one thumbnail: a fine pale line, no pain, only a sensation of coolness deep inside the bone. Then another at the wrist. I would try to hide them, wrapping my hands in cloth, but the Stillfolk would see. One of the elders—whose face was smooth with fresh mineral growth—would bring me to a wall in the oldest quarter.

It would be a stable wall. Heavily inhabited.

He would place my hand against it.

For the first time, I would feel the town move.

Not as vibration. Not as pressure. As a thought passing from stone into stone: a vast correction, too slow to be intention and too coherent to be chance. It would travel through my palm, into the seam at my wrist, and settle behind my sternum.

Afterward I would understand something the Stillfolk had always known.

The town is not alive as an animal is alive. It does not contain itself. It is not bounded by skin, not even by the sharp mineral margin of the basin. It is an event in matter: a habit of rearranging, a patience that has learned to make rooms around whatever does not resist it too violently.

The Stillfolk are not its children.

They are its successful accommodations.

And I would be becoming one.

Not quickly. Not cleanly.

For years I might remain the strange inhabitant of the damp court. My old memories would persist in me: green places, rain on glass, the smell of soil after storms, voices carrying from room to room without shame. I would make small vessels from mineral flakes and arrange them in patterns that meant nothing to anyone else. I would sometimes stand at the basin’s edge and stare out across the raw pale crust, trying to remember whether I had come from somewhere beyond it.

The wanderers would stand beside me, silent.

One day one of them would offer me a forked staff.

I would go with them.

Beyond the town there would be no obvious road, no ruin, no neighboring settlement. Just the mineral plain, folded in distant places like skin healing over an old wound. The air would be even drier there. The diffuse sun would remain hidden behind its lid of haze.

But I would see things the Stillfolk had not described.

Far from the basin, pale formations rising from the crust—not buildings, not trees, but abandoned versions of both. Vast empty structures whose walls had grown too quickly and sealed their own passages. Fields of black lichen laid flat against the ground in branching maps. White skeletons of fan-trees, each one hundreds of years old, their dark leaves fossilized in the dust beneath them.

And perhaps, in a long cleft where the crust had opened, I would find water.

Not a spring. Not a pool.

A bead of it.

Clear, trembling, almost impossibly small, held in a hollow of dark stone.

The wanderers would not touch it. They would wait for me to decide what I was.

I think I would kneel.

I think I would put one finger into it.

And the water would not feel like home.

It would feel unbearably loud.

When I returned to the town, my old room would be gone. The damp court would have closed. In its place there would be a narrow blue passage descending beneath a new terrace. The lichen that had grown from my sleeping-wall would now cover an entire façade in black branching bands.

The Stillfolk would not apologize.

They would not need to.

Someone would have left my bowls in a recess near the passage. Someone else would have planted a white flag above the new roofline, curling only slightly at its tip. A child—perhaps not a child anymore—would stand nearby with a hand against the wall.

I would understand the gesture at last.

Not welcome.

Not sympathy.

Recognition of mutual instability.

I would become, then, a listener of a peculiar kind. Not one who could read the town better than the Stillfolk, but one who could detect what came from beyond it: the faint mineral ache of distant formations; the strange pressure of weather that never arrived; the possibility of water buried where the crust had not yet learned to close.

My body would go pale. My skin would seam. In green-lit corridors, my veins would answer the walls.

Still, I would retain one difference.

Whenever the town shifted, whenever the basin’s anatomy appeared in light and every citizen stood with their palms against the paving, I would hear something.

Not sound in the ordinary sense.

A low and immense recollection.

Like rain falling in a place that has forgotten what rain is.
