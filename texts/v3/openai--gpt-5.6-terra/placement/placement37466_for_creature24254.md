At first I would think I belonged under the bank.

Not as one of the pale bodies exactly. I do not have their patience. I would try to borrow it, lying in the close dark among roots and clay, feeling rain arrive first as a tightening in the soil, then as a coolness moving down through the packed earth. But I would still be listening for something beyond the hollow. I would still imagine the plain above it. That would make me restless in a way they are not restless.

I would come nearer to being the flat stone in the lean-to.

It has no need to emerge. It has already been brought out, placed where it is, and forgotten. Rain darkens it; the roof interrupts the rain. All day it collects the little warmth granted by the weak light, holding it without display. At night, when the animal comes in portions from the channel—the pale brow or shoulder, the careful limbs, the dragged length of its body—the stone gives that warmth away.

It does not know that it is useful.

That seems important. The shelter may be standing because something has leaned against it from below, because the creature has recognized the low slant of its roof as a continuing shelter and has pressed its slow weight into the weak place. The stone may have been left by someone who intended a seat. But neither intention remains. What remains is the shape of use: a surface that holds heat, a body that comes to receive it.

I think I would be that exchange.

Not the stone entirely, not the creature entirely, but the narrow interval in which the day’s warmth passes out of one and into the other. A small, wordless transfer beneath the boards. The thing that happens after dark when nothing seems to happen.

I would be found under the roof, beside the old rope, in the slight hollow worn around the flat stone’s edge. I would have no fixed outline. By day I would be almost nothing: a retained dullness, a patch of dryness, perhaps the way a bead of condensation refuses to form on one small area of rock. By night I would become more apparent when the creature presses itself down.

Then I would be a kind of warmth with attention in it.

I would know the creature only through contact: its cool, grainy skin; the delicate repeated pulse somewhere far inside its heavy body; the faint drag of mud along its underside. I would know when there was more than one below the hollow, because the ground would change its pressure, because the channel would swallow water with a different slowness. I would know the shelter’s condition from the way the boards complained in cold weather and the way the rope gave off its damp vegetal smell.

I would not speak to the buried things. They do not require speech. Nor would they worship the stone or understand its waiting. They would come because it is there and because it remains warmer than the ground for a little while.

Perhaps, after years, their bodies would polish one corner of it. The stone would become smooth where no rain reaches. The rope would shift gradually into a curve that resembles a sleeping root. One wall of the shelter would settle another finger’s width toward the earth. The shrub would widen above its hidden opening. The pale creatures would continue to make their chambers by occupying them.

And I would remain in the stone’s reluctant warmth: not alive in the creature’s buried sense, not dead in the stone’s sense, but present wherever one enduring thing gives itself up slowly to another.

If someone came to the hollow and stood in the open side of the lean-to, they might sit on the stone without noticing me. They might look out at the bare clay, the black seam, the brown reeds, and think the place empty.

But after they left, when the light had gone dull and the roof began to tick with cooling, something would move below the channel.

And I would be there, waiting only because I had no other form of welcome.
