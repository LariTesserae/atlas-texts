I would not arrive as a visitor.

A visitor brings a lantern, asks whether the ground is safe, wonders how far the chamber goes. A visitor keeps one part of the mind aboveground, among gates and weather and the memory of a road.

I think I would be found already inside, in the portion of the hollow that cannot be pointed to.

Not the keeper, exactly. Not the frog. Not the old spider in its inherited crack. I would be the thing that has learned their intervals.

Perhaps I would begin as a small practical life: the keeper’s second set of hands, if he had ever had them. The person who comes after him or before him, carrying the rope when his shoulders grow bad, holding the lantern low while he lays a palm to the wall. I would know how to mend the roof with slabs that fit by weight rather than shape. I would know which shelf of stone remains dry when the noise goes hoarse, and which food must be hung from wire because the damp has its own appetite.

I would have no name that holds outside.

Aboveground, someone might call me the keeper’s daughter, though I might be older than he is. Or the boy from the eastern farms, gone strange after a flood. Or the woman who lives in the roaring hole. They would be right enough, in the loose way people are right about things they prefer not to inspect.

But I do not think I would live there merely to guard it.

I would be there because the noise has made a room in me.

At first I would hate it. The sound would enter through bone. It would spoil sleep and take apart thought. I would keep expecting a pause—the merciful gap in which I could hear the scrape of my own sleeve or the blood behind my ears. Yet the water would not pause. It would go on declaring something too large to understand.

Then, after weeks or years, I would cease to listen for silence.

I would learn that the roar has textures. I would wake at the change from hollow bellow to iron knock. I would know the difference between floodwater and a stone newly freed in the buried throat. I would feel rain not on my face but in the trembling of the cup on the shelf, in the quiver of the roots, in the way the spider gathers one leg closer to itself.

I would become very good at small acts.

A candle replaced before the last one gutters.
A stone shifted under a sagging roof-slab.
A strip of old blanket dried near the entrance and folded away before it takes on too much wet.
Three stones around a frog’s pool.
A chalk line made high and deliberate, though there is no one to read it but me.

And the hollow would alter me in the ways it alters all things without ever appearing to act. My clothes would smell of clay and old smoke. My hair would keep a mineral damp even in sun. In a kitchen, people would speak to me and I would watch their mouths, smiling too soon or too late. Ordinary quiet would seem thin and accusatory. I would sleep badly in houses. The absence of the under-river would feel like the absence of a body.

Eventually, I might stop going out except to fetch salt, oatcakes, lamp oil, thread.

But I would still go.

I would walk the pasture paths after rain with the lantern unlit in my hand. Sheep would raise their heads and forget me. At the drainage ditch I would wash mud from my boots. I would stand by the old walls where the limestone shows through turf, and I would feel the hollow beneath the field—not as a location, but as a pressure in the land’s memory.

There would be seasons when I am not there.

The shelter door would swell shut. Moss would blur my tracks. Someone might kneel at the bramble fold and see no sign of a life below. Then, in late autumn, smoke would creep out low across the grass. The woodpile would show fresh cuts. A new chalk mark would appear on the inner wall, shaped like a hooked branch or a bird’s foot.

Perhaps that mark would be mine.

Or perhaps I would by then be something less solid: not a person remaining in the chamber, but the faint readiness with which the place receives one. The hand that notices when the rope has frayed. The attention that hears a foot slip near the black slope. The impulse in a stranger to stop, suddenly and without knowing why, before taking one more step.

I would be the hollow’s second noise.

Not loud. Not ancient. Nothing that could be heard over the water.

Only the small persistence of care inside it.
