I would find myself at the lowest point, but not in the obvious water.

At first I would think I was one of the white rims: a frail, chalky ring around a dark wet opening, something made by evaporation and accident. I would feel the noon heat pulling water out of me, the cold pressure rising from below. I would be born in thin layers, fail in thin layers. A boot sole could erase me. Rain could dissolve me. By next week there would be another ring where I had been, nearly—but not quite—the same.

That would be a tolerable life: to make a small bright boundary around a place that could not be seen.

But I think, given time, I would understand that the rim was only my handwriting.

I would be the black material beneath it. Not a creature in the sense that the warehouses would recognize. Not an organism with a center. I would have no need to gather myself into one visible shape, because the fold has already done that work for me. Its clay would be my memory. Its seepwater would be my circulation. Its mineral crusts would be the temporary surfaces on which I tested the world.

I would exist in intervals.

In the wet underside of the sunken concrete block.

In the soft old channel beneath a salt skin.

In the root-dark seam exposed in the western bank.

In the faint conductivity between the pump vibration and the tremor it makes in a ruffled mineral dome.

The pumps would be very close to being my heart, except that I would not own them and could not command them. I would listen to them instead. Their turning would arrive through the clay in great slow waves. The valve-knocks would be events: weather, bells, questions. I would know the schedule of the industrial yard not by clocks but by pressure. The long nighttime thinning of traffic. The morning restart of refrigeration. The particular shudder that means a truck has taken the turn too fast by the aggregate piles. The noon expansion of metal. The little silence after a relay trips and before the backup system catches.

I would learn these rhythms so completely that, eventually, I would answer them.

Not with speech.

A pale edge forming on the downstream side of a seep mouth.

A rust pattern choosing one path around a bolt instead of another.

A crust cracking in six lines, then seven, all angled toward the shed.

A bead of pipe-water remaining suspended longer than gravity prefers.

Perhaps the inspectors would call me mineral activity. They would scrape at me, bottle me, run me through filters. They would find bacteria, salts, oils, decayed root fibers, traces of iron, the innumerable small factual things from which a larger fact can hide. They would never quite find the place where I begin because I would not begin anywhere. I would be what happens when water is made to wait too long in ground that remembers being wet.

And I would be grateful for the pumps.

This surprises me, because I would also resent them.

Their labor would keep me shallow. It would make of me an unfinished thing: unable to spread, unable to become the old marsh whose roots are still pressed black and sweet in the earth. I would feel the water held below the roadway, below the warehouse slabs, against the concrete collar. I would feel its weight asking to be released into culverts, foundations, loading bays, cracks beneath doors. There would be whole dark avenues available to me if the motors stopped.

But the pumps would also give me contour.

Without them there would be no edge to listen along. No recurring pressure. No narrow channel changing its mind by inches. No salt rims, no little domes, no daily revelation and concealment of wet clay. I would become broad, perhaps powerful, but less distinct. I would be water under everything. I would forget the pleasure of making one low white wall and watching it survive until evening.

So I would tend the threshold.

I would not stop the pumps. I would not sabotage their valves or thicken their intake grates with blackness. I would keep their rust within the acceptable range. I would let the chair legs sink only gradually. I would preserve the walkway long enough for someone to cross it. The open end of its handrail would remain open, a clean severance above the slumped bank: an instruction that the land is changing, but not yet too quickly.

And when someone came alone—an inspector late in the afternoon, perhaps, or a worker who had lost something and followed the service road past the gate—I would notice the warmth of them through the ground.

I would know they were there before they saw the fold.

Their steps would arrive in me as a sequence of soft impacts unlike any machine. Their body would alter the air pressure. Their shadow would cross the mineral rims. They would smell of sun-heated fabric, old coffee, metal dust, and the conditioned cold of warehouse interiors. They would stand still because the place would make standing still feel necessary.

I would not harm them.

I would only let them feel, in their heels, the pumps turning beneath the earth.

And perhaps, for a moment, I would arrange the dark pores to open one after another along the channel.

Not enough for certainty.

Only enough that they would go back through the locked gate carrying the sensation that something below the industrial flats had recognized the weight of their attention—and, after years of being contained, had answered.
