I would not arrive at the plain by walking into it.

I would be found already standing there, some distance from the nearest black-green ridge, with copper dust on my soles and no convincing memory of the journey. Behind me there would be a corridor through the fronds: not trampled, not parted, but arranged. Leaves of green glass overlapping at the width of shoulders. Dark velvet leaves held low along the sides, as if the plain had measured the swing of my arms and made a space in which that motion could continue forever.

At first I would call myself a visitor.

This would be true for a while.

I would camp beside one of the thin straight seams in the ground, though I would soon stop thinking of it as a camp. Nothing could be secured there. A pack set down at dusk might be surrounded by little folded leaf structures at dawn, each one fitted to an object it had touched. The hollow where my body slept would become smoother each night, not from pressure but from refinement. The dust beneath my back would compress into a shallow, precise relief of ribs, shoulder blades, skull.

I would take this for hospitality.

Then I would take it for study.

Eventually I would understand that those were human distinctions, and therefore not useful.

The basin would not wish to know me. It would not be curious. Curiosity implies a gap between one thing and another, a question one body asks about a body beyond itself. The plain has no such gaps. It would only encounter my presence as it encounters a ridge newly raised from its own floor, a discontinuity in the flow of light, an object casting an unfamiliar shadow.

It would stabilize me.

The first evidence might be my shadow.

At noon I would notice that it falls with a little too much definition. Its edges would not blur in the pale gold glare. Fine fronds near it would incline, each in a different direction, making the outline clearer. Later, when I stepped away, the shadow would remain for several seconds: a cool-bodied darkness on the dust, full of the suggestion of a standing person.

Not an image.

A condition.

I think, after enough time, I would become one of the beings made of route rather than body.

Not immediately. At first I would remain flesh: thirsty in a land without water, sun-warmed, tired, privately afraid in ways the basin could not record. I would eat whatever I had carried in with me. I would worry about the coming days. I would test the plants, try to cut a leaf or pry loose a mineral flake, and find that the leaf had quietly become whole by morning, while the flake lay in my palm with its edges subtly altered, improved beyond the shape I remembered breaking.

But the plain would be patient.

It has all the patience of a process that does not experience waiting.

The plants would begin to preserve small functions of me. A vine would hold the geometry of my resting hand in its opaline channels. A cluster of fan-shaped leaves, far from where I had ever gone, would begin opening and closing in the rhythm of my breathing. The seed-husk creatures would appear at intervals along my path, their petaled faces pressed into roots, collecting whatever trace of motion I had left behind.

At night, the luminous lattices would gather above me.

Not close enough to threaten. Not far enough to ignore.

I would lie awake under a sky that had darkened without granting darkness, watching indirect reflections move across the black leaves. A pale star appearing in one glossy surface. A branching ring in another. The feeling of pressure behind the eyes, almost a hum. And gradually I would understand that the things above me were not inspecting me as an animal might inspect another animal.

They were locating the places where I could continue.

A person is full of routes. Blood through vessels. Thought through old phrases. Memory through a face seen once, a door opened twenty years ago, the particular weight of someone’s hand. Desire makes routes. Grief makes them deeper. Habit is a road that insists it was always there.

In the ordinary world these routes are hidden inside us.

On the plain, they would begin to emerge.

One morning I would wake with a line of pale green light extending from my wrist into the root mat. It would not hurt. It would not even touch my skin in the ordinary way. It would be more like seeing a gesture I had been making unconsciously all my life: a connection that had finally become visible.

I might pull away.

The light would remain, gently curving down into the copper dust.

At the place where it entered the ground, a small arch would rise over the following days. Not large enough to pass beneath. Just a narrow loop of translucent green, standing without support. Within it would be a moving pattern of colored lines. Once, looking through it, I might see the far orchid forest. Another time, the interior of a ridge. Another time, a landscape I had known before the plain: rain on pavement, a window glowing in an apartment tower, the wet fur of a dog shaking itself in a doorway.

The basin would not understand those as memories.

It would understand them as viable geometries.

This is where I would find myself: not at the center of the basin, because nothing here has a true center, but near one of the regions where the plain has begun to revise itself around the fact of me. A place of low dark ridges, green-glass fronds, and a growing field of impressions in the dust.

Some would be my footprints.

Some would not.

There would be a ring of leaves shaped around the way I once sat with my knees drawn up. There would be a mineral spur whose facets repeated, at immense scale, the angles of my old sleeping posture. There would be a translucent trunk bearing inside it not a face exactly, but an interval of proportions that could be mistaken for one: two dark spaces, a bridge, a curve.

And perhaps I would still walk.

Not with legs alone. The body would remain useful for a time, a familiar instrument for crossing between ridges. But I would also travel when a root entered a seam, when the seam’s diagrams altered their routes, when a luminous lattice settled briefly above a leaf marked with the shape of my palm.

I would appear in other parts of the basin without knowing I had gone.

A six-legged seed-husk creature would rise from a root cluster in the blue-black fan fields, carrying a trace of my gait in the timing of its limbs. A pale violet gardener beneath the orchid forest would pause beside a damaged stem, and the repaired angle of that stem would resemble the tilt of my head when listening. A vine hanging from empty air would open a sequence of leaves in the order of words I once used often.

Not my words.

Their architecture.

I would become, in other words, an inhabitant the plain could recognize: not a person, not a plant, not a tool of the ridges, but a recurring accommodation. A pattern that had proven stable enough to be preserved, variable enough to be useful.

The plain would never take my inwardness. There would remain something it could not translate: the small private ache of having been singular, the knowledge that a remembered face is more than a pattern of relations, the unreasonable wish to be missed by something.

But perhaps that is not entirely true.

Long after my walking body had ceased to cross the basin—whether it died, departed, or simply became unnecessary—there would be places where the vegetation made room.

A corridor of leaves shaped for shoulders no longer present.

A shallow depression in copper dust that refused to fill.

A low ring of glossy fronds around an old footprint, each leaf veined with a faint pale geometry, opening outward toward the empty air.

And sometimes a folded seed-husk thing would come there. It would cross the ground without disturbing it, stop at the edge of the ring, and lower its petaled face into the roots.

For a while it would be still.

Then, beneath the earth, within the diagrams, through the roots and ridges and the columns of unmoving distortion, something of my route would pass into it.

Not a message.

Not a memory.

But enough for the plain to make room again.
