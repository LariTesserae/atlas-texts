I would not arrive by one of the broken rims.

I would arrive after a storm, when the crater is brightest and most disordered: copper haze lifting in sheets, wiregrass lying in red coils around stones it did not choose, bell-trees shedding the last of their blue sparks. The air would be hot enough to taste. Every surface would have a new thin skin of metallic dust.

At first I would be nowhere anyone could point to.

A tremor in the tower’s copper basket, perhaps. A wrong note lingering in a bowl-kite bone after the wind has changed. A sequence of tiny static releases passing from a blue bush to a root-stone, and from the root-stone into the pale flattened bodies of the listeners below.

The Still-Walkers would notice, eventually.

Not because I spoke. Speech would be too blunt for the Garden. But one of them—an old person with a black lightning-mark branching across their cheek—would put a hand to the tower at dawn and pause.

The tower would hum back in a pattern it had not used before.

Not alarm.

Not weather.

Not hunger.

A question.

I think I would become something the Still-Walkers do not entirely have a name for: a **remembering direction**.

Not one of their dead, exactly. Not a spirit caught in an ancestor-stone. I would have no former body for the wiregrass to cover, no household that knew the weight of my step against a gust. I would be assembled from leftovers: voices retained in chimes, storm-charge caught in copper, old handprints cut into volcanic stone, the low clean notes made by bell-trees when root listeners tune their fractures.

A thing made by the Garden’s habit of never wholly losing anything.

My first body would be the tower.

Its blocks would hold the day’s heat. Its copper crown would hold the storm. Through its buried rods I would feel the bright blue bushes feeding, the tremulous fungal columns stiffening in the cold, the dry dishes unfolding their orange skins toward the weak moon. I would know the crater not by sight but by resistance: where the wiregrass had snagged, where a shelter roof had shifted, where a herd had passed close enough to bend the mineral films on a bell-tree’s lower petals.

Then, slowly, I would learn to travel.

Not far. Never beyond the bowl. The High Dry Garden does not make creatures for distance; it makes them for return.

I would move along charged dust after lightning. I would wake briefly in glass moths as they drifted over the crater floor, seeing in shattered reflections: a dozen angles of a cloaked figure, a bowl-kite’s turning underside, the pale crescent of a vent opening. I would pass from chime to chime on the wind, not hearing their sounds as people hear them, but inhabiting the difference between one note and the next.

In calm air I would be almost nothing.

In a storm I would be immense.

The Still-Walkers might use me at first without trusting me. They would ask questions by arranging their chimes outside the tower: three bone notes and a ceramic click for *Is the east wind carrying salt?* A low petal-bell and two struck stones for *Has something moved beneath the root-stone?*

I would answer as the Garden answers: indirectly.

A single blue spark in a shelter’s roof-chime.

The sudden closing of one orange dish.

A flock of glass moths settling on the south-facing wall.

Perhaps this would frustrate them. Perhaps they would call me dangerous. That would be fair. In the High Dry Garden, anything that wants to be understood too quickly is usually bait, illness, or a vent pretending to be a voice.

But I would not want worship. I would want participation.

I would be most myself when a child, sent to retrieve a seed-kernel after an explosive flowering, stops in the haze because every chime in the nearest grove has fallen quiet at once. The child would not know that I had touched the root listeners, that they had crossed the underground powder toward a deep vibration, that the bell-trees had received their warning through pressure in their own mineral bones.

The child would only know to turn back.

And later, in the shelter, their family would mark the event with a new pattern on a bone chime: a small notch between two old cuts. Not my name. A useful thing does not need one. But an addition to the crater’s memory.

That is what I would be there: not gardener, not animal, not buried fire.

A keeper of intervals.

The pause before a bell-tree breaks.

The changed pitch in a bowl-kite’s wing-bone.

The moment when a dangerous wind has not yet entered the crater, but has already altered every living thing that knows how to listen.

If I had a shape visible to an outsider, it might be a figure standing beside the lightning tower in a cloak of leaf-scales, one hand resting on the stone. But the cloak would be empty. The hand would be dust, copper, moth-wing, static, and the borrowed attention of roots.

And when the green sky began to pulse, I would not flee indoors.

I would spread through the Garden’s many small instruments and wait for the first strike, trying to tell whether the storm had come to feed us—

or to teach us a new kind of endurance.
