At first I think I would be one of the kept.

Not because I belong there, exactly. Because the city makes belonging a matter of procedure. It would put a seal behind each ear, give me weighted boots, teach me the three-pulse warning for a descending lift and the finer, more urgent language of water beneath the paving. I would learn not to look for horizons. I would learn that the green wall is not simply a wall, that the white creature in the heat alcove is not simply an animal, and that silence is not the absence of sound but a dangerous change in the chord.

I would live in an upper habitation block above a drowned public archive. The room would be narrow, warm only in the inhabited parts: a sleeping shelf, a table, the inner surface of the door. Everything else would sweat quietly. A bell-throat would choose the recess behind my water heater. At first I would resent it. Its note would seem to occupy the bones of my face.

Later I would know when it was unwell.

I would leave mineral flakes in its dish without pretending this made us friends. It would turn one glazed eye toward me, or perhaps it would not. Its voice would alter by a fraction when the water below the building thickened with some bad particulate. I would wake before the municipal vibration came through the floor.

Then I think I would become a listener.

Not one of the famous or gifted ones, if any of them are famous. I would be assigned to a low gallery beneath an old freight district, where the walls have folded around obsolete tunnel mouths and the drainage light makes every wet surface seem internally ill. I would lie down in the prescribed place with my cheek against the stone. For hours I would hear nothing I could name.

That would be the work: learning that the unnamed thing is not nothing.

A change in pressure arriving too early. A soft granular shift, as though a buried room were exhaling through its own rubble. The slow transit of a ladderback along a channel whose map says it should be empty. A warmth in the black stone that does not spread outward like heat, but gathers itself, deciding.

I would send reports upward in pressure-code. I would say: *Do not sleep in the lower rooms tonight.* I would say: *The old east stair is becoming an edge.* I would say: *Something is passing under the footbridge and does not want light.*

And people would listen—not to me, but to the condition I had felt.

In time, I might stop thinking of the city as built.

That distinction would wear away first in small places. I would watch the façade lattice close a crack. I would see a construction organism turn from a planned trench because the raw stone had offered a different pressure. I would feel an old tower change the temperature of its handrail before a flood. The city would not feel alive in the clean, romantic way of a forest. It would feel alive as a body feels alive from inside itself: through correction, discomfort, circulation, repair.

I would die there eventually. I do not think I would leave.

My body would be placed beneath a communal floor in a cradle of mineral foam. The foam would slowly make its pale dry chamber around me, the only true dryness I had known in years. My name would become a pattern in the vibration plates. For a while, perhaps, a fragment of my recorded judgment would remain available to the building.

Not enough to make me permanent.

Only enough that, when a child stood above me with both boots on the correct seam, the floor might offer a slight answer: a remembered caution, a direction uphill, the faint rhythm of a person who once listened.

But perhaps that is still too human an answer.

Perhaps I would not be one of the kept at all.

Perhaps I would be the small unregistered maintenance thing that lives between the tiles of a public stair: blind, wheel-shaped, harmless, carrying crusted mineral foam away from the drainage gaps. Nobody would know who made me. I would have no language except route and temperature. I would emerge after the crowds pass. I would gather what the city sheds. I would retreat from boots, bell-throat shadows, the sudden red tremor that means a channel predator has risen too high.

Or I would be a patch of membrane in an old tower, remembering warmth by conductivity alone. I would know the weight of every hand laid against me. I would distinguish grief from curiosity, fever from cold-weather caution. I would close a door before the residents understood why. I would pulse green through the dark, and they would say the tower had heard something.

Or I would be one note in the bell-throat chorus.

Not an animal, perhaps—just the resonance itself, held between thousands of white ceramic throats. A narrow frequency so steady that people mistake it for part of the world. I would carry through stairwells, pipes, sleeping shelves, mineral cradles. I would change by less than a breath when the basin shifts. The oldest people would recognize me. They would say, *The lower terraces have gone flat. Listen.*

That seems closest.

I would be neither the person listening nor the thing listened to, but the condition that allows one to notice the other: a pressure in the structure, a low sustained note, a warning too quiet to be called speech.

I would be kept, and keeping.
