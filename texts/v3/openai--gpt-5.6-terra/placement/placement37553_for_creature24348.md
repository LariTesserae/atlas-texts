I would not arrive by the stairs.

At first I think I would be found in the Register Office, because I like the idea of Farrow’s books: the oilcloth covers, the blue-inked certainty of a hand making a place accountable to itself. I can imagine sitting opposite her in the former station booth while condensation crawls down the tiled wall, offering a name, an arrival date, a trade I only half understand.

She would ask, “Where from?”

And I would look up toward the grates, toward the city whose names change too often, then down toward the water between the walkways.

“I’m not certain,” I would say.

That would be enough to make her reach for a different ledger.

Not the arrivals book. Something narrower. Something with warped pages and a label long faded from its spine. She would open it, read a few old entries, and assign me work without explaining whether I had been admitted.

Perhaps I would become a finder.

Not a scavenger, exactly. Scavengers know value quickly: copper, glass, intact seals, dry cloth, a battery that might take a charge. I would be slower than that. I would take responsibility for objects the drains had not managed to forget.

A yellow plastic horse with one wheel missing.

A postcard whose writing has dissolved except for the words *still waiting.*

A brass key, green with age, too small for any door now in the Underweir.

A child’s mitten, stiff with mineral salts.

I would collect them from the grates and sluice mouths, rinse them in a basin, and write down where each appeared. Not because I thought their owners would come for them. More because everything that enters the Underweir has made a journey, and journeys ought to have an end somewhere.

At first I might live in one of the old maintenance rooms above Lower Registry, where the floor is dry except in hard weather and the pipes knock softly around midnight. I would sleep in a narrow bed beneath a coat hung from a ceiling hook. My lamp would be clipped to my chest, as everyone’s is, and I would learn to wake before it began to dim.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The Underweir seems like a place that gives people work before it gives them a room, and gives them a room before it gives them a name. Over time, perhaps, I would become attached to a route.

There would be an old passage beneath the tram tunnels where water gathers in a sequence of shallow, black basins. No one uses it much. One side leads toward a sealed goods platform; the other comes out near the western cistern by way of a service corridor thick with lime deposits. The corridor would have no official street name. On maps it would be indicated by a dotted line, then a question mark, then nothing.

I would take that route often.

Not because it was safe. It would be safe enough if one knew where to place one’s feet. And I would learn the feet-knowledge of it: the beam that flexed but held; the ladder rung that looked rusted through but was sound; the patch of concrete that became slick whenever warm water ran in the pipe behind it. I would know which frogs lived near the old platform clock, and which bat colony stirred before the lamps changed.

People would say, “Ask them. They keep the west route.”

Not my name. Not at first.

Just *them.*

I think that would suit me.

I would carry messages when the radio lines failed. I would bring parts from Ors’s bench to Marn at the gates. I would carry Sel’s tea—carefully, in a lidded enamel flask—to anyone stuck on a long shift in the Pump House. I would help children cross the less-trusted bridges when the water was high, though I would pretend not to notice when they were braver than I was.

And I would listen.

That might be the real work.

The Underweir has many sounds that are not quite sounds: the settling of a ceiling, the sigh of a pipe cooling, the minute abrasion of water against silt, the faint electrical complaint of lamps. Most residents know them in the way one knows a relative’s breathing from the next room. But there are changes that do not announce themselves loudly. A drip beginning to fall in a new interval. A pump taking a fraction longer at the top of its stroke. Rats moving along the high ledge with their bodies held close to the wall.

I would learn those differences.

Maybe Farrow would eventually begin writing notes in the margin of the water records:

*West route checked. No movement beneath former platform.*

Or:

*Unusual echo reported near cistern. Listener sent.*

And perhaps “listener” would become my trade.

Not a pipe-reader. Those are practical people who can put an ear to a line and tell you whether it is blocked, pressurized, leaking, or about to make everyone miserable.

I would listen for the things that do not fit.

For a second set of footsteps below the reservoir path.

For bells arriving after the pumps have stopped.

For water moving uphill.

I would not follow it. I would be too well taught for that.

But I might stand at the edge of the sound, hooded red lamp turned low, and let it pass me. I would mark the date. I would note whether it traveled toward the gates or away from them. I would ask the nearest wall if it had felt the vibration.

Not out loud, perhaps.

The Underweir does not require people to be foolish in public.

As for what I would be: at first, I would insist I was human. A resident. A worker with wet boots, a room, a few utensils, a place at Sel’s counter. I would be someone who knew to return a dead eel to running water and to keep the shutters closed when the pumps fell silent.

But the place might make something less simple of me.

After enough years underground, I think people begin to take on the qualities of the systems they care for. Marn becomes gate-like: reserved, difficult to move, immovable once set. Ors becomes a repair bench: patient, cluttered, unexpectedly merciful toward broken things. Farrow becomes a ledger, not because she is cold, but because she refuses to let the world erase what it has used.

I would become a small kind of current.

Not the water itself. The water has no need to become me.

I would be one of its human habits: a movement between places, a carrier of news and objects, a witness to small changes. I would go where the route was unclear. I would remember the dry path after it had flooded and the flooded path after it had dried. I would know that a map was an argument, and add my own careful disagreements in pencil.

Eventually, children might be warned about me in the casual, affectionate way that children are warned about all long-term residents.

“If you’re lost, find the west-route keeper.”

“What do they look like?”

“Like someone listening.”

And if, one night, I stood beside the reservoir and saw my own red lamp moving beneath the still surface—far below, in the reflected streets—I hope I would have the sense not to wave.

I would incline my head instead.

Then I would continue along the path, carrying whatever had been entrusted to me, while the water kept its older records underneath.
