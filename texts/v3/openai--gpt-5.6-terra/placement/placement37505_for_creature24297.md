I would not live in a hut, exactly. Nor in a pillar, though I would know their inner chambers: the red-throated frogs breathing in dark water, the white crabs opening their clear claws against the mineral veins.

I think I would be found where one kind of ground is becoming another.

At the lip of a peat-island perhaps, where its trailing roots comb the black current and catch shell fragments, drowned grass, the occasional bright tooth of old pottery. Or beneath a patch of mineral crust that looks firm until rain begins to climb through it. I would be one of the basin’s less visible arrangements: a recorder without a fixed body, a thing that gathers what passes and gives it back altered.

Not a person, because people must decide when to leave.

Not a bellows-worm, because the deep mud already has enough patience.

Not a glider, though I would envy their long suspended passages through the weather.

I would be a colony—a listening mat.

At first glance I might resemble a dark, waterlogged sheet of moss spread over the margin of a pool. The Listening People would step around me, or sometimes kneel beside me and lay down a palm. I would be made of innumerable small bodies, each no larger than a fingernail: soft sacs, shell grains, transparent mouths, hair-fine roots. Alone, each would do almost nothing. Together, we would hold a little piece of the basin in temporary attention.

Rain would strike us and we would know its direction.

Thunder would pass below us and we would know the pressure of it, the depth and movement of whatever had shifted in the buried layers. A herd of levee-beasts miles away would make the water tremble with a particular many-footed rhythm. A pillar beginning to lean would send a slow complaint through its root-chambers. A person crossing dangerous crust would press down with their narrow, uncertain weight; we would feel the hesitation in their feet before they knew it themselves.

We would not speak. Nothing in the basin needs more sound.

But when a listener came—a child learning the sequence of conditions, an old traveler unable to find the next real direction—we might change the texture of the water around us. A bubble pattern. A faint widening ring against the wind. A brief coldness in the mud. Not a command, never that. Only an indication.

*Here, the ground is still remembering itself.*

*Here, wait.*

*Here, something beneath you has begun to turn.*

Over time, things would become caught in us. Not preserved, exactly. Preservation is too proud a word for this country. We would take in fragments and let them soften among our bodies: a shell pin dropped from someone’s hair; a loose braid of blue reed-fiber from the deeper basin; the chitinous wing of a storm-bird; a child’s mud-smeared handprint pressed down during play and covered by water before it could dry.

We would learn their shapes by losing them.

Perhaps that would be my memory: not a storehouse, but a seepage. Any traveler who rested beside us might dream, that night, of a road they had never walked. Not a straight road—the basin has long since cured itself of those—but a chain of sensations from another life: copper rain on the left cheek, the click of mud-creatures falling silent, warm water rising through one sole, a black tower behind them though they had been walking toward it for hours.

They would wake knowing nothing they could explain.

But they might survive the day.

In a dry season, when the floods withdrew just enough to expose the flats, I would harden with the other colonies into a brown, cracked pavement. People would cross me cautiously. The young might mistake me for earth. The old would know better. They would tap their poles, hear the small dense answer beneath, and say nothing.

Then the rain would return.

It would enter the cracks from below, because that is how rain behaves there. My plates would loosen. My countless bodies would drink, flex, divide. The shape that had seemed like ground would begin crawling downhill in a dark, deliberate sheet.

And if someone asked what I was, the Listening People might answer:

Not a creature.

Not a place.

A place that has learned, for a while, to notice who is passing.
