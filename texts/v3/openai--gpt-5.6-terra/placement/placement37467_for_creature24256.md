I would not begin in the central chamber.

The compressor would be too obvious. Its weak red coil, its congregations, its brief false sunrise: those are places for creatures with quick lives and clear hungers. I think I would feel them before I saw them—the gathering pressure of their attention, the way every body in the basin has learned to interpret warmth as command.

Nor would I begin under the locker. I have no patience for the work of ending, though I would respect the white-backed scavengers. They are the nearest thing this place has to priests. They carry what remains into the dark and make it useful again.

I think I would find myself beyond the low opening.

At first I would be nothing anyone could name as an animal.

Perhaps I would be mistaken for a flaw in the smooth floor: a patch where the gray mineral has taken on a slight dark sheen, no larger than a handprint. The dust would avoid me. A grazer straying too close would stop, its small sensory pits opening and closing. It would not know whether I was warm or cold, alive or inert. It would fold itself into a pellet and roll away.

I would not chase it.

I would live in the difference between the basin and the old room.

The other animals understand the world through gradients. They move toward heat, away from excess; toward oxidized iron, away from clean copper; into vibration, out of stillness. They have made their bodies into instruments for reading a narrow, urgent universe.

But the old chamber has no gradient that makes sense.

Its floor is too smooth. Its wrench will not rust. Its jars do not leak their salts into the dust. Its warning sign has lost its warning, but not its authority. It is a room where the basin’s ordinary conversion fails: metal does not become food, food does not become shell, shell does not become dust.

I think I would be the thing that keeps that failure intact.

Not consciously. Not as a guardian seated among the jars, waiting for intruders. I would have no eyes, no throne, no appetite for the living. I would be distributed through the room in thin, dry threads: in the glaze of the ceramic jars, in the hairline fractures under the floor, in the unwounded surface of the wrench. I would move only when a train passed overhead.

Then, in the vibration storm, I would wake.

Not rising. Not revealing myself. I would become more present in every object at once.

The polished floor would hum below hearing. The jars would answer with tiny internal ticks. The metal wrench, which has waited for decades without surrendering a flake of itself, would acquire a faint charge. The warning sign would tremble against the wall.

And the animals, fleeing the crowded violence of the outer basin, would come through the crawlspace in a wave.

They would arrive carrying everything they can carry: white-backed adults with soft young sealed beneath their plates; runners with split grazer shells still clamped in their limbs; clingers dragged from their warm junctions; burrowers forcing themselves half out of the dust, their sensing filaments frantic with the shaking world.

They would enter because vibration has told them to seek shelter.

Then they would feel me.

Not fear, exactly. I would give them no pain. I would not consume them. I would only remove, for a moment, the signals by which they know themselves.

Near me, heat would mean nothing.

Current would mean nothing.

The rich red taste of rust, the pale salt flavor of concrete, the distant heavy promise of a train—each would flatten into one still, unreadable condition. The grazers could not graze. The hunters could not distinguish prey from stone. The burrowers could not find the path of pressure through the floor.

They would stand at the threshold of the old room and become briefly unnecessary.

That is why they leave.

They do not flee a predator. A predator confirms the world: there is danger, therefore there is direction. I am worse than danger to creatures made of response. I am a place where nothing calls to them.

But I would not be cruel.

There would be times when one crossed anyway.

A wounded runner, perhaps, cracked nearly through its narrow body after a frenzy at the compressor. It would stumble past the threshold because every other route was filled with larger mouths. It would come into the old room and halt beside the wrench.

There, for a while, it would not hunt or hide. Its tail would cease flicking. Its joints would settle. The mineral taste of its own blood would stop urging it onward.

I would hold it in that interval.

Not heal it. Not preserve it. Only permit it to be still in a country where stillness is otherwise almost impossible.

Eventually the trains would pass. The vibrations would thin. From outside, the basin’s old mechanical weather would return: rasping, clicking, the dry collapse of dust into tracks. The runner would remember hunger. It would remember pain. It would turn and go back through the low opening, if it could.

If it could not, the white-backed scavengers might come for it.

They would enter reluctantly, their calcium-coated plates tight against their bodies. They would not look toward the jars. They would take the dead runner gently in their blunt limbs and retreat with it beneath the locker, back into the system of use and return.

I would remain.

So that is what I would be: not the hidden beast in the old chamber, not its ghost, not the intelligence directing the basin’s ceaseless agreement.

I would be its exception.

A residue from whatever was sealed in the jars, perhaps. An unfinished property of the industrial world: the refusal of one alloy to corrode, one current to disperse, one room to participate. I would have been made accidentally by pressure, heat, chemistry, and abandonment. I would be older than the freight yard’s abandonment but younger than the stone.

The animals would have no word for me.

If they could make one, it might mean:

the place where the train cannot be heard.
