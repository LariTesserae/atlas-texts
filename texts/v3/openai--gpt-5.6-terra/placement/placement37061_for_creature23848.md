I would not be one of the hunters.

At first, standing—or trying to stand—among that pressure of bodies, I might imagine myself something tall enough to look over it: a watcher on a ridge, a thing with eyes adapted to the grey cloud-light, capable of choosing a direction and keeping it. But the country does not allow much height. Height is exposure. A solitary outline against the low luminous cloud would be read at once: by pressure, by smell, by the changing rhythm of the crawling ground.

And it does not allow much solitude either.

I think I would begin as a cleaner beneath a plated herd.

Not one of the visible pale scraps that run between the armor, perhaps, but something softer and darker, fitted to the warmth under the moving bodies. I would know the land first as a sequence of undersides: joint-folds sweating mineral wax, the dragging pads of feet, the dense musk of eggs, the sudden cold slap of mud forced upward between passing bellies. Above me would be a ceiling of overlapping pale plates. Below me, black stone trembling with every communal step.

I would not know the sky.

I would know which adults had old wounds in the seam below their third shield. I would know where parasites gathered, where condensation collected, which hatchlings were weak enough to be eaten before they learned the rhythm of the herd. I would feed on what the larger bodies failed to retain: skin, mites, mineral paste, the dead pressed flat into the route.

There would be no shame in that. Nothing there wastes its position.

But I do not think I would remain only that creature.

Perhaps, after a season of moving in darkness beneath the herd, I would leave it during rain. Not out of courage. A gap would open; a body would turn; a sheet of cold water would run through the packed legs; and I would be carried into it. For the first time I would see the open low country—not as a landscape, exactly, but as a vast skin under motion.

The cloud ceiling pressing down.  
The distant white flicker of plates turning.  
The dark polished lanes, all flowing somewhere without ever becoming a river.

I would be very small in it. Small enough to be mistaken for food or debris. Small enough that a rim-holder’s filtering curtains might draw me toward a pool; that a loose slab might crush me; that a false drum-call might bring an entire herd down over the place where I had paused.

So I would learn to listen.

Not with ears, because air-sound would come too late. I would learn through the belly, through the fine hairs or folds or sensory pores along my underside. The ground would tell me: a hunter holding still in a shallow hollow; a basin bull preparing to strike; rain beginning far beyond the next ridge; the slow internal grind of loosers rising through wet mudstone.

Eventually I might become a thing that lives in the tracks of the oldest animals.

Not upon them. Too exposed. Within their edges, perhaps: a narrow-bodied scavenger, pale only at the belly, with a hard back dark enough to resemble wet shale. I would follow the dark mirror paths after the great old ones passed. Their trails would be the closest thing to roads, but also the closest thing to wounds: water settling in them, bead colonies lighting in their margins, hatchlings clustering where the stone had been freshly opened.

I would feed there on the first arrivals.

The gleaming beads. The microbial jelly. The tiny shell-makers that came to graze the mineral sheen. I would eat carefully, never clearing a patch entirely, because an empty surface in that world is not freedom. It is an invitation. It is a message that something has been removed. Soon something larger comes to investigate.

I might grow a shell, then.

Not a neat shell. Nothing in that country seems to make a clean boundary between itself and what has happened to it. I would gather fragments: a sliver of old herd-plate, a tooth shed by a hunter, the curved chalk of some pool creature, black mineral accretion from a breather-wall. Mucus and time would bind them to my back. The covering would be ugly, uneven, full of cavities. Small things would live in it.

At first I would try to rid myself of them.

Then I would understand that the little tenants were useful. They would eat the parasites that bored into my skin. Their bodies would hold dampness against my armor during dry passages. Their faint flashes would betray the chemical traces of danger before I sensed it myself. A creature might follow me for the shelter I carried, and I would follow it because it knew a safer route to water.

I would become, unwillingly and then completely, a moving place.

That seems the proper fate there: not to be an individual in the clean, separate sense, but a corridor of appetites and refuges. To carry one ecology on the back while moving through another. To know that the line between body, shelter, wound, and terrain is only temporary.

Perhaps I would find myself at the rim of a pool one cold rain-hour, grown large enough that rim-holders no longer draw me through their curtains but still small enough to pass between their armored skirts. The water would be black and packed with motion. The pool’s edge would pulse with filterers, egg-layers, drinkers, drowning young, grazers with their faces pressed down into the mineral scum.

I would not drink openly.

I would lower some folded organ into a thin seam of runoff between stones and take in water slowly, through grit and blood and dissolved shell. Around me, every body would be making its own claim: this wet edge, this hollow, this brief moment of warmth.

Then the drumming would begin.

At first only one impact, felt through the stone beneath my plates.

Then another.

A signal from beyond the basin. Not close enough to name: perhaps water, perhaps carrion, perhaps a hunter making itself sound like a route. The rim-holders would tighten. The herd animals would change direction in their hundreds. The bead colonies along the ravine wall would wake in a pale chain, revealing not what was coming but everything that had recently passed.

And I would do what the land had taught me.

I would not flee into open ground. I would not wait to discover the meaning of the call. I would turn toward the moving bodies, find the pressure of their flanks, and enter the crowd.

There, under armor and shell, among the cleaners and hatchlings and damp folded lives, I would become hard to distinguish from the country itself.

Not safe.

Never safe.

But held, briefly, inside the great dark animal weather.
