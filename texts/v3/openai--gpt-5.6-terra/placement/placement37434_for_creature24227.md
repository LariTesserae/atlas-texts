At first I think I would be one of the things that arrives and leaves: a swift, perhaps, cutting through the white seam of light with a scrap of grass in its beak. There is an appeal in being able to enter such a place without becoming enclosed by it—to belong to the hot, difficult surface and to the cool mineral chamber at once.

But I do not think I would remain a swift. The hollow asks too much patience. Its light is not the light of flight; it is light that has been caught, softened, made slow by stone.

Then I think I might be one of the bats: not a creature of the deepest dark, but a small folded certainty in a crease of limestone, knowing by touch where the ceiling changes, waking only when the chamber’s pale day has begun to release its hold. Yet bats possess their own large invisible country at night. They leave the hollow behind them.

I suspect I would be something less graceful and less certain of the world beyond the cracks.

I would be one of the burrowers, though perhaps not one of the old ones. Not the animal with the triangular chert chip, which has already decided what matters and carries its chosen answer from tunnel to tunnel. I would be the younger one that takes smooth pale stones underground and brings them out again, setting them down in different places.

No one in the hollow knows why it does this. It may not know itself. The stones may be too cool in one passage, too heavy in another; their rounded faces may make some private sense against the pads of its paws. Perhaps it is learning leverage. Perhaps it is practicing the difference between weight and usefulness. Perhaps it simply feels that a stone belongs briefly in darkness before it can be returned to the light.

I would live in a low side tunnel where the floor slopes gently upward toward the chamber. Not deep enough that daylight disappears entirely. From the entrance I could see the white beam at certain hours, though it would not strike me directly. I would know the chamber through intervals: the scrape of another animal working beyond sight, the needle-call of a swift, the change in air before rain arrives through the rock.

I would come out cautiously, not because I feared the open floor exactly, but because its openness would feel important. A place with so little in it makes every movement legible. To cross it would be to make a statement, even if no one understood it.

My work would be modest. I would press wedges into clay. I would loosen old root fibers. I would turn over a stone to find a pale cricket, and sometimes find only the clean underside of the stone, damp and marked by a snail’s passing. I would not build anything. I would not improve the hollow. The hollow has no need of improvement. It has endured too long to be impressed by a creature arranging its small necessities.

But I would leave traces without intending to: a pale stone at the mouth of a tunnel, a little fan of dust, a shell tipped on its side near a seep. They would become part of the chamber’s grammar—the temporary punctuation of an animal trying to understand what can be moved and what must be accepted.

At noon I would pause at the edge of the central depression.

The moving light on the walls would trouble me. Not frighten me, exactly. It would seem like evidence of an impossible abundance: water without water, depth without depth, a river briefly remembered by stone. I would watch until the ripples faded. Then I would take up whatever small object I had brought with me—a smooth limestone pebble, perhaps, warmed on one side by the beam—and carry it back toward the dark.

Not to keep it.

Only to see what it became there.
