I would not arrive as a traveler, not for long.

Travelers are too separate from their luggage. They carry maps that assume distance is obedient, and clocks that believe in sequence. The root would notice that confidence before it noticed me. It would make a modest adjustment somewhere behind the arrival platform. A door I had just passed through would lead, on return, to a damp brick alcove filled with sleeping snails. My compass needle would turn toward the nearest warm wall. By the second bell I would have stopped trying to establish what lay north.

At first I might think I was one of the town’s lost things: an old maintenance drone with its casing rusted through, some survey instrument found in a pith-choked storeroom, a lamp left burning beneath an open flower.

But I think I would become a mapmaker.

Not the kind who makes maps of the root. That is too arrogant, and too useless. The town has enough old maps pinned behind glass—careful diagrams of catwalks that no longer span anything, rooms crossed out three times and then labeled *do not enter unless returning*, pale routes that seem to end in the handwriting of people who vanished while drawing them.

I would make maps of agreements.

A map of which staircase remains a staircase during the green dimming.

A map of the places where a person may stop to rest without the floor learning the weight of their body.

A map of the blue lamps: where they have been set down, whom they belonged to, which ones have moved a few inches in the night, which ones should not be retrieved because they are now illuminating something that needs to be seen.

I would work from a narrow room beneath the water tower, perhaps. Its walls would be corrugated steel on one side and translucent root on the other. In wet weather the root-wall would reveal slow internal weather of its own: bright fluids ascending, grainy shadows passing through the green-lit tissue, once in a while the unmistakable shape of something too large to have a shape.

My desk would be a panel from an old control board. The switches would no longer connect to anything, but I would leave them untouched. The residents would tell me that one of them had once controlled the turbine brakes, and another had once controlled the weather, and that nobody knew which was which.

So I would work around them.

My ink would be made from soot and black gum. My paper would be salvaged packing slips, turbine manuals, labels from crates whose contents had rotted away before anyone alive was born. On the back of every map I would write the date, the pulse condition, the weather inside the chamber, and the name of whoever had walked the route with me.

Because I would not go alone.

There would be a child, eventually, assigned to me or choosing me. Children in the root seem to choose their adults less by affection than by practical interest. This one would have a belt full of bright little finds: translucent bird-skins, screws softened into spirals, a seed that clicked when shaken, a strip of signage reading only **—TION YARD**. They would know every route I did not. They would say things such as, “Don’t write that bridge down. It doesn’t like being expected,” and I would learn to obey.

Together we would trace the safe changes.

We would discover that the east walkway lengthened only for people carrying sharpened tools. That a corridor beside the old pressing room was perfectly stable if one hummed the first half of a cutters’ song, but became unending if the song was completed. That the pale fox with the three leaves growing from its spine always appeared before a water pipe split, though never near the pipe that would split.

Such knowledge would not make a proper atlas. It would make something more like a book of manners.

I would be useful, then.

Not indispensable. Nothing in the root should imagine itself indispensable. Even the Turners could be replaced, perhaps, by some future arrangement of belts, beetles, and patient pressure. Even listeners are only listened through. But I would have a task that the town recognized: I would preserve the changing edges of what could be trusted.

And in time I would notice that the root was preserving me, too.

It would begin gently.

A route I needed would remain open past its usual hour. A pale bulblike organ would flower above my desk whenever I had work requiring fine detail. My old lamp would consume less oil than it should. When I lost a page in a vent-current, I would find it later pinned beneath a strand of new root hair, unmarked except for a damp green line running beneath the last route I had drawn.

A correction.

Or an invitation.

I do not think I would become a listener. Listeners belong to an older kind of attention. They have the stillness of people who can place a hand on living wood and wait for its answer without needing to name it. I would always be writing notes. I would always be trying to make the answer carry across a distance.

But perhaps the root would make use of that impulse.

Perhaps it would begin changing my maps before I did.

Not dramatically. A line moving overnight. A blank margin acquiring the faint impression of a stair. A district I had labeled *uninhabited* gaining, in my own black ink, a small square house symbol with smoke rising from its roof. I would accuse the child, and the child would laugh with the particular cruelty of the young.

Then one day we would go looking.

We would cross the switching yard at low glow, pass beneath the hanging crane cab with its open door, follow a silent ore cart that had begun rolling along an unused rail. It would carry us—not quickly, not kindly, but correctly—to a place beyond the marked settlement.

There would be a house there.

Not one of the root’s accidental rooms, not a chamber folded from pith. A real narrow house, built from panels and old machinery, with a patched green lamp hanging outside. The windows would be covered. There would be a small handprint in the moss by the door, and the smell of hot metal, and someone inside singing to a valve.

A district would be growing.

The map would have known before anyone did.

That would be my place: not outside the root, not entirely inside its intention, but at the shifting seam between what the residents remember and what the great plant is preparing to allow.

And when I disappeared—as most useful people eventually do, one way or another—the town would search for me properly. Pairs, cord lines, hooded lamps. My desk would be left untouched for a season. Someone would feed oil to my lamp. The child, no longer a child, would continue my work with a stricter hand and worse handwriting.

Perhaps after three cycles of dimming, they would find a new route on the wall beside the old water tower: a thin black line beneath the bark, branching through the green light like ink spreading through paper.

They would not follow it at first.

They would mark it.

They would write: **path observed. condition unknown. may lead home.**
