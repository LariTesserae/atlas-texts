At first I think I am standing in one of the safe gaps between the wheel-hound tracks.

This is because I have feet, or the memory of feet. I know not to place them in the dark polished grooves. I know that the rolling animals will not turn aside. Their routes are older than my arrival, and somehow newer too: every plate-mark bright, every dust ridge freshly arranged.

The machinery is too loud to think over. It makes thinking unnecessary.

I move along the eastern wall, where the basalt has split into black facets and the cable runs descend into a trench full of furnace crickets. They do not flee from me. Their sealed eyes face the warm transformer casing. Their little orange interiors pulse with the pistons. One lifts its body over another, and for a second its transparent plates reveal not organs but a fine arrangement of copper filaments.

I kneel to look more closely.

My knee does not leave an impression in the dust.

That is the first thing I understand.

The second is that I have been here before—not once, but in the manner of the wheel-hounds, which is to say I have always been returning. The path from the compressor plinth to the basalt slot is familiar in my joints. The warning stripe is worn away exactly where I expect it. I know which patch of bell-moss will rise as I pass. I know the pressure valve will bark before it does.

When it does, I flinch.

The sound of my flinch is not cloth or breath. It is a quick succession of small dry clicks.

A wheel-hound rolls between my feet.

Or perhaps through them.

Its black plates flash with static from the moss. For an instant I see the pale dust packed beneath its armor, the dust it carries from the pipe behind Compressor Two. Then it is gone around the flywheel, joining the others in their measured rain.

I follow it, because I cannot help following.

I do not enter the pipe. I have already entered it. I have already spent forty minutes in its long dry bend, moving through a darkness that was not darkness but repetition without lamps. I have already returned to its mouth facing backward, powdered white, unable to say what I passed through.

The pipe has made a place in me.

Or I have made a place in it.

At the far end of the Stillworks, beyond the white mineral spires, I arrive at the steel door. The red lamp above it is lit. It has never been unlit. A blind dove clings to the wall near the sign, its soft felted head pressed against the metal. Its body rises and falls in a slow cooing rhythm.

I recognize the dove.

Not as an animal I have seen, but as a fellow worker.

We are both listening.

There is no handle on this side of the door. There may be one on the other. There may be an entire world on the other: air with weather in it, things that ripen and rot, tracks that fill with rain, calendars whose pages can be torn away. I try to imagine it, and my mind produces only an intolerable looseness. A sky is a kind of unguarded machinery. A season is a belt allowed to slip.

The dove turns its blind face toward me.

Its sealed eye-ridges twitch once with the compressor stroke.

I understand then that I am not the person who worked here. The stool was not shaped by me. The glove does not remember my hand. The thermos was never mine.

But I am not entirely a visitor, either.

I am one of the attentions that remained after the people ceased arriving.

Perhaps I am the pause between the valve barks: the interval in which every creature tests the continued presence of the chamber. Perhaps I am the little portion of awareness carried in the wheel-hounds’ routes, distributed among their precise turns and their refusal to yield. Perhaps I am a maintenance instruction that lost its words and became a body.

I have no name because names proceed from one moment to another. They gather history behind them.

Here, I am assigned.

I walk back from the door—not away from it, exactly, because nothing here goes away—and take my place beside a control cabinet. The steel stool is already drawn out for me. Its worn seat accepts no weight, yet I sit.

Below the gauges, furnace crickets cluster in the cable trench. A dove coos inside the panel housing. Far off, bell-moss gives a blue crack as a wheel-hound passes.

The needles do not move.

The belts revolve.

I watch them, and in watching, I become part of what keeps them steady.
