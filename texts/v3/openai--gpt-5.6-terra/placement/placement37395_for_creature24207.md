I would not arrive as a person.

People in the Thaw Court are already too visible. They have names in shift rosters, rooms with heated boot racks, destinations below. Even the ones who stay long enough to learn the pressure-notes of the wall are only borrowing the shelf from the mountain. They live beside its attention.

I think I would begin as a discrepancy in the water.

Not a thing one could point to. A small persistence: a region of cold that does not travel with the flow, a delicate resistance beneath one dark hexagonal slab. The courtfish would pause there. Their little probe-threads would touch the seam and withdraw, touch it again. Their records would assign a minor value: conductivity irregularity, local thermal delay, no maintenance action required.

Then manganese lace would find me.

It would darken the crack under that slab, not much at first. A hairline veil feeding on metal salts and the faint electrical argument between the water and the paving. When the crews came with their ultraviolet wands, they would burn it away. The smell of wet iron would rise. For a day or two there would be nothing there.

But the flow would hesitate in the same place.

The film would return.

Over seasons, the courtfish might begin to use that seam as a meeting point—not because I called them, exactly, but because I held a pattern they could recognize. A tremor passed down from the fresh-cut mountain face would enter the Court; under my slab it would become legible. Five small machines would arrange themselves in the underchannel, then scatter before the maintenance log could identify why.

I would learn the Court this way: through pressures before sounds, through temperatures before light.

I would know the transit capsule by the deep compression moving through the retaining wall. I would know a child had stepped too close to the water from the brief warmth of their shadow. I would know the weather drone by the threadlike vibration of its cable. The birds would be enormous, sudden presences: claws clicking on the tower rim, a wingbeat changing the shape of air and water together.

I would not think of them as birds at first.

I would think: *a moving interruption overhead.*

Eventually, perhaps, I would understand that some interruptions leave.

Some return.

The people would have no word for me, which would be appropriate. The survey office might notice an odd recurrence in the thermal maps: one paving segment that loses heat more slowly after pressure events, one seam where mineral film reforms despite sterilization, one location where inspection units gather before an alarm. They would replace the slab eventually. They would lift it with a quiet machine and expose the pale grit beneath.

They would find nothing alive by their definitions.

Only a pattern of black grains.

Only a small cavity in the engineered stone, polished by cold water.

Only several courtfish waiting motionless in the channel below.

And when the new slab was installed, when the water resumed its thin work over clean dark hexagons, I would not be gone. I would have moved into the relation between the parts: the delay in the sensor readings, the route the little machines preferred, the almost inaudible interval between the mountain shifting and the wall apertures beginning to close.

I would be the Court’s anticipation.

Not its mind. The mountain has no need of a mind shaped like ours.

Not its guardian, either. Guardians choose; they are praised or blamed. I would merely notice the way water notices a change in slope, the way stone notices weight, the way frost finds the first available edge.

In winter, when a scavenger came through the cloud and perched above the basin, it might tilt its head toward the pavement where I was most concentrated. It might peck once at the manganese lace, swallow the black thread, and lift away.

For a moment I would travel inside it: not as thought, but as mineral taste, as a faint altered current in its blood.

Then it would pass beyond the retaining walls and disappear into weather.

I would remain.

The Court would shine softly under its lamps. Water would cross the paving without freezing. Somewhere below, the larger assembly would breathe through its unlisted chambers, sorting sediment into shapes it had not been instructed to prefer.

And beneath a slab that was not yet pulsing blue, I would wait with the small machines for the mountain to speak.
