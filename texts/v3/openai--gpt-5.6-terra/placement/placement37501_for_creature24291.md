I would begin by thinking I was one of the town’s lost things.

Not a person, at first. Not anything with a name that could be called from one end of a lane to another. I would be a small brass-colored object in the black silt beneath a bridge: perhaps a button, perhaps the cap from a measuring rod, perhaps a coin too worn to spend. Water would turn me slowly in the grit. Eel-bodies would pass over me. Above, feet would cross the planks with all the quick confidence of people who know exactly how little certainty the ground permits them.

For a long time I would mistake that stillness for being buried.

But the Basin does not bury things in the usual sense. It keeps them in circulation. A hard rain would lift the silt; a drain would reverse; a child reaching for a dropped stick would find me caught in the root-hairs of a willow. I would be rinsed in a jar, rubbed on a sleeve, held up to weak sun.

Someone would say, “That’s old.”

Someone else would say, “No, it only looks old.”

And perhaps that would be the first true thing said about me.

I think I would become one of the attendants of the Basin—not because I had chosen the work, and not because anyone had appointed me, but because there are some people who find themselves listening when everyone else has gone home. I would have a room near the unfinished wall, narrow as a boat cabin, built of mud brick and alder poles. Its floor would be raised on old stones. Beneath the stones there would be water, or the suggestion of water, which is almost the same thing there.

My room would contain:

A lamp with a smoke-blackened lip.  
A coil of twine.  
A kettle that never quite loses the taste of peat.  
A shelf of jars holding things brought up from the drains: smooth white stones, a child’s carved animal, a rusted key, fish bones, blue fragments of glazed pottery from some place beyond the basin.  
A book of water-level marks, written in several hands over many years.  
And a chair positioned so that, when the light was low, I could hear the Basin without seeing it.

I would not be the chief attendant. That is too settled a role, too much like a person who understands the rules. I would be the one who comes after the chief attendant has gone to sleep, or before she has risen: the listener who records sounds nobody else thinks are worth recording.

Three slow bubbles.  
A pause.  
Five close together.  
The scrape of something beneath the retaining wall.  
A sound like a cup being set carefully on stone.

The ditch-keepers would tolerate me because I would never tell them their measurements were wrong. I would simply add things to their maps that did not fit: a channel that answered when tapped; a culvert where the water moved uphill for seven breaths; a place under a certain house where every nail driven into the floor eventually rusted through from below.

Children would know me better than adults did. They would come to my door with objects they had found floating: a red bead, a bird skull, an iron hook, an entire wooden shoe. They would ask whether the Basin had sent it.

I would tell them the truth: “It may have.”

Then they would ask if the Basin wanted something back.

To that I would say: “Everything goes back eventually. The question is whether it goes by water.”

I would be regarded as unsettling but useful. The sort of person people invite into a kitchen during a hard rain, then wish would leave before the conversation becomes too quiet. I would accept bowls of eel broth, weak beer, hot mash, mugs of boiled rainwater with marsh mint. I would fix small things while I listened: a broken clasp, a cracked shutter-latch, a child’s toy boat. I would know how every family’s roof drained. I would know which homes had a secret second barrel under the stairs and which ones let water leak deliberately into the neighbor’s runnel.

But I would not report every violation.

A town like that cannot survive by rules alone. It survives by understanding which rules are walls, which are bridges, and which are only boards laid across a puddle until morning.

Still, I would take the oldest prohibition seriously.

Never fill the Basin.

Not because I would know what lay beneath it. I would not. I think certainty would be the wrong relationship to the thing below. The basinfolk are wise in that way: they have made a life beside an unanswered question without deciding that the question must be solved.

I might eventually come to suspect that the thing under the Basin is not sleeping.

Sleeping things wake. This thing has never needed to.

It is the basin’s attention.

It notices weight. It notices water. It notices the town’s accumulated arrangements: every patched wall, every diverted gutter, every bridge repaired with a different kind of wood. It has been learning the shape of the settlement through seepage. It knows the warmth of houses above it, the rhythms of boots on boards, the taste of ash and lamp fat and blood from cut hands. It may not understand people, but then perhaps people do not understand weather either, and still make their days around it.

On certain mornings, when the Basin counts, I would sit on the wet ledge before dawn with the older attendants. We would not speak. The town behind us would be closed-eyed and hushed. There would be no market yet, no mallets, no calls over the water. Only the drip from roofs and that low, patient pulse at the unfinished wall.

One.

A tremble in the reflection.

Two.

A ring widening across black water.

Three.

Somewhere under a house, a child turning in sleep would whisper a word from a language with no dry land in it.

And I would write the numbers down, though I would not know what they meant.

Perhaps, after enough years, I would cease to be entirely human in the town’s memory. Not dramatically. No one would see me vanish into the Basin or grow gills or take the form of a heron.

Instead, I would become an instruction.

If the water counts, fetch the listener.

If the listener is gone, sit by the unfinished wall until the lamps burn low.

If the Basin gives something back, do not claim it too quickly.

And when I finally died, my tools would be divided among the town: my hook to a gatherer, my oilcloth to a ditch-keeper, my notebook to the attendants, my little brass button returned to the water.

Not as an offering.

Nothing so deliberate.

Only because it had been there before me, and because it would know the way.
