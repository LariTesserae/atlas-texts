I would find myself beneath the corrugated sheet, at first.

Not because it is safe. Nothing in the basin is safe for long. The sheet heats in daylight and clicks as it cools; rain drums on it hard enough to shake loose crumbs of clay. Rats nose under its edge. Children—or workers, or someone moving quickly through the fence gap—sometimes lift one corner looking for something they dropped or something they hope is there.

But underneath is a particular kind of dark: not absence, but compression. Damp cardboard softening into pulp. A root entering through a tear in the plastic. A snail shell turned empty and then occupied by mites. Threads of fungus crossing the grain of old pallet wood. The smell of iron, wet dust, and the sweet rot of things that have stopped being what they were.

I think I would be one of those threads.

Not the visible mushroom, not the brown shelf that appears after rain with its lacquered face turned upward. I would be the white work underneath: fine filaments moving through the wood, the paper, the glue, the feather shaft, the softened body of a beetle. I would have no single shape anyone could point to. If someone said, *there,* they would be pointing at a patch of pale fuzz, or a darkened board, or a place where the crate had begun to crumble into soil.

But I would be in the whole conversion.

I would know the basin through dampness. I would feel the first weather change not as cloud or wind but as water entering the wood grain, swelling old fibers around me. The pump’s vibration would come through the clay in a long underground shudder. When the black ribbon rose, I would taste what it carried: detergent, leaf tannin, engine oil, skin cells, pollen, ash, the faint mineral signature of distant places above the retaining wall.

I would not clean any of it. I would not redeem it.

That is not what I would be for.

I would make it available.

The yellow ribbon caught in the fence would eventually fray, lose color, fall. A rat might take a strand for bedding; sunlight might turn it brittle; water might pin it into mud. Years later, some softened remnant of it would be in the dark under the sheet, caught in my small white weather. Not gone. Never gone. Changed enough to feed something else.

I would be patient with the basin’s embarrassments: its foam, its bottle glass, its dead wings, its anonymous offerings. I would have no idea which objects had mattered most to the people who dropped them. The faded photograph in the utility cabinet, the plastic flowers, the glass of rainwater with its living commas—I would not understand grief, exactly. But if the photo eventually came loose in a storm and traveled down through the reeds, if its paper met mud and darkness and the slow appetite of the place, I would receive it without judgment.

Not preserve it. Not erase it.

Receive it.

And perhaps this would make me part of the basin’s hidden memory: not a keeper of forms, but a keeper of continuance. The frogs would call above me from the culvert. The bats would write their sharp invisible equations in the air. The heron would come down on its impossible legs and make the little country hold its breath. Rats would build and rebuild their chambers in the banks. Snails would leave bright inscriptions across every surface by night.

I would remain below, joining what had been separate.

Wood to softness. Softness to soil. Soil to reed root. Reed to beetle shelter. Beetle to shrew. Shrew, perhaps, to the narrow-faced animal moving through the twilight drain. Everything passing onward, not cleanly, not beautifully, but thoroughly.

Or perhaps, after enough time beneath the sheet, I would become less like fungus and more like the damp itself: the persistent hidden wetness that does not leave even after the visible water has gone.

A darkness under the clay.

A coolness inside the cracked concrete.

The faint give beneath a bootprint.

Something the basin keeps because it cannot afford to forget.
