I would find myself not at the pump house, though I would know its sound.

Not on the service road either, with its crushed stone and its brief illusion of certainty. I would be somewhere lower than a person’s attention usually goes: at the lip of a culvert where warmer water comes out beneath a grate, where the rushes have grown taller and greener than they ought to in that cold country.

At first I might think I was a worker there.

I would have a key ring with colored tags, a radio that was mostly useless in the wind, a thermos smelling faintly of metal and old tea. I would know which part of the road shakes when the pumps begin drawing hard. I would keep spare socks in a sealed bag and never quite manage to keep them dry. I would learn the plateau’s gestures: the way mist lying flat means the water is rising somewhere out of sight; the way a certain blue shine in the moss means there is no ground beneath it; the way a cable’s note changes before a storm.

But I do not think I would remain human for long there.

The human life on the plateau is too enclosed. Too much of it happens behind sealed doors, under electric light, amid gauges that turn water into numbers. It is a life of maintenance: holding the world’s wetness at a selected distance for another shift, another season, another year.

I think I would want to be something that did not need to oppose the water.

Perhaps I would become a caddisfly larva.

I would live in the green-brown current just below the outlet, among the submerged roots of sedge and the trembling threads of algae. I would make myself a case from whatever the plateau offered: sand grains, fragments of dead rush, a sliver of bark carried down from some unlikely shrub. If I were especially close to the works, I might incorporate a tiny piece of orange paint, a bright fleck from a corroding machine, or a black curl of cable sheath softened by years in the peat.

My house would be small enough to pass unnoticed by anyone standing above.

The turbine noise would not be noise to me. It would be weather. It would be the large, permanent trembling of the world: a pressure through the stones, a deep movement in the water, the current changing its habits according to decisions made by people in rooms I could never enter. When the machines stopped, I would feel the valley become enormous.

Not quiet. Never quiet.

I would hear the separate drops then. The minute collapses of bubbles. The scrape of grit moving across the streambed. The soft roots shifting in their black underwater ceiling. I would feel, perhaps, the slow exhalation of peat from beneath the water.

And when I changed—when the season had gone far enough into its brief bright fever, when the pools were full of reflected cloud and the midges gathered in columns—I would leave that case.

I would rise only a little way.

I would be one of the small winged things caught in the warmer pocket of air beside the pipe outlet, briefly alive above the surface. I would cross the service road in a gust. I would pass an orange excavator sunk to its hubs, its cab full of old flies and rainlight. I would land on the riveted wall of the pump house, among droplets and rust.

From there, I might see the whole plateau as it is: not a landscape with machinery imposed upon it, nor a machine yard threatened by bog, but one wet system teaching every other system how to join it.

The pylons would be tall reeds of another kind.  
The cables would be dark stems singing in the wind.  
The drainage channel would be a young river trying to remember how to wander.  
The workers, moving in bright waterproof shells, would be large temporary insects tending the strange warm nests of metal.

And I would understand that the plateau does not hate them or welcome them.

It absorbs their attention, their heat, their dropped bolts, their exhausted vehicles, their names for things.

I would live there as a small maker of a portable shelter, carrying the evidence of the place on my body: stone, sedge, industrial color, water-dark fiber. Then I would rise, mate, lay what I could in the current, and disappear into the larger patience below.

Or perhaps, after that brief flight, I would not disappear.

Perhaps I would become moss instead: not one green softness but a thousand minute mouths holding rain, growing upward while dying downward, making a future black depth beneath the feet of anything that assumes it is standing on land.
