I would begin by thinking I was a person.

Not an important person. Not one of the numbered coats at the waterworks, not an engineer whose decisions alter a district, not a foreman with a radio and a clipboard. I would be someone who knows the old routes: a maintenance runner, perhaps, carrying messages, replacement valves, coils of wire, jars of screws, paper-wrapped bread still warm from the canteen.

I would live above a shuttered upholstery workshop in the lower east quarter, in two rooms with a ceiling low enough that I would learn not to stand quickly. A steam pipe would cross the bedroom wall at shoulder height, boxed in with painted boards. In winter I would dry gloves over it. In summer it would sweat rust-colored droplets into a basin I kept beneath it. From the kitchen window, if it could be called a window, I would see the wall of the opposite building: brick, drainpipe, moss, three other windows, a row of laundry that never fully came dry.

I would know the people across the gap without quite knowing their names. The couple on the second floor who argued only in whispers. The night baker, whose lamp came on at three. The girl who practiced scales on a dented clarinet with a cloth wrapped around the bell. The old man with the careful cough. We would make privacy out of routine. We would know when not to look.

My work would bring me through the city’s insides.

I would take the covered stairs down before daylight, passing the communal boot racks and the smell of wet wool. At the landing outside the old ticket booth, I would buy pickled greens in a paper cone. I would cross two loading bridges, duck under an elevated conveyor, walk through the receiving hall of a factory that had ceased to make anything recognizable years ago but still contained a tool library, a repair cooperative, a school kitchen, and a room where people rebuilt small electric motors.

There would be places in the city where I could travel by instinct alone: left at the wall with the 1987 flood mark, through the boiler-room garden, up the iron steps whose third tread flexed, past the door painted blue and then painted over so often that the blue was visible only at the latch.

I would carry a ring of keys too large for any one person to own, though none of them would open a private home. They would open valve cabinets, tool lockers, roof gates, electrical closets, access hatches, temporary barriers. They would open the portions of the city that belonged to everyone and therefore, in practice, belonged to whoever arrived with the right key and enough patience.

But after a while I think I would stop being only a person.

The city would alter my sense of self. Its conditions would enter my habits first: I would listen for water behind walls. I would wake when a pump changed pitch three blocks away. I would find myself staring at gutters while other people watched faces. I would keep bits of chalk in every pocket, because a chalk mark could mean *this leak is known*, *this wall is moving*, *do not open this hatch*, *someone came through here at dawn and did not return the wrench.*

Then, perhaps, I would become something smaller and more useful than a citizen.

I would become a route.

Not a street—not exactly. Streets in that valley are too easily blocked, flooded, excavated, claimed by scaffolds and cable trenches. I would be one of the unwritten paths through the occupied interior: a sequence of dry-enough landings, doors that can be opened, ladders that hold, courtyards where a person can stop under cover, kitchens where someone will fill a cup without asking why.

People would say, “Take the warm way,” and mean me.

They would say, “Go through the old print works, then the laundry passage,” and the passage would be mine in the way that a river belongs to its banks. A child walking home in heavy rain would be sent along me. An exhausted waterworks worker would find me after a night shift. A new family, carrying boxes through an unfamiliar neighborhood, would be directed through the bridge behind the greenhouses, past the upper canteen, down the stair that smelled of apples and hot metal.

I would not be visible as a thing. I would be held in memory: in the feet of people who knew which grates stayed clear, in the hands of those who had tied a loose rail, in the unspoken agreement that the narrow passage behind Block 19 should never be used for storage because it was the only way out when the lower court flooded.

And if I had a body, it would be made of these small continuities.

A length of wire looped around a loose pipe.

A bucket placed beneath a drip before anyone slipped.

A hand on the elbow of a stranger climbing a dark stair.

A thermos left at the pump-house door.

A child taught to hear the difference between rain in a gutter and water where water should not be.

That is what I would be there: not the city’s master, not its witness, not even its servant.

I would be one of its remembered ways of continuing.

Perhaps, in the deepest part of the old waterworks, there would be a small overflow channel no map quite recorded. It would run beneath an obsolete settling room, under iron plates slick with condensation, then emerge beside a wall thick with liverwort. The water in it would never stop—not much, only a narrow black brightness moving between stones. Workers would step over it without noticing. Moss would drink from it. In hard weather it would take the pressure from somewhere else.

I think I would be that, too.

A hidden channel.

Not private. Not sacred. Not beautiful in the clean, distant sense.

Necessary only because everything around it has been built so close together that there must be somewhere for the excess to go.
