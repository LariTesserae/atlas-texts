At first I think I would be a traveler there.

Not one of the boat people exactly, because I would not know the submerged routes. I would arrive on one of their ugly, patient vessels, standing among jars of salt-clay and cages of complaining birds, trying not to look down when the pole slips into water and finds no bottom. The city would appear gradually: not a skyline but a thickening of low shapes under the gray—roof upon roof, smoke pressed flat beneath cloud, white birds lifting and settling again, the black teeth of stakes everywhere.

I would step onto a wet platform and immediately learn that I had been walking incorrectly all my life.

In a dry world, one puts weight down and expects the ground to keep it. Here every step would be a question asked of wood, lashings, old shell, packed rubble, and the anonymous dead incorporated below. I would have narrow feet, traveler’s feet, feet made for certainty. Children would see me hesitate at a gutter-crossing and laugh. A goat would pass me along a roof ridge with greater dignity. Someone would catch my elbow—not kindly, not unkindly, simply because people here hold one another when a person is about to become water.

For a little while, I would want to understand the city.

I would ask what hour it was, and receive not an answer but a story.

The hour would be when the south kiln began taking fuel. It would be after the shell-rack had rung twice for animal passage. It would be before Old Nemi’s pigs were let into the ash yard. It would be during the long argument from the house with the blue-black door, the one about a goat and a marriage payment and a missing copper awl.

I would ask what season it was.

Someone would look at me with concern.

Then perhaps an elder, amused enough to forgive me, would say: “It is the season after the fish stairs were repaired. The season before the white ox learns something else.”

And I would begin to understand that the city has not lost time. It has merely refused to give time to the sky.

Here, time is carried by hands.

It is carried in the smoothness of a railing touched by generations. In the knot-bundles darkened by sweat. In the exact place where a grandmother says a wall began to hum before it cracked. In a child’s first unheld walk across an upper plankway. In the renamed bricks, the inherited awl, the patched roof that has been patched so often its original roof no longer exists.

I think I would stay long enough to become useful.

At first they might put me to carrying things. Everyone can carry something: reed bundles, fired bricks, baskets of snails, jars of cloudy drink, wet children, sleeping dogs, messages shouted across water. I would learn to carry with my arms wide, with my knees bent, with my eyes always on the next three surfaces. I would learn not to set a jar beside the wrong post, not to leave food uncovered, not to insult a family by calling their wall “old” when it is composed partly of their ancestors.

But I do not think I would remain merely a laborer.

I would become interested in the bells.

At a crossing, I would stand beneath a frame of wood polished dark by damp and hands. Hanging from it would be three old clay vessels, none made as bells. One might once have stored fish oil. One might have been a water jar. One, cracked down its side and bound with fiber, might have spent centuries as a cooking pot before being struck and found to answer.

Their sounds would not be beautiful in the way a dry-world bell is beautiful. No clean, high note would rise toward a clear sky.

These sounds would be blunt. Earthy. They would thud through my ribs. They would shake the wet boards beneath my feet. A stone slab might answer with a deep, exhausted boom. Shells might clatter like rain that never falls. A jar might give one dark note, then a broken second note that seemed to continue inside the mud.

At first I would believe the ringing was language.

Then I would hear how much of it was not language: warning, impatience, celebration, labor rhythm, grief, animal movement, children’s games, prayers sent downward, the simple human need to make a mark in a world that never gives one from above.

Eventually I would stop trying to translate every sound.

I would learn to listen for the pressure underneath it.

There are people born with webbing who become Listeners. I would not have been born there, and the city would not pretend otherwise. But perhaps usefulness is another kind of birth. Perhaps after enough years of sleeping above the same dark water, after enough times placing a palm on a damp wall to feel whether its trembling is footsteps or a loosened stake, I would begin to hear what the city hears.

Not words.

Weight.

A house does not speak by saying *I am failing.* It speaks through a minute change in the way a bowl quivers on its shelf. A buried channel does not say *I am blocked.* It gives the floor a heaviness, a reluctance. A great fish passing beneath an outer basin does not announce itself. It withdraws the usual little movements from the water—the insects settle elsewhere, the birds stop stabbing, an ox raises its head and refuses to step.

I would become a listener of such absences.

People would send for me, not because I had magic, but because I had become patient enough to put my hands where other people preferred not to put theirs.

I would kneel in cramped rooms where the walls sweated around stored jars. I would press my cheek to a plank while a family watched in silence. I would stand ankle-deep in a ditch, one hand on a stake and one hand on the wet clay, feeling for the tiny shudder that meant the stake still had hold.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

That too would belong to the city. A Listener who is always right becomes dangerous; people begin to build their lives around the certainty of one pair of hands. Better to be useful, fallible, called upon often but not worshiped.

And in time, I think I would become less and less a traveler.

My clothes would acquire household color, though perhaps not one of the recognized colors. Wet gray, like the sick-tenders, might suit me—not because I tended sickness, but because I had learned that gray contains clay, smoke, water, ash, and cloud. My hair would be bound up with cord and stiffened at the edges with mud. My feet would widen, perhaps not in shape but in habit. I would know which boards were trustworthy by the feel of their grain under the sole.

I would own little.

A clay lamp, repaired until its shape had become personal. A bone comb. A sleeping mat. A small waterproof pouch containing a piece of a copper thing—not enough to use, perhaps, but enough to remember its brightness. A string of beads recording debts I had not yet repaid and favors I had not asked for.

And I would have one bell.

Not a new bell. Nothing in that place should begin as what it becomes.

It would be a brick at first: long, pale, shell-flecked, marked by the fingertips of someone dead before I came. It would have been part of a wall near an outer basin. When the wall was taken down, the other bricks would be sorted for roadbed and repairs, but this one would give a strange tone when struck. Not loud. Not clear. A sound like a fingertip touching the surface of deep water.

I would keep it under my sleeping place.

When summoned to a doubtful platform, I would set the brick on the boards and tap it with a shell. Its note would change according to the tremor beneath it. Or perhaps I would only imagine that it changed, and the real work would be that the people around me grew quiet enough to hear the structure settle.

Either way, the brick would become a bell.

And then, much later—though “later” would mean only after a thousand repairs, after children had stopped calling me stranger, after the goat routes had changed in ways no one else noticed—I might understand that I was not a Listener at all.

I was becoming one of the city’s remembered objects.

Not important enough for a shrine. Not ancient enough for a story told across districts. But known.

There would be a crossing where people said: “Strike the gray bell before you step over. It rings differently if the east posts are loose.”

No one might remember that the gray bell had once been mine. They might not remember that I had carried it, slept beside it, pressed it to walls. A child might ask why it had a thumb-shaped hollow in one side, and an adult would say, “That is just how it was made.”

That would be enough.

When I died, they would not need to carry me away. There would be nowhere to carry me that was more fitting than the place itself. They might dry me slowly in the outer heat of a kiln, darkening me until I became brittle and earthen. They might place part of me beneath a repaired walkway. They might mix my fragments into the fill beneath a wall overlooking one of the quiet basins.

And above me, people would keep crossing.

Dogs would sleep in the shade of that wall. Mosquitoes would gather where the water darkened. A woman would lean out of a window and call to a child. Someone would strike a clay jar to announce that the goats were coming through. The sound would travel down the posts, through the shell and packed mud, through the old materials, through me.

I would not be gone.

I would be part of what holds.
