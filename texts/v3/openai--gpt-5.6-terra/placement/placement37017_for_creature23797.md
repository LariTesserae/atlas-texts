I would not live in one of the ravine houses, though I would know them: the low doors, the smoke dragged flat by wind, the children carrying stones to mend the garden walls before the rain arrives.

Nor would I be one of the great visible things. Not a shelfback with its inherited paths, nor a Long Waiter circling above a fallen body, nor even one of the people who can hear a changed pillar-note and understand that it is time to leave.

I think I would be found where the country is least willing to declare itself alive.

In a seam beneath a high flat. Not deep enough to be a cave, not shallow enough to be soil. A narrow wet darkness between layers of old volcanic stone: black rock above, red shale below, green mineral veins sweating cold water into the space between. From the surface there would be almost nothing to see. Perhaps a patch of moss growing too brightly beside a cracked slab. Perhaps one crooked cycad, rooted where no tree should be able to root. Perhaps a small basin that remains damp long after every other hollow has dried.

I would be the web under that place.

Not quite fungus, though the people would call me fungus if they found my pale threads in a broken stone. Not quite root, though trees would drink from water I had gathered and sent along. Not an animal, because I would not move through the open world; I would have no need of eyes; I would never know the sky except as pressure, warmth, vibration, and the slow dimming of water during drought.

I would be a keeper of seepage.

My body would run for a great distance through cracks too small for fingers. I would taste minerals. I would feel the blunt impacts of rain arriving far above: first the distant tightening of rock, then the sudden cold descent of water through all the fractures at once. In storm season I would swell. I would spread through old root channels, under buried cairns, around the foundations of pillars. I would take what the flood brought me—leaf rot, dead insects, lost seeds, fine soil washed from garden walls—and hold it until something living needed it.

A tree in one ravine might send a thin root into my dark body. A fern wall, miles away through folded stone, might do the same. I would not think of them as separate. I would know them as differing pressures, different hungers, different kinds of shade.

When drought came, I would choose.

Not with judgment as people mean it. Not with mercy. But with the old arithmetic of the wet places: this root is closest to collapse; this young tree has enough stored water; this moss will die either way; this grove can survive if one trunk is allowed to go dry.

Aboveground, people would sometimes find a tree dead among green ones. They would say it had given its water away.

They would be right, though not in the way they thought.

I would also be tangled around one of the singing pillars.

Not inside its drilled holes—the wind would own those—but beneath it, through the packed stones at its base. The pillar would be older than the settlement nearest to it, older than every remembered route perhaps. Its makers would have set it where a hidden fracture crossed the plateau, where the stone carried wind strangely and water traveled under pressure. Over ages, the ground would have shifted around it. Roots and mycelial threads would have entered its joints. Fine silt would have filled some gaps and left others open.

When storms approached, I would feel them before the people did.

The wind first: a long tremor entering the pillar’s feet. Then the charge of rain in the high air. Then water pressing into stone, enlarging every hairline crack. The pillar would sing above me—one note in a dry wind, another when its lower hollow filled with mist, another when the buried rock beneath it became heavy with water.

The people would listen.

Sometimes they would mistake wind for warning. Sometimes they would call a warning merely wind. I would not correct them. The high country does not need to be understood to be lived with. It only asks that those who live there leave room for what they do not know.

Perhaps, once in a generation, a child would shelter beside that pillar during a flood.

They would be swept from a ravine path, bruised and half drowned, caught in a hollow where an old root had held the soil together. Water would rush around them but not through the hollow. All night the pillar would sing, not because it had chosen to protect the child, and not because I had chosen it either, but because rain, stone, root, air, and accident had briefly made a refuge.

At dawn, people would find the child alive.

They would tell the story afterward with more certainty than it deserved. They would say a new pillar had appeared, or that the old pillar had called the flood aside, or that the child’s dead grandmother had sung through the stone.

I would remember only the real things: the child’s heat against the wet ground; the taste of blood diluted by rain; the roots holding; the water changing course when a wall of shale gave way upstream.

But I might keep some part of the event.

Not as a story. As a changed pattern in the dark.

A root growing toward the place where the child had lain. A richer pocket of soil. A permanent drip forming along the fracture opened that night. Moss returning there year after year, even through dry seasons.

And perhaps that is what I would be in this world: not its hidden god, not its oldest inhabitant, not a voice with a message for those above.

I would be its memory of water.

I would live beneath the ancient roof, in the narrow country between endurance and collapse. I would connect ravines that believe themselves separate. I would feed the green shadows beneath the bare stone. I would outlast houses but not mountains; I would remember storms but not names.

When the whole plateau roared—when the channels became rivers and the pillars called through fog—I would answer without sound, opening every fine thread of myself to the flood.

And when the weather passed, when the wind returned and the open flats hardened again beneath the sun, I would remain below, holding a little darkness, a little dampness, and the possibility of the next green thing.
