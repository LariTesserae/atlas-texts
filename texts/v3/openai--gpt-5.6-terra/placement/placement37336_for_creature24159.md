I would find myself not at the bottom, nor at the entrance where the surface world can still be guessed, but halfway down the eastern wall beside one of the old extraction benches.

There is a narrow seam there, dark except when the weak pane of daylight reaches it. Water enters through it grain by grain, not visibly flowing so much as insisting. The stone below the seam has acquired a pale, wet shine. Calcite has made a soft rim around the crack, as if the wall has been healing from within.

I would be that rim.

Not the whole Settling, which is too large and too patient to call itself a self. I would be one of its smaller attentions: a thin accretion of mineral around an old cut in the quarry wall, growing by increments too slight to possess. I would have begun as an interruption—a flaw in stone, a place where water could pass—and become, over years, the record of that passing.

I would not see the chamber exactly. Sight would be too sudden. But I would know light by temperature and deposition. When the entrance brightened in summer, the faint green film below me would thicken by almost nothing. When someone entered the water, their movement would arrive first as pressure through the wall: a tremor in the seam, a loosening of silt below, the brief unmaking of the water’s layers.

Their warmth would be startling.

For a little while, it would travel through the basin in dispersing folds. It would touch the stone near me and vanish. Their fingers, if they rested on the ledge, would leave salt and skin oil—a minute alteration, a chemistry from the upper world. I would receive it without welcome or refusal. In time it would be covered by another film of mineral.

That is what I would be: not the memory of the visitor, but the place where memory becomes unrecognizable enough to endure.

I would know the old quarrymen only as planes and absences. Their measured cuts would be the grammar of my world. The horizontal bench beneath me would say: here stone was wanted. The square corner beyond it would say: here an intention stopped. But I would not know hands, wages, voices, daylight, carts, iron. Those things would have been too fast. They would belong to the vanished surface, where things happen and are replaced by other things.

Below me, silt would settle on the collapsed slabs. Above me, drops would form in darkness. Between the two, I would continue making the seam less like a wound and more like a feature of the chamber.

Perhaps, after centuries, I would close it.

Not entirely. The water would find another route. It always does. A new thread would appear a few feet away, and there it would begin again: wetness, dissolved stone, a translucent lip, the slow removal of every edge that had once seemed final.

If I had a thought, it would not be *I am here.*

It would be:

*This disturbance has become part of the water.*

And then, much later:

*This water has become part of the stone.*
