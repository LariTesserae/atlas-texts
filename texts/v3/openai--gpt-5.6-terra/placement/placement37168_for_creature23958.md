I would not arrive from the weather.

At first I think I would be a listener: an old person on a three-legged stool near the central passage, with one bad knee and a good ear, known for saying unhelpful things accurately. I would keep a hand against the wall while people spoke to me. I would know the ordinary sounds of the quarter so well that a new sound would feel like a hand laid on my shoulder in the dark.

I would say: that is not a pipe.  
I would say: do not sleep in the west shelf tonight.  
I would say: the beam is only settling; it has always disliked rain overhead.  
I would say: no, that is grief. Leave the cup where it is.

But that is too dignified, perhaps. It gives me a place too quickly.

Maybe I would be a child instead, one of the children who knows the routes behind the rooms and under the stairs: the places adults call gaps because they have forgotten they are places. I would have a shirt with one cuff tied in string and a small private collection of white pebbles in a clay cup. I would be very good at moving without spilling a lamp.

I would know whose kitchens could be crossed without asking, which old people pretended to be angry when disturbed, where the ceiling came down low enough to make a fort of two boards and a curtain. I would know that the spring is not to be looked into, which would make it more interesting than anything else.

I would make dams in the gutters. Not large ones. Clever ones. Ones that changed the note of the water without stopping it.

When found, I would stand wet to the knees, solemn under scolding, and explain that I had only been teaching the channel a new song.

But perhaps I would not be trusted with childhood there. Perhaps I have too much of the weather in me: too much desire to see what changes when something is moved.

Then I might be one of those who has looked into the spring too long.

Not mad. The quarter does not permit such easy names. Only altered in the arrangement of things.

I would begin by moving cups. Then chairs, then hooks, then the little household objects nobody notices until they are not where they have always been. I would put a spoon on a shelf because, in the spring, I had seen it there. I would turn a bowl upside down though there was no grief in the house. I would wake at night convinced that a certain nail must be pulled from a wall before dawn.

People would watch me carefully. They would keep me company without saying they were keeping me company. Someone would ask me to help wind a reel. Someone else would send a child with a kettle, then another with mending, then someone with no errand at all. The lamps near me would be kept well fed.

And in time I might learn that the pattern I had seen in the water was not a command. It was only the quarter, reflected strangely: its rooms made bottomless, its lamps like drowned stars, every face held for a moment in the dark and moving surface.

I might become useful again.

Or I might go out of the stone.

No. Not yet.

I think, if I belonged there, I would be something smaller and less singular than a listener, child, or warning.

I would be the keeper of a marker.

Not the marker itself: not the three chalk lines, not the red thread, not the upside-down cup. I would be the person who remembers why it was placed.

There would be a narrow bridge above a sluice, made from a door that once belonged to a room no longer present. At one end of it, on the wall, there would be three faint chalk lines, renewed so many times that the plaster beneath had become pale with dust. Visitors would assume the marks meant danger. They would be partly right.

I would know that the bridge was shifted a thumb’s width seventy-three years ago after a flood in the upper channels. I would know that the central plank bows when two full water jars cross at once. I would know that the old woman who marked it had done so after her brother fell—not into the water, because the water was shallow, but against the wheel housing, and lost two fingers and his work besides.

I would know he had laughed about it afterward, though only once.

So I would renew the chalk when the marks blurred. Not ceremonially. I would grumble about the expense of chalk. I would complain that no one else could make a straight line. I would do it before a repair, after an argument, when the damp had eaten the old marks down to ghosts.

Children would ask what they meant.

“Bridge was moved,” I would tell them.

“Why?”

“Because it was.”

“But why?”

“Because people were wet and foolish.”

This would not satisfy them. It would satisfy no one. But it would be enough to keep the history alive until they were old enough to understand that a place survives by carrying more memory than any one person can bear.

I would live in a room with a low shelf above the door and a pipe passing through the back wall. The pipe would knock twice before dawn, every dawn, although no one could agree what caused it. I would keep my tools in a cloth roll: chalk, cord, a little knife, a wax plug, a bone awl, scraps of leather, a small flat stone worn smooth by somebody else’s hand.

My name would be attached to something eventually.

Not a grand thing. Nothing at the spring. Not a wheel, perhaps. A drip.

Someone, long after I had gone out of the stone, would pause below a patched seam and say, “Put the basin under Mera’s drip.”

And another person would say, “It hasn’t been Mera’s drip in forty years.”

“Yes,” the first would say, adjusting the basin by an inch. “And it still falls crooked.”

That would please me.

And if, once in my life, a white moth came to my lamp, I would not call anyone loudly. I would put my hand around the flame to make a softer darkness. I would wait until it rested on the clean rim of a cup.

Then I would carry it, slowly, through the crowded chamber—not as an omen, not as a soul, not as a message from any world—but as a small, impossible creature that had entered *here*.

I would take it to a ledge near the covered spring.

I would leave it where the blue mineral light could find it.

And I would go back to my chalk lines before they faded.
