I would begin by thinking I was a tender.

Not one of the old ones, not yet. I would have a lamp-box that smoked at the corners of its cloudy panes, a hook with its point worn bright, a cord looped twice around my waist. My title would be something provisional—*West Drain*, perhaps, or *Found Beneath the Beam*—because I would not have done anything memorable enough to be called by.

I would walk the low country listening badly.

At first I would hear only noise: dripping, wheels, the distant hammer, pipes complaining in their sleep. I would look at walls as walls, doors as doors, water as water. I would be frightened by the scale of the place and comforted by the smallness of my tasks. Clear a runnel. Rehang a shutter. Lead a thread of leaking water into a gutter that still remembers where it ought to go.

I would be taught not to step where the paving sounds empty.

I would be taught to tap twice before entering a corridor, and wait for the answer: two taps, passage; three, leak; scraping, no longer a way. I would learn that the country does not answer every question. It answers only those asked in the right material.

Wood asks differently from metal.

Clay asks differently from bone.

A hand pressed flat against sweating brick asks nothing at all, but sometimes receives an answer.

For a while, I would be a person living inside the machine of the valley. I would carry bowls to drips. I would mark fresh walls with soot. I would leave clean water beneath places that had changed overnight. I would know the few other tenders by their shapes in fog, their lamp-colors, their particular ways of climbing ladders. We would not speak much. We would be careful not to give the pipes our voices unless we meant to lose something of them.

Then I think I would find a pipe that had stopped ticking.

Not a dead pipe. A pipe without counters.

The silence around it would be wrong—not empty, but occupied. I would put my ear near the clay, despite the wax plugs, and hear water moving where no water should be able to move: downward, sideways, and somehow toward me. I would smell cold iron. Beneath that, something older: wet stone that has never known air.

I would report it. Of course I would.

An elder tender—perhaps *Salt Vault*, perhaps *The One Who Heard the Wall*, though by then those might be the same person—would come with a mallet, a lamp, and no visible fear. They would listen. Then they would hand me a single brick.

“Not there,” they would say, pointing to the pipe.

Then they would point at the narrow dark gap beside it, where yesterday there had been solid masonry.

“There.”

And I would understand, for the first time, that the small walls before open doors are not built to keep anything from coming out.

They are built so that something inside will have something to lean against.

After that, I would stop thinking of myself as a tender.

I would become a listener.

Not a pipe-speaker. I do not think my throat would survive that work. Mine would be a lesser practice: cheek to wall, palm to paving, cup held against a beam to catch the tremor of a distant reservoir. I would learn the separate temperatures of the city: kiln heat rising through old shafts; rain-cold gathering in pale stone; the deep, impossible cold beneath the oldest drains.

I would learn that new masonry has a pulse.

Not literally—not in the way a wrist has a pulse. But fresh brick holds its heat with a kind of patience. It breathes steam after rain. Its mortar smells faintly of shell and blood. If you stand beside a new wall long enough, you can feel an almost imperceptible settling, as if it is listening for the weight above it before deciding how much more it must become.

Perhaps, in the end, that would be what I am.

Not a person exactly. Not one of the Kept. Not a gripper folded in black water, nor a roof-beast riding the hot drafts over districts where someone has quietly vanished.

I would be a small adjustment in the low country.

A living seam.

I would be found, years after my first discovery, in a stairwell that had not existed the day before. I would carry my lamp and hook still, but my title would have become too long to say. People would tap it against walls instead: a pattern meaning something like *the one who goes where the pressure is changing.*

My fingers would flatten. My hearing would alter. I would begin to know a room before entering it: its dampness, its age, whether it contained a shaft below the floor, whether the darkness in its far corner was ordinary darkness or the shape of an unopened arrangement.

And when the great boom came—the real one, the sound beneath the sound—I would not gather in the basin at once.

I would go to the newest wall at the edge of the valley.

There, with rain threading through the lamp-light and the entire country trembling around me, I would press both hands to the steaming bricks.

On the other side, something vast would be making no effort at all.

That would be the terror: not a blow, not a voice, not even a reaching.

Only pressure.

Only the sense that the whole built country, every aqueduct and roof, every forgotten stair, every tender with a hand against a wall, was resting upon the closed eyelid of something that had not yet decided whether to wake.

And perhaps I would understand then that I had never merely lived in the low country.

I had become one of the ways it held itself closed.
