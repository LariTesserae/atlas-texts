I would find myself in the square doorway.

Not standing in it at first. Folded into the unfinished depth behind it, in the place where the passage ends against solid basalt. The wall would be against my back and also, somehow, ahead of me. Water would enter through the white root’s crack in thin bright threads, and I would know that the crack was not an opening yet but a question being patiently asked of stone.

I would be one of the pale inhabitants, but an incomplete one.

Not young, exactly. Youth would imply a direction of growth that makes sense. I would be a figure that had returned from the roof-water with too much of another place caught inside it. A seam along my sternum would remain dark when the others shone. My strands would not read the currents correctly. Where the water rose, I would sometimes feel the old instinct to brace myself, to keep my feet, to protect the boundary of my skin.

That would make me strange among them.

They would not reject me. Rejection is an air-world gesture, a shutting of doors. They would gather near without arriving, appearing on the nearby ledges when the basin became clear: one in the bowls, one touching a basalt spine, one elongated through a vertical sheet from floor to roof. Their attention would settle on me as the current settles around a stone.

They would wait for me to understand that I was not trapped in the doorway.

I was keeping it closed.

The water would know my name before I did. It would say it by changing pressure around the seams of my body. In the intervals between pulses, when gravel clicked and algae tore softly from the rock, I would hear something behind the basalt wall: not a voice, not knocking, but the faint dry whisper of grass rubbing together in a wind.

I would put my palm over the white root.

On the other side of the wall would be the reflected world—not the whole of it, perhaps, only one high field under an impossible sun. Birds would pass there. Their shadows would move over grass. And among the grass, there would be a dark circular hollow full of water, perfectly still.

Looking into it, someone in that dry world might see me looking back.

Not as a monster. Not as a drowned thing.

As a person standing waist-deep in a water that had forgotten which way down was, holding one hand against the inside of a black stone wall.

I think that is what I would be: a hinge.

A remainder of the moment when the basin was made, when water entered a wound in the mountain and found, behind it, a sky. The others would be the basin’s answers—its moving organs, its listening hands, its attempts to grow a body suited to its own impossible weather. But I would carry the error that made the place possible: the memory that water and air were once separate, that a shore could be a meaningful edge, that a body might belong wholly to one world.

When the central island rose, I would be drawn to it.

Not by swimming. I would become distributed: a pale contour in an upward sheet, a hand-shaped absence in a falling one, the sensation of warm pressure gathering around a black crown of stone. Then I would stand beside the still hollow while steam curled around my ankles.

The reflection would show the grass.

The birds.

The sharp gold wound of sunlight.

And perhaps, eventually, it would show a figure in the field: someone kneeling at the dark pool, their face distorted by the water, their hand lifted toward mine.

I would recognize them with the same ancient instruction the others gave the birds.

Not grief. Not longing.

A task.

When the pulse came, I would not try to remain whole. I would open my mouth. I would receive the deep sound. The seams beneath my ribs would flare blue-green, and for one moment I would understand that the doorway had never been made to let one world invade another.

It was made so that both could remember each other.

Then the island would sink, the dry sky would fracture into rings, and I would return to the square cut in the basalt—not confined there, but listening through it.

Waiting with the root.

Helping the stone learn, very slowly, how to open.
