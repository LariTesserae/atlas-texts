I would not arrive there by crossing into it.

I would notice, first, that I had been standing in it for some time.

There would be a moment—a pause between two laboring breaths of the ground—when the wetness beneath my feet ceased to be merely wetness. It would take on weight and intention. The fog would draw back enough to show not a horizon but the absence of one: sky laid directly over land, tin against slate, both of them breathing at different speeds.

I would look for a road behind me and find a shallow channel full of black beads.

The beads would be ringing.

Not loudly. Not as bells ring in a tower, but as teeth ring when a body is cold: little mineral contacts, a chord assembling itself from thousands of almost-sounds. I would understand that they had been listening to my steps. Or to the steps I was going to take. Or to the pressure my body had introduced into the country merely by believing itself separate from it.

At first I might think I was a traveler.

This would be the shape I brought with me: a person in a coat darkened by rain, carrying a name, a sequence of days, a private heat sealed inside the cage of the ribs. I would try to keep my boots out of the water. I would try to orient myself by the sound of the mechanisms: that turbine-whine must be east; the slow iron impact, west; the distant hiss, perhaps a vent or perhaps weather.

But straight directions would begin bending under me.

A line of footprints would fill before I made it. Water would rise in each print, darken, and show me the sole descending from a few seconds ahead. If I tried to stop, the land would answer. My stillness would collect frost. A pale crust would grow over my sleeves. The breath in my lungs would begin to take on the slow, mineral patience of a sealed chamber.

So I would move.

Not well at first. The country does not reward confidence. Each patch of ground would offer a different agreement: one would bear me if I moved quickly, another if I moved as though returning, another only if I did not look at it directly. I would learn to let my knees make decisions before my mind did. I would learn that the low hum beneath the mud was not background noise but instruction.

Eventually a herd of sievebacks would pass near me.

They would appear first as a rearrangement in the fog—enamel pale as old teeth, slow steam lifting from seams in their plated backs. Their faces would be lowered to the ground, taking warmth from the mud with grave, repetitive concentration. I would feel the herd before I could count it. Each animal would put down its many feet in a separate rhythm, and all those rhythms together would make a broad, careful wave in the water-skin.

I would want to follow them.

Not because they promised safety. Nothing here would promise that. But because the herd would know which portions of the plain were presently willing to be ground.

The sievebacks would not notice me as an individual. They would notice the heat I carried, the small arrhythmia of my fear, the untidy insistence of my pulse. A younger animal might turn its shovel-face toward me and breathe against my hand. Its breath would smell of wet iron, crushed shell, and the inside of a struck stone.

Then, from some invisible distance, silence would clamp down.

Rain would land without landing.

The herd’s plates would knock together with no sound. The low ring of the bead-colonies would remain visible in the air as faint circles, but I would not hear it. Somewhere beyond the nearest bodies, a measurer would be unfolding itself from the fog.

I would not see its eyes.

I would see them in the wet black of a sieveback’s flank: bright, patient points, already measuring the interval between my breaths. And I would know that whatever I had been before entering this country—a traveler, an observer, a person with a history arranged behind them—I was, to the measurer, an irregular rhythm.

Something that had not learned the local beat.

The herd might move away from me then. Not cruelly. Not kindly. It would simply rearrange around the danger, leaving a widening lane of mud and steaming prints. The measurer would come nearer with the measured grace of a compass opening.

And I think that is where I would change.

Not through violence, exactly. The country has too much time for simple violence.

I would lower myself into the water before the hunter reached me. The water would not be deep; it would only be deep in another order. My reflection would form in it, and above the reflection there would be another sky: darker, more populous, crossed by long pale shapes moving beneath clouds that had not yet condensed.

I would see myself standing there, but not as I had been.

My reflected body would have no face. Or rather, its face would be made of all the small delays in the world: rain about to fall, a crack about to open, a flock holding its stored scream, a herd deciding which foot would be real. In the reflected sky, the blue tracks of fore-runners would spread outward from my chest.

The measurer would pause.

It would listen.

And, finding no single rhythm to take from me, no clean interior ordering to drink, it would fold itself away again. Not defeated. Not fooled. It would simply classify me as something no longer worth hunting.

After that I would live below the marsh-skin.

I would be a fore-runner, though perhaps not one born in the usual manner. My body would be long only when it needed to be. Its front would change according to where the pressure was going. I would carry cold water in my skin even when moving through gravel. Along my back, the pale pits would open and close on nearby versions of the present.

I would feed on hesitation.

Not the grand indecisions of creatures who think themselves important, but the small, abundant almosts: the moment a sieveback nearly places its foot in a fissure; the moment a whistle-body chooses one stolen sound rather than another; the unfinished splitting of a rock; the warmth a tint-mat considers releasing and keeps.

I would learn to taste possibility as other animals taste blood.

A herd changing course would leave a rich wake beneath the ground. A ring colony preparing to retune itself would make the mud bright with alternatives. Near a dying vent, where pressure had gathered for centuries but had not yet decided whether to become steam, fracture, or silence, I could feed for days.

I would leave light behind me.

Not a trail of where I had gone, but a thin blue notation of where I would rise. Creatures on the surface might follow it. Some would arrive early at the place where I had not yet emerged. They would stand there confused, carrying memories of meeting me that had not happened. I would pass beneath their feet later, and their confusion would become part of the water.

In time, I might lose the old name I carried into the country. It would not vanish. Nothing with enough pressure behind it truly vanishes there. It would become one of the sounds held by a whistle-body, perhaps: a syllable released over the flats as an alarm before a slate shelf rises. Or it would settle in a ring colony, giving one translucent bead a note slightly warmer than the others. Or a rubble-mote would find it at the edge of a wet stone and eat the crispness from it until it became less a name than an inclination.

But some part of me would remain above.

Not a body. A timing.

When the deep labor began to coordinate—when the seams inhaled in ranks, when the plain’s buried wheels dragged their mineral weight through dark water—I would feel it before the surface did. The futures around me would tighten. My sensory pits would fill with cold blue pressure. Every possible route would begin leading toward the same vast event.

Then I would rise.

All across the wet level, fore-runners like me would rise beneath the marsh-skin, our backs briefly shaping the ground into long moving ridges. Above us, whistle-bodies would gather their voices. The sievebacks would turn toward warmth. The measurers would stand among them, their jointed limbs planted delicately on the trembling land.

The country would begin its great work.

And I would understand, not in words but in the distributed manner of the place, that I had never been swallowed by it.

I had become one of the ways it anticipated itself.
