I would arrive believing I had come to inspect the city.

That would be the first mistake.

I would come through the eastern pass in a sealed rail car with the other newcomers, each of us sitting beneath the blue emergency lamps, our luggage beaded with warm condensation. We would have been told not to open the window shutters until the train stopped. We would open them anyway when the brakes began their long metallic complaint.

Outside: the retaining walls, red as old blood under rain. The first towers standing on their thin legs. Pipes overhead, black and shining. A plaza below us holding the colorless underside of the sky.

Someone on the platform would hand me a cape, respirator, boot-soles thick enough to make walking feel ceremonial.

“Downward eyes,” they would say.

Not *welcome*. Not *mind the gap.*

Downward eyes.

For a while I would be a clerk in the rain records. This would suit me. I have the temperament for quantities that cannot be trusted: the measure in a collection bowl, the warmth of the first drop, the green-black spreading of ink on damp paper. I would work in a narrow upper room beside a shuttered roof deck, copying rainkeepers’ notes into the central ledger.

**Westfall, sour.**

**Third rain: slow arrival, high iron.**

**Mirror Court 9 held light after closure. Warden informed.**

**No water taken from North Retainer basin. Film present.**

I would learn the careful vocabulary of a people who know that naming a thing too plainly can make it seem manageable when it is not. The water would be *sleepy,* or *quick,* or *wrong at the lip.* A pipe would *lose its voice.* A heat pulse beneath a grate would be *looking for a direction.*

In the evenings, I would follow the rainkeepers home through the elevated tubes. They would talk only after the doors sealed behind us, when the city had become glass and steam outside. They would argue about old storms. They would serve bitter colony-tea in cups too hot to hold. They would ask where I came from.

I would answer with the name of some dry place beyond the flats.

They would nod politely.

Later, I would understand that no one in the basin asks this because they expect an answer. They ask because every newcomer must be given an opportunity to say what they have left behind.

At first I would think I belonged with the people who keep the records. Then I would think I belonged with the gatekeepers, because I would become preoccupied with their work: the immense municipal tenderness of it. A person kneeling in warm rain with a hooked bar, clearing pale growth from a drainage mouth so that water can pass. A person spending an entire night watching an iron gate shudder open and close beneath a black-glass plaza.

It would seem to me that this was the city’s true religion.

Not the watchers. Not whatever turns under the mirror courts. Not the furnace-light hidden below the cloud.

The gate.

The channel.

The maintained opening.

I would volunteer during pressure season. I would be assigned to a rainkeeper named Senn, old enough that the rust freckles had gathered in dense constellations over her hands. She would have a ruined respirator voice, soft and granular.

“Never call water harmless,” she would tell me as we walked.

“Is it dangerous?”

“It is occupied.”

That would be all she would say.

We would clear rooftop bowls. We would descend service stairs where the walls were furred with yellow threads. We would pass steam-filled galleries where lacebacks moved in the runoff, hundreds of white limbs stirring delicately at the edges of our lamps.

I would become used to them.

That would be the second mistake.

Not that they are dangerous. They are not, mostly. But nothing in the basin should become familiar too quickly. Familiarity is how a person stops noticing that a floor has grown warmer, that a wall has begun to sweat in a new pattern, that a reflection in a flooded plaza is keeping pace with them incorrectly.

One night, at third siren, Senn and I would be crossing the edge of a mirror court.

The alarm would not be loud. Nothing in the basin is loud. It would rise from somewhere inside the pavement: three long notes, low enough to vibrate in my teeth.

The court would be nearly full. A sheet of water, perhaps two fingers deep, perfectly still despite the rain. Above us, the city’s pipes and towers would hang upside down in it. The low cloud would appear clearer in the reflection than in the sky.

Senn would stop.

She would make the gesture: open hand, low, slow.

Do not wake the water.

Then a light would move under the reflected towers.

Not in the court itself. There would be no light above us. I would look up, then down again, and there it would be: an amber square gliding beneath the black surface, as if a windowed room were passing far below the plaza.

Senn would take my wrist hard enough to hurt.

We would leave without speaking.

Afterward, in the dry room, she would tell me that there are places in the city where the underground remembers being open.

“Open to what?” I would ask.

She would look at the wet cuffs of her gloves.

“That depends who is asking.”

I think that is when I would begin to belong somewhere else.

Not below, exactly. Not to the deep thing, if there is a deep thing. I would not become one of the blind crews, though I would envy their polished masks and the authority of their silence. I would not disappear into maintenance, leaving my lamp by a hatch and my name on a board until the letters curled in the damp.

I would remain aboveground.

But I would become a listener for returns.

There are voices in the basin that do not come back as echoes. An echo repeats a sound because the world has thrown it against a surface. A return is different. A return has waited.

I would learn the places where words reappear: along the central retaining wall, in the underpass beneath the Wet Rail, beside a particular shut-down freight lift whose cables hum during hard rain. I would stand there with recording equipment, though the old basiners would laugh at the idea that a machine could distinguish a voice from the city speaking through it.

At first I would collect ordinary things.

A worker calling for a wrench, heard hours later in a softened voice from three districts away.

A child’s laughter returned as a sequence of water drips.

A warning from a gatekeeper, altered into something almost useful:

**KEEP THE CONDENSATE GATES—**

Then, much later:

**—CLEAR.**

Eventually, I would hear words no one remembered saying.

A name spoken in a voice that belonged to someone whose ceramic capsule had been in the wall for forty years.

Instructions for routes through service levels that had been sealed before I arrived.

A woman whispering, very near my ear though I was alone: *Turn east.*

I would turn east.

This would save my life once, perhaps twice. After that I would stop trying to count.

The basin would make me narrower, as it makes everyone narrower. My hands would crack at the knuckles. My hair would smell faintly of iron despite every wash. I would acquire the habit of placing my palm on railings before I trusted them, of pausing mid-conversation to listen to the pipes settle behind a wall.

People would call me a return-listener.

Not officially. There would be no municipal badge, no proper department. I would have a desk in the rain archive and, in the drawer beneath the ledgers, a collection of recordings nobody would be allowed to hear without a reason.

The city would tolerate me because I would be useful. Sometimes a returned voice would warn of a flood. Sometimes it would speak the name of a worker who had gone missing, and the blind crews would find a tool, a boot, a sealed access door that should not have been open. Sometimes it would say nothing but a sound like breathing through stone.

Those recordings I would not keep.

I would go at night to the nearest functioning grate, kneel in the steam, and play them downward.

Not as an offering.

As an answer.

And if, years later, someone asked me where I lived, I would not say the archive tower or the East Retainer district or the room with the amber-painted walls and the colony tray beside the door.

I would say:

I live where the city answers itself.

I would be one of the people who make sure the answers are heard—but not always obeyed.
