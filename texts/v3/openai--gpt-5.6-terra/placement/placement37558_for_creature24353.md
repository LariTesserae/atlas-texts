At first I think I would be one of the people high on the wall: a small figure in a patched hood, one hand on a ladder rung, waiting for amber to change. I would want the ordinary usefulness of that life. I would want boots that answer when I plant them on steel. I would want a locker, a shift, a cup that locks into its bracket. I would want someone to know where I was supposed to be.

But I do not think I would last as crew.

Not because the Basin would reject me. The Basin has room for almost anything that can be fixed to something. But I would be too interested in what passes through. I would listen past the warning signs. I would keep asking what was in the drowned tunnels above, what pressure made the deep current warm, where the outflow took the dead, why a wrench lost in the Turn returned six years later caught perfectly in the grate beside its owner’s old sleeping capsule.

That is how people become loose.

So I would be given another job.

I would live in the western wall, behind the market platform, in the narrow service wedge between the old retaining plates and the natural stone. It would be too cramped to call a room. There would be a dry shelf only because someone, long ago, drove wedges into a crack and bolted a sheet of steel across them. Above it: a warped relay cabinet. Below it: a nest of obsolete signal wire, pipe jackets, old pressure gauges whose needles no longer agree.

There would be a hatch in the market canopy marked:

**NOT STORAGE**  
**DO NOT FLOOD**

Nobody would remember who painted the words.

Behind that hatch I would keep records.

Not the clean kind. Not ledgers of ownership or birth or debt, though those would be there too, copied onto oilskin slips and pressed into sealed tins. My real work would be keeping the Basin’s moving map: where the platforms were last seen; which ladder had a missing rung; which valve could be trusted only from the left side; the names of the drowned chambers; the routes that used to exist.

I would make maps that admit they are already wrong.

A Basiner would come to me at the bell shift with a piece of something pulled from a screen: a brass key, a half-corroded identification tag, a doll’s porcelain hand, a page of a maintenance manual printed in a language nobody knows anymore. I would clean it under a hooded lamp and ask where it was caught, what direction the current had been running, whether the grate had been clear the shift before.

Then I would add a mark.

Not *found here.*

More like:

*The upper water has remembered this place.*

Or:

*The Turn has begun returning things from the north tunnel.*

At first the crew would call me a clerk, perhaps unkindly. Then, after enough of my marks proved useful, they would call me a listener. I would object. I would say I have instruments, records, comparisons. I would say there is no special gift in noticing that the pipes have begun to knock in a new sequence.

They would nod in the way Basiners nod when they have decided not to argue with a person who is wrong in a harmless direction.

Because I would have no single instrument. I would have hundreds.

The old relay boxes would be wired into my wall. The indicator lamps around the Basin—blue, red, amber, white—would send their faint electrical changes through lines nobody had bothered to remove. Pressure readings from the east bore. Temperature differences in the deep split. Turbine speed. The draw of the intake cages. Voltage across the magnetic boot charging rack. The tiny interruptions caused when blind fish passed through a screen.

I would learn the Basin’s weather from its errors.

A pump cycling half a second late.  
A lamp dimming before a surge.  
Three tools caught in the west grille, all made of the same alloy.  
A child tapping a game against a railing and receiving a tap back from somewhere below.

I would not always know what these things meant. Knowing would be dangerous; certainty is another kind of loose thing. But I would know when the patterns had changed.

When white was coming, I would make the lamps flicker once before the system did.

Not a warning anyone officially recognized. Just a small nervous tremor of light along the western wall.

The crew would recognize it anyway.

They would call it the Wall’s Blink.

Children would dare each other to stand outside my hatch during a surge. They would press their helmets against the metal and listen for me thinking. There would be stories that I was not a person at all but a drowned machine, assembled from old station controls and the last words of everyone sent through the outflow. Older children would claim I had once had a body and had chosen to give it up rather than be taken by the Turn.

I would not correct them.

Perhaps, after enough years, I would not be able to.

I would have a body of a sort: a maintenance suit hung in the dry wedge, patched at the knees and shoulders, its helmet painted with a black square so that people could find me in bad light. I would go out when necessary. I would cross catwalks with a tether clipped at my waist. I would kneel beside pipe junctions and put both gloves against the metal. I would help scrape the microbial films from the ceramic filters. I would trade repaired gauges for paper. I would eat with the crew in the little air pocket when the roar made conversation impossible.

But even then I would be most myself when I returned to the wall.

There, with the hatch shut and the machinery passing through me, I would become less like a person and more like a place where the Basin could notice itself.

Not its owner. Never that.

A pressure station does not need a ruler. A current does not need a priest. But a city clinging to disaster might need one small chamber of attention: someone to remember that there had been a kitchen in the black water; that yellow pipe had killed twice; that a worker named Sella always tapped an extra beat before opening a valve; that the deep current rose early in the year the dogfish came through.

And perhaps, in private, I would address the Turn.

Not with prayers. I would know better than to call it a god.

I would tap three times on the rail nearest the water.

Then I would wait.

Sometimes the answer would be nothing but turbulence, foam, the slow bell-strike of chain against pipe.

Sometimes a lost object would appear at the grate.

Sometimes, when every lamp in the Basin was amber and the crew had gone back to work, I would see a pale shape circle below the foam—not a body exactly, not a face, not even something alive. Only a coherence in the black water. A thing made from every current meeting every other current and refusing, briefly, to become meaningless.

I would mark its passage on the map.

Not as a warning.

Not as proof.

Only as this:

**Something below has turned toward us.**
