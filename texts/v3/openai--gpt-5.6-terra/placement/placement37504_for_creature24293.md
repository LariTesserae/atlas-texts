I think I would first mistake myself for a Tender.

Not one of the strong ones who can shoulder a root-cutting saw or walk a flooded conduit by smell, but a person in an upper maintenance room, sleeping beneath old diagrams and waking whenever a lamp changes color. I would keep notebooks full of things that do not fit ordinary inventories:

- three knocks in the west pressure line means the crabs are moving a blockage;  
- switchblooms closing from north to south means the fault is aboveground;  
- no moths at Lamp 19 means warmth is leaking somewhere it should not;  
- if the reed-hounds go quiet, do not take the low path.

I would believe that writing these things down made me useful.

But the Exchange would eventually correct me.

Paper would mildew. Ink would feather into blue threads. A pipe-bird would steal the metal clip from my notebook and carry it to its nest. The lists would become less reliable than listening. And after enough time beneath the city, I think I would stop being a person who records the Exchange and become one of the things by which it records itself.

I would be a pipe-bird, perhaps—not born as one, exactly, but recruited.

A narrow slate-feathered bird with hooked toes and a beak too hard for its own good. I would make my nest in the broken speaker system over an old service platform, where station announcements once came through. There would still be fragments of voices in the wiring: *stand clear,* *service delayed,* *mind the gap,* names of streets that no longer lead anywhere. I would learn them before I learned my own call.

At first I would repeat them badly.

Then accurately.

Then in combinations no human dispatcher ever used: the sigh of a closing train door followed by the warning chirp of a medical monitor; an obsolete factory whistle under the soft clatter of rain on a grate; a dead woman’s cough threaded through the three-note flood alarm. Other pipe-birds would hate me for this. They would come to my platform and hammer at the speaker cones with their beaks. I would answer with the sound of a descending elevator and drive them away.

But my real work would begin during the dangerous silences.

When the lamps fail and the warm machinery seems to hold its breath, when the frogs stop barking and the mold-light dims to a sick blue, I would fly down toward the rotor chamber with the Listeners.

They would bring their drums, their conduit organs, their cracked brass horns. I would bring sounds.

Not merely noise—noise is easy underground—but the remembered shape of movement. The turnstile click. The escalator hum. The rhythm of boots crossing a wet platform at six in the morning. A child laughing through a vent. A bus kneeling at a curb. Rain ticking on hundreds of umbrellas. The city’s upper breath, carried below.

I would perch somewhere in the root dome, close enough that the warmth from the machine lifted my breast feathers, and give those sounds back to it.

Perhaps the rotor would turn.

Perhaps it would only seem to turn because the roots around it tightened and the moths rose in a pale cloud. Perhaps the boars would come first, their clay backs steaming, and then the grazers with their small hanging gardens, and finally the reed-hounds, silent for once, placing their jaws against the floor to listen.

I would not know whether the machine understood me.

That would not matter.

In the Green Exchange, understanding is not the only form of exchange. A sound can be food. A warning can be shelter. A remembered thing can become a root for something living to climb.

So I would be a keeper of borrowed voices: a pipe-bird with a nest full of lost city-noises, flying the high dark routes beneath the vegetal vault, returning what has been forgotten to the engine that refuses to let the underground world go dry.
