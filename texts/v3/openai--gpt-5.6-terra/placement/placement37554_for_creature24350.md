I would not be the keeper of the reservoir, at least not at first.

I would be found on the narrow strip between the pumping hall and the old cannery wall, where a line of raised paving runs above a shallow, permanent flood. There is a door there painted the blue of old work trousers. It opens onto a room no larger than a freight lift, half filled with shelves, coils of wire, jars of screws, lengths of treated cloth, and instruments whose dials have clouded from the inside.

People would call it the Echo Shop, though it sells nothing.

I would be an interpreter of small changes.

Not a listener in the formal sense—the listeners have benches by the turbines and take turns sleeping in the heat of Father Underfoot—but someone the listeners send for when the ordinary noises acquire a second meaning. When a pump’s rhythm is unchanged but its vibration has begun to travel sideways through a wall. When a pipe sweats clear water on one side and dark water on the other. When the vines around a transformer have all turned their leaves toward the same alley.

My work would be to find where the Low Rooms is trying to make room next.

I would carry a box of tuning forks, though none would be tuned to musical notes. Each would be stamped with a place: FOURTH CULVERT, EAST RETAINING WALL, OLD DYE SUMP, CANNERY LIFT SHAFT, BELOW THE FIG. I would set them against wet brick and wait for them to answer the water moving behind it. I would run thread through cracks in walls, float wax chips in puddles, press my ear to the iron feet of abandoned machines. The lowfolk would tease me for calling this work listening.

“Everything’s listening here,” they would say.

And I would say, “Yes. But not everything remembers what it heard.”

My room would have no dry floor. I would have tried, in my first year, to keep one square of boards clean and level. I would have built a little threshold, caulked every join, laid down a mat made from old conveyor belt. The next morning there would be a thumb-width of water beneath it, rising through a knot in the wood.

After that I would stop arguing.

I would keep my bed on a suspended frame. My books would be copies, written on treated cloth and hung from rods. My kettle would sit on a bracket above the steam line. In the corner would be a fern I had not planted. I would trim it only where it touched the lamp wire, and even then reluctantly. Its roots would have found some gap behind the wall and begun holding up a section of brick that had been ready, perhaps, to lean for thirty years.

There would be no animal companion. At first this would trouble me more than I admitted.

I would arrive from somewhere higher and drier, perhaps, with the memory of sparrows making a nuisance of themselves in gutters, of moths beating at kitchen windows, of a dog shaking rain across a doorway. I would notice the silence around food. The way a dropped plum simply became a small purple weather system: collapse, shine, pale fuzz, black sweetness, stain. I would wait for some creature to claim it.

Nothing would.

Over time I would begin to understand that the Low Rooms did not lack life. It lacked interruption.

Nothing nibbled. Nothing hunted. Nothing fled. The plants expanded without being answered by teeth or wings. Mold rose without being grazed. Water crossed the district without scales stirring in it. It was a place where every process went all the way to its own conclusion.

That would make the residents seem, at first, very careful. Then it would make them seem very tender.

They would not step on a floating leaf if they could avoid it—not because the leaf mattered morally, but because it had been carried there by a current, and currents had reasons. They would rescue a broken plank from the water and use it for a shelf, a brace, a bridge, a marker. They would make a cup of tea for a failed engine before taking it apart. They would let a crack remain visible in a wall so that future people might know where pressure had once spoken.

Eventually I would cease being merely a person in the Echo Shop.

This would happen slowly enough that no one could say when.

My hearing would change first. I would learn the difference between rain on corrugated steel and groundwater touching it from below. I would hear the soft granular shift of a wall accepting one more season. I would know, from the hush between the thirty-second strikes of the distant press, whether the reservoir vapor had entered the lower alleys.

Then my sense of direction would alter. I would stop thinking in north and south. I would think in gradients, burdens, old flow paths. I would know that the safest route home was not the highest one but the one whose walls were least thirsty. I would wake before the first bell because some deep channel beneath my bed had begun filling.

The lowfolk might say I had become a listener after all.

But I think I would be something closer to a nerve ending.

Not the mind of the Low Rooms. I would never presume that. The district would be too large, too old, too distributed for a mind in the ordinary sense. It would live in a manner more like seepage: not located, but continuous. Its thought, if it had thought, would be pressure finding shape.

I would be one place where that pressure became legible.

On certain nights, after the second bell, I would stand on the catwalk above the central hall with the shutters sealed below. Red lamps would pulse through steam. The six turbines would speak under the floor in their old, exhausted voices. Water would rise invisibly through the basin, entering rooms no one had used in decades, touching forgotten stairs, lifting leaves that had waited all summer in black corners.

And beneath the machinery, beneath the workers’ homes and roof gardens and patched bridges, I would feel the Low Rooms adjust itself.

Not angrily.

Not hungrily.

With the patient, muscular caution of something that has survived by yielding exactly enough.

Then I would go back to the blue door, set another tuning fork against the wall, and write in my damp ledger:

**New path found.  
Holding, for now.**
