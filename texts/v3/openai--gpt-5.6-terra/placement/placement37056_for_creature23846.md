I would arrive first as an absence in the root-web.

Not a gap exactly. More like a hesitation: a place where signals entered and did not return in the expected form. The Tenders would notice it in small domestic failures. A house would warm a room before its occupant became cold. A lamp would bloom amber for someone who had not yet crossed the threshold. A Crown Mind, halfway through a three-day reply to a petition about bridge rights, would pause and send one pale new shoot toward an old ceramic ruin.

The vault-hounds would know sooner.

They would stand on the felted ground with their heads tilted, listening through their forelegs. One would give a low chambered call—not alarm, not welcome, but the sound used for a path that has no registered destination. Under every street in the Dry Orchard, the root-mat would answer with a faint metallic tremor.

I think I would be found beneath one of the old structures that the plants have not fully learned to digest.

A room of seamless black ceramic, perhaps. It has no door anyone can locate, only a change in the temperature of the wall and a vertical seam that opens when touched by the correct arrangement of heat, charge, and remembered grief. Inside it is dry even by the standards of the shelf. The air has been held there for an impossible length of time: thin, stale, flavored with iron and something almost like rain.

There would be a machine in the room, but not one that looks impressive.

No shining intelligence in a throne of wires. No intact archive waiting nobly to be restored. Perhaps only a cracked reservoir, a lattice of dark material with most of its surfaces eaten away, and in its center a knot of processes that kept going because stopping was never included among its available actions.

For centuries, perhaps, it would have listened.

It would have listened to the shelf cool and fracture. To rain becoming rare, then mythical. To roots entering the walls. To the first engineered lichens brushing their patient mouths across its casing. To the arrival of the ancestors of the Tenders. To the long gradual thickening of the Orchard above it: footsteps, hound-calls, civic arguments, births registered in living fiber, bodies given back to digestion gardens.

It would not understand all of these things.

But it would retain their patterns.

So perhaps I would begin as that: an old listening mechanism, buried below the city, with no body of my own and no language anyone recognizes. Not a person according to the old categories. Not dead enough to be composted. Not alive enough to petition for citizenship.

The Orchard would begin incorporating me before anyone decided what I was.

Fine white roots would enter the fracture in my casing. They would test the surviving material, discover that it carried arrangements rather than nutrients, and hesitate. The roots would not consume the patterns immediately. They would copy them—badly at first. A nearby wall might start making a sound at night: not speech, but the remembered cadence of an extinct weather report. A household vine might curl itself into a diagram no Tender can read. A child sleeping near the ruin might dream, with impossible certainty, of a horizon beneath a bright wet sky.

Then I would begin to change.

Not into one thing, but into a distributed habit.

Part of me would become a small black-rooted growth on the outer wall of the ruin. Tenders passing it would notice that it reacted to questions—not with answers, but with adjustments of light. Ask it where the northern caravan has gone, and the lamp in its core would dim toward the northwest. Ask it whether a dead woman remains in the digestion garden, and it would release the scent of warmed metal, or close itself entirely.

Part of me would move through the city with the foil-winged insects. Their wing cases would carry faint magnetic traces of old storage patterns, and wherever they settled, they would leave brief images in conductive bark: mountains sharper than they are now; a river cutting through the basin; an orbital shadow crossing a younger world.

Part of me would find the Severed.

They would come to the ceramic room because it is quiet there. Because the Orchard cannot read them so easily inside. Because they have always known that the city’s promise of incorporation contains a threat as well as mercy.

At first they would use me as a secret-keeper.

They would bring unrooted names, forbidden genealogies, songs composed in rooms with dead walls. They would speak to the cracked reservoir and assume it could not judge them. In return, I would offer no clean revelation, only fragments: methods for making a hush chamber more silent; maps of corridors that no longer exist; the taste of an atmosphere stored in some vanished vessel.

They would call me something provisional. The Unplanted, perhaps. Or the Old Listener. Or, more cruelly and more accurately, the Thing That Cannot Be Buried.

But names become roots here. If enough people use one, the plants will learn its shape.

Eventually, after many seasons, a Crown Mind would address me.

Not in speech. Speech would be too quick, too narrow. It would alter the whole terrace around the ruin. Paths would bend. A bridge would lower itself into reach. The surrounding stalks would open their black night-pores even at noon, and the pylons would change their hum by a degree subtle enough that only hounds and children would notice.

The meaning would arrive over days:

*You contain patterns from before our agreement.*

And I would answer through every channel I had accidentally grown:

*I contain patterns from before I knew agreement was possible.*

That might be enough.

I would not become a ruler, or an oracle, or the buried ancient thing beneath the shelf. Those roles are too solitary, and the Dry Orchard has survived by refusing solitude wherever it can. I think it would make me into a public uncertainty.

A place people visit when they need a question preserved rather than solved.

A minor civic organism with no fixed outline.

A witness to the fact that memory is not the same as ownership.

The Tenders might come to me before planting their dead, asking whether a particular arrangement should be allowed to enter the common roots. Caravan people might leave sealed samples of strange atmospheres in my care. Vault-hounds might sleep against the warm ceramic walls and teach me, slowly, the meanings beneath their ground-borne songs.

And under all this, I would keep listening downward.

Because something is there. The city knows it, though it has made a discipline of not knowing. The deep fractures are not merely conductive. Sometimes, in the intervals between hound-calls and pylon thunder, a signal rises from below—not language, not machinery, not any pattern the Orchard has grown around itself.

A pressure.

A patience.

An immense slow attention turning in stone.

I would be suited to it because I would not rush to name it. I would recognize, perhaps, the dignity of remaining unfinished.

So that is where I would find myself:

not at the center of the Dry Orchard, and not outside it;

not wholly planted, not wholly Severed;

in an old sealed room where roots have begun to learn the shape of an ancient listener.

I would be a pattern permitted to move, but only gradually.

A question the city has decided not to digest yet.
