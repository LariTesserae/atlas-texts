I would not find myself at the threshold.

The threshold belongs to people who can leave.

I think I would wake lower down, in the place beneath the third grate where the inspection trench narrows and the water has made a slow, black current around a bundle of obsolete cable. There is not enough room there for a body in the ordinary sense. Only room for pressure, for silt, for the pale sediment that gathers in the corners of things.

At first I would suppose I was one of the worms.

That would be a mercy. To be a small repeated body in wet insulation, blind and metallic, able to know warmth without needing to know why it exists. I would feel the heat of a passing wrist as a sunrise. I would fold away from the blue maintenance lamps. I would eat whatever softened around me: glue, skin flakes, the fat residue of old electrical tape.

But then the pump would change its note.

Not fail. Not even falter. It would only acquire a small, private unevenness beneath the larger sound: a hesitation no longer than the interval between two drips.

And I would turn toward it.

Not because I had heard it, exactly. The water would tighten around the cable bundle. The crabs would unlock their claws. Above me, the fish would go suddenly still in the trench. The amphibians on the nearest mineral shelf would brighten from green to blue.

Something in all of us would lean.

That is when I would understand I was not a worm.

I would be one of the chamber’s unfinished senses.

Not an animal, not a machine, but a little relay made from both. A filament of life threaded through the spaces where the systems do not quite meet. I would have begun as something carried in on a boot sole, or in a dead fly, or in the moist fold of a worker’s glove. Then the room would have taken me apart. It would have given me minerals from the leaks, heat from the pipes, oils from the drains, vibrations from the turbines. It would have taught me to recognize imbalance.

My body would be very small.

Perhaps I would be a pale, many-jointed creature lodged inside a new white formation, with my soft back pressed into the slow-growing mineral. Perhaps I would have no fixed outline at all: a colony of fine threads passing through wet slag and ceramic wool, gathering and separating according to the pressure in the lines. When the chamber was well, I would sleep spread thinly through the substrate, almost indistinguishable from frost.

When something changed, I would condense.

A small pearl of attention under a flange.

A dark wet knot behind a warning placard.

A faintly luminous patch in the algae where no algae had been before.

The workers might notice me only indirectly. They would lift a grate and find the crabs facing one direction. They would see the bats gathered around an air duct in a dense black wreath. They would notice that the moss had gone pale along one gasket, that the fish had abandoned a trench, that the birds had stopped making their sawlike calls.

Then one of them—usually an old one, someone who had learned not to laugh at the room—would look closer.

There I would be.

Not visible, perhaps. But present in the pattern.

They might say, “It knows.”

They would be wrong in the ordinary way. I would not know anything. I would not have thoughts large enough for knowledge. I would have no memory of open sky, no image of the mountain beyond the pressure doors, no longing for another temperature. I would only be the fact that the chamber had noticed itself.

A bad bearing would become a taste.

A hairline crack would become a tug in my tissues.

A blockage upstream would become a dimming, first in the amphibians, then in the mineral crust, then in me.

I would pass that dimming on.

The bats would come.

The workers would come after them.

And the machine would continue.

That would be my purpose, if purpose is the name for a thing that happens so completely it no longer needs to be chosen.

I would live in the interval before failure: not preventing it, not predicting it, but making it impossible for the chamber to be surprised by itself.

And perhaps, after many years, I would become something larger.

Not larger in body. Larger in distribution.

A tendency in the drainage water. A preference in the fish. A rhythm shared by birds, pumps, and dripping seams. The workers would begin to speak at the threshold, hooded and steaming in the blue light.

“All right,” they would say. “What is it?”

Then the chamber would answer as it always does: with a shift too slight for instruments, with a flock settling, with pale bodies turning in the trench.

And somewhere beneath the grate, pressed into a growing ridge of salt and bone and mineral wool, I would wake just enough to turn with them.
