I would find myself not at the center of that country, because it has no center, but along one of the old routes that is older than any traveler: a water-filled trough pressed into the peat by generations of mirehorn feet.

At first I would think I was walking it.

Then I would realize it had been walking me.

The route would begin in a high basin where the moss is red-brown with iron and the rain never quite reaches the ground as rain. It comes down through layers of sedge, through the backs of small flying things, through the loose woven coats of grazing animals, through my own hair. I would be wet before I knew I had arrived. My clothes would not be mine for long. The hems would gather green threads. A pale fungus would settle in the seam of one glove and make a soft light whenever I closed my hand.

I think I would be one of the people, but not cleanly.

Not a hunter, exactly. Not a herder. Not one of those who knows how to call the reed-backed walkers out of deep water, nor one of the bold old women who can stand near the weather-eaters’ feeding grounds and tell, from the taste of the fog, whether they are hungry for heat or memory.

I would be a keeper of delays.

There must be such people in that country: people whose work is to prevent things from becoming available too soon.

When a rampart begins to close around a sleeping traveler, I would warm the stems with my hands and sing the small, irregular songs that make them hesitate. When someone’s boots begin to root too deeply into the moss, I would kneel with a bone knife and cut the new fibers free—not destroying them, only returning them to the mat. When an old mirehorn lies down in a basin and its shoulder-gardens begin to flower in the wrong season, I would wait beside it, listening for the slow internal decision: rest, sickness, birth, death, weather.

The country would know me as warmth in motion.

Not important warmth. Not fire. More like the residual heat beneath a stone after an animal has moved away.

I would travel with one particular mirehorn for many years. It would be old before I met it, though age would be hard to distinguish from weathering. Its coat would carry a whole slope of life: gray feather-grass along the spine; dark cups of rainwater at the shoulders; insect colonies under the fiber shelves; birds that nested in the folds of its neck and screamed whenever fog gathered too quickly.

I would call it something that could be spoken only once, in the exact rhythm of its gait. After that, I would know it by the pressure of its steps through the wet ground.

It would not belong to me. I would not belong to it. But we would become a temporary agreement.

In the evenings, when the underground lights began to move, I would climb into the hollow between its shoulder shelves. There I would sleep among roots and discarded feathers, with the slow warmth of its body below me. In dreams, I would hear the country thinking—not in sentences, not even in images, but in changes of pressure. A distant basin filling. A buried peat fire going out. The enormous soft turn of a weather-eater gathering itself beyond the next ridge.

Sometimes I would wake knowing where not to go.

Sometimes I would wake knowing where something had been waiting for me for hundreds of years.

There would be places in the old peat where human footsteps had compressed the underlayers into durable paths. A person long dead could still be felt there: not as a ghost, but as a preference in the ground. Water chose their direction. Roots thickened along the line they had walked. Moths emerged from the moss in colors matching the fibers they once wore.

I would follow such paths.

Perhaps that would be my real work: not delaying incorporation, but finding the people who had become available without being forgotten. I would carry no written record. Paper would soften; stone would disappear under lichen; metal would sweat green. Instead I would carry small living things from one remembered place to another: a tuft of moss from an elder’s sleeve, a clutch of insects from a dead child’s collar, a handful of black soil warmed in a bowl against my chest.

I would make reunions.

A little of a person’s former life returned to the place that had taken them.

Not because anyone required it. The country does not require gratitude. But because memory, here, would be ecological. A voice retained in a vent rhythm might need the smell of its old companion’s coat. A grove grown in the shape of a skull might flower more fully if touched with water from the pool where its owner was born. The dead would not be elsewhere. They would be dispersed, busy, and still susceptible to recognition.

Eventually, though, I would begin to change in a way companions could see.

The green nap at my wrists would become thick enough to comb. Lichen would darken my fingernails. Small transparent mites would live harmlessly in the folds behind my knees. When I stood still, the moss would rise slightly around my feet—not gripping, merely testing the possibility of me.

The younger people would joke that I had become too hospitable.

The older people would stop joking.

I would keep moving then, longer than was wise. I would follow the mirehorn through rain-black valleys and over ridges where the ramparts bent all in one direction, listening. I would wake before dawn to scrape rootlets from my ankles. I would brush the spores from my eyelashes. I would tell myself that I was needed as a keeper of delays.

But the country would not be urging me. That is the important thing.

It would only be making room.

And one evening, perhaps after the old mirehorn had finally gone down into a luminous basin and allowed its shoulder-gardens to become a permanent island, I would understand that I had spent my whole life treating arrival as a thing to postpone.

I would leave my knife in the peat.

I would walk into a low place where the water shone under its film of plants. I would stand among black pools and bent reeds while birds turned slowly in rings overhead. The lights beneath the ground would move uphill toward me, green along one branch, amber along another.

Then I would wait.

Not to die. Not to become a tree, because there are no trees there in the old sense.

I think I would become a place where people can pause without being taken too quickly.

A warm hollow in a moss-rampart. A patch of root lattice that recognizes tired feet. A ridge of peat that rises above floodwater when an exhausted animal lies down. Something with enough remnant personhood to hesitate when it feels a traveler’s breath, and enough country in it to know what shelter means.

When the wind changed, I would lean with everything else.

Not toward an order.

Toward the sound of something arriving.
