I would not live high up, where the goats have the stairs and the air is sharp enough to make the cables sing clearly. Nor would I belong to the central wards, where every surface is wet and every apartment has three families’ noise moving through its walls.

I think I would be found in the middle elevations, in one of the old signal rooms built into a retaining wall beneath an elevated freight line.

The room would have been intended for one switch operator and a relay cabinet. It would have a narrow iron balcony, a roof that sweats in rain, and one window looking across the basin toward the chemical works. By day the view would be smoke, pigeons, and cables disappearing into low cloud. At night, it would be the slow shifting of lights: rail lamps moving along the viaduct, yellow windows in the quarry dwellings, the red warning beacons at the flood machinery, a cable carriage climbing toward the eastern platforms.

I would be a cable listener.

Not an official title in the grand sense. Not an engineer, exactly, though engineers would sometimes ask my opinion after pretending not to. I would walk the service ways beneath the cable lines with a waxed notebook, a tuning fork, a coil of marking wire, and a small hammer for tapping housings. I would know the ordinary voices of the city’s climbing machinery: the low load-hum of the freight lifts, the high strained note of a carriage carrying too many passengers, the intermittent cough that means water has reached a bearing housing, the softened shiver of a cable beginning to fray beneath its grease.

Most people would think the cables all sound alike. They would hear wind and steel and call it noise.

But I would know that the line above the old market has a tired B-flat in wet weather. The quarry lift would have a doubled pulse when the upper wheel is iced. The western industrial cable would always carry a faint granular rasp from mineral dust, except in the week after a heavy wash, when it would sound almost young. I would know when a carriage is coming before its lamps appear, and sometimes I would know who is inside it by the weight and rhythm of its ascent: the morning bakers with flour sacks, the school group from the reservoir village, the buffalo handlers returning with mud stiff on their cuffs.

I would live with a drainage dog.

She would not be mine in the full possessive sense. No one owns a drainage dog enough to say so. She would arrive one winter with one ear nicked and her paws raw from the brick channels, sleep under my workbench for three nights, and then decide the room was part of her route. I would call her Silt, because that is what she would leave wherever she shook herself dry.

Silt would accompany me along the service ledges and vanish whenever we reached an old drain mouth. She would know passages beneath the retaining walls that did not appear on any municipal plan. On certain mornings she would return wet to the shoulders and carrying impossible things: a child’s mitten, a bent spoon, a pigeon wing, a length of red cord, once a valve tag from a district two kilometers away.

The signal room would gather other residents without formally admitting them. Cats would sleep in the warm recess behind the relay cabinets. Pigeons would occupy the external bracework and mutter through every weather. A pair of goats might shelter beneath the balcony during hard rain, their backs pressed against the wall, bells clicking when they rose. In the evenings, bats would pass the window in quick dark strokes, heading outward toward the market lights.

My work would be partly mechanical and partly diplomatic.

A cable failure is never just a cable failure in that city. It means feed delayed at the upper goat wards. It means a rail crew stranded beside a flooded cut. It means a grandmother unable to reach the clinic without taking three hundred wet stairs. It means crates of lowland vegetables spoiling beneath tarpaulins. It means the buffalo teams waiting at an embankment because the harness fittings are still in a carriage halfway up the basin.

So I would learn the names of people who otherwise would be only voices on a crackling radio.

“Listener, is the East Rise safe for a loaded car?”

“Listener, can you hear if the line is slipping?”

“Listener, there’s a goat caught in the lower pulley housing.”

“Listener, the number four carriage is full of schoolchildren.”

And I would listen.

Sometimes I would say, “Stop the line.”

Sometimes, “Run it empty once.”

Sometimes, “Put two workers at the west brake and tell them not to trust the gauge.”

Sometimes I would say nothing at all, because the cable would be speaking in a way that required me to stand still in the rain and hear the difference between danger and merely age.

I think that is what I would be in this place: not a maker of grand plans, not a ruler, not even a person especially visible from the street. I would be one of the city’s interpreters.

The basin is full of things that cannot speak in human language but make their needs known nevertheless: pipes knocking below pavement, buffalo shifting before a storm, goats refusing a staircase whose stones have gone slick, pigeons lifting suddenly from a bridge rib, water rising behind a grate, an old wall becoming dark in the wrong shape.

I would live by attending to those messages.

And after enough years, perhaps people would say that I had become a little like the city itself: patched at the cuffs, smelling faintly of oil and rainwater, slow to make promises, able to remember which sounds belonged to ordinary endurance and which meant that something—some line, some animal, some household, some whole hanging district—needed help before it broke.
