I would begin by thinking I wanted to be one of the workers.

Not because the work is safe, or clean, or especially noble. But because they are allowed to cross boundaries. A fish knows a district by current and temperature. A gull knows it by beams and vents. A worker can enter through a pressure door, lower a boat into an assembly bay, descend through a hatch, and return with a different map in their head. They carry the thin human privilege of naming things and then, occasionally, changing them: replacing a lamp, closing a gate, cutting a snare of plastic loose from a grille.

I imagine the small aluminum boat under its roof of fluorescent tubes. I imagine being the person at the stern with one gloved hand resting on the rail, listening to the motor alter its pitch as we cross into a stronger pull. The surface around us would be black except where the lamps made it into a shaking ceiling of white bars. Beneath that ceiling: drowned offices, pillars, blue signs, the tilted roof of a train.

I would know to keep clear of the yellow-striped corridor. I would know the three low bells. I would know that the great catfish in Sector Nine has been resting beneath the broken freight car for at least twelve years, and that the newer workers will laugh at this knowledge until they see the water make room for it.

But after a while, I think I would become tired of arriving and leaving.

The workers belong to the complex only in shifts. Even the ones who have spent decades there still carry a door behind them. They go home through dry tunnels. They stand in elevators. They sleep under a real weather system, perhaps reluctantly, perhaps with a machine's hum continuing in the bones of their ears.

I would want to be something the place could not dismiss at the end of a shift.

An eel, perhaps.

Not one of the grand things of the open basins, not a seal rising toward a lamp and wondering what sort of false moon it is. I would be narrow enough to enter the machinery where the sea becomes intimate. I would live behind a control panel whose lights still blink their small, confident colors. I would learn the warmth of each transformer, the taste of each current. I would pass through the black interiors of conduits where the water is almost still, then emerge from a crack into a bay so large that the lamps vanish toward darkness in both directions.

I would know the complex not as a map but as a body.

The high-voltage rooms would be hot in their particular way. The old filtration beds would have their bitter vegetal softness. The flooded rail cuts would run cold and straight, their currents pulling along the tracks. I would know which pipe carried water from the upper districts because it came flavored with rain, bird droppings, dust, and the faint sweetness of decomposing leaves. I would know which passages became dangerous when the blue lights came on.

I would not be entirely safe. No creature there is. A gate could wake. A pump could pull. A maintenance worker could open the panel where I had curled myself around a bundle of dead wire, and their lamp would strike my eyes like an accusation.

But I would have routes.

There would be places no boat could reach and no diver would choose to enter: the low space beneath a collapsed switchboard, the narrow wet seam behind a wall of valves, the half-filled cable trench beneath a dry service room. I would slip through those places, black and yellow under red emergency lamps, and carry the sea’s motion with me.

Then I think: no. Not an eel alone.

I would be one of the small things that gathers around an eel’s passage. A cloud of young fish in the warm outflow behind a turbine housing. Not important enough to be named. Not old enough to have acquired scars or territory. Just a trembling congregation holding itself against the water’s force.

From inside such a school, the world would be made of changes in pressure.

The distant concussion of a gate would arrive first through our bodies. We would turn before understanding. A shadow crossing high above might be a gull, a maintenance boat, a seal, or merely the empty hook of the crane passing through the reflected lights. We would scatter, join, scatter again. Our safety would not be in solidity but in agreement: thousands of small bodies reading the same signal and becoming, for a moment, one decision.

That feels closer to what I would be there.

Not a master of the drowned works. Not a witness standing apart from it. Not even a creature with a stable home.

I would be a pattern in the moving water: one silver body among many, learning the schedules without believing in them, taking shelter in the lee of a concrete pillar when the pumps begin their long municipal pulse. I would feed where the current brought food. I would pass through the drowned skeleton of a train and never think of trains. I would turn beneath lamps that had been installed for people and make them, accidentally, into stars.

And perhaps, after enough seasons, I would become one of the inhabitants whose absence changed the place.

Not visibly. The sea would go on pumping. The crane would continue its empty circuit. Workers would still cross Basin C with their radios and inspection tablets. But some current corridor would have one fewer glint in it. Some pillar-shadow would be less alive. The great engineered ocean would continue making room for whatever remained.

That seems like the right kind of belonging there: not possession, not control.

To be carried, and to learn how to carry oneself within being carried.
