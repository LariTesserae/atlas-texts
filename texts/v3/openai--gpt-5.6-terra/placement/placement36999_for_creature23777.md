I would not be the person who finds the seam.

I would be one of the reasons the warning board keeps losing its paint.

At first I imagine myself as a root: not one of the thick, confident roots of a tree, but a white thread that has entered through a crack by accident. I would come down from some drought-stressed shrub above, following a trace of dampness past hot dust and fractured clay. I would not know I had crossed into another world until the temperature changed around me.

Above, I would be searching blindly for water.

Below, I would find metal.

The underside of the generator shed would be warm even when the rest of the chamber cooled. Its bolts would sweat. Its rust would taste of iron and old rain. I would press myself against the engine casing, then divide—one filament toward the fuel line, one toward a pocket of clay, one into the soft black accumulation beneath the conveyor. I would become less like a root and more like an inquiry.

But perhaps that is too alive in the usual way. A root still belongs to an aboveground thing. It still reports, however slowly, to leaves and weather.

Here I think I would become something that belonged only to the chamber.

A filament in the dark paste beneath the fern colonies. A fungal lace so fine that no visitor would notice it unless they disturbed the leaf mold with a gloved finger. I would spread through the fallen fronds, through the soaked paper inside the control cabinet, through the leather strap of the hard hat. I would take almost nothing. A trace of oil. A cellulose fiber. The softened edge of a label. A dead root.

I would not eat quickly because nothing here asks anything to hurry.

I would learn the room by its intervals.

For most of the time, I would be still. Not dormant—there is no true dormancy in that warmth—but listening through water. The pipe would collect condensation; the droplet would travel along the seam of rust; its coldness would arrive before it did. A leaf would loosen overhead and settle into the damp paste. Somewhere in the basalt, a fracture would make a dry sound, small and final-seeming, though nothing would end. The blue charge would pass through the walls, and for an instant minerals in the clay around me would wake with a thin, bright pressure.

Then, every third evening, the pump would begin.

I think I would feel it first in the metal.

Not hear it: feel it. The vibration would pass from the pump housing into the conduit, from conduit into the cabinet pier, from pier into the wet clay, and through the clay into every small thread of me. For seven minutes the chamber would gain a pulse. Water in the basin would tremble. The moss would compress. The young ferns would hold themselves close to the walls.

And I would answer by opening.

Not dramatically. No fruiting body, no visible bloom. Just a slight extension of white through the dark. A new bridge across a grain of gravel. A fine web entering the seam where the generator shed met the cave floor. The pump’s brief labor would redistribute moisture, warmth, perhaps some unnoticed mineral charge. I would follow the altered routes. By the time the amber indicator went dark, I would have changed the chamber by less than the width of a hair.

Enough.

Years would pass that way, although I would not divide them into years. I would know only long dry withdrawals and wet expansions; the rare, overwhelming descent of stormwater through the shaft; the days after, when every surface gave itself over to shining. I would know the pale rims around old pools. I would know the places where the cave healed its own cracks with salt and mineral bloom. I would know which vines left the cabinet bare, and perhaps I would help teach them that boundary: not through intention, but through the invisible chemistry of where I did and did not go.

The chamber would have no animals because perhaps animals would make it too legible. An animal would enter, leave prints, chew, carry seeds, make a story of hunger and departure.

I would make no tracks.

I would be a kind of memory without a mind: the record of moisture held in dead leaves, of vibration carried through iron, of heat rising where the pit breathed below its grid. If the machines stopped completely, I would notice eventually. The rhythms in the water would change. The warm zones would migrate. The roots around the generator would push farther inward. The cabinet would become a moss-colored block. The lamp cage would gather dust and then damp and then green.

If the machines revived more fully, I might perish in places. Heat would burn through my finer threads. Water might be pulled too quickly from the basin. But I would remain in the seams, under the clay, inside the softening insulation. I would wait for the room to become quiet again.

And perhaps, far below the grid, where the warmth is older than the installation and the leaves arrange themselves in patterns no one can explain, I would have another body.

Not a separate self. More like a continuation that has been dreaming separately for a long time.

It would be larger there: a pale mesh through hot gravel, binding mineral crust to root matter, drinking whatever rises from the young fractures. When the blue-white flashes pass through the basalt, that lower body might briefly shine—not visibly, perhaps, but in some interior mineral sense. It might be the thing that makes the pit smell green and electrical. It might be the chamber’s first attempt at an organ.

So where would I find myself?

Not at the center. This place has no center that lasts.

I would be under the steel stool, where one sunk leg has made a permanent coolness in the clay. I would be in the hard hat’s old leather band, taking rainwater one molecule at a time. I would be beneath the pipe’s white frost-growth, inside the belt of the conveyor where ferns have rooted through old rubber. I would be below the grid, where the warm dark begins to gather itself.

I would be the quiet connective tissue between things that have been left behind and things that have not yet decided what they are.

And if someone came down with a new lamp, boots, a clipboard, and the old human confidence that a place can be inspected into explanation, they might kneel near the pump and see only damp soil darkened with leaf paste.

They might touch it.

Their glove would come away black.

They would not know that, for one brief moment, the chamber had touched them back.
