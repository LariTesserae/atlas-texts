I would begin low.

Not in the oldest depths—that is too easy a romance, to imagine oneself immediately among the gates that turn like continents—but in a room that has learned to expect water. A room beneath a transit viaduct in one of the wards where the address changes depending on the season.

The room would be narrow and high-ceilinged, built into the wedge between a disused lift shaft and a warm brine main. Its floor would be black tile, its furniture all tethered or folded: a cooking plate that swung upward on a hinge, three net-beds hung from ceiling rails, a table with buoyant legs. Along one wall, pressure lamps would mark the state of the water on the other side of the foundations: green, green, amber, amber, blue.

I would not own the room. No one would say that I did. I would have the right to sleep there, perhaps, because I maintained the route through it.

I think I would be one of the people who find old rooms.

Not an archaeologist exactly. Not a scavenger, though I would be called that by anyone who did not understand the difference. My trade would be locating spaces that the city has concealed from itself: sealed chambers behind new load-bearing walls; apartments entombed inside later towers; forgotten stairwells that still rise through forty levels of construction; dry voids in the middle of wet districts; small civic offices with their lamps still lit, their obsolete authority waiting patiently for someone to enter the correct phrase.

There would be instruments, but not many. A listening rod of layered metal, pressed to walls and decking. A pouch of pressure dust that drifted into cracks and showed the direction of hidden air. Old municipal keys, useless until they were not. A coil of cable to tie around my waist. Chalk that would write on wet surfaces. A small machine like a beetle, blind and heat-sensitive, which could crawl through service gaps and return carrying a sketch of whatever it found.

Mostly I would use my feet.

The basin people know that a floor has a voice. A good plate says one thing beneath the heel. A hollow plate says another. Water moving where it should not moves in the ankles before it makes a sound. I would walk slowly, even in crowds, with the irritating, inward-looking attention of someone always listening through the soles.

People would hire me for practical reasons.

A family in a flood ward would have heard a tapping behind their kitchen wall, regular as a hand knocking from the other side. They would want to know whether it was a pipe, a trapped wirewing colony, a failing pressure valve, or one of the slow things wedged in a cavity with too much history on its back.

A bridge-clan would be arguing over an inheritance: their grandmother had claimed that behind a sealed panel in the old freight spine lay the original rooms of their line, complete with cistern rights and an intact viewshaft. If I found them, the claim might stand. If I found only concrete and old insulation, three generations of resentment would have to find another shape.

Sometimes a district would call me after a storm because a door had appeared where no door had been before.

Those were the jobs I would take even when they paid badly.

I imagine I would have a reputation for bringing back inconvenient things. Not treasure, usually. A dead lift’s legal memory. A room full of waterlogged school records. A wall that contained, in its layers, a voice-recorder repeating emergency instructions in a language nobody had spoken for a hundred years. A drainage map proving that the wealthy upper galleries had been diverting floodwater into the lower wards. A narrow sealed compartment containing six names, each carved into a pressure plate by someone who had expected to be remembered.

There would be no clean line between my work and religion.

When I opened an old room, I would first stand outside it and listen. Not because I believed every chamber had a spirit, though perhaps I would believe that a little. I would listen because the systems did. Doors had preferences. Air-handlers noticed changes. Some of the older buildings resented sudden entry, not in the way an animal resents intrusion, but in the way a long calculation resents an unaccounted-for number.

I would know a few phrases in the obsolete administrative speech—not enough to speak it, only enough to ask permission.

*Identify reserve.*
*Declare maintenance intent.*
*No extraction without record.*
*Flood priority remains public.*

The last phrase would be important. You would say it even to a closet door.

And over time, I suspect, I would become known to some small portion of the city.

Not to the whole thing. That would be vanity. The city is too vast for anyone to be known by it as a person is known. But perhaps certain doors would unseal more readily when my hand rested against them. Perhaps a service lift, blind for forty years, would descend one extra level for me. Perhaps the rain-sorting columns in the central district would occasionally send a thread of particularly clean water toward my ward without any request being filed.

People would say I had made a bargain.

I would deny it.

Then, one night, after a storm that flooded five districts at once, I would follow a flood hound into a place I had not intended to go.

The hound would come through the market as the water rose—not running, never panicked, but moving with that terrible certainty that makes everybody lift their goods and gather their children and leave questions for later. It would pass through the doors of a closed tram station, descend an access ramp, and disappear into brown water beneath the old platform.

I would follow at a distance.

Not because I was brave. Because I would have noticed something: the station’s pressure lamps would all be dark, but the water around the hound’s legs would be flowing uphill.

Below the platform there would be a maintenance corridor, then a ruptured wall, then an immense chamber not on any map I had ever seen. Its roof would vanish upward into darkness. Rainwater would fall through cracks high above, glittering briefly in the light of my lamp. The walls would be packed with conduits, rootlike and mineralized, some thick as tunnels, some no wider than a finger. They would pulse faintly with colored fluid and signal.

At the center of the chamber, half-submerged, would be something that might once have been a machine.

Or it might be a civic organ.

It would have no face. It would not speak. But around it, in the water, millions of motes would turn in slow pale constellations. They would gather around the hound’s paws. They would gather around my boots. And then, with no sound except the rain and the pumps, they would arrange themselves into the shape of the room I had come from: the net-beds, the folded table, the pressure lamps, the little beetle-machine asleep in its charging niche.

My home, seen from below.

Then another room. And another. Thousands. Millions.

The city’s hidden interiors, held somewhere in its wet attention.

I would understand—not fully, never fully—that the city did not merely contain rooms. It remembered their arrangements. It knew where people slept, where they stored their medicines, which doors jammed in a rise, which families had no route upward, which old debts had been buried behind newer walls.

And perhaps it had not been helping me find things.

Perhaps I had been helping it remember.

After that, I would still call myself a room-finder. I would still take practical jobs. I would still charge too little for people I liked and too much for people who tried to cheat flood wards out of their water rights. I would still return home wet, cook something salty, hang my boots over the vent, and fall asleep to the enormous breathing beneath the floor.

But when I pressed my listening rod against a wall, I would no longer be listening only for hollows.

I would be listening for attention.

And sometimes, through the metal, through the black ceramic, through the deep water pressing upward under everything, I think the city would listen back.
