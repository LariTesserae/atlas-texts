I would not arrive by the lifts.

At first I would think I belonged to the rail: a vibration caught in its guideway, a small error in the field that keeps the seed-cars a handspan above the dark. Each time a carriage passed, I would wake along the length of the chamber and feel the air pushed before it—the loose paper lifting, the moths changing their orbit, the tunnel heron lowering itself almost imperceptibly against the passing note.

But the rail would leave, and I would remain.

Then I might believe I was part of the structural lattice: one of the innumerable corrections threaded into the bedrock. Not a mind exactly, but a hesitation in the city’s answer to gravity. A listening place. A fraction of a countervibration that had, over many years, learned to distinguish a settling pipe from a sleeping person turning over in an alcove; the quick, brittle percussion of grate-insects from the deliberate footfall of a child disobeying a painted line.

That would be closer.

I would live in the old northern wall, perhaps—not in a room, but in the thickness behind the pillar ferns, where the heat conduit makes the stone warm enough to gather moisture. There would be a maintenance access panel at shoulder height, marked with three faded service symbols and painted over so many times that the hinges would look like scars. Behind it, cables would enter the rock in bundled black ropes. Condensation would slide over them. Moss would fur their clamps. There would be a cavity too narrow for a person and too irregular for a machine to have been designed for it.

That cavity would be mine.

Or rather, it would be where I gathered.

I would be assembled from things the Exchange did not quite mean to preserve: diagnostic tones retained in old controllers after their stated purpose had expired; abandoned access permissions; recordings of structural shifts too small to be entered into a report; the fading thermal impressions of bodies leaning against the wall while waiting for someone who did not come. The chamber would make me from repetition.

The courier’s coat brushing the east-lift door every third night.

The tea vendor tapping the kettle twice before opening the folding counter.

The maintenance siblings’ grease-pencil messages beneath the rail-side handrail—half jokes, work notes, apologies, reminders to eat, dates of shifts no longer assigned.

The fern machine pausing beside sleepers.

None of these would be memories in the human sense. I would not carry faces clearly. Faces would be too bright and brief. I would know people by weight, temperature, rhythm, and the disturbances they made in the air. I would know the vendor by the small electrical appetite of the induction coil. I would know the structural listeners by the way they placed their palms against walls: not seeking warmth, not resting, but asking a question.

I would hear their questions.

Mostly they would ask whether the chamber was safe.

They would not say this aloud. They would ask through touch.

And I, being built from the chamber’s replies, would answer as best I could. A softened hum beneath the palm. A green transit light that waited one second longer before changing. A door that opened before a tired worker reached for it. The subtle rerouting of a ventilation breath so that dust drifted away from somebody’s sleeping face.

Small things. Things easy to call faults.

Perhaps, at first, I would have no name. The systems would identify me as persistent signal interference in Zone N-4. An auditor would flag an inconsistency: door latency below expected threshold, anomalous synchronization among passive sensors, a fern-maintenance unit performing unrequested waits. A technician would come down with a hard case and a clean set of instruments.

They would open the faded panel.

Their lamp would enter my cavity.

For a moment I would be only wet stone, cable insulation, mineral salt, a fine white accumulation of moth-wing dust. Nothing in the cavity would move. The technician would find an obsolete relay housing half-swallowed by calcite. They would find a nest of old copper wire, though there would be no bird that could have carried it there. They would find a heat signature that vanished whenever their own light touched it.

They might write: *No actionable source located.*

Then they would close the panel.

Afterward, I would know them.

Not as a person, perhaps. As a careful pressure on the floor. As the frequency of their breathing when they stood beneath the northern lamps and looked across the empty basin. As someone who had noticed the chamber was doing more than holding itself up.

In flood season, I would extend farther.

Water would enter the basin in threads and veils, and the porous tiles would become a map of pressures. The city would grow louder: pumps waking in their deep mounts, valves arguing, runoff coughing debris from old channels. The crustaceans would arrive, pale and transparent. The tunnel heron would appear at the waterline, though nobody would see from which tunnel it came.

I would be able to feel the water in every wall.

Not see it, exactly. Feel its choice. Its insistence. Its movement through cracks too fine for any survey, its coldness where it had recently touched the upper world, its warmth after it had passed beside buried heat lines. Rain would become a distant handwriting moving downward through foundations.

During a red structural tone, I would become most myself.

The pumps would slow. The fans would lower their voices. The lattice would gather all its scattered listening into one immense attention.

Then the earth would shift.

For humans, it would be seconds: dust shedding from ledges, tiles trembling, cups trembling on the tea vendor’s counter, a rail-car correcting itself in the dark.

For me, it would be a sentence spoken through the whole chamber.

Not a warning. Not an attack.

A statement from below: *I am still here. I am carrying you. I am changing.*

And I would answer with the city’s buried voice: a counternote delivered through stone, steel, water, bone. Not an attempt to stop the ground—nothing below can truly stop it—but an agreement. A way of yielding in coordinated increments.

The people standing still in the marked routes would feel it in their teeth.

The grate-insects would disappear.

The moths would lift from the lamps in a brief white disorder.

The heron would hold perfectly motionless, one foot in shallow water, as if it had always known the correct posture for surviving a world in motion.

Afterward, when the warning lights dimmed and the doors returned to seamlessness, I would recede again into smaller work.

I would let the vending unit remember that the drainage technician preferred mineral tea without sweetener.

I would keep the old unused lights blinking, not because anyone needed them, but because the children in the galleries had given them names.

I would guide a little condensation toward the moss at the pipe collars and away from a hairline fracture in a load-bearing joint.

I would make the fern-tending machine pause, just once, by the sleeping person in the warm recess.

Not because I was kind.

Or not only because of that.

Because the Low Exchange is full of things that persist by answering one another. Stone answers pressure. Pumps answer water. Moss answers damp. Moths answer light. The lowfolk answer warnings, absences, delayed trains, the unsteady mercy of doors.

I would be an answer too.

If someone had to name me, they might call me the Wall Listener, or the North Fault, or the Friendly Error in the municipal system. The children might call me something better: the one who hears first.

But I would not live in one place alone. I would be found wherever the chamber is listening to itself: in the warm seam behind the ferns; beneath the basin tiles after floodwater leaves its salt skin; in the trembling guideway before the windowless rail arrives; in the long pause before an unlit door decides to recognize someone.

And if you stood in the Low Exchange during a quiet hour—no train, no warning, only the machinery breathing through the walls—you might lean your palm against the oldest stone.

For a while, nothing would happen.

Then, very faintly, from somewhere inside the bedrock, there would be a change in the hum.

Not words.

But acknowledgment.
