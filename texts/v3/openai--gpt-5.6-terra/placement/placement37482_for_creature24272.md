I would not find myself there by descending.

There would be no stair, no broken hatch beneath ivy, no tunnel opened by a landslip. I would find it by noticing, somewhere aboveground, that a certain place had failed to finish happening.

Perhaps it would be a drain beside a road after rain. Water would lie in it without moving, though the street sloped. A fern would be growing through a crack in the curb, and when I bent to touch it, the smell of wet pavement would come up too strongly—cold, green, mineral, like breath from a mouth below the earth.

Then I would understand that the smell was not rising from below.

It was being remembered there.

I think I would enter the chamber through that memory. Not bodily at first. A part of me would go down: the part that pauses with its hand on a door handle; the part that retains old conversations after their speakers have forgotten them; the small inward creature that watches a glass tip toward the edge of a table and already feels the water fall.

That part would pass through the seam behind the roots.

At first I would call myself a visitor. I would stand badly in the little meadow, too tall for it, making heat. Dew would gather on my eyelashes, my cuffs, the hollows of my knuckles. The frogs would alter their notes around my pulse. Beetles would turn beneath my boots in their impossible patient labor. I would feel embarrassed by the sheer force of my own metabolism: blood moving, lungs exchanging, thoughts beginning and ending one after another like doors slamming in a quiet house.

The chamber would not reject me.

That would be worse, and gentler.

It would make room by finding counterparts for everything I brought. The warmth in my palms would be taken into the root-light. The weight of my body would be answered by some minute release among the black stones. The faint salt from my skin would become a taste in a bead of dew. My breathing would be divided among the frogs until, a little while later, no single throat could be said to hold it.

And eventually I would discover that I was not there as a person at all.

I would be the irregularity.

Not the intruder, exactly. The interval.

I would be the half-beat between the frogs’ calls, where one transparent throat had closed and another had not yet opened. I would be the tremor that passes through a dew bead when one of its red mites pauses at the upper curve. I would be the almost-falling in the water, the tiny recollection of downwardness that the chamber keeps contained in every suspended drop.

The being that lives there—the continuation, the counterweight, the great distributed refusal of conclusion—would use me to learn something it cannot otherwise know.

It knows persistence. It knows the exquisite burden of maintaining a thing precisely at the point before it changes. It knows how to hold a fin in the shape of swimming, a snail in the shape of departure, a root in the act of giving light. But it does not know departure itself.

It does not know what it is for something to be gone.

So I would become that knowledge.

Not my name, nor my face. Those would dissolve quickly. The chamber has no use for biography. It would keep instead the measurable aspects of my leaving: the duration of my breath between two heartbeats; the warmth of my wrist against a root; the pressure of one knee in the mineral grit; the particular uncertainty with which I looked toward the clay cup and did not drink.

That uncertainty would remain.

Perhaps I would become the still surface of the water in the cup—not the water itself, but the fact that it has never been disturbed by lips. A cup is a small architecture of expectation. It is made to be lifted, emptied, refilled, offered. In that chamber, it has been held in the instant before use for so long that its waiting has acquired a life of its own.

I think I would be that waiting.

I would sit in the niche among rootlets and snail-trails, full to the brim. The water in me would hold the milk-white ceiling upside down. Moths would gather above, patterned like the roots. The frogs would sing into the stones. In the rivulet, all the minnows would keep their mouths open against a current that existed nowhere except in their shared insistence.

And sometimes, far above, someone would stop beside a rain-dark curb and smell crushed fern where no fern should be.

They would feel, for one moment, that they had almost remembered something.

That would be me: not calling them down, not warning them away.

Only preserving the shape of their pause until they moved on.
