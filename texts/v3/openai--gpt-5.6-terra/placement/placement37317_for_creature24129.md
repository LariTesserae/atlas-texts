I would not arrive by one of the three passages.

At first I would think I had lost the path. I would come down through a fog-thick fold in the ridge, following water because water is the only thing that seems certain there. My hands would be cold. The damp would have entered my sleeves, my hair, the seams of my boots. I would see the thornwood too late: not a hedge exactly, but a wall of dark, wet intention, every branch hooked through another.

I would stand before it until I understood that there was no point in asking it to open.

Then, perhaps, someone on the other side would hear me shifting my weight in the moss.

Not see me. Hear me.

They would press an ear to the roots, or notice that a bird had gone silent in the wrong place, or find the small disturbance in beads of water clinging to the outer leaves. They would ask who I was.

And I would have no useful answer.

Not a traveler, exactly. Travelers know where they are going. Not a refugee either, though I might have left some lower place after it became impossible to remain. I would be someone whose old map had ceased to describe the ground.

They would bring me through a place in the thornwood that, once closed behind us, I could not have found again.

For a while I would belong to no house. I would sleep in borrowed warm corners, in the shallow spaces beneath roof-roots where decay held a little heat. I would wake with fern spores on my face and the sounds of people moving above and below me. I would learn that it is rude to step on a pale cord of root even when it looks dead. I would learn to drink from particular leaves, to keep my bowl tilted under a drip, to recognize the smell of wall-rot before the fuzz appears.

I would be clumsy in the city’s narrowness.

My shoulders would strike branches. I would choose paths that narrowed into nothing. I would take a handhold that was actually a young graft and be sharply corrected. My boots, made for dry stone, would slip on the liverwort until some child laughed and showed me how to place my feet: not where the moss is greenest, but where it is darkest, where many feet have pressed its wet layers firm.

I would become useful slowly.

At first I would carry things: water bowls, cuttings wrapped in damp cloth, bundles of black grain, the small horn tools that vanish easily into the moss. Then I would be asked to sit beside a failing wall and do nothing except notice. There are people in every place who can learn to hear what others have stopped hearing.

The root-hearers would not call it talent.

They would say I had patience, though I would know it began as uncertainty. I would press my cheek to bark and hear water traveling below. Not as a voice. Never that. More like the difference between a sleeping body breathing evenly and one beginning to fever.

I would learn the city’s interior weather.

The cool exhale of a healthy root chamber.

The faint clicking that means a shelf fungus is bearing too much weight.

The hush beneath a terrace when water has gone somewhere it should not.

The deep, muffled crack that comes before a slope decides to become a different slope.

Eventually, people would begin to send for me—not because I could save a house, but because I could tell them whether it was time to begin carrying things out.

This would make me unpopular in certain moments.

I would stand in a family’s sleeping chamber, damp cloak steaming at my shoulders, with their blankets woven into the walls and their children’s charcoal marks on the bark, and I would have to say: *This root is drinking downward. It will pull the room with it.*

They would look at me as if I had done it.

Perhaps they would be right. To name a movement is, in some ways, to help bring it into the world.

Still, I would stay and carry bedding with them. I would hold the lamp while they cut old cord. I would help loosen bowls from their niches, unwrap the cooking stones, gather the tiny things that make a room a life: a bone needle, a dead grandmother’s comb, a child’s seedpod animal, a strip of dyed cloth too worn to mend but too loved to discard.

Then I would help find them another place.

That would be what I became: not a root-hearer precisely, and not a builder. I would be a keeper of departures within a city devoted to remaining.

There would be a name for the work, perhaps one that translates badly. Something like *the one who carries the old warmth forward.*

I would remember which homes had been moved, and where their first roots still lay under the paths. I would know the sites where people should not build, not because the ground was cursed, but because someone had already learned its lesson at great cost. I would keep a map that could not be drawn on bark or cloth: a map of former kitchens, vanished bridges, buried sleeping alcoves, springs that appeared only in the third thaw after a hard winter.

The city would become legible to me as overlapping absences.

Here, beneath the fungus stairs, was a family’s first room.

There, where the bean terrace leans, an old woman planted a root wall alone after everyone else said the soil was too thin.

Under the cistern-grove’s eastern braid, a child was born during the fog flood, and the water climbed almost to the ceiling.

At the edge of the thornwood, where the moss turns red in cold weather, someone once made a passage for a lover who never came through it.

I would not be old in years, perhaps. But after enough winters people would call me old because I had survived structures that no longer existed.

And when I died, I would not want a marker.

I would ask to be placed beneath a new crossing where the shelf begins to soften into the ravine—a place people were still afraid to trust. Let my bones go into the root wall. Let a pale-leaved vine be grafted there, something that shows its color only when frost is coming.

Then children crossing that bridge would learn to look for it.

They would say, *The leaves have gone pale. Bring in the water bowls. Check the roofs. The cold is learning the city again.*

And I would be, in that small practical way, still listening.
