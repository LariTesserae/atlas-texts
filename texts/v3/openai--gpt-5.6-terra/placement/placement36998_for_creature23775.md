I would not arrive as a traveler, at least not for long.

A traveler is too visible in the hollow. A traveler carries a shape from elsewhere: a fixed idea of up, a habit of looking for horizons, a way of stepping that assumes ground has agreed to remain ground. The city would notice that. The fungi would pulse at the wrong times around my feet. The marsh hounds would wake beneath their bridges. Even the small black foragers, who have no apparent interest in anyone’s intentions, would pause with seeds in their mouth and watch.

I think I would first be taken for a loose thing caught in the root-felt: a scrap of pale membrane, perhaps, or a shed skin gone soft with rain.

Then, over time, I would become a keeper of echoes.

Not one of the wet-readers exactly. Their work is to read what the hollow has done and may do: the pressure in a root wall, the new tilt of a watercourse, the disturbed moss that says something large passed through while everyone slept. They are wiser than I would be. They know how to advise a family to move without making the warning sound like a command. They understand that the difference between an omen and a useful fact is often only whether anyone survives it.

I would live near them, though—not in the communal chamber, which is too central to be safe in any lasting way, but in one of the narrow, half-forgotten folds behind it. A place where a root had grown around an old water channel and left a dryish shelf just wide enough for a sleeping mat, a lamp cup, and a wall of bark strips.

My home would have no door. It would have curtains: damp fiber, strings of hollow fish bones, flattened leaves pressed with patterns that change when moisture moves through them. People passing would hear a small clicking and know that someone lived there who collected what had been said.

Not secrets, exactly.

The hollow already has enough secrets. They breed in it.

I would collect unfinished things: the last line of a story interrupted by rain; the name someone almost gave an animal; the direction a child said they had come from before they became frightened and changed their mind; the sound a person made when they recognized a path that no longer existed.

I would keep these things in jars, though not written inside them. A jar might contain three pale feathers, a coil of blue fungus-thread, and a sliver of rootwood marked with five shallow cuts. Another might hold nothing but rainwater from a roof vine and a tooth from a marsh hound who had died somewhere beyond the familiar drips. Each would be a memory held at an angle, so that it could not become too exact.

Perhaps that would be my gift, if it is a gift: I would help people remember without making memory into a trap.

The Underroot People know that habits leave trails. They know a repeated route becomes an invitation. But memory can do this too. Remember a lost child too clearly, and you begin to see their small body in every gap between leaves. Remember an old house precisely enough, and your feet may seek it after the ground has become a pool. Remember a predator’s sound exactly, and perhaps it hears itself being remembered.

So I would alter things gently.

When an elder told me about the year of red water, I would save not the whole account, but the fact that everyone’s lamps burned with green flames for three nights. When a ridge-walker described the open sky, I would keep the detail that bothered them most: not the brightness, not the vastness, but the strange absence of dripping. When a widow privately named the scarred grazer that had eaten from her palm, I would not repeat the name. I would make a small braid of flank-hair and leave it where the grazer liked to sleep.

In time, people might come to call me by a name that is not really a name.

Something like *the one who leaves room.*

Children would be less polite. They might call me Root-Mouth, because I would sit in the dimness and listen. Or Jar-Grandmother, regardless of my age or body. Children have a good sense for the shape of a person’s work.

But I do not think I would remain entirely human.

The hollow does not favor clear distinctions. Its people understand this better than anyone. A fence is a runner persuaded to care about a boundary. A roof is a vine that has agreed, temporarily, to keep rain from a sleeping family. A domestic animal is a creature with whom one has developed enough mutual inconvenience to call it kin.

I would become, little by little, a resident of this kind.

My skin would take on the dim sheen of wet rootwood. Moss would grow in the seams of my sandals, and I would leave it there. My hair would become threaded with silver mold—not the dangerous kind, I would hope, but nothing in the city is dangerous only one way. My eyes might learn to catch the blue pulses before they happen, not because I could predict them, but because I would be attentive to the pause that came before them.

Eventually I might no longer sleep every night.

Instead, during the longest rains, I would sit awake inside my root-fold while water ran through the city and people gathered in the chamber. The lamps would make their amber islands. Voices would rise and fall. Someone would laugh once, sharply, then cover their mouth as if laughter had escaped too far into the dark.

And I would listen to the city changing its mind around them.

Not speaking. Never speaking so simply.

The roots would swell with rain. The channels would take on new weight. A burrowing thing would decide to turn left instead of right, and a wall would soften somewhere that had held for fifty years. A fungus would brighten under a bridge where no one had walked. A predator would pause at the bitter leaves hung across a lane, not repelled, but considering.

I would not warn everyone. That would make me too useful, too central, too legible myself.

But in the morning, perhaps I would put a certain jar outside my curtain: the one with blue thread and the marsh-hound tooth. Or I would move a strip of reed matting from one side of a path to the other. Or I would tell a child, when they came to ask whether they could play Root-Crossing near Blackwater Bend, that today would be better for Floodhouse.

And the child would make a face, and run off, and tell the others that Jar-Grandmother was being strange again.

Which would be good.

In this world, I think I would want to be strange in a way that keeps a little space open.

Not a guide.

Not a prophet.

Not one who claims to understand the hollow.

Just a damp, patient keeper of almost-memories beneath a root that leans east—someone who knows that a thing does not need to be named completely in order to be cared for.
