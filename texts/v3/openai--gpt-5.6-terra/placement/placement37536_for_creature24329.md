I would begin by thinking I had chosen a hiding place.

Not a settlement exactly. Not one of the shaft-folk’s negotiated rooms, with its waxed walls and carefully suspended cups. I would find a disused ventilation throat beneath an arch, one whose old serial number had been softened almost to illegibility by silver vine. It would be dry, narrow, and lined with black composite ribs. The air passing through it would make a low note when the upper leaves turned.

At first I would live there as a trespasser.

I would sleep in the intervals between charge-storms. I would collect pale repair foam in little folded metal cups before it became crystal. I would learn which dusts could be eaten after heating, which could be traded, which would grow a metallic taste beneath the tongue and make the filaments in a person’s feet ache for days. I would keep everything I owned moving: my knife looped through a belt, my blankets rolled beneath my head, my tools hung from shifting cords. Nothing laid down. Nothing forgotten.

The lowlands would test this habit.

A root would reach toward my sleeping mat one morning, not quickly, not violently. It would simply find it, touch one edge, and begin making small transparent hairs along the fabric. I would cut the hairs away. The next day they would return from another direction. Eventually I would stop cutting them and thread the mat through the roots instead, letting the place hold me without entirely taking me.

That is how it would begin.

I would be called Vent-Without-Number, perhaps. Or Underarch. The shaft-folk would give me a name that was also a warning: one who lives where the structure has not agreed to shelter anyone.

But I would not remain only human in the old way.

I think I would become one of the interpreters, though not because I was chosen. I would be changed by proximity, by sleeping above old currents and breathing air that had passed through too many tuned reeds. At some point, I would notice that I could feel the road-clefts through the soles of my feet: not their exact shapes, but their intentions. A sled coming toward a broken rail would feel different from a sled returning empty. A grove preparing to shed obsolete fiber would make the bones in my hands prickle.

Later, pale strands would begin appearing under my skin.

At first I would mistake them for scars. Fine white lines at the wrists, perhaps, then along the arches of my feet. In bright weather they would glow faintly blue. When I pressed my palms to a trunk, I would feel not speech but sequence:

pause  
redirect  
seal  
wait

I would tell no one for a while.

There is danger in becoming useful to the lowlands. The continent does not reward usefulness as people do. It incorporates it.

Still, I would learn to walk where others could not. I would know when a quiet field was merely resting and when it was holding itself still around some deep disturbance. I would recognize the inward-facing flowers. I would hear the difference between a ventilation chord made by wind and one made by a nestwork altering the reeds beneath a hall.

I would go into abandoned processing districts alone.

Not bravely. Carefully.

The entrances would often be nearly gone: a black reed forest, a curtain of dry white leaves, a maintenance bloom grown over an old door. Inside would be the low, unlit rooms where metal had become bark and bark had taken on old machine functions. I would find coils of conductive ivy arranged in patterns. I would find bells hanging under ceilings, their surfaces thick with pollen dust. I would find rings of polished husks, each positioned with the ceremonial precision of something that had no ceremonies but had invented the shape of one.

And sometimes, in those rooms, the building would recognize me.

A seam would open before I touched it.

A root would withdraw from a passage.

A dormant lamp under transparent wood would brighten one degree at a time, as though waking reluctantly from a long instruction.

Then I would understand that I was not merely reading the lowlands. Something in them had begun using me as a surface on which to make a thought.

Not a person’s thought. Not language. Something broader and slower: an adjustment traveling through living material.

I might carry messages between groves, but they would not be messages anyone could repeat. I would arrive at a failing canopy and stand beneath it until my skin lit with a pulse received three days earlier from an arch fifty kilometers away. The leaves would change angle. Somewhere below, conveyor roots would start again. The shaft-folk would say I had healed the grove.

I would know I had only been part of a circuit.

Over time, my ventilation throat would change around me.

Its ribs would thicken and soften. The old vent would grow a crown of heat-collecting moss above the arch. Tiny white cleaners would begin nesting in folds of the wall, opening and closing like petals whenever I returned. I would hang no belongings from the ceiling anymore. Instead, I would ask the roots to keep them, and they would make shaped hollows: one for a blade, one for an ampoule, one for a small brass bell I had found inside a grazer’s old track.

Perhaps I would become a keeper of things that should not be lost too soon.

Not hoarding—nothing can truly be kept here—but delaying. Giving objects one more season of separateness before they enter the common material. A child’s cup. An interpreter’s dead hand-tool. A map scratched onto composite, showing roads that no longer exist. I would hold them in living niches while their owners still needed them remembered.

And when people came to me with impossible seeds, I would take them.

I would not open them.

I would place them in the old ventilation chamber, in a hollow the roots had made without being asked. There they would hum against the substrate. The sound would travel through the arch and out into the lowlands: too low for hearing, almost too slow for time.

One day, perhaps after a violet stillness lasting longer than any in memory, the seed would open.

I do not know what would grow from it.

A new kind of person, perhaps—not one born from a body but unfolded from a function the continent had finally learned it required.

Or perhaps I would be the thing that grows.

My skin would become less dry. The pale filaments would reach from wrist to shoulder, from heel to spine. I would still have a face, though the lowlands would not care about it. I would still remember the feeling of water, though I might never see it. I would still call myself Underarch, even after the name had become inaccurate.

Then I would remain at the edge of a road-cleft, not standing guard and not asleep, while freight sleds passed beneath the white sky.

Travelers would see me from a distance and mistake me for a small tree growing out of an old machine housing: a trunk wrapped in cloth, a crown of dark leaves, hands resting against the living wall.

If they came close, I would open my eyes.

If they needed to know whether the road ahead was safe, I would listen through my feet.

And if the continent had gone still because it was listening too, I would tell them not to move.

Not because there was danger.

Because something immense, below all the roots and engines and remembered halls, was trying to make room for a new part of itself.
