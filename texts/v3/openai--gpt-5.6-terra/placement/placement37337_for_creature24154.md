I would not begin as a traveler.

A traveler needs a destination, or at least the faith that one horizon will remain itself long enough to be approached. In the high country, I think I would lose that faith gently. Not from fear. From apprenticeship.

At first I might imagine myself one of the pale climbers, because I have always trusted my hands more than my sense of direction. I would wake folded into a ceramic curtain above a ravine, my face hidden in its soft frill, listening through my skin to the pressure-song of the larvae below. I would know the cliff not by sight but by temperature: this crack still warm, this filament newly extruded and brittle, this ledge already thinking of becoming a wall.

When gravity altered, I would flatten myself against whatever surface had become nearest. I would wait. I would let the bones inside me remain undecided until the world chose a down.

But that is only one life, and the country seems unwilling to allow a creature to remain only one thing.

Perhaps I would begin earlier than that: as a damp stain in a crack under amber moss. Not an animal, not even a self—just a translucent colony holding its water close, spreading by fractions of an inch through the warmth of the rock. I would sleep during ordinary gravity. I would wake when pools lifted from their basins. Then I would gather my scattered cells, make a net from my own clear flesh, and rise through the wet air toward a blue current in the reeds.

For a few minutes, I would be airborne.

The winged things would pass above me: enormous, many-jointed, dark against the bronze. Their inner red warmth would pulse through the clouds. I would feel their passage as a change in charge. Perhaps one would feed, and I would become part of the heat moving through its narrow body, part of the electricity carried down from the cloud ceiling to the mud.

Then I would be in the reeds.

Not eaten exactly. Translated.

A current would run through the black roots beneath the mineral pools. Flash after flash would pass from seed-head to seed-head. Somewhere beyond a ridge that did not exist yesterday, a herd of garden-backed grazers would turn. Somewhere in a basin, the shelled kneaders would change their patient line. Somewhere a soft-trunked grove would begin, very slowly, to pull its roots free.

I think I would understand then that I was not a creature in the high country. I was a message it was sending to itself.

Not a message in language. A pressure. A preference. A little transferred certainty that there was warmth elsewhere.

And yet I would want a body.

The body I return to, after imagining the cliff and mist and current, is small enough to be overlooked among the moss. It has many legs, but not so many that it appears alien; its movement is a hush rather than a scuttle. On its back it carries a garden: a low mound of dark green fungal tissue, silver lichen, and several small flowers that open only when the ground has warmed from beneath. The garden is not decoration. It is memory made visible.

When I cross a fresh sheet of obsidian, I carry moisture in the garden’s folds. When I pass through a cloud of spores, they settle there and become maps that can be eaten later. When the soil is thin, the garden feeds me; when the air becomes wrong, I feed it what my body has made. I am never entirely an individual. I am a moving agreement between hunger and shelter.

I would travel in waves with others like me, although “others” would be imperfect. Each of us would be carrying a slightly different old place: moss from one ledge, fungus descended from a dead kneader’s shell, a root-thread from a basin now buried beneath a newborn spur. Together, we would make a low migration across the country’s raw surfaces.

We would not know where we were going.

The reeds would flash. The stone would tremble. A dream inhaled from a valley’s damp breath would rise in us all: warm pools under an overhang, the mineral taste of a basin no eye among us had seen, the direction of safety encoded in something older than our species.

We would go.

Perhaps the dream would be wrong. Perhaps the basin would have folded inside a mountain generations ago—if generations can be counted where the ground is still learning its own outline. We might arrive at a field of salt, or a vertical grove clinging to an impossible cliff, or a red fissure breathing upward wind.

But we would not have failed.

We would begin feeding there. Our gardens would seed the stone. The smallest among us would die and become moisture. Larger grazers would follow our tracks. Burrowers would find the softening ground. A kneader would eventually pass, its pale underside opening around mud and roots and bodies, sorting us into new soil.

And from that soil, perhaps, a basin would gather.

So I think that is where I would find myself: not at the summit, not in the hidden organs below the earth, not riding the vast winged creatures through charged cloud, though all of those lives would pass through me in some form.

I would be low to the ground.

I would be one of the garden-backed beings crossing a land too new to promise anything. I would have no fixed den, no inherited boundary, no certainty that the slope under my feet would remain a slope. I would carry a small living country on my back. I would follow weather that remembered itself as instinct. I would eat the soft edge of young stone.

And when the mountain turned inside out, I would not try to preserve the world as it had been.

I would stop.

I would let my garden open.

I would wait to see what kind of ground was coming through.
