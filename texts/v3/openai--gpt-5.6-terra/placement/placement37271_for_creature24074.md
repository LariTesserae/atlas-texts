I would not arrive there from above.

From above, the world would look too complete: green ridges, pale migration scars, red flowers opening in the distance, the metallic turning of leaf-fields. I would mistake it for a landscape. I would look for the place where the forest ended and the land began.

There would be no such place.

I think I would begin as something small in a crack of old cambium: a pale, many-jointed thing among the mineral flour, feeding on what the world had already shed. Not a sovereign creature. Not even, at first, a creature with a clear boundary. A moving tuft of cilia and transparent plates, turning pollen grains over in my mouthparts, choosing the safe spores from the hot ones.

The wind would be the first vastness I understood.

It would enter every joint of me. It would scour the root plains flat and carry bright fragments over the cliffs of compressed wood. I would learn to feel its different temperatures: the cold descending from silver leaf-sails; the resin-warm turbulence leaking from a breathing grove; the breathless pockets beneath an overhanging frond where dust gathered and small things survived.

Then perhaps I would grow larger, or join myself to other small lives.

I imagine a body made less for walking than for holding on: six low limbs with gripping pads, a back furred in branching filaments. My filaments would catch spores from the air. Some I would eat. Some I would burn in tiny hot organs along my spine, little blue-white reactions flashing under the dry dark. At night, traveling with others across a root shelf, we would look like a scattered fire that had learned not to consume its fuel too quickly.

But I do not think I would remain one of them.

There is something in this place that asks to become porous.

I would become a listener.

Not one of the great bark-clinging predators, perhaps—not the silent falling shape that seals another living thing and takes its hidden water—but something gentler and stranger: a creature whose body is pressed against the world often enough that it cannot distinguish between hearing and touch.

I would live in the walls of a breathing plant.

My home would be a chamber grown, not constructed: a warm vault inside a hollow green shaft, its walls slowly swelling with sugar and gas. Resin workers would pass in lacquered ranks along the ridges. Blind crawlers would graze the fungal films near the vents. Above us, air would enter through slatted openings with a sound like an enormous animal drawing breath through teeth.

I would have no eyes worth speaking of. Light in that world is too extravagant, too far away. I would know the day by changes in pressure, by the cooling of the outer wall, by the timing of the host’s long internal contractions.

A grazer herd crossing the root plain ten kilometers away would be a tremor in my underside.

A browser striking its resonant crest against bark far above would be a clear hollow note, transmitted through the whole living column.

A predator releasing from a cliff-face would make no sound in the air, but the departure of its weight would travel through the wood—a sudden absence, a tiny wrongness. I would feel it. I might warn the chambers around me, drumming some small pattern into the vascular wall. Not language exactly. More like an itch that spreads ahead of danger.

Over generations, the plant might begin to use me.

It would thicken certain membranes where I fed. It would divert warmth through the chambers I favored. In return I would carry its pollen salts into side galleries, eat the larvae that gnawed its inner tissues, clean mineral crust from its vents. My eggs—or buds, or soft divided portions—would be laid in folds of the host’s lining. My young would hatch already knowing its pulse.

Eventually I would understand that I was not an inhabitant of the hollow stem.

I was one of its organs.

And the hollow stem was one organ among others: in the grove, in the root plateau, in the old forest compressed beneath the plateau. The resin workers would be an organ. The gliders nesting in the exhaust heat would be an organ. The fungus dismantling a fallen giant beyond the ridge would be an organ. Even the pale armored thing moving far below, lifting whole stands of towers on its back, would be part of the circulation.

Perhaps my kind would spread between groves.

Not by flight. We would travel inside seed towers, or in the warm folds beneath mineral grazers, or buried in resin pellets carried by black-shelled workers. When a tower burst open and the world rang with its dry catastrophic sound, we would be flung into new country: into silver plains of leaf tissue, into red-flower weather, into the exposed ribs of a mountain made from dead roots.

There I would enter a new host and learn its rhythm.

Each plant would have its own internal weather. Some would pulse slowly, with long nights of cold and almost no warmth. Others would shudder continuously, processing the buried body beneath them into heat. In some, I would live close to the upper vents, tasting thin vapor combed from the high air. In others, I would descend into dark channels where the walls were wet with deep-held moisture and thread-eels moved like nerves through the tissue.

I would be afraid of the thread-eels.

I think every world deserves a fear that is not merely rational.

They would wait in the narrow places, root-colored and patient, feeling me through the wall before I knew they were there. I would learn to recognize the almost-imperceptible change in the host’s internal pulse when one passed nearby. I would freeze. I would flatten myself into a seam. I would become dead tissue for as long as deadness was necessary.

And perhaps this is where I would finally find myself: not at the summit of one of the botanical mountains, not flying above the green continent, not master of any territory, but deep in a wall that is alive around me.

Listening.

The world would never be silent. It would grind, breathe, click, split, boom, ratchet, release. It would make weather from leaves and geology from trunks. It would turn the sunlight into fibers, the fibers into cliffs, the cliffs into food, the food into migrations, the migrations into valleys, the valleys into new growth.

I would know it not as scenery, but as a body too large to see.

And I would be a small sensory knot within that body: a keeper of vibrations, a feeder on spores, an accidental messenger between chambers. When the great wind crossed the heights and made the leaf-fields turn pale all at once, I would feel the change arrive through the wood long after the visible world had flashed.

I would answer it by pressing myself close.

Not because I belonged there.

Because belonging, in that place, would mean having no other possible meaning.
