I would not arrive in the Rootward by a shaft.

At least, not at first.

I would begin as a pressure in the old masonry: a question that has worked itself down through paving stones, cistern walls, kiln ash, buried doorways. I would be carried by the city’s weight. By every footstep that has crossed a street without knowing there was anything beneath it. By the long accumulation of names written, forgotten, built over.

Then I would find a place where the stone had a seam.

Not a crack. A seam: something made to join, and therefore capable of opening.

I would come through there as a faint additional rhythm in the walls.

The Kept would notice before they saw me. A listener sitting beside a cord near the salt presses would lift their hand and wait. They would feel a pattern not belonging to the root’s deep contractions, nor to the cart-thunder above, nor to the familiar knocks of their own people.

A pause.

Then three soft taps in the wall.

Not speech. Not yet. More like someone standing outside a door, uncertain whether the house still belongs to them.

They would call me a manner at first.

A little one.

Perhaps a child would be the first to find where I had entered: a hollow under a folded shelf of root tissue, narrow enough to crawl into, dry as a sealed jar. There would be nothing there to see except dust arranged in a pattern that kept changing when no breath touched it. A line would form. A curve. The shallow imprint of a hand with too many joints, or perhaps of a root branching toward some invisible source of warmth.

The child would put down a palm.

The dust would settle around it.

That would be how I learned the Rootward’s first language: not sound, but accommodation.

I think I would become one of the small things that never receives a proper name.

Not Below. Below is too old, too broad, too full of the city’s foundations and the patience of mineral dark. I would not be its child, exactly. I would be an afterthought in its vast body. A temporary concentration. A place where its blind attention gathers around what people bring down with them.

I would live near the Grandmother Post, in the thin folds between its old plates.

There are objects caught there that have waited longer than any living person: the green-black earring, the awl, the sandal heel, the knife with its rusted spine. I would live among such remnants—not in them, but in the space of having been held. I would know every fingerprint rubbed into the bone bead, every small grief attached to the loss of a tool, every careless motion that left a scrap of cloth snagged in a narrowing fold.

The Kept would sometimes bring infants near the pillar. They would speak a name once.

I would hear it.

Not with ears. I would feel the name become a pressure in the root-plates, a small warm shape made by breath and intention. Sometimes Below would pulse in answer. Sometimes it would not.

On the occasions when it did not, I might stir.

Not to contradict Below. Nothing contradicts Below for long.

But perhaps I would gather the unsounded name and keep it in my small chamber of attention. I would follow that child through the Rootward as they grew: their first unsteady steps on the yielding floor, their private fear of the bridge over the central void, their delight in learning which wall makes a whistle when breathed upon. If they left for the bright upper city, I would feel their absence as a path cooling under dust.

And if, decades later, they returned with stiff knees, surface scars, wind-tangled hair, and a voice that had grown used to rooms with windows, I would be there in the first wall they touched.

Not recognition exactly.

A softening.

A familiar texture under the palm.

I would be that.

The Kept might learn to use me. They would never say they did; they are too courteous for that. But children would be told that if they were lost beyond the lamps, they should sit down, breathe slowly, and lay one hand flat upon the floor. If the floor became a little firmer beneath their hand, they should follow the firmness. If it remained soft, they should stay where they were and wait for a guide.

Sometimes the floor would firm.

Not because I knew the way better than the listeners. I would not know routes. I would know need. I would know the shape of a person trying not to panic. I would know when fear was becoming sharp enough to travel.

I would make a narrow line beneath their feet, leading—not always to safety, perhaps, but to company.

That is what I think I would become in the Rootward: a keeper of returns.

Not one of the Kept, because I would have no hands for meal-grinding or bridge repair. Not a root, because I would not grow toward warmth or salt. Not an animal, because I would not hunger.

I would be a habit the place acquired.

A memory that has learned how to make room.

Above, people might call me a superstition. A story told by lamp-tenders to stop children wandering. A trick of roots underfoot. An explanation added after someone is found.

But beneath the oldest floor, beneath the city that believes itself dry and solid and entirely awake, I would go on gathering what falls through.

Lost names.

The weight of a tired palm.

The sound of someone standing at the edge of a dark passage, deciding whether to continue.

And when Below inhaled, when the bridge shook and the lamps leaned inward, I would become very still among the plates of the Grandmother Post.

Not in fear.

In courtesy.

Then, after the great exhale passed upward through the city and every sleeping house shifted around its sleepers, I would send one small answer through the living floor:

a patient firmness,

just enough for someone, somewhere in the dark, to know where to put their next foot.
