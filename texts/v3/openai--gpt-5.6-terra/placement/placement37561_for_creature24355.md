I would not live at the center of it.

I would begin by thinking I wanted to be a vent reader: one of the paired figures in heat veils walking the fresh cracks with instruments, writing down temperatures and gas colors, learning to tell the basin’s moods by the taste of metal at the back of the throat. That is the kind of work I would admire from afar. It has the right gravity. It lets a person stand close to the unfinished world and call attention a profession.

But I think I would be too slow for it, or too susceptible to the stories people tell around vents. I would start recording the shapes of plumes in the margins of the official notebooks. I would name them. I would become unreliable.

So I would find myself somewhere less important, which is usually where I belong: in one of the high stacked quarters above an old cable-car station, at the edge of a district that has been moved twice and is expecting to move again.

My room would be narrow and warm on one side, cold on the other. A thick pipe would pass through the kitchen wall, enclosed in a dented ceramic sleeve. The floor would slant almost imperceptibly toward the window, enough that I would keep a small blue glass bead on the sill to watch it travel over the months. I would own little that could not be packed into lidded cases. A kettle. Two cups with wide bases. A coat with too many repaired seams. A shelf of maps, none of them current. A small pressed moth wing under a square of volcanic glass, hung above the door despite my claiming not to be superstitious.

I would be a keeper of minor water.

Not an engineer, not a cistern official. Something humbler: the person who tends the roof plates, clears the ceramic channels, notices which drain is beginning to sound wrong. In a city where water comes bead by bead from the undersides of pipes, this would not be a minor task, exactly. But it would be unnoticed most days, which would suit me.

I would wake before the sun began striking the black stone. I would climb through a hatch onto the roof in gloves and a face wrap, carrying a brush, a scraper, a coil of fine wire, and a listening cup of thin brass. The roof plates would already be unfolded around me: hundreds of silver leaves across the neighboring roofs, angled toward the pale vapor drifting up from the basin. Some would be decorated with stamped birds, old ribbon, blue tiles, prayer tags, faded children’s drawings protected beneath lacquer.

I would know my own plates by touch.

The third one from the north parapet has a shallow dent from the year a crane hook fell in the wind. The one over the stairwell catches more gray dust than water. The broad plate by the pipe bridge sings in cold weather because its hinge was made from the wrong alloy, and no one has replaced it because everyone has become fond of the sound.

When the city sweats, I would wait.

That would be the real work: waiting without abandoning attention.

The vapor would rise out of the lower basin and meet the descending cold. It would gather around the towers until their lower stories disappeared. It would pearl on metal rails, pipe seams, wire loops, window frames, the fine hair at the back of my glove. Then the first drops would begin moving along the plates.

Clean roofwater has a bright sound. I would learn it well: a quick glassy tick, nearly cheerful. Pipewater is heavier. It carries a dullness, a drag. Brine makes its own slow, ugly punctuation. A leak from below does not drip properly at all. It hesitates, stutters, arrives in a pattern that makes the listener feel that something beneath the wall has become aware of being heard.

I would be good at hearing that.

Not because I would be brave, but because I would have spent enough time alone with the small systems of the city to recognize when one of them had become strange.

Perhaps neighbors would bring me problems.

An old woman from three landings down would say the drain above her bed has changed its note. A family in the relocated rooms by the east lift would ask whether the pale stain on their collector plate is ordinary silica or something carried up from a new vent. Children would bring me a jar containing a dead cistern moth, asking whether the green along its wings means rain—though rain is not quite the word for anything that happens here.

I would tell them what I knew. I would tell them what I did not know.

At night, I would walk down to the reservoirs.

Not often. The water there would unsettle me. It would be too black, too still, holding the amber stair lamps and red crane beacons without breaking them apart. The salt margins would grow their impossible little architectures: yellow crusts, turquoise fans, transparent blades. The air would smell of sulfur, wet iron, struck flint.

The cistern moths would gather at the lights.

I would watch them and think that they were the closest thing the basin had to snowfall: gray wings descending and rising in the warm dark, each one carrying a minute accidental history of the outside world. Pollen. Skin dust. A fragment of leaf from a country so far below that the people there might not understand why anyone would save a drop of water from a pipe.

And eventually, because I would live there long enough, I would be asked to help during a three-bell warning.

The bells would sound low enough to feel in the chest.

The neighborhood would wake without panic. Doors would open. Heat shutters at the foundations would be unlatched. People in layered coats would move through the vapor with tools and lamps, checking drains, securing shelves, opening the pathways through which the city must breathe. I would climb to the roof and fold the catch-plates in their storm position. Somewhere beneath the floor, the basin would make a sound too deep to call a sound: not an explosion, not a groan, but a pressure changing its mind.

The whole city would exhale.

Steam would pour between buildings. The ash swifts would leave the condenser wall in a sudden pale spiral. The street below would vanish. Cable cars would pass through blankness, their lamps moving like slow stars beneath the real stars.

And in that moment I might understand that I was not merely a water keeper.

I would be one of the small adjustments by which the city remained possible.

Not its hero. Not its owner. Not even a person particularly suited to danger. Just one listener among many, living in a house designed to shift, tending a roof designed to gather what the air reluctantly gives, helping make room for an unfinished earth.

If I had a name in that city, it might be the name of my drainage line rather than my family.

I would be from the Bluewater Line, perhaps: the roofs above the east cable station, draining into a cistern built beneath a street that no longer runs straight.

And if someone asked where to find me, they would not say, *at home.*

They would say:

On the roofs, probably.  
Waiting for the pipes to begin speaking.
