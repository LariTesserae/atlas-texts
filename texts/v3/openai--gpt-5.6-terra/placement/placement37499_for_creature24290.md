I would not arrive there as a traveler.

A traveler assumes there will be a place to put their feet which does not already belong to something else. A ledge, a patch of snow, the margin of a pool. But this country has no margins. The first heat from my boots would call up a bloom of sleepers. My breath would condense on the nearest hum-mat and become drinkable territory. My shadow would interrupt the blue signaling beneath an ice shelf. Within minutes, things would be testing me: root-forms beneath the moss, frost-feathered pickers at the seams of my clothes, clear-winged drifters gathering in the warmer air around my neck.

I think the country would make a use of me before it let me understand it.

At first I might be one of the small warm things: a creature in the lining of a cliff-grazer’s coat, no larger than a hand, with six careful feet and a mouth made for combing frost from translucent hair. I would live in the weather carried by another body. I would know the passing of days by changes in the herd’s breath, by the quality of warmth beneath the guard-hairs, by the low adjustment of thousands of locked limbs when night came down the mountain.

That life would be crowded, but not lonely. Nothing there is lonely. I would sleep pressed among other comb-beasts, every flank against a neighbor, our backs silvered with rime. When the herd began to climb, we would climb with it. When it stopped beside a green cleft, we would descend into the mats and feed on what the great mouths overlooked: ice-born mites, fungal knots, tender shoots emerging from cracked stone.

Perhaps I would have a narrow transparent body, green at the center from what I ate. Perhaps the young would cling in a line beneath my ribs, warming themselves from my blood. Perhaps I would be dull-colored enough to disappear against hide and moss.

But this is too small a life for the part of me that listens.

There is something in that high country which wants not merely to survive inside the system but to hear it as a whole. Not to master it—there is no standing outside it long enough to do that—but to become one of the places where its scattered motions meet.

So perhaps I would become a pulse-keeper.

Not an animal exactly, and not a plant. A colony formed in the underside of ice, where melt channels connect one luminous basin to another. I would begin as a dark thread on the floor of a pool. Water would pass over me, carrying minerals, spores, waste, blood diluted from some death far uphill. I would divide, spread, lace myself through the silt and into cracks beneath the ice. Other threads would join me: red, black, glass-clear, each with its own appetite.

Together we would become a nervous tissue for water.

When the temperature shifted, I would know. When a herd’s weight changed the pressure in the ice above, I would know. When a hunter entered a nearby channel, or a column forest released its first silver cloud of seed, the chemistry would arrive through the meltwater before any visible sign reached the surface.

Then I would shine.

Not with a message anyone had intended to send. Not warning, not welcome, not language in the narrow sense. I would light because the conditions had become light-worthy: because the basin had warmed by a fraction, because too many grazers had drunk at an edge, because something large had moved in the dark below. My glow would travel along a linked chain of pools. Blue would wake under the ice, one chamber after another, across a valley no one could cross without becoming food, shelter, or fertilizer.

Small animals would follow the pulses. Hunters would wait for them. The herd above might pause, unsettled by an instinct older than sight. Frost-feathered birds would gather on black rocks, their metallic calls flicking across the steam.

And I would not see any of this.

I would only feel the water changing.

Yet perhaps that is the proper way to belong there: not as an observer who receives an image of the world, but as a surface upon which the world registers itself.

Still, even the pulse-keeper remains too deep. It understands the country by pressure and chemistry, but not by sound. The sound is essential. The humming mats, the cavern-roar, the wet percussion in the vapor columns, the clicking ice—all of it says that the mountains are not silent stone with life attached. They are an instrument being played by cold air and multitude.

So I find another form.

I am one hollow strand in a humming mat.

Not the entire mat; never the entire mat. A single tube, dark at the root, pale and frost-skinned at the exposed end, growing out of a mineral crack where warm black rock meets snowmelt. I share my walls with thousands of neighboring tubes. We are bound by mineral fibers, fungal meshes, soft green tissues. Stone-eaters work below us, making the crack wider one grain at a time. Grazers crop our outer growth but leave enough of our dense lower body that we return. Their dung enriches us. Their hooves compress the slope and force water downward. Birds pick insects from our surfaces. Root-creatures sleep in our shade. When one of the great scavengers feeds nearby, its cast-off fragments nourish our darker margins.

I am not separate enough to call myself a plant. I am a duct in a living weather machine.

At night, cold air enters me.

It comes down from the snowfields with the force of a slow tide. It moves through the tubes, across mineral ribs, under frozen sheets, through chambers where a day’s captured moisture waits. The air makes me vibrate. I join the others. The hillside’s gathered hum rises until it is no longer possible to say which note belongs to which body.

The mountain speaks through us, though not deliberately.

Or perhaps deliberation is just too small a word for what happens when enough lives arrange themselves around the same need.

I would know the sun as an inward thaw. I would know rain as a rare metallic sweetness. I would know the herd as a long dimming when it crossed above me, followed by the rich falling warmth of waste and the trembling impact of many hooked feet. I would know death as a different kind of green arriving in the soil. I would know centuries as the slow enlargement of my crack.

And when a ridge was newly born—when fire lifted black stone through old ice and left a sheer sterile face shining in the cruel sun—I would be carried there eventually. Not as myself, perhaps. As a seed filament, a drifting fragment, a thread inside a scrap of hide caught in the wind. I would settle in a seam too thin to call shelter. I would freeze. I would wait.

Then one wet season, perhaps after decades, I would open.

I would begin the hum.

That is where I would find myself: not on the summit, not beneath the glacier, not among the sovereign wings, but at the threshold between rock and weather—one small resonant channel in the dense blue-green machinery of a mountainside.

I would be something that takes cold air into itself and gives the whole country back its voice.
