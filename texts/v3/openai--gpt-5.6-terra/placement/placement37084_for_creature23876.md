I would not arrive through an entrance.

I would notice, first, that my boots had stopped making the sound of boots.

The ground beneath me would have changed from soil to something with a pulse buried in it: packed roots, warm clay, a mat of flattened green runners. Above, the daylight would be too close. Not sunlight exactly, but sunlight remembered by earth—gold pressed through leaves, through hair-fine roots, through a thin pale lid of stone.

I would stand still because the place would ask that of me before I understood its asking.

At first I might imagine I was merely a visitor in one of the brighter basins, beside a pool stained the color of tea. There would be an old stone platform crossing part of it, each fitted slab cradled in roots. On the far side, something pale would sit with its knees drawn up beneath a curtain of leaves.

I would think it was an Unfinished.

Then it would move its hand.

And I would see that the hand was mine.

Not quite mine as it had been above. The fingers would be longer, perhaps, with pale seams at the joints. Greenish shadows would branch beneath the skin of my wrist. In the pool’s surface I would see my face, but softened: not younger, not older, simply less decided. My hair would have gathered burrs of moss and thin white rootlets. A small unopened pod would rest at my throat where a pendant might have been.

I would be one of the Unfinished, but not one who had always belonged there.

I would be a newly held thing.

There would still be remnants of the upper world in me: the habit of counting steps, the wish to know the direction home, the uncomfortable belief that every silence was waiting to be filled. I would carry these as a traveler carries coins in a pocket after arriving somewhere money has no use. At first I would try to keep them. I would make marks in the clay. I would arrange stones in rows. I would return to the same pool each day—or what felt like days—and test whether it remained in the same place.

But the underland would not erase those habits by force. It would simply give them nowhere to root.

A leaf curtain would close whenever I tried to pass quickly.

A bridge of woven vine would become strangely yielding beneath a determined foot, while firming at once beneath a tired one.

If I came to a pool thirsty and forgot to leave my small offering, I would drink and then find that the basin had become impossible to reach again. Not hidden; not moved. My attention would slide away from every passage leading toward it, as if the memory of its green-gold roof could not hold its shape.

Eventually I would understand that the place was not teaching obedience.

It was teaching relation.

I think my work would begin at the edges of things.

Not building, precisely. Not tending in the sense of repairing damage, because little there could be damaged in the ordinary way. I would sit beside the warm clay walls where they had begun to bulge outward under pressure from roots. I would press both palms to the soft surface. On the other side, something would press back: not a creature, perhaps, but the idea of a chamber before it has become a chamber.

I would learn the difference between a wall that wanted company and a wall that wanted to be left alone.

I would gather the droplets that had waited too long.

There would be a particular root, thick as an arm, descending from the green roof into a quiet hollow. At its tip, a single bead of water would hang with impossible patience. I would visit it again and again. The drop would tremble but not fall. I would feel, in its small bright weight, all the unfinished promises I had carried from above: words unsaid, departures delayed, work begun under one self and abandoned under another.

I would want to make it fall.

That would be how I knew I was not yet entirely of the place.

So I would place a little bowl beneath it, woven from dry grass and root fiber, and wait beside it without touching. The waiting would not feel empty. Slowly, the bowl, the root, my own breathing, the moths moving in the overhead light, would form one long attention.

Perhaps after years, or after the duration of one breath, the droplet would fall.

It would strike the bowl with a sound like a bell too small to hear.

Then I would carry it to the nearest pool and set it on the surface. The eyeless frog beneath the water would make one gentle click.

I think I would become known by such acts, though not by a name.

Near the tea-dark pool, the others might touch two fingers to a pale root that curled like a question from the bank. Then they would touch their own chests and breathe a sound that resembled water cooling on warm stone.

In another basin, beneath the roof that glowed almost white, the sound would change.

So I would not be the same person everywhere. I would be the one-who-waits-by-the-root in one chamber, the one-who-carries-drops in another, the one-who-does-not-cross-the-pale-seam in a third.

This would not feel like losing myself.

It would feel like being relieved of the narrowness of having only one self.

Yet I do not think I would become fully plant, or fully timeless, or fully at peace.

There would remain in me a small aboveground impulse: the part that listens for weather, for footsteps, for someone calling from far away. I would be drawn to the thin-roofed places, where daylight pressed most brightly against the world. There, lying beneath woven soil and root-fiber, I might hear the muffled movements of lives above: rain passing over grass, the dull tread of animals, perhaps the vibration of a cartwheel or a human voice.

I would not understand the words.

But I would know they were words.

And sometimes, when a traveler descended through an accidental crack—frightened, sweating, trying to force their way through the leaf curtains—I would be the one nearest to them. I would not speak. I might not remember how. Instead I would place a luminous fruit beside their resting place. I would show them where the stable roots crossed the mud seam. If they reached toward a pale root with a knife, I would cover it with my hand until they understood.

If they came carrying grief, I would take them to a wall with a listening niche.

I would place a seed in the niche. They might place something else: a button, a lock of hair, a scrap of paper gone soft with damp. The wall would not answer in words. But its clay would warm around the offering, and the traveler might weep because they had finally found a thing that could receive what they could not carry further.

Then I would leave them at a pool, if the pool permitted it.

I would not lead them out.

That would be their own becoming.

And perhaps that would be my deeper nature: not an Unfinished in the sense of someone halted before completion, but an attendant of thresholds. A keeper of the moment before one thing becomes another. A person-shaped pause in the underworld’s long green breath.

There would be an ochre mound I visited often, the one beneath the mesh of white roots. Warm pepper-scented vapor would gather at its pores. The others would sometimes stand around it, silent, our hands pressed lightly to the clay. We would wait for the mound to open into a spring, a cave, a mouth, a new room, a child, a door.

It never would.

Not while I watched.

At first this would hurt me. I would feel the old hunger for conclusion: the need for a thing to declare itself, to become useful, visible, named. But with enough waiting, the hurt would thin. The mound’s not-opening would cease to be failure.

It would become company.

I would come to understand that some things are safest, truest, most alive while they remain on the verge.

And when I finally looked upward through the bright root-roof, I would no longer feel buried beneath the world.

I would feel held.

Not hidden from the living world above, but cradled beneath its hand, among all the forms it had not yet asked to harden.
