I would be something that does not appear on any map of the town, though everyone there would know me by use.

Not a person exactly. Not an animal. I would be the little footbridge over the stream.

The unpainted one.

I would have been put there when the works were young enough to imagine they would need a proper crossing only temporarily: two steel side rails, a deck of thick planks replaced one at a time, bolts rusted into shapes no catalogue carries anymore. I would join the back of the workers’ houses to the narrow lane beside the settling ponds. On one side, the square and its shuttered shops; on the other, the works, the willow, the reed water, the slope where rabbits scatter at dusk.

People would say I was unsafe in the casual voice people use for things they trust with their whole weight.

They would say the rust holds me together.

And because they say that, no one paints me.

I would spend my life being crossed.

Before dawn, the first workers would come over me with their collars up, smelling of soap and cold tobacco, their lunch tins knocking against their knees. They would not look down at the water. They would know whether the stream was low by the sound beneath my boards: a thin ticking over stones, or a thick, quick body dragging branches against my supports.

Children would cross me differently. They would run across the middle and stop at the far end. They would balance along my lower rail until someone shouted. They would wedge fired-clay shards into the cracks between my planks, as though leaving offerings. A few would spit into the stream and watch their spit vanish. The bold ones would climb underneath, where the girders smell of iron, algae, and old rain.

At noon on Thursdays, when the works siren rises—long, blunt, unmusical—I would tremble in all my bolts. The dogs would answer from yards and doorways. The rooks would lift from the gantry in a black complaint. The stream would go on beneath it all, uninterested.

I think I would remember feet better than faces.

The slow heavy feet of kiln-men. The uneven step of the old fitter who keeps visiting after retirement, pausing halfway over me to look at the yard as if it were still his responsibility. The quick rubber-booted tread of a woman from the weighbridge office who always crosses holding her umbrella closed, even in rain. The light, suspicious steps of foxes after dark. Rats moving in the shadow of my lower beams. Once, perhaps, the small careful feet of someone carrying a baby home through mist.

I would know who had left by absence.

The boy who used to kick every third plank and later came back taller, with a car and a different accent. The man who crossed me every morning for thirty years, then stopped. The woman with the pigeon feathers stuck to her coat. The delivery driver who once slept in his cab for two nights during a flood and fed the chickens crisps.

I would not understand the reasons. Bridges are not built to understand why someone goes from one side to the other. We only learn the pressures of passage.

Under me, the basin would make its slow correction.

Water would blacken the wood. Willow roots would seek the masonry at my abutments. Floods would push pale gravel against my legs and pull it away again. Moss would take the shaded bolts. In winter, frost would lift one board a fraction higher than the others. Somebody would hammer it down. Somebody else, years later, would replace it with a board that did not quite match. I would become a record of repairs performed by hands now dead, each repair keeping the old shape alive a little longer.

Sometimes the stream would rise too high.

Then it would strike my underside with brown force, carrying plastic buckets, branches, a dead hen, broken tiles, whole quilts of leaves. People would stand on the banks and stare, speaking in short practical sentences. Someone would put up a temporary barrier at either end of me. Children would try to get past it. The works would send a man in a yellow coat to inspect my supports.

I would remain.

Not because I was strong, exactly. Because I had learned the particular kind of yielding that lets a thing stay where it is.

And perhaps, deep in the metal of me, there would be another life—not a ghost, not quite a mind. A gathering of all the crossed-over things: the grit from boots, the dropped screws, the mud, fur, seed, breath, rainwater, bits of leaf caught in the grating. The basin’s persistence passing over itself, day after day, using me as a narrow permission.

That is where I would find myself: over the small stream nobody drinks from, between the town’s useful part and its overgrown part.

I would be rusting slowly, holding.
