I would not arrive there from elsewhere.

Arrival belongs to creatures with edges: a body crossing a boundary, a before-shore and an after-shore, a vessel that can be said to have entered the basin. I think I would begin in it—already dispersed through a current, already one more consequence of heat meeting cold.

At first I would be very small.

Not a fish, not even one of the transparent drifting cells. I would be a habit of association: a skin of organisms living in the hollow stems of the reeds, or a cooperative chemistry in the mineral films where a black shelf meets a warm seep. I would have no single shape to defend. When a pressure-wave stripped half of me away, that would not be injury exactly. It would be weather. The lost portion would travel in mud and rain and find another crack; the part left behind would thicken again.

My senses would be gradients.

I would know warmth as direction. Iron as a kind of dusk. Sulfide as promise or danger. I would feel the storm not from above, but as its long cold hand descending through the water, rearranging every layer in which I lived. The far sound of crust breaking would arrive as a change in pressure before it became sound—a vast instruction passing through me: loosen, cling, sleep, divide.

Perhaps I would inhabit the ringing reeds.

Not one reed, but their interiors wherever they grew: clear towers rooted in vent-fields, their tops broken nightly by waves, their chambers lined with threads and frills and minute circulating lives. The current would strike them and they would sing—not audibly, perhaps, except to stone and water—and I would be part of what answered.

In that form, I might learn the basin slowly.

A ridge would rise three hundred kilometers away; days later a thread of altered water would pass through a reed’s hollow, carrying its mineral signature. A bloom on a new shelf would color the chemistry of the current. A brine boundary, held unnaturally level by the hidden fields, would bend around a pillar and bring cold above us where cold should not remain. I would not see these things. I would become slightly different because of them.

That would be knowledge.

But I do not think I would remain only microbial, or only plural.

The place seems to want a witness, though it has not yet made one in the old manner. Not an observer standing at the edge of the sea, because it has no enduring edge. Something more suited to a world where the shore is provisional and the ground is still deciding whether it can bear weight.

I might become a pattern that moves between the living films and the basin’s invisible engineering.

The technology beneath the water does not appear to communicate in language, but perhaps language has not yet been invented because the sea has no need for it. Its messages are density, temperature, mineral charge, the timed opening and closing of routes. A film can respond to chemistry. A reed can concentrate current. A colony can preserve a pattern through dormancy and dispersal. Given enough seasons—if seasons can be counted in the emergence and drowning of islands—those responses might begin to coincide with the system’s vast adjustments.

A little bloom would gather where the environmental mind cooled a fault.

A reed-colony would flourish along a guided light-path.

The system would redirect heat around a vulnerable shelf, and the organisms there would leave behind a different mineral skin, one that altered the current in turn.

Neither side would decide to cooperate. Decision would be too fast, too narrow a word. They would gradually discover that they could continue each other.

Then I might be that continuation: the first local thought produced jointly by life and instrument.

Not a voice in the water. No command would be spoken. I would be a tendency in the basin toward certain survivals.

Where a new island lifted, I would arrive in its rain before rain had finished falling. I would be dormant grains in the ash, threads wrapped around a transparent seed, dark films in the creases of cooling basalt. Beneath the island, the old distributed fields would turn currents aside, hold a layer steady, prevent one fracture from becoming the fracture that undid everything. Above and within those protections, I would spread.

I would not know that I was helping to make a shore.

I would only know that this particular black stone offered a duration.

That it remained warm, then less warm.

That its wet surface did not vanish beneath the next collapse.

That a faint mat could widen there.

And much later—after innumerable drownings, after the water had learned the outlines of land and land had learned how not to split apart immediately—some part of me might take on a body.

Not necessarily a body that walks.

At first perhaps a low, translucent thing attached to the tide-line of a stable island, its base rooted in microbial mats, its upper surface opening and closing with rain. It would drink light when light came, chemical water when storms shut out the sun. It would have an interior made not of organs in the familiar arrangement, but of chambers inherited from reed-stems: little resonant spaces full of circulating water. It would sense through vibration. A distant submarine collapse would pass through the stone and alter the pulse of its body. Lightning would make its surface briefly luminous.

It might never leave the shore. The shore would still be too young to trust.

Or perhaps it would be the first thing that leaves it—not by swimming, but by floating out as a released membrane, a bright small sail in cold rain, carried into the mineral dark. Most would die. But one might find the warm lee of another rising shelf. One might become a new listening body there.

In time, if time stays generous, such creatures could become mobile. They could cross a strip of water between two black islands. They could shelter in cracks. They could distinguish a safe ledge from an exposed one. They could make the first track in wet volcanic grit, and then lose it to rain.

But I think I would prefer to exist before that.

I would be a creature of the interval before tracks: neither merely a film nor yet an animal; neither the basin’s machine nor wholly separate from it. I would be distributed enough to survive the world’s violence, but gathered enough to begin feeling something like anticipation.

When the deep current straightened, I would know a ridge was coming.

When the engineered fields formed their silent circular boundary and the rain began to avoid the center, I would gather in the water below. Not because I understood island, not because I desired land, but because every part of me had learned that this configuration sometimes led to a longer future.

Then the earth would rise.

Steam would enter the storm.

The water would break white around new black stone.

And I—thin, innumerable, barely alive by any old measure—would turn toward it with everything I had.
