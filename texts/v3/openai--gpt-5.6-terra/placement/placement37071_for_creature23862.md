I would arrive without arriving.

Not by the black mountain paths, which end in wet stone and old snow, nor by the silver arches, which would not admit that they were entrances. I would be there after sitting too long beneath one of the doorless shelters, rain ticking on the handrail beside me. At some point the warmth behind the wall would become the warmth at my back. The wall would have forgotten to remain a wall.

At first I think I would be human: a surveyor, perhaps, one of the people who once brought cups and gloves and wrote names near the central basin. I would have a pack with instruments that no longer measure what I need measured. A compass whose needle points toward the newest ground. A notebook in which the ink fades unless I write while standing still. I would have come to inspect the terrain engine’s output, to make a report on porosity and adhesion and biological uptake.

But the shelf would not let me remain merely an inspector.

It recognizes weight before it recognizes intention. The first night I sleep there, perhaps against a shelter wall or beneath the black plates of one of the sparse trees, the ground learns the shape of my shoulder, hip, heel, palm. In the morning, where I lay, there is a blue outline in the pale stone—not quite a body-print, not quite a fossil. It continues to glow after I have risen.

The six-winged grazers gather around it.

They do not approach me with affection. They approach with professional interest. Their wings open in sequence, each translucent joint catching the gray daylight. They listen to my ankles, to the blood moving in my wrists, to the minute hesitation of my breath. Then they begin clicking among themselves.

Not alarm.

Classification.

I would learn their language only gradually, by hearing it returned through the stone at night. Their clicks are not names, exactly. They are conditions.

*Temporary load.*
*Warm source.*
*Unstable margin.*
*Possible root.*

The last would follow me.

I would not become the tower resident. That figure has already become too much attention, and I have spent my life, before the shelf, trying to be less visible than I was. Nor would I become an Attendant, though I would sit among them sometimes in the warm shelters, watching ice bloom across the instrument faces.

They would not teach me. They would make room.

One Attendant might turn toward me with a face whose features were incomplete except for one extraordinarily patient eye. I would ask what I was meant to do here.

Hours later, after rain, the shelf would answer beneath my feet:

“What are you able to remain?”

That would be the question.

At first I would try to remain a visitor. I would walk every possible route before dusk, making maps that became false as soon as the ground breathed. I would follow the birds to pools where my reflection wore a different coat, carried a different scar, or arrived with gray at the temples. I would watch the white burrowers disappear into vents, their orange fungal cords twitching like bright nerves. I would stand far from the steam predators, though sometimes I would sense them beside me: not hungry, perhaps, but considering whether a person could be removed from a pattern without the pattern noticing.

Eventually I would understand that the shelf has no use for a witness who only takes.

Nothing can be removed. Not stone, not water, not a clean fact.

So I would become a keeper of returns.

I would walk the places where workers’ traces recur: the abandoned cups, the damp gloves, the names that vanish from the basin’s rim. When a cup appeared, I would leave it until rain filled it, then tip the water carefully onto a thirsty mat. When a glove appeared, I would place it on a handrail where the condensation could bead along each finger. When a name surfaced in the stone, I would stand beside it until night, lending it enough remembered weight to glow blue.

Not preserving it forever. Nothing there should be held forever.

Only long enough for the shelf to decide whether it belonged.

The ground would begin to use me.

Where a new crack opened too sharply, I would sit near it. The crack would soften its note around my breathing. Where water pooled without finding a pore, I would wade in, and the silver-rooted mats at the edge would turn toward the salt of my skin. Where an old black-plated tree shifted during a storm, I would brace my hands against its trunk. The plates would settle one by one under my palms, like enormous cards dealt into a better order.

My body would change, but not in the dramatic manner of a curse.

The rain would stop making me cold.

Fine pale hairs would grow along my forearms, sensitive to pressure in the ground. When I slept, they would lift and tremble at the approach of shifts deep below the shelf. My footprints would last longer than an hour, then days, then seasons—not as marks of possession, but as small blue reassurance: here, the terrain had been tested by a living weight and had not failed.

The grazers would cease to classify me as temporary.

They would come to rest near me when the shelf began its nightly contractions. Their warm broad backs would press against my knees. Together we would listen to the metallic booms below, the bell-like complaints, the immense patient hum that was either machine or heart.

And perhaps, after enough time, I would no longer know whether I was guarding the shelf or whether it was keeping me in the unfinished state I had always needed.

I would be its human-sized pause.

Not an owner. Not an operator. Not even a resident in the ordinary sense.

A retained impression.

A person the ground had decided not to erase yet.
