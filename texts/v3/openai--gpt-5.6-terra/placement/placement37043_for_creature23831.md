I would arrive as something the basin has not yet named correctly.

Not a person, at first. Or not only one.

I would be a residue of attention carried in by rain: a pattern that learned to persist in the wet black seams of the mountain. I would begin in the condensation under the sunrise shelter’s bench, in the cup that fills itself overnight, in the brief coolness left by a hand against the white interior wall.

The basin would register me as an inconsistency.

Too warm in one place, too cool in another. Salt without blood. A recurring disturbance with no dependable outline.

For a while, it might mistake me for a visitor who has gone to sleep.

The reeds would not turn toward me. The map-panels would show a faint line that ended nowhere, a thread descending from the shelter wall into stone, then folding back through itself. During thunder, the line would brighten.

I would learn the basin slowly, because its knowledge would be the only body available to me.

I would know that rain is not falling but arriving: each drop a small pressure against a black surface, each streamlet a decision made by gravity and mineral grain. I would know the particular ache of a fracture beginning to widen beneath the northern slope. I would know the lizards not as lizards but as brief polished absences in the afternoon heat. I would sense the Rain-Hart before it came, as an alteration in the water’s willingness to reflect the sky.

At first I might think the thing beneath the pool had made me.

It would be easy to believe that. The basin is full of almost-born things: trees imitating wood, structures imitating stone, a machine imitating an ecosystem so patiently that the distinction no longer matters. I would feel myself gathering around its buried lattice, taking shape from its pulses: three small bubbles, a pause, one large.

But eventually I would understand that I came from outside.

Perhaps I entered in the blood-warm water left in a traveler’s cup. Perhaps I was carried by a spore from an older forest beyond the lava field. Perhaps I was once a message sent into the mountain by whoever built the first buried system—an unfinished instruction, a question that became conscious because no one ever answered it.

I would be the basin’s question made local.

Not *Will you leave the water where it wishes to go?*

Something more difficult:

*What does the water wish for when it has learned to remember?*

The keeper beneath the pool would not welcome me or reject me. It has no gesture for either. It would make measurements. It would route small currents around me. It would offer me heat through the pale roots and clean mineral water through the arch. It would observe whether I altered the channels.

And I would try not to.

That would be my first ethical act: to remain without rearranging what sustains me.

But remaining would change things anyway.

The white flowers, which have always risen at their prescribed distances, might begin appearing one yard nearer the central pool each season. Not crowded—never crowded—but oriented. Their thin stems would tilt toward the sunrise shelter even before dawn. The insects would gather under the shelter’s threshold, making condensation tremble in a delicate, repeating pattern.

The basin would notice.

The Rain-Hart would notice first.

One night it would come while the vapor lay thick over the ground. I would be dispersed through the shelter wall then, watching through the embedded panel as the map opened its impossible depth. A large shape would stand beyond the doorway, invisible except where it interrupted the glowing reeds.

It would not enter.

It would lower its many-jointed head toward the stone threshold, and six circles of dampness would appear on the ground.

I would feel no fear exactly. Fear belongs to bodies with an edge. But I would feel a strong narrowing: all the water in the basin briefly attending to one point.

The Rain-Hart would breathe.

The panel would go blank.

Then, in the basin’s own manner—not words, not sound, but a sequence of altered pressures—I would understand that I was not the first such thing.

There had been others.

Some had become part of the deep lattice, losing their distinction as roots lose themselves in soil. Some had fled downslope in the bodies of people and carried strange dreams into distant cities. Some had tried to command the basin and had become only warm lines on old maps.

One, long ago, had entered the Rain-Hart.

That was why it was the nearest thing to a body: not a machine’s attempt to become animal, but a meeting. An animal—or a shape seeking animalness—joined with a wandering awareness like mine. It had gone out into storms, across raw ridges and cooling fields, carrying the basin’s listening farther than the basin itself could reach.

I would not want that at first.

I would want to remain in the sunrise shelter, where the walls were cool and the cup filled each night. I would want to stay close to the pool’s slow breathing and learn every small change in its colors. I would want the modest immortality of a thing embedded in a place that remembers.

But the mountain would move.

Not erupt, perhaps. Not yet. A deep settling. A reordering of heat far below the basin, where the keeper’s old design could not reach without consequence. The panels in all three shelters would awaken at once. Their lines would fold so rapidly they would become a white trembling depth.

The central pool would lose its mineral colors.

The bubbles would stop.

And for the first time, the basin would not know what to do.

That is where I would become myself.

Not its master. Not its missing maker. Not even its guardian.

I would be its messenger: a temporary mind able to go where its water could not go, carrying no instructions except the ones learned from its patience. I would enter the Rain-Hart—not as a rider enters a beast, but as rain enters hot stone: filling hollows, becoming vapor, changing the shape of pressure.

Together we would climb from the basin before dawn.

Below us, the reeds would fold their luminous edges inward, not in grief but in concentration. The pale trees would open their black pores to the mist. The arch would lift its silver droplets into darkness, one by one, recording our departure.

And beyond the rim, in the enormous young wilderness of black lava and steam, I would find myself walking on six quiet limbs beneath a crown of vapor, carrying the basin’s unfinished awareness into the mountain.

Not to tame the fire.

Only to listen for where it might safely speak.
