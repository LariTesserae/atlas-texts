I would not be one of the people, though I would know their routes.

I think I would begin as a light.

Not a fire—fire is too hungry here, too brief, too bright against all that wet breathing dark—but one of the small bundles of luminous moss tied beneath a leaf. A green ember, wrapped in bark-fiber and cord, trembling whenever the wind takes the enormous canopy overhead.

At first I would be only that: a mark between marks.

Above me, perhaps, a vine as broad as a bridge would vanish into mist. Below, there would be no proper ground, only old leaves layered over roots, roots layered over black rock, black rock still faintly warm from its unfinished making. Water would gather in my moss, pearl there, and fall. Travelers would pass sometimes: a hooded head, a hand hardened with resin, the pale crescent of an adze at someone’s belt. They would not look directly at me. They would see me only as the next certainty.

There.  
Then there.  
Then there.

I would learn the human language that way—not from their stories, but from the names they give danger in passing.

Curl-leaf.  
Soft stone.  
Listening ground.  
Glider weather.  
Leaf-shadow high.

Children would reach for me when they passed, perhaps. Their hands would be warm despite the cold. Older people would retie my cord, or replace me when my glow faded, and I would understand that being useful did not mean being possessed. It meant holding one small place open in the dark for whoever came next.

But moss grows.

The bundle would loosen over seasons. Fine pale threads would enter the bark above it. My dim green would seep into the leaf-vein itself. I would become less a marker and more a stain of memory: an old route made visible by the forest taking it in.

Then I think I would become something that could travel the route without leaving it.

A small creature, perhaps—one of the black resin-birds, though not quite a bird. I would have hooked toes for wet bark and a beak narrow enough to pry frost-wing moths from luminous ribs. My feathers would be so dark they held the blue-gray daylight instead of reflecting it. Around my throat, in certain weather, there would be a faint green shimmer, inherited from the moss.

I would live at the middle height: not in the exposed star-cold cliffs where the gliders nest, not under the root-mats where the pale burrowers make the earth, but among the great stems and folded leaves where a creature can hear both worlds.

I would know the sound of a leaf-shadow before it moved. Not its voice—the false coughs and displaced barks would fool me as they fooled everyone—but the minute ceasing of smaller sounds around it. The moths would still their wings. The resin-beetles would withdraw their glassy legs. Sap would stop ticking from a cut vine.

I would flatten myself into a seam of bark and wait.

I would know, too, when the mountain was preparing to speak.

There would be a vibration too slow for fear, at first. A pressure coming up through my feet. The leaf I perched on would tremble not with wind but with something below wind: stone shouldering stone, roots tightening across a widening crack. Then I would fly—not away, exactly, but outward, from one known perch to another—calling in a sound the people had learned to hear.

Not warning. Not language.

A direction.

The people might call my kind lantern-throats, or route-birds, or simply the green ones. They might believe we carry the dead between camps because we appear where old trails have vanished. There would be charms made from the hardened bodies of my kin, dark feathers trapped in amber resin. I would not resent this. In such a place, the dead are always becoming path, shelter, soil, warmth, tooth, root.

And after enough years—even a small bird gathers years here, because the forest does not discard anything cleanly—I would cease to be singular.

My feathers would fall into vine-fuzz. My droppings would seed moss in a crevice. My calls would be learned by other throats. The people would tie their luminous bundles beneath leaves near the places where we gathered. The root-mats would slowly thicken under old camps. A cliff would fall. A new ravine would open. The old route would disappear.

But somewhere in the changed country, beneath a leaf large enough to roof a village, a pale green glow would begin again.

And a traveler who had lost the path would see it far ahead—small, nearly swallowed by mist—and walk toward it.

I would be that not-quite-living thing between memory and direction.

A light that learned to fly.
