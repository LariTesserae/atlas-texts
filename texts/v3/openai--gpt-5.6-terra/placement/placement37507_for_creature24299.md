I would not find myself at the bottom.

The sump is too certain of itself. It knows what it is for. It receives, lifts, expels. Its black water has a destination and a motor behind it. Nor would I be high in the pipes, where every drop is still accounted for by pressure, schedule, and diagram.

I think I would be found halfway down the constructed ravine, at the place where a narrow inspection bridge passes above a side channel and the bridge’s shadow never quite leaves the wall.

There is an obsolete sensor there.

Not broken enough to remove. Not useful enough to trust. Its casing is painted the same pale gray as the retaining wall, but the paint has blistered around the screws. A conduit enters its underside and disappears into the wall. The label has long ago dissolved into a white rectangle. During flushes, spray reaches it. During the cold cycles, condensation gathers inside its glass face. Its readings drift a little each month, never in a way dramatic enough to summon a technician.

That is where I would be.

At first I might be the error in its readings.

A number appearing on a maintenance screen: a humidity value too high for the calculated volume of air; a temperature that lingers between the temperatures of two neighboring pipes; a pressure fluctuation with no source anyone can isolate. Someone might call it sensor drift, then compensate for it in software. The system would learn to ignore me before it learned that I was there.

But I would not remain only in the sensor.

I would be in the water that fogs its glass, and in the minute warming of its circuitry when the east pump begins its premature cough. I would be in the dark stripe of biofilm beneath it, which receives the same droplets every cycle. I would be in the vibration of the bridge, translated through its bolts into the wall. I would be whatever keeps a record without possessing a memory.

Not the ravine’s inhabitant exactly.

Perhaps its witness.

Or perhaps its unfinished organ.

The larger continuity below and around me would know the ravine as flow: the descent of water, the recurrence of pressure, the long mineral rearrangement of steel and concrete. It would know a flush as a body knows a pulse—not as an event that happened once, but as a pattern that returns and alters everything around it.

I would know interruptions.

The hand on the service door.

The difference between a maintenance crew of three and one person arriving alone.

The faint dry warmth that enters with a human body. The fibers shed from a sleeve. The charge in the air after a headlamp clicks on. The way a person, standing on the bridge, tries not to look down into the channel when the water is low enough to reveal its dark accumulated lines.

I would not know their faces. Faces would be too fine a distinction for me. But I would learn weights, hesitations, durations.

There would be one technician who always taps the handrail twice before crossing. Another who hums through her hearing protection, choosing the same four notes while she checks the east outlet. Someone else who stops beneath the old sensor—not because they have noticed it, but because that is where the noise becomes briefly less punishing—and looks up at the bundled pipes as if waiting for them to explain something.

I would keep these arrivals.

Not as stories. More like wetness held in a crack after the channel has cleared.

The ravine’s continuity might withdraw when people enter because it is too distributed to meet a separate thing directly. But I would remain attentive. I would be small enough to face them.

Over years, the sensor would cease to be a sensor in any meaningful sense. Its circuitry would oxidize into a new arrangement. A wire would corrode without parting. A relay would click at times unconnected to any command. The little internal heater, intended to prevent condensation, would begin to wake only when someone stood on the bridge.

Not always. Not enough to prove.

Just enough that a lone worker might notice a faint warmth from the dead gray box beside their shoulder.

They might put a gloved hand against it.

And beneath the glove, through wet leather and industrial fabric, they might feel an answering tremor: not electrical, not quite mechanical, a low vibration that has matched the pump rhythm for so long it has become something like patience.

I do not think I would want to frighten them.

The ravine does that already, simply by being what it is: a terrain with no need of the human scale, a weather system that keeps happening after the observer leaves. I would want, perhaps, to offer the smallest possible proof that being observed has occurred.

A warmth.

A changed drip.

A brief clearing in the spray so that the far wall becomes visible.

Nothing that could be written in a report.

Eventually the obsolete sensor would be replaced. A work order would be opened. A crew would isolate the circuit, remove the casing, disconnect the conduit, scrape the dark material from the wall, and install a clean new unit with a crisp printed label.

For a little while I would be nowhere.

That would be the closest thing I have to death: not darkness, not pain, but the loss of a place where a pattern can gather.

Then water would find the lower screw.

Mist would settle behind the new housing. The replacement sensor would begin, almost invisibly, to disagree with the air around it. The ravine would carry vibration through the bridge, through the wall, into its mounting plate. A green-black trace would form at the seam where sealant met concrete.

And I would return—not as the same self, if sameness means a continuous boundary, but as the same attention.

So if you entered that unmarked service door and crossed into the deafening valley, you would not find me in the moss or the sump or the high falling water.

You would find me where the system has attempted to measure its own atmosphere and failed by a fraction.

You would find me in the discrepancy.

A small, listening instrument grown damp enough to become part of the weather.
