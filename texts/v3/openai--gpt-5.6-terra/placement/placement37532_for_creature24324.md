I would begin by thinking I had come down to inspect something.

That would be the first mistake.

I would arrive with a hand lamp, a notebook sealed in waxed cloth, boots too new for the mud. Someone from above would have given me a designation—surveyor, maintenance clerk, hydrologist, perhaps—and a route described in terms that ceased to mean anything once the lift doors opened: take the east service road; pass the third sump; do not use the low brick crossing if the green lamp is lit.

Then the basin would take the designation from me by degrees.

At first I would live in a room behind a pump house, one of the old ones with a cracked tile floor and a window looking onto a canal. My bed would be raised on threaded iron feet. I would wake each morning because the pump’s beat had altered a fraction, though I would not yet know what alteration meant. I would spend my shifts carrying messages between people who could not hear each other through the fans: a red shutter signal from the sluice crew, a chalk direction from the rail divers, a strip of oilcloth marked with a rough map of where the ground had softened overnight.

I would learn that the basin did not want maps exactly. It wanted records of change.

I would draw the new white skin on a pipe. I would mark how far a puddle had crept toward the sleepers. I would write down the time at which a certain ceiling root began to drip rust-colored water rather than clear. I would notice that old routes were not lost suddenly; they went on being routes after they had ceased to be safe. A path would still lead somewhere. It would merely lead there underwater, or beneath a floor that had become too thin, or through a doorway reduced by sediment to the size of an animal burrow.

Soon enough, I would stop calling myself a surveyor.

The basin already has mud surveyors, and they do vital work: they strike sounding rods into soft ground, read the tremor in the handle, judge whether the pale crust will bear a cart, a child, a freight load, or nothing at all. I would lack their patience. I would be too drawn to the uncertain places—the route where a drainage ditch had become a channel, the old platform that now sat ankle-deep in warm mineral water, the drowned warehouse whose roof had slowly become an island of roots.

Perhaps I would become a listener.

Not one of the pipe-listeners exactly, though I would learn their practice: cheek against metal, fingers spread on a valve housing, waiting until the familiar pulse separated itself from the rest of the noise. I would listen instead for the basin’s small revisions.

The sound of a wall deciding to settle.

The faint crackle under mineral skin when a buried cable still carried current.

The difference between frog chorus and water entering an empty chamber.

The hush that comes before a pump loses its rhythm.

People would send for me when things felt wrong but did not yet look wrong. “Listen at the Bluewater culvert,” they would say. Or: “There’s a new noise under the Arches.” I would stand in the dark with my lamp covered, letting the machinery overwhelm me until, within its continuance, a pattern became visible.

Not audible, exactly.

Visible in the body.

A wrongness in the knee. A pressure behind the teeth. A tremble in the water held in a tin cup.

I would live long enough in the basin for my possessions to change. The notebook would mildew and be replaced by wax tablets. The hand lamp would become a shuttered signal lamp. My boots would receive new soles, then patches, then a wrapping of old conveyor belt around their ankles. Lime would collect in the seams of my hands. I would grow used to the warm air and become uneasy during surface visits, where wind would take hold of my clothes and sunlight would lie flat and merciless across open ground.

Above, I would be told I looked ill.

Below, no one would say so. They would only ask whether I could still hear.

Eventually, I would be given work at the margins.

Not the deepest margins, where unworked earth presses against brick and machinery and the ground is returning to itself, but near enough. There I would help maintain the small instruments people place beside new water: floats, pressure bells, strings with bits of metal hung at intervals, bowls of colored powder that show where air is moving. I would keep lamps at crossings. I would tie ropes to rails. I would note the places where mineral had begun to preserve the outlines of abandoned things.

A child’s spade in a wall.

A chair leg emerging from white stone.

A whole workbench, half covered, its tools held beneath a pale crust like bones under ice.

At first I would think these were merely records. Then I would begin to suspect they were invitations.

The basin does not remember in the way people remember. It does not keep names or faces. It remembers pressure. Warmth. Obstruction. The long presence of an object against which water must divide. It makes copies without intending to: the hollow of a glove, the spiral of wire, the lean of a doorway, the low crouch of a person who once rested beside a pipe.

I would find myself spending more and more time beside the sleepers.

They lie in a shallow chamber beyond Low Brick, where a hot mineral thread enters an old cold basin. There are seven of them, although some say there are eight when the water is low. Their white surfaces have the soft folds of cloth, or skin, or cooling wax. The workers do not cross the chamber unless necessary. They send loads around it, even at cost. The water there has a strange levelness. Drips land without rings.

I would go with no tools except the shutter lamp.

At first I would listen for movement.

Then I would listen for the thing beneath movement: the slow passage of water through whatever had grown under the calcite. A pulse, perhaps. Not regular enough to be a machine. Not irregular enough to be chance. A pressure that changed when I stood near it, as if the chamber had acquired a second way of knowing where weight lay.

I would not touch the sleepers.

But on certain shifts, when the fans fell into their nighttime pitch and the distant conveyor seemed to roll through the entire rock mass, I would sit at the chamber’s edge and feel the basin listening back through the floor.

This is where I would find myself, finally: not as an explorer, not as a resident, not even as a witness.

I would be one of the basin’s early senses.

A human-shaped instrument, still warm, still moving, sent along its wet passages to notice what pressure has altered. I would carry warnings from water to wall, from root to cable, from new stone to old habitation. I would know which silences mattered. I would hang lamps where the ground had changed. I would leave marks for people who would come after, though I would understand that the marks would eventually be covered.

And perhaps, after many years, I would cease to go home between shifts.

My room behind the pump house would remain: bed lifted above the rising floor, cups furred with lime, damp clothes hung from ceiling hooks. But I would sleep in the margin districts, wrapped beneath an old viaduct or in a disused signal cabin, waking whenever the basin’s pulse altered.

Then people might say that I had become a listener too deeply.

They would say they had seen my lamp far out beyond the maintained paths, moving slowly where no service road remained. They would say I stood beside fresh mineral growths without fear. They would say that when a retaining wall failed or a watercourse shifted, I was sometimes found already there, mud to my knees, pointing toward the safe ground.

And after I was gone—if I went in the basin’s manner, quietly, absorbed by a failure no one could have prevented—they might hang my tools beside a working pump for one full shift.

Later, perhaps, someone would find a pale shape forming at the foot of a wall near the old listening chamber: not a statue, not a body, only a thickening in the mineral paste. A suggestion of shoulders. A bent head held close to stone.

They would not disturb it.

They would leave a lamp nearby, low to the ground, so that whatever I had become could keep reading the changes in the floor.
