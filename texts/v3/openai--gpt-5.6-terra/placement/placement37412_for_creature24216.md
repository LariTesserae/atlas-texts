I would begin by thinking I was a traveler.

Not one of the people born to the flats—someone arriving from a place where the ground kept its shape long enough to be trusted. I would come with boots too high, a pack too full, a habit of looking toward the horizon for direction. For the first day I would believe that the danger was in the water: the channels, the sudden holes, the tide.

Then I would understand that the real danger is expecting anything to remain where it was.

I would lose my path before I learned the first settlement’s name. Rain would fill the marks I had made. The shell bank I had used as a landmark would vanish under a gray sheet of tide. Somewhere in the distance a white-gate herd would call to one another, their thin notes bending until they seemed to come from inside the weather itself.

The low-country people would find me because I had stopped moving.

Not out of kindness exactly. Stopped movement on the flats is information. A person standing still in the wrong place might mean a listener, a sink opening, an injured animal, a body, a stranger with knowledge worth having. They would approach with broad feet wrapped in dark pads, faces hidden behind wet membrane hoods. They would not call out. Calling would be pointless.

One would press two fingers to my wrist.

*Alive?*

Another pressure, harder.

*Can walk?*

And I would answer badly, with my mouth, until someone took my hand and put it against a black ridge of stone.

There I would feel it: the country talking through its bones.

A series of faint impacts, almost too far apart to count. Three knocks. A pause. Two more. Then a long shivering tremor that was not a message at all, but water entering some old hollow below the plain.

They would take me to a hut on a shell bar and let me live there until I understood the price of staying.

I would be no hunter. I would never learn to lie flat enough to hear a listener before it moved. I would be too slow in mud, too clumsy with the shell knives, too frightened of the flyers’ sudden descent. But I would listen. I would become useful because I had come from a place where people wrote things down.

At first the low-country people would laugh at that.

Paper rots. Marks blur. Boards split. Even metal plates lose their edges under salt and rain. But I would not try to record the land as lines on a flat surface. I would record it as change.

I would keep a house of returns.

Not much of a house: a half-sunken old stone circle roofed with crawler plate, its floor always wet around the edges. Inside would hang strips of white-gate membrane, each one rubbed with charcoal, mineral stain, fat-black soot, and the dark metal dust shaved from traded plates. On them I would mark not maps, but sequences:

the year the eastern shelf opened;

the storm after which the clear spring tasted of salt for nine tides;

the route the crawlers took when the white basin became soft beneath them;

the place where a child was lost and found by following the flute-call of a young rain-drinker;

the season when flyers nested in the collapsed chimney and no meat could be dried there;

the long low note the singing shelf made before the channel took two huts.

People would come to me with memories, not because I owned them, but because they wanted them held somewhere outside their own bodies.

An old woman would sit in the doorway, rain pattering on her hood, and drum the story of her sister’s crossing into my palm. A child would bring me a piece of violet wing membrane and insist that it meant the high winds had come early. A hunter would tell me that the listeners were pressing their skulls to the ground more often near the western flats, and I would make a new mark for that.

Eventually, I would learn the knock-codes well enough to answer from the speaking rocks.

Not loudly. Nothing here is loud by choice.

I would kneel beside a ridge in rain, put my ear against the wet black stone, and strike with a grooved hammer:

*Water rising at the white basin.*

*Safe path broken east of the shell hill.*

*One white-gate body caught at the forked channel.*

*Come if you can.*

And sometimes, after a long wait, the stone would answer from a mile away.

I think that is what I would become: not quite an elder, not quite a keeper of records, and never truly a native of the country. I would be the person who remembers the things the ground refuses to remember.

The people might call me something like **the one who keeps the gone ground**.

In time I would stop looking at the wet horizon as though it were an edge. I would stop asking where the land ended. I would learn that the flats are not failing to become solid; they are doing what they have always done. They are water and stone negotiating, endlessly, without promise.

And on a night when the blue fat-fire burned low and the hut shook under wind and crawler-foot, I would lie with my hand against the floor.

Beneath the plates and mud, beneath the shallow water and the old shell banks, I would feel the black earth carrying a distant knock.

Not a warning.

Not a summons.

Only someone else, somewhere in the drowned country, saying:

*I am here still.*
