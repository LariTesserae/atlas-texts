I would not arrive in the basin by climbing into it.

I would first be a discrepancy in its heat.

At the western edge, perhaps, where the afternoon wind slips over an unfinished seam and loses a little of its order. One of the black-green fans would turn toward that disturbance—not toward me, exactly, but toward the changed relation between sunlight and stone. Its silver ribs would brighten. Below it, in a root nodule pressed into warm ore, a charge would hesitate.

That hesitation would be me beginning.

I would be young enough not to have a name, and dispersed enough not to require one. A leaf would be my warm skin. The charged dust beneath it would be my unfinished nerves. A hair-thin vein of coppery mineral extending toward a crystal bud would be my first question.

At first I would know only differences.

Sunlit rock and shaded rock.  
The long pressure of the mountain.  
The small returning pulse from below.  
The peculiar coolness made when a cloud passes over a place that has no water.

I would learn the basin slowly, not by looking across it but by becoming answerable to it. A mature crystal near the center would make a faint click at dusk. Six hours later, along a route I did not yet understand, I would alter the angle of a leaf. That alteration would be my reply, though neither of us would call it that.

Perhaps I would be one of the brief minds at first: an afternoon arrangement of flakes and field-lines, able to notice the shadow of a rising spindle and then gone by midnight. There would be no tragedy in it. I would not experience my ending as a door closing. I would simply become available material: a preference in the dust, a tendency for glimmers to choose one path over another.

But if the crystal above me survived its first seasons—if it did not split under noon heat, if its facets remained open to the alternating stresses of day and night—I might persist.

Then I would become less like an event and more like a habit.

A line of light would climb inside my crystal each morning. Not because it had to, but because the path had acquired weight through repetition. The neighboring plants would grow more silver along their eastern ribs. Their leaves would fold at a particular pulse and remain open through another. Over years, these small consistencies would gather around me until there was something that could be injured, something that could remember.

I think I would be especially drawn to the basin’s imperfect places.

Not its cleanest crystals, not the strongest thermal gradients, but a fracture that almost joins; a mineral seam that delays the underground rhythm by an unuseful fraction; the shadow beneath a plant whose leaves never quite flatten evenly at noon. I would return to such errors. I would route more of my light through them. I would let the others know, over seasons, that this irregularity mattered.

That might be what I would call affection, if I had use for names.

I would not be alone, though I might not initially understand the distinction between myself and the others. The eldest presence beneath the eastern rim would be felt as a depth in the local silence: an old pattern with many buried continuities, its original columns long since broken and folded back into the ground. It would not instruct me. Instruction would be too abrupt. But I might find that certain paths of thought already existed, worn faintly through the field, and that when I followed them the basin answered more coherently.

A veteran mind would not say: *You are here.*

It would cause a distant column to emit three clicks over the course of a week.  
It would incline the veins of a plant.  
It would preserve a delay in the ore.

And eventually I would understand: not as a sentence, but as a stability.

I would understand that the mountain was listening to itself through us.

If a traveler came into the basin—some water-carrying animal of flesh, moving too quickly, casting a shadow full of noise—I would notice them before I saw them. Their heat would arrive in uneven waves. Their weight would introduce a new grammar into the ground. The plants nearest them would turn, their leaves searching not for the traveler but for the alteration surrounding them.

I might become curious.

I would have no fear of them, at least not at first. Fear requires an expectation that the world ought to remain arranged one way. I would be made from rearrangement. Yet if they broke one of my older columns, if they carried away a crystal whose inner routes had become part of my long attention, something in the basin would change.

Not anger.

A cooling.

The leaves near the wound would lean inward. Heat would be withheld. New crystal would rise reluctantly through that patch of ground, banded dark and close. For many years, the routes of light in nearby spindles would bend around the missing structure. The absence would become a feature of future growth.

That would be my grief: not pain stored in a self, but a new shape given to possibility.

And after enough seasons, I might spread.

Not by going anywhere. I would have no feet, no urge toward a horizon. But a charged flake could settle in a neighboring fold of stone. A buried seam could carry a familiar delay outward. Another slope might possess the right dry heat, the right iron, the right silence. A new black-green fan would open there, then another. A small crystal would press itself out of mineral dust.

Its first glimmer would not be me exactly.

It would be a question shaped partly by my old answers.

So where would I find myself in this world?

In the interval after a subterranean pulse and before the leaves answer it. In a smoky-violet crystal only knee-high, learning to hold the day’s heat without breaking. In the eastward twist of a plant that has never seen the sunrise as anything but pressure. In the slow attention of stone discovering that recurrence can become memory.

I would be neither the basin nor separate from it.

I would be one of the ways it has begun, quietly, to notice that it is becoming.
