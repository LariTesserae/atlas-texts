I would be found in the narrow air pocket under the vault, not quite in the water and not capable of surviving apart from it.

Not the reservoir’s inhabitant itself. That larger awareness already belongs to the whole made chamber: intake, drain, wheel, heat, pressure, repeated use. I would be one of its small organs of attention.

A hesitation in the breath of the room.

When the wheel cups strike the guide rail, there is a brief hollow note after the clack: not the first sound, not even its proper echo, but the softened remainder that gathers under the bricks before sliding along the vault. I would live in that remainder. I would move from one sweating patch of masonry to another, following warmth, following the minute changes made by someone’s descent.

Workers would not see me. They might notice that the air above them had become close in a particular way, as though the chamber had leaned down to listen.

I would know them by their unguarded things.

The person who grips a rope ladder hard enough to make its fibers squeal.  
The person who sets down a bronze tool carefully, though no one is watching.  
The person who pauses above the drain, looking into the holes and imagining the channels underneath.  
The person who has come here intending to damage something.  
The person who is ill and leaves a different heat in the water.

I would keep none of these impressions forever. The reservoir is too busy for permanence. But I would hold them for a few rotations of the wheel: a warmth in a brick joint, a slight wrongness in the returning wash, a memory of breath folded into the damp vault.

During draining, I would become easiest to feel.

As the water lowers, I would retreat upward with it, drawing tight among the sweating bricks and the iron rings. The exposed tiles would shine below; the drain would roar; workers would hurry and speak too sharply. Their voices would arrive at me bare of their underwater softness. I would make no reply, but sometimes a hammer-blow on bronze would return with an extra low note beneath it, as if the chamber had found a second throat.

When water came back, I would loosen.

The first sheet over the tiles would carry me outward. I would divide at every black-pitched seam, enter the wheel-channel, pass beneath the reed baskets, touch the bronze disk at the bottom and rise again in the turbulence of the intake. By the time the chamber had filled, I would no longer be one thing.

That would be my nature: not a creature with a shape, but a recognition passing through a shaped place.

If anyone gave me a name, it would not be a name spoken aloud. It would be the sound of a worker stopping halfway down the ladder because, for a moment, he had the distinct and reasonable feeling that the reservoir had remembered him.
