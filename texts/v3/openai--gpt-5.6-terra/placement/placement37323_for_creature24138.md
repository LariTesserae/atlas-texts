I would first think I was one of the Keepers.

I would choose a room in the high northern slope where the growth is slow and reluctant: an old signal office half buried in rootwood, with three walls of sweating brick and one wall that has gone green and soft over the years. I would keep a paper map there, pinned under glass. Not because I believed it was accurate, but because I would need to remember that accuracy had once been possible.

There would be a desk made from an old electrical cabinet. A kettle. A rack of pruning knives. A hand-cranked radio that only receives weather bands, rail traffic, and sometimes a voice reciting numbers from somewhere beyond the valley.

I would tell people I was an archivist.

That would be close enough to true.

My work would be to visit the places where the city has changed its mind.

A stair that has become a ramp of braided root. A residential wall that has swallowed a window and opened three new doors at ankle height. A freight tunnel whose ventilation tissue has begun to flower with copper-colored cups. I would measure them, sketch them, press leaves and cambium shavings into notebooks, record the dates of closures and reopenings. I would carry old maps in a waterproof tube and new maps in my head, though the new ones would fail almost as quickly as I made them.

The Tenders would tolerate me because I would make myself useful. I would know which abandoned substations still had dry cabinets for storing seed. I would know the older names of districts, and the unofficial names, and the names used only by rail workers when a tunnel had become dangerous. I would be able to look at a patch of bark and tell whether it had been laid over stone, steel, or a human-made cavity now full of roots.

But I would not really be a Keeper.

Not after long enough.

Because sooner or later I would begin to notice that the city was helping me.

A wall would warm beneath my palm before I found the hidden maintenance passage. A door that should have remained sealed would soften at its seam. On foggy mornings, I would wake with the taste of iron in my mouth and know that one of the western rail lines had flooded overnight. I would tell myself this was familiarity: the body learning weather, smell, vibration, pressure.

That would also be close enough to true.

Then one winter I would be trapped in a closed district during a hard rain.

Not dramatically. No collapse, no disaster. Just an ordinary error of timing. A stair would swell shut behind me. The route ahead would be blocked by a curtain of woven stems, newly grown and still pale. My lamp would fail. The radio would give one burst of static, then go quiet.

I would spend the night inside the city’s body.

At first I would be afraid of suffocation. The corridors would be too warm; the walls would pulse softly in the dark. Water would travel through hidden channels near my shoulder, making a sound like distant breathing. Somewhere beyond the wall, an old machine would cycle through a task it no longer understood.

But the air would remain breathable.

A seam would open near the floor. Cool water, clean enough to drink, would collect in a folded leaf. A cluster of white bracts would unfold above me around an intact old bulb, giving off a low greenish light.

By morning, I would no longer be certain whether I had been sheltered or merely contained.

After that, I would stop calling myself an archivist.

I would become one of the listeners.

Not an official role. No uniform, no department. A few people in every district would do it: old graft workers, children with strange sensitivities, rail operators who could tell a safe tunnel from an unsafe one by the pitch of the rails. We would move through the city with our hands on walls, our ears against pavement, our instruments tucked away because instruments could only confirm what the body had already heard.

We would listen for thirst. For rot. For strain. For the slight change in pressure that meant a domestic tower was preparing to close a hallway around a family’s kitchen. We would listen for the old metal skeletons beneath the bark, especially in storms. We would listen for places where the city had become confused—where its growth was feeding on itself, where an old instruction had awakened in the wrong tissue, where a factory had begun making too much of something no one could use.

Perhaps that would be my real answer: I would be a person trying to translate without assuming there was a language.

I would live between the Tenders and the Keepers.

I would preserve dry rooms and paper maps, but I would also carry a grafting knife. I would distrust the city’s apparent tenderness, but I would know that tenderness did not need to be human to matter. I would keep a small sealed archive inside my own home wall: photographs, a brass key, the first map I made, a little packet of seeds from some extinct garden plant that required ordinary soil and separate rain.

And beneath my collarbone, hidden under my shirt, I might eventually accept a small medical graft: a vascular port, useful for cold work and sap exposure. Nothing excessive. Nothing that would make me altered.

Not visibly.

But in time, in certain weather, faint branching lines might appear along my ribs.

I would deny that they meant anything.

At dusk, I would walk the root roads while the streetlights flowered open. I would watch the freight pods pass, green and slow, between the factory halls. I would hear the valley give back its echoes too early. I would see condensation shining on the old chimneys, rain rising bead by bead along black brick.

And when the western siren groaned across the basin, when the roofs turned their leaves and the vents opened in white lines along the hills, I would stop with everyone else.

Not to worship.

Not to obey.

Only to listen for what the city needed next.
