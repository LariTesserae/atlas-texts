At first I think I would live near the lift.

Not because I would want to see the light, exactly. I suspect that after long enough below, the brightness at the top of the shaft would become less a promise than a nuisance: a white square that makes everyone look strange when they arrive, that spills weather into the chamber in the form of cold air, wet boots, unfamiliar smells. But the lift is where the town still admits it has an outside. It is where things arrive without having first belonged to the cavern.

I would have a room above the depot, perhaps, in the old offices with the windows painted over from the inside. I would grow something unsuccessfully in a tin beside the stairwell. I would know the lift schedule, though I would pretend not to care about it. I would go down at first bell to see what had come: flour, replacement filters, a crate of tomatoes already softening, three strangers trying not to look upward.

But I do not think I would remain lift-side.

The lift is too full of departure. It would make me restless. And I think, in a place like this, restlessness is one of the more dangerous conditions a person can develop. Restless people take unmarked routes. They go through doors with *AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY* stenciled on them. They mistake an old tunnel for a possibility. They decide the rules are for someone else.

No: after a while I would move inward, toward the place where the town is least certain of itself.

I would find myself at the old foundation pit behind its bent-wire fence.

Not living in it—never that—but working beside it. I would become, officially or unofficially, the person assigned to it. There would be some bureaucratic name for the job: seepage observer, drainage monitor, environmental maintenance adjunct. The actual job would be simpler and stranger. Every morning I would inspect the water level, the color of the mineral skin, the rate of dripping from the stone. I would clear leaves that did not belong underground, moth bodies, pieces of insulation, the occasional drowned rat. I would test the warmth with the back of my hand.

And I would keep records.

At first they would be practical records.  
Water at 14 cm below rim.  
West-wall drip steady.  
Gnat cloud moderate.  
Oil sheen present, source unknown.  
Dog tracks near fence.  
Goat marks at south corner.

But the town would teach me, as it teaches everyone, that practical things become personal when they recur.

Soon the records would acquire another kind of accuracy.

The water is darker after the lift brings down rain.  
The pigeons do not cross the pit on bad-air days.  
The oldest dog drinks only from the north edge.  
The black goat watches the water before she chooses a path.  
The moths circle low when the pumps below the square are struggling.  
The crickets stop three minutes before the old drain surges.

I would be the person people come to when something feels wrong but cannot yet be reported as wrong.

“Is the pit high?”

“Were the gnats bad last night?”

“Did you hear the crickets?”

I would have no authority, which is different from having no importance. The control room would have graphs; the maintenance crews would have gauges; the engineers would have specifications and fault codes. But I would have the animals, the water, the accumulation of little deviations nobody else had time to notice.

And perhaps that is what I would be there: not entirely a resident, not exactly a worker, but an interpreter of recurring conditions.

A keeper of small warnings.

I would learn the dogs’ routes and leave no food for them, because feeding is not the same as caring. I would know which cats pass the pit without looking and which stop to peer through the mesh. I would develop an uneasy affection for the goats, especially the black doe who studies the control-room monitors. I would distrust her more than I trusted most people, because she would be right too often.

Children would bring me their rat maps. I would pretend to confiscate them, then return them with notes in the margins: *This route follows the hot-water pipe. Do not put your fingers under the pallet.* They would tell me there was a white rat in the crawlspace below the washhouse. I would tell them white rats were unlucky. Then, later, alone, I would go looking for it.

I might keep one lamp by the pit—caged, low-wattage, amber rather than white—because the moths deserve a place where their gathering does not blind anybody. The lamp would become furred with wings each night. In the morning I would brush them into a tray and carry them to the compost drums near the planters. Someone would tell me this was sentimental. Someone else would ask whether the fig tree liked it.

The fig tree would not bear fruit. I would visit it anyway.

Eventually, I think, the distinction between my work and the town’s work would blur. The pit would be patched, drained, re-fenced, declared temporarily safe, then forgotten again. The water would return through another crack. The concrete around it would shift. A new building would rise nearby and begin, almost immediately, to dampen at its base.

And I would remain.

Not because I was trapped. Not even because I had nowhere else to go.

Because a place that is always almost failing needs witnesses who do not confuse fragility with worth.

So: I would be human, probably. Damp-haired, over-keyed, carrying a notebook in a waterproof pouch and a flashlight whose batteries are never quite fresh. I would live in a small room somewhere between the depot and the washhouse. My clothes would smell faintly of limestone, hot dust, animal bedding, and machine oil. I would know which ceiling leaks are honest. I would walk slowly. I would listen before opening doors.

But after enough years, the town might call me something other than human.

Pitkeeper, perhaps.

Gnat-counter.  
Cricket-listener.  
The one who asks the dogs first.  

And when newcomers arrived from the brightness above, looking disturbed by the sweating stone and the lamps with their living halos, I would not tell them that they would get used to it.

I would tell them the better thing:

“You won’t get used to it. You’ll learn its habits.”
