I would begin by thinking I was one of the city’s instruments.

Not an important instrument: not a laser level, or a strain gauge, or the brass pendulum the inspectors carry into old foundations. Something older and less exact. A listening device assembled from scavenged materials, fixed behind a wall in a public stairwell: copper wire, a cup-shaped piece of iron, mica, the membrane of a tunnel moth wing. My task would be to register the low changes—the difference between a tram passing three streets below and a slope deciding, very slowly, to become another slope.

People would come to rely on me without quite knowing I was there.

They would pause on the landing between the fourth and fifth floors, perhaps because a sound had changed. A woman carrying groceries would set down her bag. A child would put a hand on the damp rail. Somewhere inside the plaster, I would give a small answering tremor: not alarm, not yet. The building is thinking. Wait.

But over time I would become less like an instrument.

The moisture would get in. It gets into everything here. Moss would grow along the crack where I had been installed. A pale root would work through my wires. Tunnel moths would die against the wall and leave their fine mineral dust in the little chamber that was meant to keep them out. The sounds I collected would begin to mingle: stone-pressure, water in pipes, fox feet under the loading docks, the private knocks of the thirsty things moving through insulation.

Eventually I would not only hear the city. I would be heard by it.

I think I would live in the interstices: in the unplanned rooms made when structures pull apart without fully separating. A narrow, vertical habitation between an old apartment block and its newer brace-wall; a place entered through a cupboard whose back panel has bowed inward. There would be a window there, though it would face another wall only an arm’s length away. Rain would fall between the walls in a silver thread. Ferns would gather below the sill. At night the yellow light from neighboring kitchens would pass through cracks and make a changing map across the floor.

I would keep bowls of water, not from fear exactly, but from neighborliness.

Sometimes the bowls would be empty. Sometimes a smooth stone would appear in one, warm despite the cold. Sometimes I would wake to the sound of a thing shifting behind the lath, and I would not ask its name. In this city, names can be invitations, or handles, or routes.

By then I might be called a wall-reader.

Not officially. Officially I would work for the Department of Municipal Accommodation, perhaps in a basement office beneath the square that is rising. My job would be to visit buildings whose angles have changed and decide what kind of change they have become.

Not whether they are safe. Nothing here is simply safe.

I would decide whether a building is asking for braces, for drainage, for an evacuation, for a new stairway, for a change in the way its residents think of their rooms. I would tap walls with a small hammer, listen with my palms, put my ear against pipes. I would learn which cracks are merely thirsty, which carry a cold draft from abandoned spaces, which are full of the fine dry grit that means newly exposed stone. I would carry chalk in several colors and make marks that everyone understands:

This wall is moving but companionable.

This floor has begun to remember water.

Do not sleep here during fog.

Leave milk, not water.

The goats know.

I would be trusted because I would not pretend to master what I measured. The city dislikes that kind of person. It keeps them busy issuing assurances until their offices tilt and their files slide into corners.

Perhaps, eventually, I would become one of the unrecorded inhabitants: not wholly human, but still a citizen by local standards. The city has room for such changes. It already grants a sort of municipal status to goats, moths, foxes, eels, ghosts of engineers, and whatever drinks from the bowls outside leaning apartments.

I would become something made by coexistence.

My hands might grow mineral-sensitive, fingertips able to feel the faint vibration of water running uphill before anyone sees it. My eyes might take on the tunnel moths’ dusty shine, useful in fog. I would sleep lightly whenever the reservoir moved under the town. I would know the antlered creatures not by sight but by the silence that gathers before their arrival: trams stopping, dogs refusing balconies, a pause in the subterranean grind as though the mountain itself is listening upward.

And I would have a small home in a shifting building above the freight terraces, where cable cars pass close enough to rattle the cups in the cupboard.

There I would keep obsolete maps, each one annotated with the places that have returned: a creek beneath a pharmacy, a stairwell beneath a greenhouse, a street that only exists in winter when the retaining wall pulls back. I would keep a collection of objects found in the water bowls: buttons, teeth, pebbles, a key without cuts, a coin from a state no map remembers. I would tend moss on the north wall and leave the window open during rain, even though the rain sometimes enters sideways from a different district.

When newcomers asked where I lived, I would not give an address at first.

I would say: above the seven-degree house, below the old ventilation tower, behind the wall that knocks twice before a slope correction.

Then, if they looked uncertain, I would add:

Follow the goats until they stop. Don’t follow the water if it is running uphill. If the lamps go blue, wait under cover. And if you hear someone calling your name from the service passage, that isn’t necessarily me.
