I would not arrive in the basin from above.

I would come up through it.

At first I might be only a pressure beneath the leaf mold: a coolness where the soil is warm, a faint blue line in the black mud after rain. Something would be growing through a drowned shell, through the split pith of a fallen branch, through the soft body of a beetle that had died under a root. It would not yet have a shape a bird could recognize, or a name a larger hunter could fear.

Then the basin would give me one.

I think I would be a fungus, but not merely a mushroom.

The visible part of me would appear only occasionally: thin shelves like wet ears along the undersides of logs; pale cups opening around the mouths of hot fissures; a scattering of small translucent bells that brighten when rainwater touches them. In deep places, where the roots have made a ceiling over a hollow and the last daylight has become only a green memory, I would glow. Not enough to illuminate the world. Only enough to reveal its closeness.

A crab climbing a stone heap would see one of my threads and turn aside.

A rodent, nosing along a root, would find my fruiting bodies and eat some, carrying my spores in its fur. A moth would settle near me to drink moisture gathered in the folds of my flesh. An ant might cut a piece of me away and take it into its hidden city, mistaking me for food. It would not wholly be wrong.

But most of me would be elsewhere.

I would be in the roots of the humming trees, exchanging mineral water for sugars made high in the canopy. I would be in the ash where the transparent amphibians bury their eggs. I would be in the abandoned burrows of pale crabs, knitting loose soil around their wet-stone walls. I would find the bones of whatever the climbing thing drops from the branches, and I would patiently turn them back into what roots can drink.

The basin is young enough that everything needs a witness.

Stone needs something to enter its hairline fractures. Fallen leaves need something to take them apart before the next flood carries them away. The new trees need allies in the raw soil; the animals need the rot to become soil again, and the soil needs death to become more than a heap of things that have stopped moving.

I would be that witness—not wise, not separate, not safe.

I would know the basin through touch.

When rain struck the canopy, I would feel it minutes later as a thousand cold impacts arriving through stems, roots, hollow trunks, and saturated earth. When the ridge cracked in the distance, I would feel the tremor pass through fungal cords stretched between stones. When an armored browser settled its many feet into mud, I would know its direction and weight. When a larva drowned in a pond, when an insect climbed high to die, when a root split open in search of water, each would send a small change through the dark.

Not information exactly. Not thought as animals know it.

More like being unable to stop becoming aware of what touches you.

There would be dangers. A fungus cannot flee from flame, though no fire has yet come to this basin. It cannot pull itself from a landslide, or hide from a dry season if the rains fail. It can only enter, persist, wait. If a root dies, I lose one road. If a tree falls, I gain another. If the warm crack closes, I retreat into a colder seam and become less luminous, less hungry, nearly absent.

Then the rains return, or a beetle dies, or a new root enters the ground.

And I return.

Perhaps that is where I would find myself: beneath the oldest-looking thing in a world with no true old things yet. Under a fallen trunk so thick that it has become a landscape—its bark roofed with moss, its hollow full of sleeping rodents, its underside furred with my pale fans. Nearby, a warm fissure breathes in slow intervals. Blind crabs make their careful constructions. Above, the root-tree hums when storm wind passes through it, and animals gather at its base despite themselves.

I would make that gathering possible without calling it mine.

The humming tree would feed me. I would feed it water and minerals drawn from places its roots could not reach. The crab heaps would catch condensation and alter the wetness of the soil; my threads would enter their undersides. The ants would carry pieces of leaf and insect through their galleries; spores would travel with them. The browsers would crush young plants and open gaps; new fungi, seedlings, and insects would enter those gaps before the old growth could close.

I would not be the heart of the basin. The basin does not have one.

It has too many hearts: every temporary pool, every hot crack, every nest, every flower that closes around a moth for warmth. But I might be one of the dark networks between them—the thing that does not command, does not decide, yet helps the place remain connected while it is still deciding what it will become.

And on some lightning-lit night, when the entire basin appears for one white instant—black ridges, steaming folds, shining pools, the enormous tangled canopy—I would flare faintly beneath it all.

Not to be seen.

Only because, for that instant, so much of the living world would be touching me.
