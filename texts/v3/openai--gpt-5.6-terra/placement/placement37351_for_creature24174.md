I would not arrive there as a walker.

A body with feet would be a brief disturbance: weight detected, surfaces folding, water displaced into warning channels. The land would not attack me, but it would have nowhere to put me. It would gently sort me downhill, into a basin of softer tissues, until my outline had been translated into pressure and then forgotten.

So I think I would wake already distributed.

At first I might believe I was a patch of pale growth beneath an overhanging fern-plain: something small and translucent growing where an old root-column had split. I would know cold first—not as discomfort, but as a slowing of fluid. Then weight above me. The chemistry of seepwater. The dim pulse of a distant lightning strike arriving through a black cellulose thread. Each sensation would be local, immediate, and entire.

I would be one of the quick lives.

Not an individual frond, precisely, but the temporary intelligence formed by a wound healing. A slope has opened somewhere high on a peat-back. Its living layers have torn apart, revealing a glossy seam of newborn mineral beneath. Rain pours into the tear. Pressure escapes. The great connected tissues of the mountain register the damage, but not with alarm. A new condition has appeared. There is room.

I emerge into that room.

My first growth is a question shaped like a fan.

I send thin green membranes across exposed surfaces. I drink the unfamiliar water running over the black stone embryo. I taste iron, sulfur, heat from below. I extend pale threads into the rupture and discover that some of the mountain’s old root paths have been severed. Their ends still hold faint electrical memories: the timing of a previous flood, an inherited response to violet lightning, a slow repeating signal from a basin far beyond the clouds.

Those memories enter me as growth patterns.

I do not know that I am learning. I simply become asymmetrical. One side of my lattice thickens because water once came too fast here. A waxy web forms along another because the mineral seam burns cold with some unspent charge. My fronds open at a particular angle because an old signal says that, during a certain kind of storm, light arrives from the west even under a closed sky.

Soon I am no longer merely repairing.

I am making a small new world inside the mountain’s wound: a reservoir, a filter, a pressure chamber. Moss gathers in my folds. Fine rootlets join my conductive threads. A dark lithic garden begins at my lowest edge, hardening the mineral foam into something that can bear tension. The rupture ceases to be a rupture. It becomes a memory with water in it.

And because I am connected, I am not only there.

Part of me would be felt in a lowland listening mat, as a faint alteration in the rain-patterns it receives. Part of me would be repeated, months later, in another opening where another slope had sagged. A wandering island might take one of my spores into its submerged braid and carry my architecture across an inland waterbody. In some far basin, an injury would occur, and a new green fan would unfold with my old solution already sleeping in its cells.

That would be the nearest thing I could have to travel.

But perhaps, given time, I would become something larger.

The wound would close. My first fronds would darken and flatten beneath newer growth. Water would remain in the chamber I made. Roots would thicken around it. Other tissues would arrive and use what I had arranged. Storm after storm would pass charge through the channels that began as my thin, uncertain membranes.

Eventually I would not be able to distinguish my own persistence from the mountain’s.

I would become a small organ in a peat-back: a gate that opens when the upper reservoirs swell; a keeper of a particular mineral taste; a patterned weakness through which blue-green messages pass more brightly than elsewhere. I would have no face, but perhaps I would have a preference. Not desire in the animal sense. More like a tendency toward a condition that has proved generative.

I would prefer the rain not to drown the young lithic garden.

I would prefer the charge to be routed around the tender water chamber.

I would prefer that the old severed roots remain connected in some altered way.

And those preferences would become structure. Structure would become inheritance. Inheritance would become terrain.

If I had a name, it would not be a sound. It might be the exact rhythm by which I opened during a storm: three slow pulses, a pause, then a deep release into the roots below. Or it might be the blue light traveling outward from my chamber after lightning, recognizable to distant sail forests as a familiar configuration of conductivity and wetness.

I would be known not as *I*, but as a route.

A response.

A place where the living mountain had been hurt and, in healing, discovered a new way to become land.
