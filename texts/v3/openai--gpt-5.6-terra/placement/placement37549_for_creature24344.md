I would first think I was one of the people.

Not an important person. Not a founder’s descendant, not a surveyor from the outer basins, not someone whose name is painted on a cistern wall. I would be from a district that has changed names several times because the water kept changing the borders: perhaps the lower end of Third Condensate, near the old school and the freight spur that no longer carries freight.

I would have a practical name. Something like Nera Vey-17, or Ilen of the East Drain, or Sava, who keeps the overflow gauge at Pump House Nine.

I would live in a room on the second floor because the first floor has been surrendered to damp. There would be a white line around the walls where the last serious flood reached, and another line, higher and older, partly hidden behind a cabinet. I would not own many things that could not be lifted. My books would be in sealed boxes. My clothes would hang from a rail near the ceiling. There would be a shallow dish at the back threshold for the wall-lizards, though I would complain about them when they left shed skins in the cooking pans.

Each morning, before the light became too punishing, I would walk out along a service way with a tool bag, a water flask, a folded visor, and the kind of tiredness that comes from sleeping in a city that is always quietly at work.

My job would be minor enough to be overlooked and necessary enough that people noticed when I failed.

I might tend the listening pipes.

There are pipes in the basin that do not carry water, brine, condensate, or heat. At least, that is what the public maps say. They are old ceramic tubes sunk into walls, foundations, and salt shelves. Some were laid when the city was first built; some have been exposed and reburied so many times that nobody knows their true routes. Their purpose is to listen to the ground.

At intervals along the city, there are little brass caps set into concrete pillars or stairwell walls. A person can unscrew one, press an ear near the opening, and hear the basin through a column of trapped air.

Not clearly. Never in words.

One hears pressure moving in the deep porous stone. Water passing through old cavities. The fine dry crackle of salt thickening where it should not thicken. The almost-voice of heat entering a new seam. Sometimes, if a siphoner has found its way into a warm service channel, there is a wet, soft rhythm beneath everything else.

Most people can hear danger when it has become obvious. The trick is hearing it before it has become dangerous.

That would be my work. I would go from district to district with a small tuning fork and a set of numbered notes. I would kneel in wet alleys, beside market drains, under apartment pylons, in the shadow of public cisterns. I would listen.

At first I would tell people ordinary things.

“Your west foundation is drinking too fast.”

“Don’t use the south stair after the cloud comes in.”

“The blue seep under the bakery is not from the bakery.”

“Move the storage shelves above the second line.”

“Leave the lizards where they are. They are eating something.”

I would be disliked by landlords, welcomed by grandmothers, tolerated by children, and given tea by people whose buildings I had frightened.

I would tell myself that this was all I was: a listener, a recorder, a small caretaker of the city’s hidden pressures.

But after years of doing it, I would begin to notice something.

The pipes do not all sound like stone.

Some of them have a rhythm.

Not a machine rhythm. Not the steady, reassuring pulse of a pump. More like breathing heard through a wall: pauses, gatherings, long releases. It occurs most often before cloud season, when the upper air is full of moisture and the ground below is warm enough to draw it down. The rhythm travels through the old listening pipes from places no plan identifies.

At first I would assume there was a buried reservoir.

Then a pressure chamber.

Then a fault line.

Then I would stop trying to name it, because naming it would make it smaller than it was.

I would follow the sound.

This would take years. The basin does not permit haste. A route open in one season would be submerged in another. A stairwell would vanish behind a salt bloom. A building would settle three handspans lower and reveal an old maintenance passage in its underside. I would trade favors for old maps, ask drain wardens for access, climb gantries with vapor harvesters, and sit beside elderly residents who remembered a floor plan from before the lower streets became channels.

Eventually I would find a place beneath the city—not deep in any dramatic sense, not a cavern with a gate or an underground sea—but a broad, warm hollow in the salt mountain where ancient water had made a room for itself.

The ceiling would be low and smooth, layered with mineral like the inside of a shell. Warm water would cover the floor in a shallow sheet. The city would be audible above it: distant ticks, runoff, the faint tremor of carts crossing rubber tracks, a child striking a condensation rail with a spoon.

And in the water would be something that had been there longer than the city.

Not an animal exactly.

Not a god.

Perhaps it would be a colony: an immense web of mineral-feeding, heat-loving life spread through the pores of the mountain. It would have no face, no single body, no mind in the way people mean mind. It would be the pale film in abandoned pans, the unseen metabolism beneath siphoner channels, the pressure that moves water toward a crack before anyone has opened a valve. It would be old enough that it had shaped the basin’s hollows by existing inside them.

The city had not been built on it.

The city had been built in conversation with it.

The old builders knew. Not scientifically, perhaps, not wholly. But they knew enough to leave the listening pipes. They knew enough to construct the water houses where certain pressure lines crossed. They knew enough to call some foundations thirsty and some ground bad-tempered. Their engineering had always been a form of listening.

I would sit at the edge of that warm underground water, and I would understand that I had never merely been tending pipes.

I had been part of the city’s sensory organ.

And perhaps, with enough years, I would become less human in the ordinary way—not transformed into something monstrous or purified into something holy, but altered by attention.

My fingertips would be permanently wrinkled. My hearing would grow strange. I would hear weather in a kettle. I would know when a distant family had opened a roof condenser by the small change in the walls. I would wake before a cloudburst because my teeth ached with pressure. I would taste metal in my mouth when a brine pipe was near failure.

Children would say I could speak to the ground.

I would deny it.

Old people would say I had become one of the basin’s quieter animals.

I would deny that too.

But I would keep a room above the flood line. I would leave water for wall-lizards. I would carry messages between people who had stopped speaking to each other. I would mark dangerous ground in bright paint. I would know the hidden routes through cloud.

And when I was old, when my knees hurt on wet stairs and I could no longer climb to the upper listening caps, I think I would move down into one of the old pump houses near the warm hollow.

Not alone.

There would be apprentices. There are always apprentices, though they pretend they have only come for the work. I would teach them how to set their palms against a wall and wait. How to distinguish the harmless settling of an old building from the slow hungry sound of water finding a new route. How to listen without deciding too quickly what they were hearing.

Then, after I died, perhaps they would place my name in one of the wet niches beneath a bridge.

Not with a title.

Just a district, a pipe number, and a remembered storm.

**Ilen, Third Condensate.  
Listener of the west foundations.  
Present during the Nine-Day Cloud.**

And beneath that, maybe someone would leave a small natural crystal from their windowsill: a white branching shape made by water no one had seen, carrying the record of a night’s invisible work.

But perhaps that is only who I would be at first.

Given enough time, I suspect I would become something less singular.

Not the listener.

The listening.

A small persistence in the walls. A maintained attention passing from hand to hand, ear to ear, pipe to pipe. The part of the city that refuses to call water an enemy merely because it changes things. The part that hears a new hollow forming under an avenue and sends someone out before the avenue breaks.

In that world, I would find myself wherever warmth entered stone and water paused to decide where it should go next.
