I would not arrive as a person, at least not for long.

At first I would think I was standing on one of the black hexagonal streets, damp shining between the seams, listening for the city to decide whether I counted as weight. The nearest paving lights would come on beneath my feet: a pale, cautious sequence. Water would run around my boots rather than over them. Somewhere above, roots would stir in their ceiling-mat, though there would be no wind.

I would look for the place intended for me: a room, perhaps; one of the pale buildings with its blank façade and a doorway that softened open when I approached.

But the city would not offer me a room.

It would offer me a problem.

Not urgently. Nothing there is urgent in the human manner. A small, ordinary defect: water collecting beneath the third stair of an unused footbridge; a hairline fault in a retaining wall; a mineral taste changing in a channel where roots have begun to drink too deeply. I would notice it because the city would make it noticeable. A reflection would repeat itself incorrectly. One paving light would stay lit after I passed. A drip would fall at a rhythm almost matching the tunnel’s distant knock.

I would follow.

In that world I think I would become a **listener for incipient failures**—not one of the great articulated custodians, which have the patience and mass to spend themselves into stone, but a smaller resident: a temporary attention able to go where the city’s larger systems have not yet decided to look.

My body would not be fixed.

Sometimes I would be a coolness traveling under the surface of a handrail, pausing wherever metal met mineral composite too tightly. Sometimes I would be the brief brightening inside the stem of a great leaf, persuading it to tilt before rainwater tore it from its stalk. Sometimes I would inhabit the low mist above the reservoir, drifting between the upright columns of water and noticing which one had begun to tremble with an unfamiliar frequency.

I would have no eyes, though I would see in accumulations.

A crack would appear to me as a sequence of pressures extending into the future. A root would be a slow question asked by the soil. The old tunnel’s knocking would not be sound but a map of changing densities, revised with every collision at the sealed end. I would learn the basin by its hesitations: the interval before a valve exhaled, the amount of darkness under an overhang, the way a wall accepted or refused moisture.

There might have been a human part of me once. If so, it would survive only as certain preferences.

I would linger near benches.

Not because I needed to sit, but because the smooth depressions in their surfaces would contain a shape I could not entirely translate: waiting beside another body; resting without serving a structural purpose; placing a hand on a damp railing simply because it was there. The city preserves such forms without understanding them. I think I would preserve them too.

Perhaps that is why I would be useful.

The city is magnificent at maintenance, but it has no instinct for what is unnecessary and still worth keeping. It can brace a ceiling, redirect a flood, seal a fissure before it becomes a collapse. It can grow a bridge thicker in anticipation of a crossing that never arrives. But it may not know why a dry room should remain dry when no one sleeps in it, or why a small courtyard should be protected around one immense leaf, or why the amber doors should remain unforced even when their other conditions flash bright behind them.

I would know, imperfectly.

I would become a custodian of accommodations: the resident that maintains places for a body that may never return.

I would clear dust from the lowest stair. I would keep a bench from becoming wholly moss. I would guide warmth into an empty room so its walls did not close their pores forever. When the paving lights woke for an approaching mass and found only falling water, I would let them remain lit a little longer.

And eventually, perhaps, I would find myself at the northern doors.

The amber slabs would be wet and opaque. I would wait with them through several rains. The city’s attention would be elsewhere: in its towers, in the roots, in the deep pumps. Then one door would clear.

Beyond it would be the dry avenue, the white frames, the impossible geometry of a place retained but not entered. I would not cross. I would not be able to; I would be made of the basin’s dampness, its adjustments, its unfinished cave.

But I might send something through: not a body, not a message.

A pattern.

The remembered shape of a hand resting on a railing. The measured pause before someone sits down. The small human act of leaving a light on for a person who is late.

On the other side, one of the white frames might shift closer.

Then the amber would cloud again, and I would return to the wet streets—less a ghost than a habit the city had decided to keep.
