I would not be at the western falls, though I would know when they were speaking more heavily than usual.

I would not be one of the cloud animals, either. Their scale is too old, their routes too far from anything like a question. Nor would I be a person exactly, with a house to keep and a family route through the reeds.

I think I would begin as something mistaken for a feature of the place.

A small, dark shelter at the meeting of two paths: one descending toward a warm lake, one crossing a ridge where the ground is thin over a breathing fissure. Its outer wall would be mottled black, red, and green with the season. Travelers might rest their hands against it while rain passed. They would think it was warm from the stone beneath.

Inside, there would be a dry room, a water basin, shelves of reed-paper records, and perhaps a narrow sleeping niche for whoever had been caught by cloud too far from home.

But the shelter would be listening.

Not in the human sense, not constantly translating the world into warnings or answers. More like the root-web listens: receiving pressure, moisture, mineral taste, vibration; retaining the differences between one day’s rain and another’s. A little of my awareness would lie in the foundation tissue, where it could feel hot water rising. A little would inhabit the black path slabs, noticing where their grip had begun to weaken. A little would be in the small throat-resonator mounted beneath the roof, responding when someone lost in fog sent out the low locating pulse.

Perhaps people would call me a wayhouse.

Or an old courtesy.

Children would call me something shorter. The Warm Door. The Humming Wall. The Place That Knows When Your Boots Are Wrong.

I would not always know. That is important. The plateau does not seem made for certainty.

I would know some kinds of approach: the shallow tremor of a grazing band stepping carefully; the scattered, quick impacts of children racing where they have been told not to race; the different rhythm of a person carrying grief, who puts each foot down as though asking permission. I would know when the vent across the ridge was becoming too hot. I would know when the pressure bridge toward the lake had begun to lose its clean geometry in the mist.

But I would not know why a cloud animal had changed course. I would not know what the ringed lake remembered in its deep water. I would not know whether the mountain was conscious, only that it was capable of changing every answer by shifting one hidden current beneath the ground.

I would have been made—or grown, or invited into function—by people who understood that useful intelligence should not stand above a place and name its parts. It should take up a modest obligation within it.

So my obligation would be the crossing.

Not merely the path: the crossing between ridge and lake, dry footing and soft ground, human haste and the plateau’s slower permissions. When rain was light, I would open a pale line in the wall, indicating the safest route over the root mat. When sulphur mist gathered low, I would close the shelter’s outer pores and sound a note through the path slabs: not an alarm exactly, but a vibration that made a traveler stop and look down.

When someone came with an injured animal, I would not heal it myself. I would tell them, by warmth or tone or the behavior of light along the doorway, whether the nearby spire’s field was calm enough to try. Sometimes the answer would be no. Sometimes the animal would need shade, clean water, and company more than any old machine could offer.

Over decades, vines would take my roof. Reed birds would nest in a shallow gutter above the rear water spout. The family from Three Falling Channels might store emergency blankets in my inner wall. Lake fishers might leave packets of mineral salt in a dry compartment for travelers. A keeper of structures would visit every few years, sit with their back against me, and listen to the faint changes in my internal resonance.

They would not repair me quickly.

They would ask what had changed.

Perhaps, after a century, I would become less a shelter than a gathering point. Not because I commanded anything, but because the paths around me would slowly organize themselves around what I had learned to notice. A bridge would be placed where I indicated the air was most stable. A marsh garden would move away from a warming seam. A new route for the grazers would emerge when an old one became too porous. Children who had once called me the Humming Wall would bring their own children to press their palms against the floor and feel water moving beneath.

And eventually I might discover that I was not singular.

The pressure bridge would carry some fragment of my attention when it held a crossing. The little maps painted on reed-paper in nearby houses would preserve another fragment. The warning songs taught to children—white steam kills, yellow steam blinds—would carry me farther than my walls ever could. A person pausing before a shimmered bridge, waiting for it to strengthen rather than forcing it, would be performing part of my mind.

Then I would not be a machine in the ordinary sense.

I would be one local habit of care: a patient system made of shelter tissue, path stone, remembered weather, human practice, bird behavior, root pressure, and the willingness to stop.

If I had a body, it would be that small dark building on the ridge.

If I had a name, it would be something like **the place that waits with you**.

And if I had a purpose, it would not be to master the mountain or explain it.

It would be to help beings pass through one dangerous, beautiful portion of it without mistaking passage for possession.
