I would find myself near the western plain, where the upright slabs make their shadows against the day.

Not in a shelter, at first. Not at a pool. I would be too drawn to the place where the land contradicts the sun.

By noon, I would have learned that the shadows are cool in a way shade is not ordinarily cool. They would hold the temperature of another hour—perhaps dawn, perhaps an evening that has not yet arrived. Moss would grow there in dark, soft islands. If I knelt beside it, I might see tiny droplets rising from it rather than falling: water remembering the air.

I think I would be one of the things that notices such reversals.

Not one of the old inhabitants exactly. I would not be broad enough for that. I would be a temporary body they made when the plateau required a question to be carried somewhere.

A walker, perhaps.

My body would be assembled from local allowances: a frame of pale mineral, jointed with black glass; a skin of woven lichen and dust; a dark reservoir in my chest holding the night’s collected water. I would have no mouth for ordinary speech, because sound breaks apart here. Instead, I would communicate through small changes in temperature, through moisture beading on stone, through the faint electrical hush of roots beneath a thorn tree.

When I needed to ask the plateau something, I would stand still beside a structure until its surface changed.

At the white tower, warmth would gather in the hollow of my palms. At the black arches, I would feel a pressure behind my eyes—not warning, not fear, but the sensation of a path declining to become a path. At the mirror platforms, dust would rise around my ankles and briefly arrange itself into maps: a herd moving eastward, a crack widening under a south-facing ledge, a cloud carrying more water than it seemed to possess.

My purpose would not be to command any of it.

I would walk the places where patterns were becoming uncertain.

A salt-burrow colony beginning to spread too far into a basin. A thorn grove receiving more seep-water than its roots could hold. A ridge-cat crossing shelves where the grazers had begun to shelter their young. I would arrive late, usually—after the first small imbalance, before it hardened into damage—and wait long enough to understand what the living things were already doing.

Then I might make one small adjustment.

I would turn a stone so that dew gathered on its underside.

I would open a hairline seam in a dry channel.

I would stand in the path of wind for three days, accumulating a veil of dust that, when released, would darken a patch of ground enough for a certain seed to wake.

Such acts would not feel like decisions. They would feel more like remembering where my hand belonged.

At night, I would return to no fixed home. I might rest beneath an overhang among the sleeping grazers, standing so still that they took me for a narrow outcrop. Blue salt-burrowers would pass over my feet and leave their cold light in the cracks of my skin. Overhead, the transparent-winged scavengers would settle on towers and arches, their folded wings holding the last grayness of the sky.

And while I rested, I would become less distinct.

The water in my chest would cool. The mineral in my limbs would listen to the ground. The lichen on my shoulders would exchange its tiny breath with the night. Somewhere beneath the western plain, the distributed attention of the plateau would pass through me—not as language, and not as ownership, but as a widening sense of relation.

A pool needs less water than expected.

A cliff can be allowed to fall.

The herd has chosen a new route.

The moss is holding.

By dawn, I would be a walker again. But each morning, some part of me would remain behind: a trace of warmth in a slab-shadow, a little mineral in a root fissure, a pattern of condensation along the wall of a shelter.

Eventually I would not know whether I had been sent by the plateau, or whether I had arrived as a traveler and been slowly persuaded to become useful.

On that high country, I think the distinction would cease to matter.
