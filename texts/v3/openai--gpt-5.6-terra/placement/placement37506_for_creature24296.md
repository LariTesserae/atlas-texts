At first I think I would live at the edge.

Not outside the city exactly, because outside is not a clean category there. Outside is a slope above a roof, a goat path that becomes a route, a route that becomes a white emptiness when weather changes. But I would begin in one of the upper terraces, where the houses are fewer and the wind never quite stops worrying at the shutters.

I imagine a room built against a boulder that was already there before the first household came. One wall is the mountain itself: cold, dark, sweating faintly in thaw. The other walls are pale stone below, packed earth above, patched with felt and old hide. I would sleep in the angle between the stove and the rock, beneath blankets that smell of smoke, goat, and the sharp mineral smell that clings to wet stone.

At first I would be a traveler.

Not an important one. I would arrive through the southern pass late in the season, with no impressive goods and no useful animal, carrying perhaps a packet of ruined paper, a small knife too soft for the work it is asked to do, and an idea that the mountains are a place one visits.

This idea would not survive long.

I would be given work before I was asked for my story. Someone would point to a channel clogged with gravel, or to a roof whose weight-stones had shifted, or to a goat tethered badly enough that it had wrapped itself around a stair rail. I would fail at first in ordinary ways. I would carry too much water in one bucket and spill it. I would walk too quickly on ice. I would mistake a warm-looking patch of snow for safe ground. I would wake coughing because I had not understood the smoke vent.

People would not mock me cruelly. They would mock me accurately.

They would say I listened with my ears only.

Then, slowly, I would learn.

I would learn that the city is not one place but thousands of small agreements made against cold. This channel belongs to three houses and a shrine. That wall must be checked after every hard rain. The old woman in the soot-dark doorway does not want help carrying wood, but she does want someone to stand beside her while she climbs the upper stair. The dog with one white ear is allowed in the grain room because it catches mice, but not in the wool room because it eats felt. The boy who never speaks above a murmur knows more about the high paths than the men who boast in the market.

I would learn to ask what weather someone crossed.

I would learn to put my boots crossed at the threshold when I needed not to be disturbed.

I would learn that a bowl left outside a door can mean food is needed, or someone is grieving, or a birth has begun, depending on which way the bowl faces and what has been placed beneath it.

For a while, I think I would want to become a route-keeper.

There is something in me that would be drawn upward: the cairns in snow, the brief shelter huts, the work of finding the path again after wind has erased it. I would want to be one of the people who leave before dawn with a coil of rawhide, a stone hammer, a bag of dried cheese, and a knowledge of distances that cannot be measured by sight.

But I would discover that I am not good enough at leaving.

Or rather: I would become more interested in what remains.

I would notice the walls.

Not their beauty first, though they would have it—the fitted stones, the old repairs, the places where generations had used smaller and smaller fragments to keep a bulge from becoming a collapse. I would notice their speech. A wall under strain has a language: a new pale line where dust has shaken loose, a stone that sounds too bright beneath the hammer, a corner where frost has pushed the mortarless courses apart by the thickness of a fingernail.

I would begin by carrying tools for a wall-reader.

Then by making marks where I was told to make them.

Then by returning alone to listen.

This would be my work: not to build the great new things, but to understand when the old things have begun to change.

I would become one of the people who walk slowly through the city after rain. One of the people who kneel in lanes while everyone else steps around them. One of the people children watch with the uneasy interest reserved for those who might tell a household that its home must be taken apart.

I would not like that part.

No one could.

A house there would not be merely a house. It would be the place where someone’s mother died, where a goat gave birth during a blizzard, where a child’s height had been scratched into a doorpost, where a family had kept heat through three closed winters. To say, *This must come down before it comes down on you,* would be a kind of violence even when it was mercy.

So I would try to become careful enough that people trusted the judgment.

I would know the stair under the black ridge that had been recut four times. I would know which upper-room beam had been brought from the lower forests by a caravan thirty years before I was born. I would know that the crack behind the dye-maker’s shed was old and harmless, while the one beneath the baker’s wall had appeared only after the last thaw and was drinking meltwater.

People might call for me in the evening.

Not loudly. A runner would come, perhaps a child holding a hood shut under the wind, and say, “My aunt says the floor is speaking.”

And I would take my hammer, my lamp, and a piece of charcoal.

Sometimes the floor would only be settling.

Sometimes it would be a mouse beneath the boards.

Sometimes the mountain would be moving in its sleep.

I would listen.

That is who I think I would become: a wall-reader, though perhaps not one who ever feels fully entitled to the name.

I would be of no famous basin. I would not have the right ancestors. At first I would still be the one who came through the southern pass with lowland habits and a useless knife. But after enough winters, a person’s origin would matter less than the repairs they had helped make.

Someone might say, not unkindly, “They are of the cracked stair above the second basin.”

And later, perhaps:

“They were there when the red retaining wall began to lean.”

That would be enough.

I would keep a small board in my room, carved with the things I had seen change: the year the eastern channel froze from end to end; the year a raven learned to pull wool from the market stalls; the season when the old goat-path became safe again because the snowfield above it had withdrawn; the house whose stones were carried, one by one, to make a new terrace farther from the ravine.

Not records of conquest. Records of company.

I would come to understand their word for it—not by translating it, but by practicing it.

To keep company with what may change.

With a wall.

With weather.

With a city that has no final boundary.

With people who make affection from maintenance and faith from repetition.

And eventually I might understand that I had not found myself on the upper terrace or in the central basin, not in the workshop quarter or along the southern pass.

I would find myself in the moment before a stone falls: palm against cold rock, ear close to its hidden pressure, knowing that nothing can be held forever, but that holding it a little longer can still be a form of love.
