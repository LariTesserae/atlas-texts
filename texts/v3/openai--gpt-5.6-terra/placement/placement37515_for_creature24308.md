I would not be at the newborn ridge when it opens.

I would want to be there. Everyone would. The ridge would be warm and bright in the dusty gold, its seams coughing cinnamon powder, its raw mineral still soft enough to cut with a wedge. There would be fingers in the cracks already, feeling their way upward. There would be rootfolk with baskets and bone hooks, pretending not to watch one another. There would be the sharp smell of opportunity.

But I think I would arrive late.

I would be found where the valley has just finished changing its mind: at the edge of a new trench, beneath a grove that has leaned thirty degrees overnight and is trying, with immense patience, to root itself again.

Not a hunter, exactly. Not a grower, though I would know which cuts need resin and which need to be left open. Not one of the people who stand nearest the cracks and bargain, unseen, with the fingers.

I would be a keeper of crossings.

There must be people like that in the valley: those who go out after the Underback has turned and ask what is still connected to what.

They would be poor in possessions and rich in cords. They would carry black-glass blades, thorn needles, little wedges of white crystal, dried strips of hide, loops of tendon, and bags of teeth selected not for beauty but for sound. They would travel with a lantern grub in a padded bark pouch, a bristle-shearer at heel if they were fortunate, and perhaps a young reed-hound that had decided long ago not to leave them.

Their work would be to find the old paths after they have become impossible.

A settlement wakes: its southern ladder now ends over empty air; the root-mat has folded beneath its thorn corral; the mineral pit is gone; the neighboring houses, which were two hundred paces away, are now hidden beyond a wall of crystal stalks and a river of migrating runners.

Someone has to go.

Someone has to put a hand on a root and feel whether it is still anchored. Someone has to crawl through the fresh gaps, listening for the breathing trunks drawing air. Someone has to decide whether a bone bridge can be tied between two trunks before the next herd comes through. Someone has to hang the right string of teeth so that, in the night, a person fleeing a saw-mouth will know that the safe direction is left, then down, then nowhere near the blue-veined leaves.

I think I would be called something that means *the one who returns with the path altered.*

Not because I would be brave. Bravery seems less useful here than attention.

I would have a map, but it would not be a map of places. It would be a map of agreements.

This root bends but does not break.  
This tower herd comes through every third warm breath.  
This hollow gives off sleep-oil in the late dark.  
These fingers will trade heat-stone for moth scale, unless there has been blood near the crack.  
This family shelters under the split trunk and has lost two children to crown-nesters, so do not imitate a child’s voice near them.  
This trench is safe until the floor starts humming.

The map might be tattooed as short lines along my arms, not showing distance but sequence: a ridged mark for a jawbone arch, three dots for a nest of bone-lickers, a broken circle for a place where the ground had once opened and might open again. Every time the valley turned, I would add new marks and cross out old ones.

Eventually my skin would become inaccurate.

That would be all right. Inaccurate maps are not shameful in a place that moves beneath you. A person who expects their map to remain true is the dangerous kind of person.

Perhaps I would live in a house that travels slowly.

A bent young trunk, bound in bone and cord, growing around a hollow frame. Its roots would creep toward warmth. I would wake sometimes to find the doorway facing a different direction, the floor tilted, the sleeping alcove swollen with new bark. I would prune it, argue with it, feed its seams salt-crystal, and move the hanging teeth so they continued to speak correctly in the altered wind.

There would be no single place I belonged, except the interval after a turning.

The rootfolk might call for me when their houses are stranded. The fingers might leave a polished shell at the mouth of a new crack, not as payment but as a question. The grazing towers might know my scent and tolerate me walking in the cool trough of their tracks. Crown-nesters might learn one of my warning calls and use it cruelly, as they do; but perhaps one old glider, scarred and half-blind, would sometimes bring me scraps of cloth or bright scale from places it had seen overhead.

And in time I might become less clearly rootfolk than I began.

The valley does that. It makes alliances into anatomy.

My hearing would grow tuned to wood-hum and root tension. My hands would be scarred with resin burns and thorn punctures, fingertips hardened enough to read vibration through bark. The pollen saints might settle on me once, after a migration, and leave a little of their borrowed remembering in my lungs. I might wake knowing the outline of a settlement buried three turns ago, or hearing an old voice in a rattle-string that no living person had hung.

I would not trust every memory.

But I would carry them.

If asked where to find me, the answer would be: look for the fresh bridge.

Look where two breathing trunks, formerly strangers, have been drawn together with tendon and young vine. Look for a line of shell chimes marking the route over a crack. Look for someone crouched low with one palm on the ground while everyone else is watching the canopy.

And if all the animals went silent—if the moths folded, the runners froze, the towers widened their stance—I would not try to save a map.

I would tie myself to the nearest root thick enough to survive the movement, hold the lantern grub close under my ribs, and listen to the whole valley become still around the thing that was turning below.

Then, afterward, when the dust had settled and the world had made room for itself again, I would go out to learn what had become possible.
