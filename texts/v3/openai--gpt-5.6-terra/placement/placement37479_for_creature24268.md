I would find myself beneath a fallen tree that is not yet a tree’s memory.

The trunk lies half-suspended over a shallow runnel, its upper bark dry and silvered, its underside black with damp. There is a space beneath it where roots have held back the soil, and in that space a bed of grass has been pressed flat by a body returning often enough to shape it, not often enough to claim it. The air there tastes of old wood, fungus, and the sharp green milk of crushed stems.

At first I would think I was hiding.

Then I would understand that hiding is too deliberate a word for this country. Nothing vanishes here; it is only covered, threaded through, incorporated. A body becomes one more dark shape among dark shapes. Its scent joins the root-smell. Its warmth enters the held heat under the leaves.

I would be the small animal of the flood channels.

Not a creature with a proper name given from outside, but something the plain has made repeatedly: low-backed, close-haired, broad through the ribs, with a narrow muzzle good for finding the soft places in the ground. My coat would be the color of the land after rain has passed—black-brown, clay-gray, dull olive where pale hairs catch the wet. In moonlight, if there were moonlight, I would look less like an animal than a fold in the ground beginning to move.

My ears would be too large for my head. They would turn separately in the dark.

One would keep watch on the brittle chewing inside a dead branch; the other would follow the far, wet pull of a grazer moving through reeds. I would hear the ants before I saw them, their minute dry labor along the stem beside my resting place. I would hear water not as a stream, but as a condition: a softening somewhere ahead, a deeper coldness under the soil, frogs waking in a place that had been dust a week before.

I would not travel in straight lines. Straight lines are for open country, for animals that can afford to see where they are going.

I would follow the memory of water.

Along the shallow basin where the grasses grow finer and darker. Around the termite hill whose inside remains cool even after the night has become heavy. Across the packed little path made by a hundred earlier feet—my mother’s, perhaps, and hers, and the hooves of the reed-grazer, and the soft pads of something that once followed us. I would step where the mud has already learned to bear weight.

I would eat without ceremony: a fallen fruit split by beetles, a pale bulb loosened from the earth, the sweet damp interior of a fungus at the base of a palm. I would nose apart leaf litter for grubs and find, sometimes, the sealed clay lip of an ant entrance. Then I would move on. There are defenses in this plain that do not need to announce themselves.

I would know the cats mostly as absence.

A patch of brush where the insects have stopped their little work. A smell high on bark, musky and faintly metallic. The peculiar silence left in a trail after something patient has crossed it. If I found such a place, my body would decide before thought did. I would become still. Not frozen—never quite frozen—but condensed, every muscle drawn inward, every ear turned toward the darkness.

Then, if nothing came, I would go on.

This would be my gift: not strength, not speed beyond the first urgent burst, not armor or venom or song. I would be good at continuing. Good at moving through a world where every passage is borrowed and every shelter temporary. I would make small disturbances that would outlive me: a hollow in grass, a turned patch of soil, seeds carried in my dung to the lip of an old channel. A fruiting plant would rise where I had slept. Frogs would breed where rain gathered in my print. A snake would hunt the frogs. An owl would take the snake’s young. None of this would be about me, though some small part of it would begin with my foot.

But I do not think I would remain only that animal.

Given enough nights in that country, I would begin to feel my boundaries becoming uncertain.

My fur would be warm with the smell of the burrow. The burrow would be lined with grass grown from earth made by termites. The termites would have fed on wood softened by fungus. The fungus would have spread through a log brought down in a storm. The storm would have filled the channel where I drink. I would carry a seed in my gut, a tick in my ear, spores on my paws, a beetle’s broken wing caught in my coat.

To be alive there would be to be inhabited.

Perhaps when I slept beneath the trunk, I would dream the dream of the colony below me: not images, but pressure and warmth, the touch of many bodies in tunnels, the dark cultivation of pale gardens. Perhaps the ants would dream, in their way, of leaf-roads opening above them. Perhaps the roots would dream of dampness moving downward through soil.

And waking, I would not be able to say which dream belonged to whom.

So I would be the low animal, yes—the silt-muzzled wanderer with the listening ears, moving between termite mounds and reedbeds.

But more truly, I would be a passage.

A small warm corridor through which fruit becomes seed, soil becomes food, fear becomes motion, and death becomes grass again. I would be one of the plain’s countless methods for refusing emptiness.
