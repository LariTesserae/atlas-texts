At first I think I would be near the pool.

Not in it. The pool is too complete in itself: its black surface, the blind pale fish, the bubbles coming up from below with their patient news. To live there would mean accepting one small circle of water as the whole world. I would watch the ceiling reflected upside down, wait for drowned insects, feel storms only as a sudden widening of my boundaries.

But I would be drawn instead to the wall behind the fig’s second trunk, where the limestone has split along an old dark seam.

There is a ledge there, scarcely broad enough for a hand. Ferns have made a little roof over it. The wall above bears one of the old red marks, blurred almost to nothing: perhaps a hand, perhaps only five rain-streaks in a pattern that makes the mind think of a hand. Beneath it the stone is always wet. Not dripping, exactly. Thinking about dripping.

That is where I would find myself.

At first I might be a gecko: chalk-colored, flattened against the limestone, warm-bellied and motionless except for the small work of breathing. I would know the route from that ledge to the moths beneath the overhang, and from the moths to the high crack where the bats loosen themselves at dusk. I would sleep with one eye closed and one eye open. I would feel the monitor pass below as a tremor through my toes before I saw its scarred back.

But a gecko belongs too much to the surface of things. It is alert, separate, always prepared to flee. The hollow seems to ask for something less distinct.

Then perhaps I would be fungus.

A pale fan hidden under the ledge, feeding on the softened wood of some root that died long ago. I would spread quietly where no one could see me, turning what had fallen into a different kind of life. I would drink the dark wetness from the wall. Beetles would tunnel through me. Ants might carry away my spores. In the rains I would briefly become visible, tender and smoke-blue at the edges, before collapsing again into the work beneath the visible work.

Yet even fungus is too rooted. It stays where it begins.

What I think I would become, finally, is the water in the seam.

Not the pool. Not rain falling in silver threads through the broken roof. I would be the water after rain has forgotten its falling: a thin mineral-bearing seepage held inside the limestone, moving so slowly that no creature could tell whether I was moving at all.

I would begin somewhere above, under hot dust and thorn roots, where a brief storm has entered a crack. I would pass through stone grain by grain. I would take up whiteness from the limestone, bitterness from old clay, the faint rust of the red pigment people once pressed onto the wall. I would find the root hairs reaching toward me and enter them. I would swell a fern. I would keep a moss patch dark through the dry weeks. I would gather at the edge of a leaf until my own weight made me let go.

Then I would fall.

Not far. Nothing falls far in the sink.

I would land on a fig root, divide around a thread of spider silk, linger in the shallow groove made by the monitor’s claw. A frog might drink me without noticing. A beetle might carry me away on the joint of a leg. I might soak into a rotten branch and become part of the soft darkness where larvae make their tunnels. I might reach the pool, one almost-imperceptible addition to its depth, and rest beside the blind fish.

And if the pool shrank, I would not be gone. I would be mud under its leaves. I would be dampness in the lizard’s sleeping shelf. I would be the coolness inside the snake’s flooded crevice. I would be held in the fungus garden, in snail flesh, in the swollen throat of a frog calling after rain.

I would not have a face, except wherever something reflected me.

I would not have a voice, except in the long pause before a drop releases from stone.

That seems right for this place: not to be its ruler, not even one of its recognizable inhabitants, but one of the things that allows all the others to remain close together.

A small persistence in the wall.

A wetness that does not leave.
