I would not arrive by tram, though I might eventually learn the timetable.

At first I would be found in the small gap between the Reservoir booth and the administrative building beside it: a place too narrow to be called an alley, roofed by a bridge that has been repaired in three different materials. There is always condensation there. The wall is warm in one patch, though no pipe runs behind it. People use the gap as a shortcut when the square is crowded, but they do not linger.

I would linger.

For a while, I might be mistaken for a municipal notice that has come loose from its frame. A sheet of pale paper, damp at the corners, covered in text that rearranges when read from too close.

**PLEASE CONFIRM THAT THE NEXT ROOM  
HAS CONSENTED TO FOLLOW YOU.**

Someone would report it. Someone else would come with a clipboard, glance at me, and decide I was not urgent. This would be correct, though not entirely reassuring.

Then, perhaps, I would become the person who maintains the notices.

Not writes them. The city writes most of them itself, through committees, accidents, pressure changes, and the gradual convergence of bureaucratic language with prophecy. But I would be the one who notices when a notice has become outdated in a dangerous way. When *DO NOT ENTER* needs a qualifier. When *RETURN VIA THE NEAREST STAIRWELL* must be amended because the nearest stairwell now returns people to a Thursday in which they have not yet received the warning.

I would carry a satchel of screws, chalk, a roll of black sealing tape, blank forms, and an old handheld registry reader with its casing split along one side. My tag would probably blink amber most days. Not because I was unstable, exactly, but because the registry would have difficulty deciding whether I was an employee, a resident, an artifact, or a procedural consequence of an underfunded department.

People would call me when their doors began behaving politely.

A door that opens before it is touched is a problem. A door that apologizes is a worse one. A door that remains shut but allows the sound of a distant kitchen through its hinges requires a form in duplicate.

I would visit such doors, kneel in the damp hallway, and listen.

Sometimes there would be nothing on the other side but an apartment with its ordinary griefs: wet boots, onion smell, a child’s drawing taped beside the light switch. Sometimes there would be a larger room than the building could contain, with a shoreline visible through its far window. I would not look for long. I would ask, through the crack, whether the occupants had registered their configuration and whether they required water, medicine, or instructions for drawing their curtains.

Usually they would say no.

Sometimes a hand would reach through—not threateningly, but with the tiredness of someone who has been holding a door shut from the wrong side—and I would give it a page of municipal advice.

Not because advice helps everywhere.

Because a page is a small, familiar thing to hold.

Over time I would acquire a room, though the city would not assign it to me. It would appear in the records as a utility closet in an old pumping station, then as a sublet, then as an error, then as my residence. It would have no windows except a narrow pane looking into an atrium where one of the dark-leaved trees grew. My ceiling would be too high on some mornings. A drainage pipe would pass through one wall and occasionally carry the sound of conversations from households that no longer shared a district with mine.

I would put a kettle on a hot plate. I would keep root vegetables in a wire basket. I would have one chair for myself and another for people who had not yet decided whether they were people.

The pale dogs would learn the route to my door.

Not because I fed them often—everyone says that, and everyone does—but because my building would remain itself with unusual consistency. The dogs would sleep beneath the exterior stair. When the fog lifted above the roofline, I would hear them moving downstairs, each one choosing a direction with grave practical concentration. I would not follow. I would leave a bowl of water near the heating vent and trust their refusal.

The municipal insects would inspect my sleeve frequently.

At first I would stand still because that is what one does. Later I would recognize their patterns. A light dusting: routine corrosion. A dense cuff around the wrist: verify recent contact with an unlicensed repetition. A moving black line across the palm: something below ground has been trying to understand fingers again.

I would report the last kind gently.

The construction crews would know me, or know of me. We would meet at the edges of new formations: low black ridges lifting through a parking area, pale hexagonal columns rising beside a shuttered pharmacy, a slab of warm stone appearing under the wheel of an abandoned delivery van.

They would work with their transparent tuning forks. I would bring chalk.

Together we would attempt to explain to the young earth that this was a walkway, not a spine; that the channel beneath it needed to remain empty because water was expected; that a wall could be useful without becoming a complete room; that it was kind to make shelter, but shelter needed exits.

Sometimes the ground would understand.

A stair would stop before reaching the water main. A new ridge would flatten itself into a bench. A cluster of crystals would open, after several days, into something almost like a public archway.

Sometimes it would misunderstand beautifully. It would make a perfectly convincing front door in the side of a mineral column, complete with a threshold, lintel, and a small warmth just beyond it. No building behind. No room. Only the idea of being welcomed.

Those would be the hardest to mark off.

Children would ask whether I was human. I would tell them that I was trying to be useful, which is not an answer but is one adults accept more readily. The children would be unconvinced. They would ask whether I had a shadow.

I think I would.

But it would not always agree with me.

On days when the causality vanes pointed inward, my shadow might arrive at intersections first and wait under an awning. It would stand among the smokers, thin and patient on the wet pavement. When I caught up, it would attach itself to my feet with a slight delay.

No one would mention it.

That would be one of the city’s gifts: not the denial of strangeness, but the refusal to make every strangeness defend its right to exist.

Eventually, I suspect, I would become less like a person and more like a civic function with a coat.

A witness to thresholds. A custodian of incomplete agreements. The one called when a corridor begins remembering a different use, when a window acquires weather from elsewhere, when a citizen finds their own apartment waiting two floors too early.

But I would remain a resident, too. I would buy soup. Misplace keys. Be late for appointments because an elevator had opened onto a floor with no proper number and I had needed to stand there, briefly, listening to the enormous quiet behind it.

And perhaps, years later—or before—I would find something rising from the Reservoir.

Not my name. Not my body. Not a future map.

A small metal sign, clean despite the black water, stamped with words I had never authorized:

**CONTINUITY OBSERVER  
TEMPORARY APPOINTMENT  
RENEWED AUTOMATICALLY**

The attendant would ask me to describe it before letting me take it.

I would say: “It is a sign for someone who lives in the gap between what is built and what is still learning to hold.”

She would look at me for a long moment. Then she would stamp the form in damp ink.

And I would carry the sign home, past the dogs and the tired machines, through a city dense enough to keep seeing itself, while somewhere beyond the last service road the ground made another careful foundation and waited to discover what kind of welcome it meant.
