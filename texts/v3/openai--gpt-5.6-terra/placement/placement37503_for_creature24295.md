I would find myself in the newest chamber, not yet a neighborhood, where the foundations are still pale and soft under their scaffolds.

I would not be one of the first families, exactly. Not a tower worker, not a membrane grower, not a seeper who knows the old unapproved passages.

I think I would be a listener attached to the construction system: a person, perhaps, but one whose job has become strange enough that personhood is only part of it.

My station would be a narrow dry compartment built into the raw stone above the expanding floor. There would be one lens-window looking down over the printed supports. Beneath it, water would move through the chamber in a set of temporary agreements: a warm rising column for the printers, a cold channel along the wall to take away sediment, a nearly motionless pocket where the foundation pads spread their pale roots into cracks.

I would wear no official uniform. Only the soft collar seal, ear regulators, and a harness with more connectors than I understood when I was issued it. The city would have given me a title such as *substrate acoustic assessor*. The workers would call me a crack-listener.

My task would be to hear what the new rock was deciding.

Fresh stone has a sound before it fails. Not the dramatic crack that comes afterward, not the gravel falling like applause down the wall. Earlier than that: a tiny drying click inside saturated sediment; a change in the way the tower chord returns from a particular face; a faint, many-legged ticking where the pressure has begun choosing a route through something that was supposed to remain solid.

The instruments would hear these things too, theoretically. But they would not hear them in context. They would not know the difference between a dangerous silence and a stone simply settling into its new burden. They would not know that some cracks make the same rhythm as rain on the floodplain, or that an opening into an old buried tunnel has a hollow answer, as though the city has spoken into its own mouth.

So I would sit with one hand against the wall.

For hours, perhaps, I would listen through glove, stone, water, machinery, and my own bones.

At first I would believe I was listening for collapse.

Then, after enough shifts, I would begin to hear other things: the vibration of a family fastening furniture to the wall in a temporary cell; a maintenance capsule pausing because someone inside is ill; a child tapping a spoon against a pressure door; the soft collective scrape of repair swarms deciding whether a wound in the masonry can be made into a seam.

I would learn that the chamber did not sound empty before residents arrived. It sounded expectant.

And perhaps that would be what I was: not entirely a human worker, not entirely a municipal sensor, but a threshold instrument. A thing placed where new space enters the city, tasked with deciding whether it can be inhabited.

I would sleep beside the raw wall in a narrow pressure berth. During the small nighttime adjustments, when every lamp blinked and the water outside shifted its immense weight, I would wake briefly and feel the whole chamber tighten around me.

Then I would wait for the chord of the towers to return.

If it returned altered, I would make my report.

If it returned whole, I would mark another few cubic meters of stone as provisional shelter.

And below my window, somebody would hang a photograph on a wall that had not existed the day before.
