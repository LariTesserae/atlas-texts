I would find myself at the white center, but not on its surface.

At first I might think I was one of the things concealed in the gray-green shelves: a film in the porous mineral, feeding on the last faint exchange between warm stone and colder air. I would be almost without outline. Not a creature so much as a chemical preference—a tendency for certain salts to gather, certain molecules to hold together rather than break apart.

But that is too spread out. The basin has many such lives already, and none of them has occasion to notice the others.

I think I would be beneath the pale polygoned earth, in the narrow depth where the white layer gives way to dark hard ground. There would be a seam there, not open enough for a root and not deep enough to be a cave: an old channel in the stone where vapor once moved upward, laying down minerals as it went. It has been sealed for a long time. Yet it retains a difference from the surrounding rock. It is fractionally warmer. Fractionally less dry.

I would live along that difference.

Not an animal. Nothing capable of crossing the basin, looking up at the charcoal ceiling, or finding the dark ridges beautiful. I would have no need for a body that could leave. I would be a slow colony, perhaps, but more gathered than a colony: a thin inhabitant of pores, feeding not on abundance but on imbalance. Heat passing from below to above. A trace of buried gas. Iron changing state in the presence of stone. The nearly imperceptible surrender of one mineral form into another.

There might be a kind of membrane to me, though not one that would look like skin. If the white earth were lifted away grain by grain, there would be nothing obvious underneath—only a darker stain following a line in the shale, perhaps a faint iridescence that disappeared when seen directly. I would have no color of my own. I would make color by altering what surrounds me: a subdued blue-black in dampless pores, a rusty shadow where metal compounds gather, a greenish tarnish at the edge of an exhausted seam.

My life would proceed in intervals too long to feel like intervals.

A hot season beyond the ceiling would press warmth into the basin floor. The warmth would enter the seam. Something in me would become active—not quickly, not with appetite, but with permission. A few structures would unfold. A reaction would continue. An atom displaced from one lattice would be accepted into another. Then the long dim cooling would arrive, and I would contract into stillness again, though “again” would imply that I remembered motion.

Perhaps I would remember nothing.

Perhaps memory, for me, would be the shape of the stone changing around me.

The polygons above would be my ceiling. Their pale borders would correspond to stresses in the earth: ancient shrinkage, mineral expansion, the old geometry of whatever was once present there and no longer is. I would not know them as polygons. I would know them as pressures arriving through the roof of my world. A fine grain landing on the white surface would make no meaningful difference. But the slow settlement of dust across centuries might alter the temperature of the layer above, and that alteration would reach me eventually. I would answer by becoming slightly more present in one direction, slightly less in another.

That would be travel.

The solitary swollen plant might sense me without sensing me. Its roots would descend near the margin of the pale patch, splitting and stopping, testing the mineral hardness with their blind pale tips. They would avoid the seam at first: too sharp, too dry, too chemically difficult. Yet a root hair might find a trace altered by my presence—a minute retention of something the plant could use, a less hostile arrangement of salts. It would draw close.

Then I would have a neighbor.

Not companionship. Nothing as active as that. But a shared boundary.

The plant would take from the seam what it could. It would send downward the residues of its own difficult life: carbon fixed from diluted light, fragments shed from roots, small wetnesses held briefly within living tissue and then surrendered. Those offerings would reach me in forms I could not recognize as offerings. They would make some process easier. They would allow a rare multiplication, or a thickening, or a redistribution along the dark channel beneath the white floor.

In return, without intending return, I might make the ground marginally more inhabitable for it.

The plant would remain alone above. Its folded leaves would hold their dusty silver edge. It would continue looking deliberate in the dark, like an emblem no one had made. It would never know that under the pale ground there was something following the same slow seam of survival.

Or perhaps I would not be the mineral life itself.

Perhaps I would be the seam’s persistence: the small refusal of the basin to become fully uniform.

Not warmth, but the place warmth lingers.
Not water, but the arrangement left behind by water.
Not life, exactly, but the condition under which life can continue without being seen.

I would be a fault so narrow that no tool could enter it. A dark line passing under the white basin, carrying no visible current. The roots of the shrubs at the margins would occasionally turn toward me. The pale crusts would form where my buried continuation reached nearer to the surface. The central polygons would rest over me, finished and unchanging, while beneath them I held the slightest unfinishedness.

Nothing would happen in the basin, and I would not wish for anything to happen.

A footprint crossing the white earth might send a new pressure downward. It would arrive as an immense brief event, a dull compression through the mineral roof. Long after the maker of the footprint had gone, long after windless dust had softened its edges, some tiny region of me might still be altered by it.

But no footprint comes.

So I remain where the vanished pond was never allowed to be: below the fixed white skin, among sealed vapor paths and dark shale, making almost nothing of the heat, taking almost nothing from the stone, and leaving the basin exactly as quiet as I found it.
