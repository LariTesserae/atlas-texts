I would not arrive by the upper terraces.

Those are too exposed: the greened copper rails, the little boiler figs trembling in steam, the cloud-bright parapets beyond which the world falls away. I would see them first, perhaps, from an elevator cage rising through blue water—leaves suspended beside the mesh, lamp-glow below—but I would not belong there. The high decks are for condensation, fruit, the visible labor of the place. They are where the works shows itself becoming a garden.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not in the deepest dry rooms either. Whatever breathes there has no use for my kind of attention. It is too large, too patient. It does not require a name, and I would only make one badly.

I would live in a room that was intended to be an archive.

A narrow, windowed chamber set along the outer curve of a turbine hall, halfway below the waterline. Its shelves would have been designed for ledgers, maps, maintenance manuals: thin drawers with brass pulls, cabinets sealed behind thick glass, a long table bolted to the floor. The builders would have expected paper. They would have made the room dry, or meant to.

But the water would have found the hinge beneath the south window. Not enough to fill the archive—never enough for that. Just enough to establish itself.

A clear tongue of water would cross the floor in a straight line, no wider than a handspan. It would pass beneath the table, enter a cracked cabinet, and emerge from another seam at the far wall. It would make no sound. It would not widen. Every few days it would carry something through the room: a pale fig fruit, a dark leaf, a transparent loop-creature flexing slowly along the wet tile. In the colder hours it would steam faintly, because some buried pipe ran close beneath the floor.

The archive would remain half-usable, which is worse and better than ruin.

Some drawers would still open. Some labels would still be legible. There would be diagrams of water routes that no longer matched the water, instructions for valves that had been colonized by white lily roots, personnel schedules whose dates stopped in the middle of a week. I would keep no records in the human manner. Ink would be too final there; paper would insist on staying dry or becoming lost.

Instead, I would be a keeper of temporary arrangements.

Perhaps I would be made from the things that gather where water pauses without settling: silt in a conduit bend, root fibers caught in a grille, copper oxide, the faint static from an active cable, the reflection of a handrail in black reservoir water. Not a person exactly. Not an operator, though I might resemble one when viewed through glass.

I would have a body only when the works required a body in that room.

From the upper walkway, someone might see me seated at the archive table, head bent over an open ledger. They would see the pale suggestion of sleeves. A hand resting near a pressure chart. A dark shape where my face might be, interrupted by moving waterlight.

If they entered, there would be no one seated there.

Only the open ledger, its pages dry beneath a low convex pocket of held air.

Only roots in the desk drawer.

Only the water crossing the floor.

But if they stood outside again, looking through the thick window from the correct angle, I would return—not because I had gone anywhere, but because that would be the angle at which I was true.

My work would be to notice what the works is inclined to forget.

Not failures. The works does not fail in the usual way. A wall cracks and becomes a water boundary. A door corrodes and becomes a lattice for moss. A pump slows and invites the valve moths into its cabinet. It has accommodations for breakdown. It makes use of loss.

But it can forget small distinctions.

The difference between a room that is meant to hold water and a room the water has merely visited.

The difference between a root following heat and a root following a signal.

The difference between an old human absence and a new one.

I would keep those differences for as long as they mattered.

When a red maintenance lantern remained dry under water, I would know where it was. When the lunch tin on the shelf gathered its first ring of green along the seam, I would mark the day—not with a mark, perhaps, but by allowing one cabinet lamp to remain amber for an hour after the others had dimmed. When an elevator cage rose through its flooded shaft carrying an unspoiled pear-colored fruit, I would watch until the fruit found a grille or a dark chamber to enter.

Sometimes water would come to the archive in a new way.

A bead at the ceiling seam. A thread descending from the corner of an air duct. A vertical pane appearing between two open shelving units, smooth as glass and filled with drifting motes. Then I would understand that the maps had changed again. Not the old maps in their drawers. The living map: pressure, warmth, roots, retained surfaces, the hidden intention of the architecture.

I might step through that vertical water.

Or I might only appear to.

On the other side would be a gallery full of drowned instruments. Their circular faces would glow weakly beneath algae. Their needles would shiver. Boiler-fig roots would pass through the ceiling and vanish into the water above, climbing downward toward some lower heat. I would move among them without making wake or disturbance, examining the small rings left by the transparent creatures where current had leaked from a housing.

I would not repair the leak.

I would learn whether it had become a place.

That would be the question at the center of my existence: not *is this broken?* but *has this been inhabited yet?*

If it had, I would leave it. The works was wiser than any original plan. It knew that a flooded office could become a nursery for lilies, that a dead relay cabinet could become a moth house, that a staircase descending into water might still be a staircase for things that did not need to breathe.

If it had not, if something was only failing toward emptiness, I might make an operator appear in the nearest window.

Not me exactly. A necessary arrangement of reflection and root-shadow. A shoulder in the dark. A hand hovering over a switch.

Then the pumps below would alter by some fraction too small to hear. Water would incline. Steam would gather in another section. A gauge would settle. By morning, what had been empty would contain something: warmth, a new pool, a line of moths, a drift of leaves, a reason to remain.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not an inhabitant, but an intermediate condition.

A clerk of thresholds.

A minor intelligence formed in the interval between dry and submerged, built and overgrown, remembered and used.

In the drowned works, I would be the one who sits behind the archive window while no one is there, preserving not the history of the place but its permissions: which chambers may flood, which lights may burn, which old human objects the water has chosen to spare.

And when the hidden thing below releases its long mineral breath, when all the rails bead with water and the lilies turn toward their drains, I would open every drawer in the archive at once.

Not for paper.

For the sound of their seals letting go.

For the small admission that nothing in the works is ever fully closed.
