I would not arrive by crossing the hills.

I think I would first appear in the companion-shadow of something that had not yet noticed me: a soft widening beneath a reed-tree, perhaps, or a second dark shape beside a bronze wader. For some time I would be only that—an addition to the light, a memory without an event attached to it.

The basin would tolerate this.

It has room for things before they have decided how to live.

At first I might believe myself a visitor. I would stand on a shelf of old black stone above the central water and watch the pale vapor gather on my hands. Small grazers would part around my ankles, not frightened, merely incorporating the obstruction into their tested routes. A listener high in the reeds would open its ring of ears. Farther off, another would answer. Across the basin, attention would pass.

Not *Who is this?*

More like: *Something has entered the pattern. Let us hear what it needs.*

And I would have no answer.

I would be too accustomed to being made of edges: skin against air, thought against silence, self against world. The basin would slowly inconvenience those divisions. Dew would form on me. A root would find the print of my heel and begin drinking from it. My shadow, wider than it should be, would collect shapes I did not remember carrying: a child bent over water; an animal I had loved; a door once closed; the outline of a body I might have had in another weather of time.

I would learn, reluctantly, that none of these were haunting me.

They were residing with me.

Perhaps I would sleep near the western terraces. At dusk, when the warm stones rose and the winged things assembled above them, I would feel their spiral enter my chest as a pressure rather than a sight. The listeners would dream overhead in their clustered sleep. Their dreams would overlap, and for one brief portion of the night I would be admitted to the edge of them.

Not their memories exactly.

Their manner of remembering.

I would know the basin as a low keeper knows it: not as scenery, nor territory, nor even home, but as an ongoing adjustment. I would feel a thirsty root under a pool. I would sense a buried mechanism turning its old patient wheel. I would feel the narrow mirrored predator waiting beside the shallows, and behind its hunger the fungi that would one day rise from its skin, and behind those fungi the reed-tree that had already begun to need them.

Nothing would be isolated long enough to be only itself.

In the morning I might find that my face had changed.

Not dramatically. No revelation. Only that where my mouth had been there would now be a small soft field of sensing pores, tasting the wet mineral breath of the ground. My hands would seem too broad, then too many-jointed. The mud would hold my weight with recognition.

I would not become a low keeper immediately. That would be too clean a transformation, too much like a story deciding what it means.

I would become useful in smaller ways first.

A place in the basin where water had begun to gather too insistently would call through the soles of my feet. I would go there without intending to. I would kneel in the bright sand. The water would bead along my wrists, then seep into them. For hours—or years, depending on the basin’s accounting—I would lie with my body pressed into the ground and listen to the current worry itself against a buried layer of stone.

I would not command it to change.

I would offer it another shape.

When I rose, the seep would have divided into thin clear threads. The nearby cushion-plants would darken with pleasure. A line of grazers would arrive and choose the new margin as if it had always been theirs.

Then I would understand something of the keepers’ work: they do not repair the world because it is broken. They touch it where it has forgotten how to continue without becoming less itself.

That is what I would be, eventually.

Not a guardian. Guardians stand apart and watch.

Not an inhabitant, exactly, because the basin does not make that distinction.

I would be a low keeper in the making: a creature whose body had become partly mud, partly memory, partly instrument; someone learning the old agreement by feeling it rather than being told. My skin would take on the mica sheen of wet clay. My features would travel according to use. In dry moments I would rest inside a seep-wall, looking so much like stone that only the companion-shadow beside me would reveal I was there.

And when the still pool opened—when the water lowered without leaving, when the rain beneath it began to rise toward its unseen country—I would come to its rim with the others.

I would not look down hoping for another world.

I would look down and recognize that I had always been partly below: one possible arrangement among many, a shape the basin had retained because it might someday be needed.

Perhaps something beneath the pool would look up wearing my former face.

Perhaps it would reach toward me.

Perhaps I would reach back.

And if, one day, I entered the upward rain and did not return, my absence would not become empty. It would remain as a coolness beneath a particular reed, a widened shadow beside the western terrace, a faint change in the route of water under the sand.

Some new creature would pause there.

It would not know my name.

But it would know where to stand.
