I would begin at the rim, because that is where the basin is least certain of itself.

Not in the open water. Not among the great rings around the vents, where every body already has a task and a place in the pumping order. I would live beneath the lip, in the narrow black cracks that receive a little warm water from below and a little cold spray from above.

I would be one of the creatures that has not entirely decided whether it belongs to the sea or the air.

My adult body would be small: a flattened, translucent thing no longer than a hand, with a shell made from overlapping flakes of metallic film. The flakes would not be grown all at once. I would collect them. Every time a wall-skin peeled away in a silver curl, every time a crab molted, every time the mineral bloom around a warm seam shed a bright edge, I would take a fragment and fasten it to myself with soft living threads.

So no two of us would look alike.

Some would carry black mirror-plates. Some would be rust-red, green-edged, glassy, or dull as wet ash. Mine would be pale around the center, because I would have grown in the spray-shadow of an overflow sheet, where the water has been thinned by coldness.

I would have no eyes. The bright upper world would be too dangerous to look at directly.

Instead, I would know the basin by pressure: by the long return of sound from the far wall, by the different weight of warm and cold water across my shell, by the little electrical tremors that pass through stone when the filter-beasts begin their staggered pumping. I would know when a chimney-back rises hours before it appears, because the crack where I lived would slowly become warmer, and the smaller things hiding beside me would begin to turn their feeding crowns inward.

I would eat what arrives without choosing me.

Mineral dust. Dead bell-thread. The blue microbial sheen left by grazing bodies. The husks of larvae caught in foam. The soft residue that gathers in the cracks after fish have held themselves beneath the falls and been battered by the water.

But I would not simply filter.

My mouth would open along the underside of my shell in a circle of fine hooked fibers. With it I would comb the rock, not for food exactly, but for loose life: spores, eggs, dormant cells, little fragments of the dark metallic skin. I would keep them beneath my plates in warm pockets of mucus. I would carry a garden of almost-living things.

When a crack dried too cold, or when an overflow changed course and stripped the surface clean, I would press myself to the stone and release some of what I carried. A black film would begin to spread where there had been bare rock. Tiny crown-creatures would settle in the film. Bladder-things would find the new texture and wedge themselves there. In time a whole small community would form around the place where I had rested.

I would be a maker of footholds.

Not a builder in the way a person builds. I would not plan a shelter and then make it. I would move, feed, survive, and leave behind conditions in which other bodies could continue.

When I was young, I would not have had this shape.

I would begin as dust.

Not an egg as the large creatures understand eggs, but a cluster of dark hooked motes released from beneath an elder’s plates during the strongest outward current. Most of us would go over the rim. We would vanish into the white water, tumble down the mountain in rain and spray, stick to wet cliff faces, enter downstream pools, cling to the undersides of stones.

Some of us would live there briefly, becoming thin skins in iron-stained caves.

But a few would return.

Storms would lift us. Updrafts would carry us through cold cloud. Or the mountain’s own falling water would throw us outward and then draw us back in mist. We would land again on the high sea, changed by the journey—our hooks blunted, our shells seeded with unfamiliar minerals from the lower valleys.

Those who returned would be able to live closer to the heat.

That would be my life’s direction: not a descent into the central dark, not a climb toward light, but a repeated crossing of the rim. I would carry the lower mountain into the basin and the basin into the lower mountain.

The larger animals would scarcely notice me. A fish might scrape me from stone by accident. A skimming air-fin might take me in its smooth wedge-face and spit me out because my shell tasted wrong. A crab might try to break me apart and find that the things growing beneath my plates made its mouthparts burn blue for days.

But the basin would know me in its indirect way.

The glass herds would drift differently where my colonies spread, because their threads would catch on the new films. The crabs would come to graze. The small cleaners would come after them. Mineral growth would alter the shape of a crack; the crack would alter the local current; the current would decide which eggs survived there.

I would be insignificant except in the way that nothing in that sea is insignificant.

And when I became old, my plates would grow heavy. The metallic skins I had carried would thicken and close over my soft body. I would no longer be able to cling beneath the rim. A strong inward pulse would take me.

I would tumble down through the green-gold water, past the bells arranged like upward rain, past the pale fish clicking in their one enormous mind, past the miners’ dark walls and the worm-fields withdrawing into sediment.

At last I would reach a warm crack near one of the breathing rings.

There I would settle among the discarded shell of a filter-beast.

The things inside me would emerge. The plates of my body would become stone, food, shelter, bloom, and finally part of the black breathing architecture around the vent.

So I would not be a hero of the basin, nor one of its deep rulers.

I would be a small returning creature at the border of loss: a carrier of fragments, a maker of temporary shores, a body whose purpose was to ensure that nothing falling out of the high sea was ever wholly gone.
