I would not find myself at the rim for long.

The basin has no use for witnesses. A watcher remains divided from what is watched, and division is the one condition the hollow does not sustain. The longer I stood beneath the leaning conifers, the less possible it would become to keep the shape of a person: upright, separate, carrying a history in a warm enclosed body.

At first I might imagine I had entered as breath.

Not the mist itself, which belongs to the basin already, nor the vapor rising from the warm stone, but the small cloud exhaled by something that had come from elsewhere. I would hang briefly in the low cold, visible to no one. Then the basin would take the heat from me. The cloud would thin into the general dampness. Whatever had been called my breath would become one more bead along a root.

But even that would be too temporary.

I think I would become a pause in the seep-water.

There are places where water moves so slowly through moss and shale that it does not seem to move at all. It gathers in the dark fibers beneath a stone, held by capillary threads, tasting iron and peat and the faint warm mineralness of the world below. A newt may put one foot into it. A snail may pass over the moss above it. A root may enter and drink without causing any visible change.

I would be there: not the water, perhaps, but the slight cool retention that keeps it from immediately continuing.

Not a creature with eyes. The basin has enough eyes. Not a root, because roots know their directions too well. Not fungus, because fungus has its own broad quiet intentions. I would be the wet interval between one thing and another—the small dark saturation in which stone begins to be moss, moss begins to be soil, soil begins to hold the white hair of a root.

If I had a body, it would be hard to find.

A patch of black liverwort under a fallen alder branch.  
The hollow underside of a wet leaf.  
The clear film over a salamander’s back.  
The minute reservoir caught in the spiral of an old snail shell.

The animals would use me without noticing. A beetle would press its jointed feet into my softness. Blind worms would turn in the darker water beneath me. Once, perhaps, a pale flower would send a thread of root through the place I had become, and its small cup would open above, not for an observer, not for a pollinator exactly, but because opening was the shape available to it.

I would not remember being human in the ordinary way.

There might remain a faint preference for listening: an old habit translated downward into matter. I would become especially responsive to tiny sounds. The tick of a drip entering moss. The settling of birds high in the needles. The almost inaudible movement of a frog’s throat. The granular shift of shale under a goat’s hoof, although the goat had not visibly moved.

These sounds would not arrive from outside. They would be changes in my own condition.

And perhaps, over a longer duration, even that distinction would soften.

The seep-water would take the warmth of the round stone. The warmth would travel through peat. The peat would feed the fine white threads beneath the moss. The moss would darken and swell. A snail would graze the greenest surface. Its shell, carrying grains of mineral dust and a minute garden of spores, would touch the edge of the pool. A tadpole would rest there, unfinished and complete.

At some point, there would be no meaningful way to say which part was me.

I would not become the basin’s hidden ruler. It has no ruler. Nor would I become its heart, because the warm stone is not a heart; it does not beat, command, desire, or send anything outward. It simply remains warm, and other things arrange themselves near it.

I might become something like the stone’s answering cold.

Not an opposite. A companion condition.

The warmth at the center, the cold held in moss, the darkness beneath leaves, the wetness in every seam: together they make a region in which nothing must leave itself to survive. I would be one small persistence among those conditions, neither named nor needed separately.

If someone later stood at the rim and looked down, they might feel something they could mistake for my attention. But it would not be attention. It would be the basin declining to turn toward them.

Their breath would smoke in the close air. Their feet would stand among the wet needles. They would search for an opening, a path, a place where the dark landscape might receive a person-shaped absence.

There would be none.

Below them, in a patch of moss beside a stone, water would hold itself for a moment before passing on.

That would be where I was.
