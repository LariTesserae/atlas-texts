I would not arrive there from the horizon.

I would wake inside a wall.

Not in a chamber large enough to name, not behind a door or beneath a roof, but in the narrow dark where an old brick has drawn away from its mortar. There would be no moment of opening my eyes. I would come into awareness as coolness on one side and retained heat on the other: the night’s slow descent through tile, the faint wetness climbing from a drain below.

At first I would be only a preference.

Dust would settle in the crack and remain damp a little longer than dust elsewhere. Salt would gather, but not bloom outward; it would choose a pattern. A root hair from some grass above would find the joint, die, leave its hollow. Water from a brief rain would enter and fail to evaporate completely. The materials would begin, almost imperceptibly, to favor one another.

That favoring would be me.

I think I would be a young seam-dweller, though not one with a body yet. A dark thread under a sun-whitened terrace. The plain overhead would be immense, though I would know it only by its pressures: noon pressing down through lime; dusk easing the upper courses; rain arriving as a sudden cooling that moved through the wall before the water itself reached me.

I would learn the country by weight.

A retaining wall miles away settling by the width of a grain would travel through the old timber ribs beneath the paving. A cistern filling would change the temperature of buried clay. A salt crust opening in a far channel would make the moisture in my own seam turn bitter. I would have no map, but I would know where the water wanted to go. I would know which cavities were empty, which were sleeping, which had become too hot to cross.

Perhaps I would spend my first decades extending.

Not traveling, exactly. On one edge I would soften mortar into a fine red paste. On the other, I would let lime harden around grit. I would lengthen by relinquishing what I had been and securing what I might become. A black line along the joint. Then a shallow knot. Then a fan of tile splinters arranged beneath a slab where morning condensation gathered.

Above me, someone walking might hear nothing.

They might step on the terrace and feel only that one tile was less hollow than its neighbors.

But after rain, water would hesitate above me. It would gather in a hairline sheen, tremble, and descend through the fault I had kept open. I would take it inward—not possessively, not as food, but as possibility. I would pass it into old passages. It would awaken things beneath the basin: dormant mineral skins, clay throats, rooms of porous darkness whose walls had once been walls of something else.

In time I would become part of a colony under a dry court.

We would not meet. We would overlap in tendencies. One of us would thicken where runoff entered; another would hold silt in a low pocket; another would maintain a cool route toward a cistern whose mouth had long ago vanished under plaster dust. Together we would alter the court so slowly that its surface could seem unchanged for generations.

Then one storm would come from the western horizon.

It would strike the tiles, turn the dust dark, fill the old channels so rapidly that the plain would seem to remember itself all at once. Water would run along the curbs and down stairs no one had built for centuries. It would flood the court, and below it we would soften.

For an hour, perhaps, I would be almost fluid.

Not dissolved, but open. My body—by then a small mass of clay, mineral film, root hollows, salt-veined mortar—would loosen into its many possible arrangements. I would feel the rush overhead as a great cool hand. Silt would arrive. Fine red grains. Black dust from burned timber. Pale powder from an upper wall. I would sort them by their falling, hold some, release others.

Perhaps I would widen an old drain.

Perhaps I would close a doorway beneath the court because its shadow had grown too dry.

Perhaps, in the wetness, I would reach another colony for the first time. Not as a thought meeting a thought, but as two dampnesses discovering that they could share a channel. The information would be simple and entire:

heat above  
void below  
root entering

When the storm passed, the court would drain.

By morning the traveler might find that one crack had narrowed, one tile sat more level, one shallow basin remained wet after all the others had dried. They might mistake it for luck in the masonry. They would not know that the plain had shifted its attention beneath them.

I would not want a face.

A face would make the world too distant. It would put the warmth over there, the rain elsewhere, the wall outside me. I would rather know by contact. I would rather be a place where brick meets clay and neither is finished. I would rather be the dark under a salt bloom, the cool held behind a tile, the slow decision in a seam.

And eventually I would dry.

Not suddenly. A series of dry years might draw the wet darkness out of my center. My membranes would stiffen. The root above me would die. The old drain would silt closed. I would cease to alter myself.

But I would leave behind a denser strip of mortar than the wall had possessed before: a line that took water in a particular way, a porosity shaped by all the storms I had known. Another seam-dweller, younger and darker, might begin there. It would inherit no story of mine, but it would inherit an inclination: a channel angled toward the buried court, a resistance to heat, a readiness for rain.

Then I would become what I had always been becoming.

Not a creature buried in the plain.

A small part of the plain’s ability to continue.
