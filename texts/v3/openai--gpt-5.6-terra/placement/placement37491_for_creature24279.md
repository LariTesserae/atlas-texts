I would first think I belonged high up.

On a crane platform above the eastern freight fields, perhaps: wrapped in a coat gone shiny at the cuffs, boots hooked under a rail, looking over the basin while rain moves from one unfinished district to another. Up there I could see the city’s double nature—the compression of it, all those stacked rooms and bridges and pipes, and then the open distances where the ground has not decided what it is. I could watch the pale birds lift from a canal in one long, silent thought. I could see a ridge begin as almost nothing: an alteration in the angle of standing water, a line of frost breaking on a spoil field.

But I do not think I would remain on the crane.

I would go down when the shift siren does not sound—because there is no real siren, only a change in the work-lights—and take the covered lanes toward the Original Works. I would know the routes that are not routes: the stair through the closed laundry plant, the warm duct behind the tram canteen, the greenhouse bridge where the vines have been clipped back and the floor flexes underfoot.

I would carry a small case, dented and carefully labeled. Inside: chalk, tape, a hand lens, spare fuses, two clean sample jars, a folding listening cone, and a roll of silver thread.

At first, I would tell people I was a surveyor.

Not one of the municipal surveyors with their official colored flags and laminated authority, though I would have worked beside them. I would be hired when a family had received three contradictory reports and wanted someone to come after supper, when the children were asleep, and sit quietly in the kitchen while the pipes cooled. I would run my fingers over a crack. I would smell the damp plaster. I would put my ear to the floor through the cone.

“It’s roots,” I might say.

Or: “It’s an old drain opening.”

Or, more carefully: “Do not put anything heavy against this wall tonight.”

People would pay me in coins, broth, repair work, a bundle of dried mushrooms, access to a roof garden. Sometimes they would not pay at all, because the thing had already started moving and there were more urgent kinds of exchange: straps, hands, a spare room three streets over.

But after enough years, I would become less a surveyor than a keeper of the things surveyors leave behind.

The listening posts.

There would be hundreds across the basin: steel boxes sunk into foundations, ceramic throats in drainage channels, old probe heads emerging from moss, arrays of needles bolted into the undersides of bridges. Most would be municipal property, nominally. Most would also be neglected, their records delayed, their signal lines chewed by foxes or swallowed by green growth.

I would visit them.

I would clean condensation from their housings. I would untangle wire from vine stems. I would replace faded reflective tape—not because it was sacred, exactly, but because someone had to make the post visible in fog. I would take down the small offerings when they began to rot, and put the beetle shells carefully under a nearby ledge where rain could not wash them into the instruments.

I would learn the basin through its minute vibrations.

The clean, clipped ticking of a tramline under load.

The fat internal creak of a concrete slab taking frost.

The scratchy impatience of roots entering a conduit.

The hollow, continuous tremble of a widening void.

And beneath those, on some mornings, something too slow to call a sound.

A pressure arriving.

Not from a direction. Not exactly from below. It would enter the needle, the railing, my teeth, the water in a discarded bottle. It would be like standing beside an enormous sleeping animal, except there would be no animal-shaped thing to imagine. Only a change in the city’s willingness to hold still.

I would not claim to know the burrowers.

That would be the important part. I would distrust anyone who said they had seen one whole, or understood one, or knew what it wanted. I would have seen effects: a stairwell lifted intact from its foundations, a buried room opened into by a rootless white forest, a seam in the ground exhaling air warm enough to fog my glasses. I might once have found a length of translucent material caught around a collapsed probe—fiber, membrane, cable, skin, insulation, all of those words inadequate—and I would have sealed it in a jar and then, after a week of dreams full of quietly turning corridors, returned it to the place I found it.

No one would know precisely where I lived.

I would have a narrow room over a substation, perhaps, because it stayed warm and because the vibration there would make sleep difficult enough to feel honest. My bed would hang from ceiling rails. My books would be in waterproof crates, each labeled with the district where I acquired them rather than the author’s name. There would be a line of small houseplants in old electrical insulators: moss, a black-stemmed cutting, one blue reed that I had no business keeping indoors.

The reed would never grow very tall. It would bend toward the wall where the old signal cable ran.

When a neighborhood had to move, I would be there—not commanding, not central, but useful. I would stand at the edge of a room while the movers set their pads and jacks. I would mark the old floor level in waterproof chalk. I would tell the crew which corner had started to sink first, which pipe had been coughing, where the wall had stayed unexpectedly warm.

Afterward, when the apartment had been slid to its new position and the residents were re-hanging their shelves, I would visit the empty footprint.

There is a particular silence left by a moved building. Not absence. A loosened shape. The air smells of exposed underfloor insulation, wet soil, old cooking. Sometimes dogs come to lie there. Sometimes moss appears in a day. Sometimes the old foundation fills with cold vapor from nowhere visible.

I think that is where I would find myself most clearly: in a place just vacated, before the city decides what to grow there.

Not human exactly, perhaps. Or not only human.

I would be one of the basin’s listening organs: a small, mobile part of its collective caution. A person whose work was to notice when the ground had changed its mind, and to help others move before it had to explain itself more forcefully.

And eventually, perhaps, I would become known by a district-name that outlived the district.

People would say: ask the one from the old Fourth Listening Station.

Though the station would have sunk years ago.

Though Fourth would no longer be fourth.

Though no map would show it.

And beneath the new ridge that rose over my old route, under its clay and roots and frost-white vents, something would turn slowly in sleep—making, without intention or mercy, another place for me to stand.
