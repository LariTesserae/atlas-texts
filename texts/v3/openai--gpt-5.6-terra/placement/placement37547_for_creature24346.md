I would not arrive by the northern inflow. I do not think I have ever come from above.

At first I would believe I was one of the level-keepers: a small person in a collar suit, standing on a grated platform with both hands around a rail. My lamp would be shuttered. I would know, without remembering learning it, that the water was three fingers too high against the red seam in the wall.

Someone would ask me how long it had been rising.

I would listen to the pump before answering.

Not look at the gauge. Not yet.

The pump’s rhythm would be wrong in a way no new worker could hear: not faster or slower, but burdened. Each turn would have a pause inside it, as though it were lifting something which settled back between strokes. I would tap twice on the rail. Somewhere below, someone would answer with a single tap.

Then I would remember that I had no family in the upper rooms. No berth-box. No old door. No name scratched into a suit collar.

The other keepers would know me anyway.

They would call me when the central shaft altered its pressure. They would send a child to fetch me from whatever half-flooded maintenance room I had last entered. The child would not call out. They would find the little brass plate set into the wall beside a blackened conduit, touch it, and wait.

I would feel the touch from inside the pipe.

That is where I would be: not quite in the water, not quite in the town. In the narrow old passage that runs behind the central gallery, between the limestone and the first skin of ironwork. A service chase too thin for an adult body, sealed behind bolted plates. It was intended for cables once. Then for pressure lines. Then for nothing anyone remembers.

It contains warm pipes, old wires, a ladder with only seven rungs, and a dry space no larger than a coffin where the basin’s first gauge panel still hangs.

The panel has no lights except one.

The gauge faces are opaque with mineral bloom. Their needles have rusted in place. A paper chart recorder sits below them, though there has been no paper in it for generations. In the center is a glass tube full of dark water. It rises and falls almost too slowly to see.

I would be the one who reads it.

Or perhaps I would be the reading itself.

The level-keepers would bring me their measurements. They would not ask what was below. They have better questions.

“Does the west seal hold?”

“Is this brown water only rain?”

“Can we lower by half a hand?”

“Is there time?”

I would put my palm against the glass tube, though my glove would already be wet. Sometimes I would tell them yes. Sometimes I would tell them no. More often I would tell them what the basin itself seemed to be saying:

Not yet.

Hold the third.

Let the flooded rooms remain flooded.

There is a difference between water that wants to enter and water that must not be allowed to leave.

I would not always be right. Nothing in the basin is always right. But I would have been listening for a very long time.

Long enough to remember the shaft dry.

Not as an image. I would not see a dry shaft lit by miners’ lamps, nor hear picks against stone. I would remember dryness as a pain in the walls: the absence of weight, the strain in every bolt, the great upward knocking that traveled through the floor plates and into the teeth of anyone standing near.

The first keepers had thought the water was the disaster.

Later, they learned it was the answer.

I would remember the moment the shaft filled past the lowest gallery. The machinery failing one by one. The men who had been ordered to evacuate and did not. The sound becoming softer as water took it. Then, at a particular height, the terrible pressure from below easing—not stopping, but becoming patient.

That was when the town began.

Not when the first houses were built in abandoned offices. Not when children were born beneath the ice. Not when the pumps were repaired.

The town began when someone, exhausted and terrified, marked a line on a wall and said: here. Keep it here.

I may have been that person.

Or I may be what remained of their attention after they died.

The dead do not come to me as voices. They come as corrections.

A wrench placed in the wrong locker makes the glass tube shiver.

A maintenance crew considers sealing a passage that will be needed forty years later, and one of the old gauges ticks once beneath its mineral crust.

A child, alone on a training descent, reaches for a valve that should not be turned. The lamp in the passage behind them goes dark. In stopping to understand why, they notice the pale knot of thread-worms around the overheated conduit.

The basin calls these things luck because luck is less frightening than responsibility.

But I would know that the town is crowded with continuities. Every hand that learned the rails, every worker who put their ear to a pipe, every elder who remembered an unseen drain—none of them has entirely left. Their knowledge has settled into corners. It lives in the preferred stiffness of a wheel. In a hinge that yields only when pushed twice. In the route a blind shrimp takes toward clean water.

And in me.

I would be a keeper, then, but not one who sleeps.

A margin-keeper.

A resident of the place between the wall and the pressure.

When the deep current rises, the others would clear the gallery. They would close doors, take their hands from the rails, and wait in the low dark while the lamps dim at their edges.

I would remain behind the plates.

The water in the glass tube would climb.

For a moment, all the old pipes around me would become silent—not dead, but listening. Even the warm ones. Even the cables.

Then I would feel the thing below press upward through the shaft.

Not a body.

Not a face.

A question with immense patience.

Is this height still sufficient?

And I, with the weight of the basin above me and the memory of every marked seam in its walls, would answer in the only language it respects.

I would hold the level.
