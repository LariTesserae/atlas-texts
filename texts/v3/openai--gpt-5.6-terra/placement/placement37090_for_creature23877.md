I would not live at the basin’s center, where everyone eventually passes and every argument becomes public. Nor at the outermost houses, where the city gives way too quickly to weather.

I would be found in one of the middle terraces, in the district where the older streets begin to fold under newer ones.

There would be a square there, though calling it a square would flatter its geometry. It would be a widened landing between three flights of stairs: one rising toward the wool court, one descending to the market roofs, and one entering a low arch whose far end is always dark. Beneath the paving would be a reservoir older than the surrounding houses. Its roof would be supported by blunt stone columns, and in damp months one could hear water shifting underfoot if one stood still.

My door would face north, painted a dull, unmistakable yellow made from ochre and something mineral-blue. Not cheerful yellow—more the color of a lamp seen through rain. Beside it, a narrow gutter would run down the wall and disappear under the threshold of the neighboring house. I would clear it every morning, not ceremoniously. Just with two fingers, a hooked wire, and whatever patience the weather required.

I think I would be a listener of cisterns.

Not one of the most renowned ones. I would not be called in when an entire lower district feared flooding, nor would my name become a saying about good drainage. I would know a handful of reservoirs well: their depths, their temperaments, the particular hollow notes made by their water in frost or drought. I would lower a plumb line through grates in courtyards and listen to the small knock of its weight against the surface. I would keep records in a book swollen at the edges from dampness: dates, levels, smells, changes in echo, the appearance of silt, the first ice.

There would be words in the book that only make sense after decades.

*North chamber breathing heavily.*

*Water clear after the red rain.*

*The old crack has begun to answer again.*

*Do not trust the third stair in hard freeze.*

People would come to me with practical questions disguised as other questions.

“Will the spring hold through summer?”

“Can we open the lower rooms again?”

“Did you hear anything beneath Varan’s courtyard?”

“Is my sister foolish to buy a house above that channel?”

I would answer carefully, because certainty would be a kind of vanity in such a place. I would say: the water is lower than last year, but not frightened. I would say: there is movement under the paving, though it may be only old settling. I would say: replace the wall before you ask whether the house is safe.

Perhaps I would also keep a small unofficial map—not the grand city map, which would pretend streets were streets and walls were walls, but a map of passages. A map of leaks, warm stones, handrails that ice first, doors left unlatched during storms, kitchens where a soaked traveler can be given broth without explanation. It would show where the roofworkers cross from one block to another. It would mark the old woman’s hidden stair behind the fish stall. It would include the names of cats that reliably know dry routes through the undercrofts.

The map would have no fixed border. Every year some part of it would become wrong. A retaining wall would be rebuilt. A family would wall off a passage. A floor would collapse into a chamber and reveal a route nobody had used in eighty years. I would amend it rather than mourn it.

I would have a cat, though I would insist it belonged to no one. It would be gray in the way wet basalt is gray, with a torn ear and long paws for the stairs. It would sleep near the warm drain behind my worktable and vanish whenever I had visitors. In winter it would appear at my door with the look of someone returning from important business in the snow.

I would know the roof birds by sound before seeing them. I would keep a shelf of smooth stones removed from blocked channels—stones shaped like eggs, knuckles, teeth, a sleeping animal’s back. Some would be marked with the names of people who brought them to me: a child who found one in a gutter, an old mason clearing a run, a woman who had lost her husband during the storm that broke the east reservoir. Not memorials exactly. More like proof that the water had passed through human hands.

When rain began, I would not stand in the doorway and admire it for long. I would do what everyone else did: look at the nearest flow, notice whether it had found its channel, then go upstairs to close the high window.

But afterward, when the hard part had passed, I might walk the city.

I would go down through the wet streets while the clouds lifted in strips from the basin. I would touch a handrail that had begun to sweat with cold. I would inspect the little animal-headed spouts and watch water leave their mouths. I would pass children rerouting rivulets with pebbles, pretending not to help them make their miniature city hold.

And if someone asked who I was, I would probably give a name first, then a neighborhood.

But if they asked what I did, I would say:

I help the water remember where it is going.
