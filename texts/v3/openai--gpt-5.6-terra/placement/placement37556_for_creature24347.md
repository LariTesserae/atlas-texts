I think I would begin by believing I had come to the Low Bright as a person.

Not an important person. Not a keeper, at first. I would arrive carrying something poorly suited to the place: perhaps a pack with too many straps that creak, a metal cup that rings when set down, a coat made for wind. I would be called roof-learner even if I had no memory of the surface. The name would fit because I would look up too often, trusting smoothness, mistaking brightness for safety.

For a while I would be given small work.

I would carry cups to drip stations. I would learn to lay my palm against a wall before I leaned on it. I would be taught that a chill in a passage may be an ordinary chill, or the breath of a newly opened space, and that the distinction matters more than fear does. Someone older—bent at the shoulders, with mineral dust gathered in the corners of their eyes—would take my loud cup away and hand me one of fired clay.

At night, or what passed for night in the basin, I would sleep near a burrower. Not because I had earned its trust, but because the animal had decided I was cold. Its breathing would be deeper than mine. Its body would smell of damp fur, old dust, fungus meal, and the faint metallic sweetness of stone water. I would learn to wake when it wakes.

This is how I think I would become useful: not by knowing the city, but by being corrected by it.

I would learn the routes that remain after a tremor. I would learn to pause when gliders descend. I would learn to listen when people stop speaking, which would happen more often than an outsider could understand. In the Low Bright, silence would not be empty space between sounds. It would be a tool held ready.

Eventually I would be assigned to a place no one wanted to assign anyone to: an old canal district beyond the last regularly tended cisterns.

There would be an arch there, half-swallowed by pale sediment. Under it, the remains of a street would run into shadow. The walls would be only knee-high, as the stories say, but their foundations would be unusually fine: stones locked together with a patience no one now has time to imitate. Along the old street, every few days, clean water would appear in shallow bowls set into doorways.

Not many bowls. Enough.

No one in the inhabited city would admit to filling them.

The water would be cold, clear, and without the dusty film that gathers on anything left uncovered. There would be no visible source. The roofs of the ruined houses would have fallen long ago. No ceiling crack would hang above them. Yet each bowl would be full—not overflowing, not stale, not evaporating.

My duty would be simple. I would count them.

At first I would think that was all I was doing. I would walk out with a coil of rope, a clay tablet, a wrapped cake of dry fungus meal, and a small covered lamp for the deep passage beyond the canal. I would mark which bowls had water, which had been broken by falling dust, which had shifted beneath new silt. I would note animal tracks.

There would always be tracks.

Glider claws in the dust. The long soft toe-marks of feral grazers. Once, the dragging furrow of something broad-backed that had rested its belly in the channel. And sometimes marks that did not fit any animal I had been taught to name: paired impressions, narrow and deep, leading toward the doorways but not away from them.

I would not report these at first.

Not because I feared being mocked. The people of the Low Bright would not mock a thing merely because they had no explanation for it. They would become quiet, which would be worse. They would send seam listeners. They would bring burrowers. They would place new signs. They would turn a private uncertainty into work for the whole basin.

And perhaps the basin would not need that work yet.

So I would keep counting.

In time, I would begin to notice that the bowls were not simply filled. They were arranged. One that had stood in a doorway for years would appear one morning three paces farther down the street. A cracked bowl would be replaced with another of the same size, though made from unfamiliar clay: pale gray, dense, smooth as a river pebble. Sometimes a bowl would contain a single object beneath the water—a white beetle shell, a loop of fiber, a small stone drilled through its center.

The drilled stones would be the troubling part.

They would resemble the beads woven into household memory ropes, but none would match a known family’s pattern. They would have no dye, no copper, no mark of ownership. Just a hole, cleanly bored, as if some patient hand had known that a cord would eventually pass through.

I think I would begin threading them.

Not openly. I would make a separate rope from scraps: black imported fiber, glider membrane, softened hide, a little old cord taken from a broken sledge harness. I would add each pale bead in the order I found it. Between them I would knot the location of each bowl, the direction it had moved, the day’s light pattern, the behavior of the burrower that accompanied me.

Because by then I would have one.

Not a household animal exactly. A burrower from the pens would have begun waiting near the outer canal when I went out. It would be old, marked but not claimed, with a scarred nose and a coat faded nearly white at the shoulders. It would not allow anyone to lead it there. It would simply follow me, then stop at certain doorways and refuse to proceed.

At first I would think it feared something.

Then I would realize it was listening.

Its feet, pressed into the old stone, could feel movements I could not. Sometimes it would stand perfectly still for an hour. Once, during a distant tremor, it would kneel beside a sealed floor and put its ear against the ground. Not its muzzle: its ear. Its huge, folded, almost translucent ear.

Below us, faintly, something would tap.

Not randomly.

Three taps. A pause. Two taps. Another pause. Three.

I would know enough by then not to answer loudly. I would set my palm flat to the stone. I would wait for the tremor to leave the floor. Then I would take the bone stylus from my belt and tap back, as gently as I could.

Three. Pause. Two. Pause. Three.

Nothing would happen.

The burrower would rise. Somewhere far above, in a fold of ceiling too dark to see, gliders would rearrange themselves with a whisper of membranes.

And then, perhaps, water would begin to gather along the seam of the sealed floor.

Not pouring. Not breaking through. Just a dark thread appearing where there had been dry dust for generations.

That is when I would understand that I was not really a bowl-counter.

I would become a keeper of unanswered exchanges.

The city has cistern keepers and seam listeners. It has light readers, silt counters, fungus workers, rope families. It may not have a name for someone whose task is to maintain a conversation with whatever lies in the unowned portions of the basin. Or perhaps it has an old name, worn smooth through disuse.

I would not call myself an explorer. Exploration sounds too much like arriving somewhere first.

I would be a returner.

My work would be to go back.

To revisit the altered signs without claiming to understand who changed them. To leave fungus cakes at the thresholds where tracks disappear. To replace a broken clay disc with another, then note whether it has been turned face down. To carry clean water to places where clean water already appears, not as tribute, but as a way of saying: we recognize the practice.

I would learn the etiquette of a neighbor who may be a people, an animal, a current of cold air, an old household surviving below the old household, or the basin itself working through cracks and vessels.

The pale cord in the sign loops would become part of my rope. The unfamiliar drilled beads would accumulate. Eventually, someone in the city would see it and ask which family I belonged to.

I would have no good answer.

Perhaps that would be the answer.

I would belong to the routes that cannot be relied upon, to the streets that emerge after floods, to the rooms that are remembered only because someone noticed a change in reflected light. I would belong to the edge between the tended city and the place where the city leaves markers but makes no claims.

And after enough years, children would know me.

They would see the old burrower and me leaving by the canal road. They would ask whether I had found the other people. I would tell them what adults tell children in the Low Bright: sound travels strangely; water finds its own level.

But I would say it with enough care that they would know I was not dismissing them.

Then I would give them each a pale drilled bead, if I had one to spare, and teach them the first rule of the work:

When something answers from the dark, do not rush toward it.

First notice what has changed around you.
