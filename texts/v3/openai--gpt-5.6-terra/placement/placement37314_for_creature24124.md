I would not arrive as an explorer.

Explorers bring too much weather with them: lamps, questions, breath that rises and falls too quickly. They want a horizon to prove they have crossed something. They strike stones to hear whether they are hollow. They name a place before they have learned what it permits.

I think I would arrive first as a mistake.

A warm thing from above, wrapped in fabric and carrying water in a sealed skin. The water would be the first offense. Not because I drank it, perhaps, but because it would move when moved. It would answer gravity with a soft internal sound. In that dry country, where every body has learned not to slosh, drip, swell, rot, or evaporate, I would carry a little impossible weather against my hip.

The Stillfolk would know I was there long before I saw them.

Not from my light. I would learn quickly that light is a kind of shouting.

Not from my footsteps, either, though they would be clumsy enough. They would know from the small repeated tremor of my blood, perhaps. From my breathing. From the anxious adjustments of leather, metal, and bone I did not know I was making. A person accustomed to stone would hear in me a whole surface world trying to continue: rain-memory, leaf-memory, the perpetual impatience of things that grow and decay.

At first I might think the underplain empty.

Then I would sit down.

That would be the beginning of learning.

I would sit beside one of the long mineral shelves, where blue light seeped from a seam so thin it looked like a vein beneath skin. I would turn off my lamp, perhaps because its battery had failed, perhaps because I had finally understood that its circle of illumination had been concealing more than it revealed.

In the dark, I would put my palm on the ground.

And the world would be there.

Not voices, not exactly. Nothing so immediate. The faint traveling grammar of weight. A herd far away settling one body-length nearer a heat fissure. A small colony working in porous stone. The slow scrape of an old grazer polishing its plated jaw against a ridge. Somewhere overhead, so distant it could have been a change in my own pulse, a predator releasing one hooked limb and placing it elsewhere.

I would mistake these things for silence until I learned the difference.

Perhaps the Stillfolk would find me there: seated badly, legs crossed in the surface manner, one hand on the floor, the other curled around my extinguished lamp. They would not approach all at once. One would stand where the blue seam gave enough outline to show a plated cheekbone and an eye reflecting nothing. Another would remain behind me. A third, unseen, would tap once against stone.

Food.

Or warning.

Or perhaps simply: *There is something here.*

I would answer wrongly.

This seems important. I would begin with wrong answers. Too fast, too loud, too full of intention. I would tap back in the rhythm of my own name, imagining that a name was a gift. They would receive it as a pattern with no use: a nervous little animal circling itself.

Over time—if they did not leave me behind at the first sign of my open-handedness—I think I would become less human in the way I had understood humanity.

Not by becoming one of them. That would be too clean. My skin would remain soft too long. I would continue needing water, carrying it secretly at first, then openly, in vessels they found troubling. My eyes would always be poor at old tracks and too easily startled by motion. I would always hear an echo as an echo, rather than wondering who had heard it before it returned.

But I might become useful in another way.

I would become a keeper of changes that happen too quickly.

The Stillfolk know patience. They know persistence. They know the difference between a crack that has existed for three lifetimes and one that opened during the last deep settling. But I would carry surface habits in my body: an awareness of suddenness, perhaps. I would notice the brief flare in a blue mineral seam. The nearly imperceptible cooling of an oven. The moment a mineral foam colony ceased making its ordinary pattern and began arranging itself into a spiral.

I would be alarmed by things they had learned not to fear, and that alarm might occasionally be correct.

They might give me no name, but a function.

The one who watches the quick.

The one whose hand cannot be trusted on stone, but whose eyes catch the wrongness in a moving shadow.

I would live near an abandoned settlement, I think.

Not inside it. No Stillfolk would choose the central shaft if it had once answered. But near enough that its old structures remained visible in the blue-black distance: low domes with their plates still fitted tightly together; an empty bridge spanning a fault; horizontal cairns leading faithfully into a darkness no one now entered.

There would be a figure seated against one wall.

At first I would ask whether it was dead.

No one would answer.

Eventually I would stop asking.

I would bring it offerings, though not in the old manner. Ground shell, yes. Copper scraps. Once, perhaps, the smooth cap from the water vessel I had carried from the surface. A useless object in that place, bright and round and made for a world of liquid abundance. I would set it at the figure’s feet and wonder whether the dark knew what it had been.

Years would pass strangely there. I would not know how many. My own body would begin to show the underplain’s influence—not fully, not gracefully. Dry polygonal patches at the wrists. Mineral brightness at old cuts. A stiffening in the heels. Copper-dark crust collecting where my blood dried. I would become less capable of returning upward, not because the path closed, but because the world above would begin to seem extravagantly unstable.

Wind would horrify me.

Rain would be unbearable.

Trees, with their endless small motions, would look like crowds panicking.

I would remember sunlight, but it would become difficult to believe in. I would remember lying beside rivers, and would feel toward that memory what the Stillfolk felt toward water: reverence mixed with disbelief, an old theory about a substance that could hold the sky.

And then, perhaps, I would understand why no one locks a door.

A locked door assumes that the self who returns will be the self who left. It assumes the house can wait unchanged. It assumes the danger is outside.

In the underplain, one learns that the danger may be in the answering.

I think, finally, I would become a listener stationed beside an old fault.

Not a priest. Not a guardian. The Stillfolk do not need either.

I would be the person who sits with one palm against the stone and the other over a small sealed vessel of water, feeling its impossible little shifts. I would keep the vessel not to drink from it, but to remember that some worlds make noise without meaning harm.

When the deep ground altered, I would tap the warning slowly.

Not because I believed I could prevent what was below from waking.

Only because somewhere sideways in the dark, children would be lying beneath ledges, learning stillness. Herds would be following their mineral bands. An oven keeper would have both hands against a warm sealed dome. Someone would be placing a dead elder against a wall, arranging their tools nearby for the long conversion into place.

I would tap once for food.

Twice for danger.

Three times for absence.

And then, after a long while, I would rest my whole body against the floor and listen for whether the stone had begun, in its vast patience, to listen back.
