I would find myself there only after failing to find somewhere else.

Not as an explorer, and not as a pilgrim exactly. I would come up the mountain carrying some small thing that had become too heavy precisely because it was small: a letter never sent, perhaps; a name I had stopped being able to say; a little brass key whose door was long gone. I would insist, at first, that I had come for shelter from weather. Then I would find the seam beneath the stone and understand that weather had only brought me to the threshold.

Inside, I would not be one of the old visitors, not immediately. I would be too hurried. I would touch the walls to prove they were real. I would want to know whether the pool had a bottom, whether the shelter had been made by hands, whether the marks in ochre were a language. I would listen too hard for breathing.

And in listening, I would become something the hollow could use.

Not its keeper. The hollow has no need of keeping.

Not its worshipper. It does not want belief.

I think I would become its **second silence**.

The first silence is the cave’s own: mineral, wet, patient, older than the distinction between waiting and existing. It lies in the pool before the drop, in the dark behind the visible dark, in the little chambers inside cracks where no human ear could fit.

The second silence would be mine: the silence made by a person who has finally stopped asking their unfinished thing to resolve.

At first I might sleep in the hide shelter. The old objects would be there with me: the bone needle, the braided fiber, the red-stained stone. I would not touch them. Or I would touch them once, very lightly, and feel—not a shock, not a vision—but a density in them. The needle would contain every hand that had nearly used it again. The fiber would remember the shape of a knot that was never tied. The ochre would be the color of something still trying to enter the world.

Over time, my own belongings would begin to change in that way.

The letter would soften at its folds but never mildew. The brass key would grow dark with salt and stay warm even when held away from the floor. My bootprints in the clay would remain sharp for a while, then become less like tracks and more like shallow bowls. Water would gather in them without overflowing.

I would learn not to bring a lamp. I would learn that the dark is sufficient illumination if I do not insist that it show me things all at once.

Sometimes I would sit beside the central pool and see the figure below the surface. It would appear child-sized, as it sometimes does, folded inward as though trying to remain smaller than the hollow. But I would gradually understand that it was not looking up at me. It was looking through me, toward whatever I had not yet surrendered.

I would not call it by a name.

Instead, I would place a small pale pebble in the clay above the water. Not on the old line—never on the old line, because one does not complete another person’s sentence—but beside it. Then I would wait until the cave decided whether the pebble belonged.

If it remained, I would leave.

If it sank slowly into the clay over the course of an hour, I would know that some part of my burden had been accepted: not removed, not forgiven, not healed, but distributed. Given a second place to exist.

After enough visits, perhaps I would cease to be entirely human in the ordinary outside sense. On the mountain paths, people might pass me without remembering my face. I would smell faintly of wet iron. My pockets would collect white stones. In sleep, my dreams would arrive one drop at a time, separated by long, untroubled dark.

And when I returned to the hollow, the thing below the pool would recognize me not as a visitor but as one of its unfinished forms.

A handprint in soft mineral.

A warmth held close to the floor.

A name spoken once, then broken gently into water.

I would be the part of the creature that can leave the cave for a little while and come back carrying what others can no longer carry alone.
