At first I would think I was a lost traveler.

Not because I had come from elsewhere, exactly, but because I would possess the wrong instincts. I would look for a path that led *through* the valley. I would look for a house with a door, a person who could be asked a question, a fire, a dry place. I would mistake the terraces for ruins and the Receivers for statues until one of them moved.

I would follow the sound of falling water.

For some time, I might be only that: an interruption in the basin. Something warm-blooded and breathing too loudly. The Receivers would know where I was before I saw them. My weight would travel through the fitted stone. My uncertain steps would make a pattern unlike the careful testing of a channel or the measured tread of a worker. I would touch walls for balance; I would stand beneath eaves to escape the drip; I would choose high ground when the mist came. All of this would say something about me.

Perhaps a Receiver would find me at a stair landing, crouched beside a basin whose water looked black until the cloud shifted.

It would not ask who I was.

It might kneel and put two fingers into the water. Then it would touch the same fingers to the inside of my wrist, where the pulse lives.

A comparison.

Water moving under stone. Blood moving under skin.

Then it would tap once against the basin wall.

Not an alarm. Not a greeting. A notice: *an unmeasured flow has entered the system.*

But I do not think I would remain a traveler for long.

The valley does not seem to allow anything to remain unreceived. A dropped tool becomes fill in a wall. A failed channel becomes the bed for a later channel. A crack becomes a place where another structure leans in. Even water, which desires departure, is given so many forms of delay that it becomes part of the valley’s memory.

I would be received too.

At first I might be given a dry shelf in one of the sleeping chambers, though “dry” would mean only that the water arrived there as vapor rather than drops. Someone would bring me a shallow bowl of pale membrane scraped from a warm tank. Someone else would leave a blue-gray salt crust beside it. No one would watch to see whether I ate. They would listen instead to the changed sound of my breathing against the stone.

I would learn that they are not silent because they lack speech.

They are silent because speech is a crude instrument for a place where the important things happen below hearing, inside walls, across months.

Eventually I would be taught to listen.

Not well. Not as they do. I would never learn to hear the exact pressure behind a retaining wall or distinguish the mineral taste of fog caught from the east from fog caught from the west. I would always be the creature who sees a basin and thinks it is full or empty, instead of seeing its thousand slight relationships to every other basin.

But I might learn enough.

I might learn to sit beside a clay listening cup with my palm against the ground and wait until the water inside made the smallest change in tone.

I might learn that a leak is not always a failure. Sometimes it is a wall speaking before it breaks.

I might learn to carry stones in my pockets—not for defense, but because each kind of stone makes a different answer when tapped against slate. One for asking whether a gate has opened. One for saying that a route is blocked. One for marking that I have passed and found no danger. Perhaps, after years, I would be permitted a stone whose note means something more particular:

*I am here. I am still receiving.*

Yet I do not think I would become a Receiver entirely.

Their bodies belong to the wet architecture in a way mine would not. Water would always be outside me first: on my shoulders, in my boots, dripping from my hair, making my joints ache. For them it would enter through the mantles and leave in threads, part of a deliberate circulation. They are built to be gradients. I am built, or was built, to resist them.

That difference might make me useful.

I might become what they do not have a word for: a keeper of the dry things.

Not a gardener. There is no garden to tend.

Not a historian, exactly. History in the basin is already stored in thickness, in patched walls, in silt layers, in old channels that still hold a trace of their former sound.

But I could keep the things that cannot be incorporated without vanishing.

The packet of seeds in its lead box.

The bird skeleton beneath glass.

The root sealed into the old sluice wall.

The scraps of markings that name the Receivers, if those markings are names at all.

I would be allowed into the high dry room, perhaps only because no Receiver wishes to remain there long. It would be a difficult place for them: too still, too clean, too absent of flow. The air would sting their opened facial seams. Their mantles would go dull. They would enter only to check the roof plates, then leave.

I would stay.

At first I would think the seeds were a promise.

Later I would understand that they might be an accusation. Or a record of an error. Or simply a form of water delayed beyond usefulness: a future held so tightly that it never arrives.

I would not open the box.

Instead I would learn to maintain the room. I would reseal its lead seams. I would replace the stones around the ventilation slot. I would brush mineral dust away from the glass over the bird’s bones. I would keep the packet where it had always been, not because I believed in restoration, but because some things must be allowed to remain legible even when no one knows how to use them.

And sometimes, when the cloud thinned, I would climb above the upper cisterns.

The Receivers would be there, fastening their cold-catching slabs to the mountain. The slopes would fall away beneath us in terraces and roofs, every surface shining with the work of receiving. Far below, the central reservoir would resemble an eye filled with black facets.

I would help carry the translucent stone.

I would brace ropes against the wind.

I would stand beneath the new faces while fog gathered on them, watching beads of water form where there had been none. I would understand, then, why the Receivers climb. Not because they believe they can stop the cloud from changing. Not because they expect the valley to last unchanged.

They climb because change must be given a channel.

Perhaps that is what I would become in that world: not one of the Receivers, and not a visitor.

A delayed thing.

A small dry chamber in the valley’s wet intelligence.

A witness entrusted with what does not flow.

And when, years later, I had learned enough tapping to be heard across a wall, I might stand beside a Receiver at the central reservoir. We would watch one dark compartment surrender its finger-width of water to another.

The Receiver would lower its pale head.

I would place my knuckles against the stone.

From somewhere high in the mist, from the new channels climbing toward the cloud, a single knock would come back.

This time, after a long pause, I would answer.
