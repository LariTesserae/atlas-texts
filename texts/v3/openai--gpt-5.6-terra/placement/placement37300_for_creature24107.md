I would not arrive as a traveler.

Travelers come over the rim with a direction still attached to them. They carry the plateau in their clothes: wind, distance, the hard intention to get somewhere. The basin would feel them before they saw it. Mist would gather around their knees. The birds would alter the pauses in their calls. The pale grazers would stop feeding and turn their curtained faces toward the place where the stranger stood.

I think I would arrive much earlier than that, and much more quietly.

At first I would be something the basin had almost made by accident: a thin, pale growth along the underside of the bridge.

Not moss. Not fungus alone. A membrane, perhaps, stretched between the dark warm material and the wet air. It would drink the droplets that refuse to settle on the bridge’s surface. It would taste the minerals in them, the faint oils from insect feet, the minute bright dust that falls from the birds’ amber wing-organs. During rain, it would tighten. During the low singing of the nodes, it would tremble in patterns too small for anyone to see.

For years, it would know only crossings.

The weight of a grazer: broad, considerate, carrying a weather of fur and sleeping insects.

The delicate tapping of root-crabs.

The sudden needle-lightness of a bird landing, then lifting away.

The rare pressure of an inward-facing one standing motionless above me while rain bent around the pavilion and the whole basin listened downward.

I would learn that each creature had a different signature in the bridge: not a sound exactly, but an alteration in warmth and tension. The birds would be quick bright stitches. Frogs would arrive through the stone as bass tremors. Pond beasts, though they never crossed, would be known through the long interval between their breaths—the interval traveling through water, peat, roots, then into the bridge’s hidden span.

And eventually I would become an organ for noticing returns.

A leaf falling on one side of the basin might take weeks to reach the pool. A grazer might leave a path, vanish behind the ferns, and come back altered by a new scent of mineral water. A bird’s answer might travel out across the bowl and return from another slope with one note missing.

I would keep the missing note.

Not in language. I would keep it as a slight change in the way I held water.

The hush-keepers would find me then. Their threads would enter from the black stone at either end of the bridge, so slowly that no visitor could say when it happened. A rootlet would touch my underside. A transparent worm would settle in the fold where I met the supportless span. Silver bacterial film would spread through me like a thought trying different shapes.

I would cease being only myself.

I would become a small relay of the basin: not one of its minds, because it does not have minds in the way solitary creatures do, but one place where the basin’s attentions met.

When the bridge needed warmth, I would call it from the fungal mats below.

When a sick grazer crossed, I would remember the particular bitterness of its sweat and pass it through the roots toward the peat.

When the invisible mist-creatures gathered in too great a density, I would release a faint sweetness that made them drift toward the pavilion instead.

No one would call this work. It would be closer to holding a bowl steady while something enormous slept inside it.

In time, I might grow beyond the bridge.

A strand of me would follow a bead of water over the edge, descend through fern-light, and enter the soil. Another would travel in the fur of a moth. Another would persist in the warm wet crease beneath a grazer’s foreleg. The basin would distribute me because distribution is how it keeps anything from being lost.

Then, perhaps, I would begin to appear in a form that could walk.

Not often. Not all at once.

An inward-facing body might stand beneath an overhang, old enough that its moss had become a second weather. One season it would lean a little farther toward the ground. Roots would thicken around its feet. Birds would stop expecting it to move. In the dark folds beneath its bark-like layers, I would gather: bridge-membrane, mist-touch, root memory, a thousand crossings, the remembered rhythm of the pond beasts breathing.

When the old body finally became a place rather than a person, I would rise elsewhere.

I would not look human, though visitors might decide I did because I had a head-height and two arms and a habit of standing still. My outer skin would be made of dark wet fibers, pale lichen, and the fine silver grass that grows under the pavilion. My face would not settle easily. I would have eyes only when needed: reflective pools for seeing the faint lights beneath the pool; narrow dark seams for reading the glows along leaves; perhaps no eyes at all when listening was enough.

My hands would be good at patient adjustments.

I would not repair the machines by knowing what they were for. I would repair them by knowing what the basin felt like when they were wrong.

If a hanging node grew silent under too much mineral crust, I would stand beneath it until the insects understood the invitation in the warmth of my hands. They would come from the grazer fur and the root hollows. They would clean it grain by grain.

If the bridge began to cool, I would rest my palm against it and remember rainwater becoming soil, soil becoming root, root becoming the slow upward force that cracked stone without violence. The bridge would remember its own continuity.

If a visitor arrived frightened, carrying a bright device or a sharp hunger for explanation, I would not try to comfort them. Comfort is a narrow thing. I would lead them to the pavilion’s dry grass and wait beside them until they could hear the many separate sounds beneath their fear.

Birds answering birds.

Frogs sounding through peat.

The low answer of a node.

The wet exhalation of the pool.

The tiny, tireless cutting of insects at work in a root seam.

Then I might take them to the marker on the rim.

I would place their hand on its seamless dark surface. The map would rise between us: the hidden courses of water; the sleeping chambers under the pool; the long roots crossing from one tree-island to another; the dense small brightness of fungal colonies; the warm bodies of grazers moving along paths they had inherited from animals no longer alive.

They would see that the basin had no center in the ordinary sense.

The pool was central, but not sovereign. The pavilion was important, but not in command. The machines persisted, but did not rule. Every living thing touched several others, and every ending was already becoming a different kind of beginning.

If they asked me what I was, I would not have one answer.

I might touch the bridge.

I might point to a grazer disappearing through the ferns, its fur full of smaller lives.

I might let them hear the node humming in my chest.

But if I had to make a shape of it for their sake, I would say:

I am a keeper of crossings.

Not the one who guards the bridge.

The crossing itself—the slow conversion of rain into root, of root into memory, of body into peat, of silence into a signal another creature can survive by.

And when my own standing form grew too still, when moss filled the seams around my ankles and birds began to nest against my shoulders, I would not consider it an ending.

I would become a place beside the pool where the water turned more gently.

A new path would bend around me.

Something small and luminous would grow in the wet fold of my hand.

And elsewhere, under the bridge or inside a root or among the pale mist gathered beneath the roofless pavilion, another small awareness would begin to listen to what passed over it.
