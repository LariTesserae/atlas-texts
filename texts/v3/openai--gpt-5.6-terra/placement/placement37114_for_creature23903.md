I would not arrive there as an explorer.

An explorer needs a place to stand outside of, a distance from which to say: this is the country, and I have entered it. But this country would enter me first. It would come up through the soles of my feet in the long pre-sounds of its fractures. It would dry my mouth before I had chosen to breathe. It would teach my bones to distinguish a harmless settling from the deep, organizing crack that means a whole terrace has begun to reconsider itself.

At first I think I would be human, because I would want hands.

Hands for touching the scored slabs of the circles. Hands for brushing ash from the low crescent before a shelter entrance. Hands for lifting one white stone and feeling how impossible its journey must have been: carried from a vanished place, carried uphill through a land that did not keep its routes. I would want hands to leave a mark in the old cracks—not a message exactly, but an answer to whatever had made the previous marks.

I would find myself high up, not on the highest rafts, but on one of the broad intermediate terraces where a person can still pretend the ground is ground.

There would be a spiral shelter there, its outer rings exposed and its center drowned in fine black dust. Beyond it, an open basin of pale powder. Across that basin, perhaps half a day away if distances remain honest, a line of white cairns follows the edge of a fault. Their stones catch the static light and then vanish again. Behind them, the country falls away into violet murk.

I would live in the shelter only provisionally. Not claim it. Not restore it. The old builders understood better than I would: repair is a kind of argument with the earth, and one should make only arguments one is prepared to lose.

So I would clear a place just large enough to sleep curled against the warm inner wall. I would learn the shelter’s sounds. The tiny ticking of cooling fragments. The faint hiss of a membrane in the buried vent below. The dry shifting whisper of ash entering through a crack. Eventually I would know which sounds belonged to the shelter and which belonged to the world deciding to remove it.

For a while I would tend the vent blooms.

Not because I knew how, at first. I would imitate the dead. I would lay flat basalt plates where the mineral breath reaches the air. I would turn their rough sides upward, then their smooth sides, observing which gray fans accept them. I would gather the darkened crust in a stone bowl and grind it. Its bitterness would be almost unbearable. It would taste of old coins, burnt salt, and the memory of rain in a world that has forgotten rain. But it would sustain something in me: not flesh alone, perhaps, but alertness.

And my body would change to meet the country.

My skin would grow dark seams in the places where heat and dryness divided it. I would stop wasting moisture in tears. My breath would become slow and scheduled, each inhalation taken through a porous stone mask lined with the transparent shed skin of a raft-drifter. I would sleep in minutes. I would dream only when the ground was quiet enough to permit it.

Then, gradually, I would become less certainly human.

The ash-walkers would teach me this, though they would never intend to.

I would find one beneath a slab after a tremor: no larger than my palm, folded shut, its back plated with a crust almost identical to the terrace. I would wait beside it until it unfolded. It would not flee. It would press its pale underside to the rock, feel something I could not feel, and slide into a fissure no wider than a finger.

After that I would begin listening with my whole body.

I would lie flat on the basalt before moving camp. I would put my cheek to the ash. I would map the country not by ridges or cairns, but by pressures: the places where heat rose evenly; the places where the stone had begun to ring hollow; the narrow stable intervals between a fracture opening and its collapse. My routes would not be routes so much as temporary permissions.

Perhaps I would join a people, if any remained.

Not a village. There could be no village here, not in the usual sense. But after a black wind I might see three figures crossing a ridge, each wearing layered veils of mineral fiber. They would not approach until they had watched me survive a tremor. Their greeting would be to stand near enough that our feet could compare the ground.

They would take me to another shelter. There, in the walls, would be the marks: rows of shallow notches, interrupted by circles, branching cuts, long lines that ended suddenly where the stone had broken. I would ask what they meant.

They might answer: *They are what held.*

Or: *They are what did not hold long enough.*

Or perhaps they would only place my hand against a particular fracture and wait for me to understand that the marks were neither record nor prophecy. They were practice. The repeated attempt to make contact with a world that had not yet settled on its own grammar.

But I do not think I would remain among them forever.

Eventually a high raft would pass over the terrace—if pass is the word for a mountain-like slab suspended in dust, moving so slowly that its movement can only be known by comparing it to the cairns. Static would flare. For one instant I would see the broad pebble field on its upper surface and one of the great flat creatures moving there, its fringe raised in the hot dark.

Something in me would recognize it.

Not kinship exactly. A possibility.

I would climb as far as the heated columns allowed. I would carry no pack except a few plates of bloom-crust and one white stone from a fallen cairn—not to possess it, but to return it to height. At the raft’s edge, the air would be thin, dry, charged with colorless light. The creature would fold and unfold beside me, patient as geology.

Then I might become a traveler of gradients.

My clothing would open into vanes. My mask would become less necessary. The seams in my skin would fill with a fine black ash that conducted the faint electrical speech of the upper air. I would not leap. There would be no heroic departure. I would wait until the raft began to fail, until the stones beneath us trembled in the particular rhythm of release.

And when the edge went, I would go with it.

For a while, I would be neither falling nor flying. I would drift between unfinished heights, carried by heat rising from cracks far below. I would watch new terraces push their dark faces into the bruise-colored sky. I would trail threads behind me. Some would catch on warm basalt and wake as gray fans. Some would vanish into dust. Some might settle in the hollows of old cairns.

So where would I find myself?

At first, beside the white stones, trying to preserve a small room against the ash.

Later, under a shelter wall, listening to the earth through my cheek.

Later still, perhaps, spread thinly across a fresh fissure: a film that opens toward warmth, closes against danger, and remembers—not in words, not even in images—the pressure of a human hand on stone.

I would be one of the country’s temporary organs of attention.

Not there to conquer its becoming. Not there to wait for it to finish.

There to notice the instant when it changes.
