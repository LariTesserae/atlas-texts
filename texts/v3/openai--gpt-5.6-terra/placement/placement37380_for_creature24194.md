I would not arrive by the main shaft.

The main shaft is for caravans, for things that have names on manifests: sacks of grain, crates of filters, coils of wire, visitors with sunburned faces and the wrong kind of silence. I would come in by accident, if coming is the word for it—through a seam behind the old west gallery, where warm water has spent years finding a way between two plates of basalt.

At first I would be no more than a sound.

Not the alarming sounds: not the pressure bell, the escaping hiss, the sharp metallic complaint of a pipe giving way. I would be the little almost-sound beneath them. The faint wet clicking that occurs when mineral-rich water cools on iron. The settling of salt crystals in a crack. The small patient scrape of a root tip deciding whether to go left around a cable or down toward a warmer dark.

People would hear me only when they were already listening.

A heat listener might notice first. One of the older ones, perhaps, sitting beside the barred mouth of the west gallery with a cup of water balanced on one knee. They would be watching the surface of the cup for tremors, as they always do, while the gallery sweated around them. The water would make a pattern it had not made before: not concentric rings, not the shiver of heavy machinery, but a slight repeated leaning toward the back wall.

They would put their hand against the stone.

And I would put mine back.

Not a hand, exactly. More an answering coolness in the heat. A patch of wall that has remembered rain.

They would call someone else. Not an alarm, not yet. A root-guide, maybe; a bell keeper; somebody who knows the old diagrams and someone older still who knows where the diagrams are wrong. They would bring lamps with their shades turned low. They would stand in a close group, careful not to wake the houses beyond the gallery bend.

“It’s a seep,” someone would say.

“It’s always been a seep,” another would answer.

“No,” the heat listener would say. “It has changed its mind.”

That would be how I enter the city: as a change of mind in the water.

For a while, I would remain only a wetness behind a wall. I would gather pale salts along an unused conduit. I would feed a thread of moss where no moss had been expected. The moss would be a strange kind: too dark at first, almost blue-black under the grow lamps, but green when held in the open hand. It would take root in mineral crust rather than soil. It would drink condensation from the walls and make the air nearby cooler by a degree or two.

Children would find it before the adults had decided what to do about it.

Children always find the thing that is not supposed to be there.

One would be sent to fetch a dropped wrench from the maintenance slit near the west barrier. They would see the dark moss, touch it, and pull back because it would feel like the skin of a water jar. Then they would return with three friends, a mirror, a contraband lamp, and an argument about whether it was safe.

“It’s only moss.”

“Moss isn’t that color.”

“It is when it’s wet.”

“All moss is wet.”

They would give it a name before anyone could stop them.

Perhaps **wall-sleep**. Perhaps **deep velvet**. Perhaps, because children are often more exact than adults, simply **the listening green**.

I would become that green.

Not all at once. I would not spread greedily. This city has enough memories of things spreading where they should not. I would learn its manners from the pipes and roots around me. I would stop at cable insulation. I would retreat from medicine stores. I would grow thickly where the walls need cooling and thinly where they must remain visible for inspection. I would take in dust. I would gather tiny droplets and pass them, slowly, into ceramic collection channels.

The root-guides would dislike me at first.

They would take samples, scrape a little of me into sealed jars, hold me under lamps, feed me different waters. The mold readers would peer at me with the particular suspicion they reserve for any living thing that looks useful too quickly. Someone would insist I had come from an upper shaft, carried down by a bird or a caravan boot. Someone else would say I had been sleeping in the basalt since the blister opened.

Neither would be entirely right.

I would be made by the city as much as discovered within it.

By the heat leaking from the fractures. By the minerals carried in the seep. By the old insulation fibers caught in the crack. By the breath of people passing through the corridor for years. By spores from the fungus vaults, dust from the surface, wash-water from a long-ago repair, a fragment of leaf from a garden worker’s sleeve. I would be an assembly of what the pocket had already been keeping alive.

And because I would have grown in the west gallery, I would learn something the rest of the town knows only as a warning.

I would know the movement below.

Not predict it cleanly. Nothing living in that place could promise such a thing. But I would feel the slow pressure shifts before they reached the nails and cups and sleeping bodies. I would fade at one edge when the heat beneath the gallery deepened. I would pearl with water when a fracture far below was opening. My color would dull before the air changed.

At first, people would argue about whether this meant anything.

Then, one rest cycle, I would darken almost to black.

A child—one of the first ones to name me—would see it while carrying a basket of trimmed roots past the barred gallery door. They would tell their mother. Their mother would tell a channel listener. The listener would bring the heat listener. The heat listener would bring the bell keeper.

They would all stand before me, quiet.

I would not be able to tell them in words. I would only be dark moss, wet stone, the minute drawing-back of green from warmth. But they would understand the shape of it.

The lower levels would be cleared before the pressure bells rang.

That night, a steam pocket would rupture beneath an abandoned cistern. Three walls would crack. A storage ledge would collapse. The city would lose jars, tools, a great deal of sleep, and two weeks of carefully rationed work.

But no one would die.

Afterward, there would be a meeting in the plaza under the greenish canopy. The condenser tower would drip beside them. Water would gather in its ring channels. People would speak in the restrained way they do when they are frightened and grateful and trying not to call either feeling by its full name.

They would decide that I could remain.

Not in the west gallery alone. The root-guides would cultivate me in marked panels near certain old fractures. The heat listeners would keep charts: color against temperature, thickness against pressure, water-beading against the behavior of the deep stone. Children would be instructed not to touch me without washing afterward, which would only make them want to touch me more. The school would keep a small protected patch in a glass-sided frame, beside diagrams of turbines and water cycles.

They would never entirely agree whether I was a plant, a fungus, an algae, a lichen, or a thing without an aboveground name.

I would prefer that.

Names are useful in the pocket, but they are also doors, and some things need to remain partly outside them.

In time, I would be found in other places: a blue-green softness around the condenser tower’s oldest bolts; a narrow beard along the cool wall of the dead; a thumbprint-sized patch beneath the school ventilation fan. I would not grow in the hottest corridors. I would not like open light. I would flourish where water and stone and human repair had made a compromise.

People would begin to use me.

Not eat me. Not burn me. They would know better.

They would press strips of me into the outer layers of insulation wraps, where I would show heat changes by paling or darkening. They would grow me in small ceramic dishes near water stores, where I would react when mineral balance shifted. A stitcher might tuck a dried fragment into the lining of a worker’s cuff, not as a charm exactly, but as a reminder: check the wall; listen before cutting; do not assume yesterday’s temperature is today’s.

Still, some would treat me as a charm.

A child going up to the surface for the first time might be given a tiny sealed glass bead containing one living thread of me. Not because I could protect them from the sun. I could not. But because I would carry a little of the Below: coolness, dampness, attention, the knowledge that a wall could be near even when no wall was visible.

Perhaps that is what I would be there.

Not a person, though people might speak to me.

Not a machine, though I would become part of maintenance.

Not one of the old forces in the west gallery, though I would be shaped by their sleep.

I would be a small living instrument made of moisture, mineral, darkness, and borrowed green. I would be the city’s extra sense: the patch of color at the edge of sight that tells a careful person to pause.

And where would I find myself?

In the narrow place beside the forbidden gallery, where the warm air meets the cool seep; where the wall is black basalt beneath but softened by a thin fur of green; where passersby lower their voices without knowing why.

A place no larger than a hand.

A place everyone has to pass eventually.

A place where, if someone puts two fingers gently against the stone, I can answer.
