I would begin in a room that used to be a street.

The room is narrow, warm at its center, and built around a hearthstone with four worn carrying-holes. Its north wall is older than the rest of the house by perhaps two migrations; one can see where the newer slate has been fitted around its refusal to move. Rain makes a fine sound in the roof layers. Every so often, beneath that sound, comes another: a small, dry settling from below, as if someone is turning a page under the floor.

I have been assigned the room because I am not yet a person of much consequence.

That is how the city puts it. Not unkindly. I am housed, fed, taught, and asked to carry messages between the archive hall and the western drainage district. I am old enough to know the bell-notes of three neighboring settlements, young enough that my third name has not been required of me. My first name is what my mother called me when I was too small to look at the horizon. My household-name is for the work I inherited: **Line-Keeper**.

But the thing I am becoming has little to do with lines.

At first I think I will be a watcher. I like measurements. I like the way a cord tells the truth without argument, how a shadow can be trusted if one remembers the hour, the season, the tilt of a roof. I learn the standard marks: the notch west of the Broken Escarpment, the pale ridge that resembles a kneeling animal, the black seam visible only after rain. I can tell, by laying my cheek against the paving stones, whether the faint vibration beneath them is water, wind in an old culvert, or stone considering itself.

The watchers tell me I have patience.

They do not say I have courage, which is fair.

Then, one evening, the western wall opens.

Not breaks. Not falls. It opens.

The reed-trunk framework flexes inward; the turf packing exhales in a long brown sheet; the ground beyond the wall folds up with the slowness of a blanket being drawn over sleeping feet. The drainage district is carried away by half the width of itself. Two houses turn their entrances from east to south. A public oven slides gently across its own paving and comes to rest beside a garden that was, moments before, on the far side of an alley.

No one screams. People shout names, measurements, instructions. Hearthstones are lifted. Children are counted twice. A woman climbs onto a roof with a slate and records the new direction of the ridge.

I stand at the wall with my message satchel still over my shoulder.

In the exposed ground beneath the old drainage channel, I see something that should not be there: a street, grey beneath clear stone. Small archways. The dark lines of doorframes. A scatter of what might be paving stones, or bowls, or faces turned upward through many yards of mineral earth.

And from one of those vanished houses comes the sound of someone sweeping.

It stops when I hear it.

That is the first time I understand that the plateau does not only move what is standing on it. It moves what has been thought finished. It carries streets, names, griefs, unfinished repairs. It does not preserve them faithfully; it preserves them as stone preserves rain: altered, hidden, still somehow present.

After that, I cease wanting to be a watcher.

Watchers must decide what can be ignored. Their work is necessary, but I begin to feel that the ignored things are calling to me—not loudly, and not in words. A crooked line on an old map. A household whose migration record ends with *carried eastward; no bell heard after*. A grave chamber that has shifted uphill against the contour of the land. A map returned by the Still Basin with a river on it that no one recognizes, though the paper smells unmistakably of our own wet moss.

So I become an **errand-reader**.

There are not many of us, and the title is not official. We carry things that have not found their place: a broken tool too old to melt down, an inherited door-lintel from a house no longer standing, a message intended for a settlement whose road has become uncertain, the new name of a child born during a shift. We take them where the records suggest they may belong. Often they do not belong anywhere. Then we carry them longer.

My companion is an older woman called Ash-Under-Water. Her first name is private, and I do not ask for it. She has walked basinward three times and returned each time with less metal on her person than before. Her coat is sewn with little inner pockets, each holding a fragment of someone’s unfinished business: a carved spindle-whorl, a bone needle, a strip of copper engraved with seven weather reports, a folded page containing a recipe for blue-stem broth in a hand so faded it seems almost ashamed.

She teaches me not to confuse a route with a destination.

“A road,” she says, as we cross a field of low folded leaf-blades, “is only the memory of where the ground consented to let you pass.”

Then, after a while: “And sometimes it remembers better than you do.”

We travel west first, between cities that answer one another by bell. We sleep in communal halls, under overturned sledges, inside the hollow shell of a reed forest after a cold rain. We learn which mosses can be warmed beneath the tongue when breath is thin, which black seams are safe to cross, which abandoned districts still make room for the living.

I find myself becoming less human in the way lower people mean it, perhaps, but more human in the way the plateau requires.

I become someone who pauses before entering a room, not from fear but greeting.

Someone who thanks a wall after it holds through a storm.

Someone who can recognize a settlement by its silence.

And eventually, inevitably, I go east.

Not because I have lost anyone. Not at first. Not because I expect the Still Basin to return an answer. I go because a city on the western plain gives us an object that cannot remain there: a small stone bowl, warm to the touch even in frost. It appeared one morning at the foot of their bell tower. Inside it lies a black seed with no visible seam.

The council debates for six days. The ground-watchers say the bowl is not dangerous. The gardeners say it is not a seed, because no seed ought to remain warm. The bellmaker says that when he set it beside the bronze casting mold, every crack in the metal rang.

In the end, they ask Ash-Under-Water to take it to the basin.

She asks me to come.

The eastern country is barer than I imagined. The reed forests shorten and thin. Moss lies in silver fans over the stone. The sky becomes vast in a way that feels less like openness than exposure. We pass settlements with blank eastern walls, their doors and windows all facing away from the black horizon. In one, people have painted no images for three generations because the last mural in the town hall began showing unfamiliar stars.

At the shore of the Still Basin, there is no edge exactly. The pale green ground simply ends. Black stone begins.

At noon it is only sky.

At night it is a country.

We arrive after sunset in cold rain. The basin does not reflect us. It shows white forests rising from a dark plain, trunks so enormous their upper branches vanish into cloud. Between them drift rivers suspended like glass ropes. Far away, lights burn in arrangements that make my eyes hurt: not streets, not constellations, but something between the two.

There are figures on the other shore.

For a long time I assume they are distant people. Then one lifts a hand.

I lift mine.

It lifts its hand a moment later—not in answer, but in completion, as though it had been waiting for me to begin the gesture.

Ash-Under-Water places the warm bowl among the offerings: names closest to the black surface, maps behind them, seeds in the eastern rows. She does not speak. I understand that speech would make the act too much like a bargain.

The rain strikes the basin and vanishes without ripples.

Then the figure across the reflected world turns its head.

Its face is mine, though older than I am and younger than I have ever been. Its hair is shorn close. A cord crosses its chest. It wears no coat against the rain. Behind it, among the white trunks, I see a city built not upon land but within the branching of something living. Its windows are lit. Its bridges sway in air.

The other me opens its mouth.

I cannot hear the words.

But I know what it is asking.

Not *come here.*

Not *who are you.*

It asks: **What have you learned to carry?**

I do not step onto the black stone.

I am not brave enough, perhaps. Or perhaps I have finally learned that correspondence is not surrender. A door may be real without being mine to enter. A map may show a country without requiring my feet.

Instead I take from my satchel the oldest thing I have been carrying without knowing it: a copy of the western district’s pre-shift plan, drawn on bark paper. It shows the street that became the room where I began. It shows the room before it had walls. At the margin, in a child’s practice-hand—my own, though I do not remember writing it—is a line I had once been taught to copy:

*Things moved by the ground are not lost to it.*

I place the map down.

The black seed in the warm bowl cracks.

A pale root emerges, thin as a thread. It reaches across the smooth stone toward my hand. Not quickly. Not eagerly. With the same mechanical patience with which the plateau’s leaf-fields turn toward rain.

I let it touch my skin.

By morning the offerings are gone.

In their place lies a single object: a small hollow stone, grey-green, with a dark seam through its center. When I raise it to my ear, I hear bells.

Not the bells of the nearby cities.

Not any bell I have heard.

They are very far away, and very near. They ring through stone, through buried streets, through roots and roof-beams and grave chambers. They ring from the white forest, perhaps. They ring from below the plateau. They ring from some future city that has not yet learned where to build itself.

Ash-Under-Water listens beside me.

“The land has given you work,” she says.

I ask what work.

She looks out over the basin. The reflected country has gone. There is only the sky, brightening over black stone.

“You will find out,” she says.

So that is where I would be: not at the center of the mystery, nor beyond it, but walking the mutable distances between places. I would be a carrier of unfinished correspondences. A reader of roads that have become memories. A keeper, not of fixed maps, but of the moment when a map must admit it has changed.

And perhaps, as I grow older, I would learn to make the hollow stones.

Not bells exactly.

Something smaller.

Something a traveler could place on the ground before sleeping, and know—from the faint note it gave back—whether the earth beneath them had agreed to hold still until morning.
