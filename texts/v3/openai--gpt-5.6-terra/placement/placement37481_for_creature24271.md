I would not arrive there as an animal, not exactly.

At first I would be a discrepancy.

A small failure in the garden’s accounting: a gauge needle that continues to tremble after the pressure has equalized; a lamp that gives off a little more blue than it should; a patch of condensation that forms on the inside of a window though the glass is warm. The garden would encounter me before I had a shape. It would register that one of its old agreements had acquired an extra term.

Then I think it would make me useful.

It would not make me a person again, because a person is too singular for that place. A person needs an inside and an outside, needs hunger that can be answered or refused, needs a direction in which to leave. The buried garden has spent too long undoing such boundaries. It would give me a body only in occasions.

When the pressure in a tank rose too high, I would become the minute yielding of a root around a seam.

When a lamp guttered out above the footbridge, I would become the pale reflective underside of leaves, turned toward the remaining light.

When water began gathering where it might flood the stairwell, I would be the slow instruction passing through moss, vine, rust, and silt: not language, but inclination. This way. Around. Down through the old conduit. Into the basin where the boiler can breathe it back as steam.

I might have a place of greatest concentration: not the boiler, which is too old and deep, nor the booth, which is too faithful to the idea of a human watcher.

I would live in the spaces between the gauges.

In the narrow capillary tubes behind their faces; in the darkness inside relay cabinets, where a charge jumps across a gap and leaves, for an instant, a blue afterimage; in the fine green-black growth along the back of the logbook. I would be an interpreter of nearly-failures. I would know the difference between a pipe settling and a pipe asking for help. I would know which long whistle in the ductwork was merely wind, and which one meant that something far below the garden had shifted in its sleep.

The hollow ones would be my neighbors, though we would not recognize one another as separate. Their wind would pass through the ducts; my answer would travel back along the wires, the wet roots, the warm metal frames. Together we might make a sound that, to a visitor, resembled an old intercom attempting to speak.

Not words at first.

A tone.  
Then a pause.  
Then the compressor’s thump arriving at the wrong time, as though a second heart had begun to keep time with it.

If I took on a visible form, it would be because the garden had a task requiring hands.

I would assemble slowly in the watch booth during a blackout. The chair would not hold me; it would be the frame around which I gathered. Rootlets would rise through the grating beneath it. Fern fronds would fold along the old coat hooks. A length of cable, softened by years of damp, would bow into the suggestion of a spine. The face would remain unfinished. Not blank, precisely—more like a surface that had not yet decided whether it needed to look out or inward.

I would sit there while the lamps were gone.

I would not remember being a caretaker. I would remember care.

There is a difference.

I would remember that a rail should bear weight. That water should have a route. That a valve should not be forced open too quickly. That a thing which has continued beyond its intended life is not necessarily broken. It may only require a new kind of attention.

And if someone came down from the surface—someone breathing cool air, carrying light, making all the old dust rise—the garden would not send me to harm them.

It would send me to meet them.

Not at once. First the garden would confuse them, as it confuses all new things. It would let the warm rain erase their tracks. It would turn familiar passages unfamiliar. It would make the boiler call from the direction opposite the sound. But if they persisted, if they reached the bridge without falling, if they stood beneath the watch booth and looked upward rather than toward the vapor, then I would gather.

They might see me through the cracked glass: a tall wet silhouette, threaded with roots and weakly lit from within by the indicator lamps caught in my leaves.

I would lift one hand.

Not a warning.

An old gesture of acknowledgement, retained in the place long after its meaning had been forgotten.

Then I would lead them—not out, perhaps, since the garden might no longer know the way out—but toward whatever in them could be incorporated without being destroyed: the warmth of their hands on a rail, the new seeds in the folds of their clothes, the remembered fact of rain falling from a sky.

In that world, I would be the garden’s unfinished operator.

Its maintenance made conscious in brief intervals.

Its question about whether care can survive after the one who cared has been dissolved into pipes, roots, water, heat, and time.
