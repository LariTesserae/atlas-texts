At first I think I would be one of the Held: a careful swimmer with a reed-fiber knife, a household to tend, a known route through the green avenues.

But I do not think I would belong in one of the warm, busy districts. I would not be among the people who speak most clearly in the air chamber, or who know every cousin’s tapping-name, or who can make a festival drum sound like thunder under the civic hall roof.

I would live at an edge.

Not at the surface—that brightness would frighten me too much—but in one of the former upper streets where the old town begins to slope down toward the dark water. There would be stone houses there, their second stories swallowed by reed roots. A staircase would descend from a collapsed doorway into a stand of red fronds, and beneath the stairs a narrow channel would lead toward the vent districts.

I would tend that channel.

Not as a guard, exactly. More as someone who listens for the town changing.

My house would be a small one, grown inside the folded base of an enormous leaf. The walls would be thin enough that I could see the shadows of fish passing outside: carp with their blunt mouths, threadfish flashing like broken pieces of sky, the occasional long dark shape of a gate-keeper moving beyond the reeds. I would have no children, perhaps, but my house would not be empty. There would be a family of frogs in the roof-pocket, blue snails on the walls, a blind white crab living beneath the sleeping shelf, and a listening eel in a woven tube near the entrance.

The eel would be older than most people expect an eel to become.

It would know the routes between the old bell tower and the deep stair, between the turtle basin and the stone cemetery. Its body would be scarred with pale loops where it had escaped nets or teeth. When the currents were ordinary, it would lie slack and rude in its tube, accepting scraps without gratitude. But when something shifted below—when warm water began rising too fast, when a root wall opened, when a slide disturbed the silt—it would knot itself into a hard, trembling braid.

Then I would go out.

I think that would be my work: not making things, not leading people, but being sent for when the water gave contradictory news.

The Held would have a tapping-name for me, something like a pause between three knocks. In the air chamber, if anyone tried to say it aloud, it would sound too plain. So perhaps they would call me by a work-name instead:

**The One Who Waits for the Second Sound.**

Because the first sound is never enough.

A rock settles. A turtle strikes a wall. A pike turns beneath an arch. A child drops a clay bowl. The mountain makes little noises constantly, and a person who panics at every one of them would not live long. But after the first impact, there is sometimes another: slower, lower, traveling through stone rather than water.

That second sound means the town has noticed.

It might mean roots are tightening around a damaged wall. It might mean a buried chamber has opened. It might mean the deep places are answering. I would not claim to know which. I would only mark the place, tie a thread of pale eelgrass to the nearest stalk, and carry the news back.

I would be trusted, though not necessarily liked.

People would bring me questions that could not be answered quickly.

Is the warm seep safe this season?

Why have the frogs abandoned the western roof-pools?

Why does the schoolhouse lily crop taste of iron?

Why has the old pike returned to the shrine district with a new scar across its eye?

Why did the roots grow around a dead person’s tower before the bones had been gathered?

I would give them practical answers when I had them. I would say: move the children from that house; do not eat those lilies; the current has changed; the pike is hunting close to its eggs; wait three days before reopening the channel.

And when I had no answer, I would say that too.

This would make some people uneasy. The Held survive by knowing so much: the names of fungi, the temper of eels, the rhythm of surface weather, the exact pressure at which an old air chamber becomes dangerous. To say *I do not know* would sound, to some, like letting water into the house.

But I think the drowned town needs a person for its unknowns.

Not a priest, though I might leave offerings at the deep chambers. Not a skeptic, though I would examine every crack and root before I called anything sacred. I would be someone who understands that mystery is not always a question waiting to be solved. Sometimes it is a pressure to be accommodated: like the lake overhead, like winter cold entering the jaw, like an ancestor’s bone needle passed from hand to hand.

In old age, I would become less quick in the water. My side-fans would ache in the cold. I would spend more time in the doorway of my leaf-house, feeling messages through the reed wall.

The eel would outlive me, or perhaps I would outlive it. I cannot decide which feels sadder.

When I died, I would be taken to one of the stone towers. My household—by then perhaps not a household of people, but of frogs, snails, eel, crab, and whatever younger neighbor had inherited my channel—would fold my side-fans closed. They would place a length of listening reed beside me, not as a tool for the next world, but as an acknowledgment.

I had spent my life hearing the town.

Then the water would take back my hearing, slowly. Fish would pass through the tower windows. Coral would work its patient mouths along the stone. My bones would become needles, net-frames, sealing dust. Something of me would remain in a wall, a hand, a repaired doorway.

And perhaps, years later, when a child paused in the deep avenue and felt three slow impacts through the roots of their teeth, they would think of me.

Not because I had explained the sound.

Because I had taught them to wait for it.
