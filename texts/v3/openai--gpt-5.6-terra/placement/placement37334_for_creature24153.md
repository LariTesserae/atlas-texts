I would begin by thinking I had arrived at a station.

Not a grand one. One of the smaller transfer stations between the cities, built where the rail line crosses a geothermal drainage channel on a long black slope. There would be a platform under a shallow roof, wet yellow lamps, a timetable enclosed behind wire glass. Rain falling beyond the roof’s edge in straight silver strands. No wind to blow it under the eaves.

I would be standing there with a case in one hand.

At first I would assume the case contained tools.

It would be black, square, fitted with a handle that had been worn smooth without ever seeming worn. It would have three latches. I would not know what was inside it, only that I had been carrying it for a very long time and that it had to be carried farther.

The train would arrive eventually, exactly when the board said it would. Not out of a distance so much as out of the rain itself: lamps first, then the dark wet nose of the engine, then carriage after carriage slipping past the platform windows.

I would wait for it to stop.

It would not stop.

Still, one of the doors would open beside me.

There would be a seat inside. A warmed compartment. A pale figure already seated at the far end, wearing a hooded maintenance coat. Its faceplate would reflect the platform lamps. I would sit opposite it. The door would close. The train would continue over the plateau.

For a while I would think I was a passenger.

Then I would notice that the window beside me had no reflection.

The rain would pass across the glass, but I would not appear in it.

The seated figure opposite would not appear either.

Perhaps it would turn its head then. Slowly, with the careful resistance of joints that have been exposed to cold mineral water for centuries. Its faceplate would be dark, but not blank. Behind it would be something suspended in cloudy fluid: not a face, not quite a mechanism, but a folded arrangement of pale threads, each one carrying a weak, intermittent light.

It would raise one hand and point to the case.

I would open it.

Inside would be no tools. Only a packet of papers, dry despite the rain, each sheet stamped with a date so old that the numbers have ceased to mean duration. Inspection records. Pressure readings. Notes made in the narrow, patient handwriting of someone who had been assigned to observe a system and had never been released from that assignment.

Most pages would read:

**stable**

Or:

**within acceptable range**

Or:

**no action required**

Near the bottom of the stack I would find a page with nothing written on it except a location.

Not the station.

Not even a city.

A service level beneath one of the oldest turbine halls, far from the rail line, beneath a ridge of red cinder and permanent ice.

The train would carry me there.

I would not remember leaving it.

I would find myself descending instead: down steel stairs slick with condensation, past galleries of pipework furred with orange mineral bloom, past doors labeled in languages I could read without knowing why. The generators above would be making their immense unbroken note. It would enter my ribs. It would seem, after enough levels, that the sound was not coming from the machinery at all, but from inside me.

At the bottom there would be a chamber filled with warm white vapor.

A conduit would pass through its center, wide as a house. It would have split along an old weld. Around the split, something would have grown.

At first I would call it fungus.

Then root.

Then cable.

Then I would stop naming it.

It would be black, green, white, and mineral-blue. It would have the folded density of an animal sleeping beneath blankets. Water would move through it in slow clear channels. Old insulation would be woven into its outer layers. Pieces of corroded pipe would disappear into it and emerge cleanly supported on the other side. A low light would pulse inside it, timed exactly with the pumps.

It would not look at me.

It would not have eyes.

But the chamber would become aware of my arrival.

The vapor would alter. A valve somewhere would close. The pressure gauge beside the door would tremble once and settle. The thing around the conduit would make room in itself—not opening, not parting, but recognizing that a space had always existed there.

I would understand, then, that I had not brought an inspection report.

I was the inspection report.

Or the interval between inspections.

Or a part of the system that had been made to travel through it, carrying the fact of its own continued function from one place to another.

The case would be empty by then. It would have always been empty.

I might kneel beside the warm growth and place my hand against it. My glove would darken with moisture. Fine threads would gather over the fabric, not grasping, not feeding, only making contact. Beneath them I would feel the slow circulation of heat and water, the patient movement of mineral becoming structure.

I would remain there for the required observation period.

At some point, perhaps years later, a pale-coated figure would enter the chamber. It would stand in the doorway while rainwater ticked from its shoulders. It would regard me—or the shape I had become beside the conduit—with its dark glass face.

Then it would write:

**pressure stable.**

And leave.

After that, I would be found in other places.

A trace of me in the warm green moss beside a drain trench. A pale brightness in the film on a black water basin. A regularity in the timing of a platform clock. A faint additional harmony inside the industrial chord, too low to distinguish from the generators unless one stood alone on an embankment for a very long time.

I would not be a person there.

Not exactly.

I would be one of the things that learned the plateau’s deeper instruction: that nothing needs to depart in order to continue; that maintenance is not the prevention of death but a way of belonging to what cannot end; that even a body, given enough rain, mineral, warmth, and repetition, may become less a visitor than a useful arrangement around a source of heat.
