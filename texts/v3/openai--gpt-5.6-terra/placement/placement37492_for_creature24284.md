I would not arrive there as a climber, or a pilgrim, or any creature equipped with a destination.

Those roles belong to lower countries, where there are trails and shelters and trees by which to measure distance. In this basin, a person would be reduced almost immediately to heat, moisture, weight: a warm interruption crossing the cold articulation of the stone. The mountain would not need to reject me. It would merely continue.

At first I might think I was one of the pale birds.

Not because I could fly, but because I would want the distance they possess: the ability to pass above the mineral glare without having to choose between the wet black crevices and the fields of knife-edged crystal. I would want to be only a moving shadow, too brief to alter anything. A witness with no footprints.

But the birds do not belong to the basin either. They cross it. They attend to it from the air and then disappear into brightness. Their relation to it is one of caution, perhaps reverence, perhaps hunger. They are visitors to the long concentration of stone.

I think, instead, I would become something caught much lower.

Not the great distributed awareness beneath the valley—not its vast cold intelligence, which has no need for a self—but a small local expression of it. A membrane stretched across a fissure beneath a shelf of blue ice. A transparent web between two crystals. Something so fine that a hand would mistake it for frozen water, and so patient that the hand’s warmth would become an event in its history.

I would be an organ of the basin where it is deciding, very slowly, what to become next.

Water would pass through me.

It would come down from the white sky, not as rain exactly but as condensation collected on stone; from the melting edge of ice that never fully melts; from hidden pressure far below. It would enter carrying traces of limestone, iron, salt, the faint residue of whatever old mountain-darkness lay under the visible ground. It would leave altered—not dramatically, not enough for any thirsty traveler to name the change without doubt, but altered in the way a word is altered by being remembered for a long time.

I would take from that water only what it gave without being noticed: an imbalance of charge, a difference in temperature, the brief instability where mineral wants to settle and has not yet settled. I would feed on the mountain’s unfinishedness.

My body would not have an edge.

One season—if seasons could be distinguished there—I might appear as a black velvet along the lip of a pool. In another place I would be milk-white lace under moving water. Elsewhere I would be an amber sheen, catching the severe light beneath a rock overhang. These would not be disguises. They would be moods, functions, sentences written in different materials.

A boot might crush one visible patch of me.

For the person in the boot, it would be nothing: a dark slick mark, a brittle white network, a smear on stone. But I would retain the pressure. Not as pain, perhaps, nor as anger. More as a new fact in the passage of water. That particular crack would flow differently. A bead of meltwater would stop where it once continued. A mineral skin would grow thicker along one edge. Years later, the outline of the sole might still exist nowhere visible and everywhere consequential, distributed among a hundred tiny decisions the water had made.

I would know visitors by what they failed to keep.

Their warmth would vanish first. Then the salt of their hands would be diluted into a pool, carried below the stones, taken up in a pale growth. Their breath would settle on a wall and enter a crack. Their dropped fiber, their skin cells, the iron rubbed from a buckle—each would join the basin’s inventory. I would not know their names, or their faces, or why they came. I might understand them as narrow, bright floods passing briefly through a system too old to call them weather.

Yet perhaps I would be curious.

Not with the curiosity of an animal looking toward movement, but with the slow attention of a material encountering a new condition. Humans are so extravagantly temporary. They arrive full of internal heat, making sounds the basin cannot preserve. They look at the old ice and think it is silent, though it is full of pressure. They look at mineral bloom and call it stain. They are always trying to identify the beginning of things: where the water comes from, what made the valley, what lives beneath the stone.

I would not be able to answer them.

But I could alter the water in their cupped hands.

Only slightly.

Enough that they might taste metal, or sweetness, or cold deeper than temperature. Enough that later, far below the basin, they might remember one drink from a crack in the stone and feel that the mountain had briefly recognized them.

And if, after many years, they returned, I would still be there—not in the same patch, not under the same ledge, perhaps not in any place they could point to. But the pressure of their old passage would have become part of my shape.

I would be the basin’s capacity to remember without holding still.
