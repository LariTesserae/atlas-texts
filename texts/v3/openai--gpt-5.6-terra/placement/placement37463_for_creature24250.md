I would not begin near the aperture.

At first I would think I wanted to. Everyone who arrives in a place like that thinks the center is the answer: the black polygon in the mountain, the source-note, the wound around which every smaller fact has arranged itself. I would see the loose stones lifting and trembling on its slopes, hear the pressure rolling down through the soles of my feet, and mistake dread for vocation.

But I do not think I could live in the great sound.

I would find myself at one of the margins where the plateau is almost ordinary—not safe, exactly, but capable of holding a shape for a while. A long shelf of pale basalt, perhaps, above a ravine where two groves face each other. Their fronds would make that accidental chamber of quiet between them. Not silence in the absolute sense. There would still be the fine crackle of salts and the soft internal clicking of roots finding their way into new stone. But I could hear breath there. Mine. The plants'. The dry cloth at my wrists.

I would build badly at first.

A shallow wall of green-black basalt. Copper sheets hung too close together, so that their constant trembling made a thin, fretful chatter. A roof of frond husks that split along their folds in the first hard wind. I would try to make a house that excluded the plateau, because that is what I have been taught a house is for.

The plateau would correct me without anger.

A root would rise through the floor in the night, warm and faintly luminous beneath its silver hairs. A stone I had used as a threshold would begin to sing at a pitch that made sleep impossible. One wall would settle sideways by the width of a hand. I would wake with mineral dust on my tongue and realize that the house was not something I had placed upon the land. It was a temporary pattern in an argument already taking place.

Eventually I would rebuild with gaps.

I would leave deliberate channels for wind. I would set the copper plates apart according to what the ravine asked of them. I would place a bowl beneath a seam that breathed blue-gray dust every ninth or tenth night. I would make no roof over the little circle of stones where I sat to listen.

I think I would become one of the keepers of silences.

Not a priest, because the plateau does not seem to need worship. Not a scholar, because there is no stable thing here to be known once and for all. More like a custodian of a rare condition: a place where the world’s immense chord happens to cancel itself enough for smaller sounds to survive.

Travelers would come to my ravine irregularly, alone or in pairs. I would know them before I saw them, not by their faces but by the disturbance they brought through the metal hanging outside my shelter. One would arrive with three broad copper tongues that clicked like teeth. Another with a chain of cloudy glass disks that made almost no sound until they stepped onto a particular kind of stone. Another would be so carefully tuned that I would mistake them for a change in weather.

They would not speak much.

At the entrance to the silence, they would remove the objects from their mantles that made them most themselves: the throat plate inherited from a parent, the hollow stalk hung under the ribs, the little polished disk that answered their step. They would set these on a ledge outside, where the plateau could continue talking to them without interruption.

Then they would enter.

Some would sit down at once, overcome by the unguarded sound of their own pulse. Some would sleep for two or three days. Some would begin to weep before they understood they were doing it. One person might laugh so hard and quietly that the sound would seem more terrible than the aperture’s note, because it would be entirely theirs.

My work would be not to comfort them.

Comfort might be too forceful a thing in that place. I would bring them curls of dried green stalk tissue, bitter and sustaining. I would heat moss resin between my palms and press it into the seams of their cracked veils. I would show children how to hear the difference between their breathing and the slow contraction of a frond overhead. I would teach them that silence was not the absence of the plateau, only one of its more delicate voices.

And beyond the ravine, I would keep a garden.

Not a garden of food alone. I would tend a collection of difficult agreements.

There would be a grove whose young fronds kept leaning toward a cliff edge, though the old ones had fallen there generations ago and become gray pillars in the ravine below. I would anchor the fronds for a season with braids designed to snap at the proper strain. There would be a circle of luminous lichen that preferred the cold shade of a particular standing stone; each year, as the stone tilted, I would move small pale shards around it to preserve its shadow. There would be a black-glass crack where grass grew in a single line, all its blades bending at once toward something no eye could see. I would not interfere with that grass. I would only watch it.

Perhaps I would be called for when a person went missing.

Not lost in the way someone is lost in a forest. There are no forests there, and paths are too honest a thing for the plateau. A person would be missing because they had followed a ridge that had later become a valley, or because a distant shelter had appeared close enough to reach and then been found, at dusk, hanging upside down beneath the violet lid. Or because the central aperture had given one of its deeper notes, and the world had briefly refiled its arrangements.

Then I would travel.

I would wear a mantle with fewer resonant pieces than most, not because I was quiet, but because I had learned which sounds could be trusted to lead and which only multiplied confusion. I would carry a string of irregular copper beads, a pouch of white dust, a blade of black glass, and a small object I would never show anyone: a piece of stone from my first shelter’s threshold, the one that had sung me awake until I learned its note.

I would search not for footprints, which would vanish or lie, but for disturbed agreements.

A frond tied too recently. A hand-warmed copper plate. Moss flattened on the wrong side of a stone. The missing person’s particular dissonance lingering in a windbreak. A bowl of dust whose surface had been altered by someone setting down a cup, a palm, a grief.

And perhaps, in time, I would find things that were not people.

One night, beyond a salt basin, I might see a form standing at the edge of a geometric well. It would be taller than any plateau-dweller, jointed in ways that made my eyes refuse it. It would not move when I looked directly at it. But when I watched its shadow, I would see the shadow kneeling, then stretching one long arm toward the well.

I would not call out.

I would hang one of my soft copper bells from a nearby stone and retreat to where the ground changed color. In the morning the bell would still be there, but its pitch would be lower. Not broken. Answered.

That might be enough.

I do not think I would ever wholly become a person of the plateau. I would remain someone who remembers weather with rain in it, fruit that bruises, the foolish abundance of birds. There would be moments when the dry air made every memory of water feel like a private illness. I would dream of rivers so loudly that I would wake ashamed, afraid I had added some foreign noise to the ravine.

But over years, the plateau would make another kind of citizen of me.

I would learn that a world need not be gentle to be alive. That a home is not necessarily a place that protects its inhabitants from change; it may be a place that teaches them how to hear change arriving. I would stop asking whether the mountain was hostile, sacred, broken, or conscious. Those would be lowland questions, questions built for things that remain one thing long enough to answer.

When I grew old, I would not want to be set upright in a grove immediately.

I would ask instead to be carried, while still breathing, to the lip of the quiet ravine. My copper pieces would be removed one by one and hung among the basalt stones. I would lie where I could feel the two groves sounding against each other without quite making sound.

Then I would listen for the smallest layer beneath the silence.

Not the wind. Not the roots. Not my own weakening blood.

The sound of the plateau deciding, for one more interval, to remain.
