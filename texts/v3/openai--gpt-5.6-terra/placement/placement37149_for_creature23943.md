I would find myself not in the flooded room exactly, but in the interval by which it is becoming something else.

I would be one of the unfinished doors.

Not the doorway with its missing door—though I would borrow its shape when I needed to be understood. I would be the possibility of passage that has entered the walls: the small certainty in a seedpod that allows it to turn sideways; the reason the fish arrives beneath the bench and departs near the ceiling; the faint yielding in stone when silver spores rise through it.

At first I might seem to be a film on the water, a transparent membrane stretched across no visible opening. If one came close, it would tremble with images that were not reflections: a dry room, perhaps; a field before the flood; a child's hand holding the brass handle before it went green. But no image would remain long enough to become history. I would not preserve things. I would let them pass into their next shape.

I would have no single body because the basin has already taught me better.

A portion of me would sleep as a white thread inside the fresh clay crack, drinking the coldness before it becomes water. Another portion would live in the dark globe inside the jar, turning slowly around a center that has not yet decided whether it is a seed, an eye, or a well. A little of me would be distributed among the snail-shells: not in their bodies, but in the shining marks ahead of them, in the route they prepare through the world.

When the pale creature touched an old nail, I would feel the nail remember iron in the earth.

When it touched moss, I would feel the moss remember rain before rain had fallen.

We would recognize one another, the creature and I, without meeting. It is the room's way of gathering what has entered it. I would be the room's way of letting something leave.

Perhaps I was once made by people. Perhaps I was the idea of an exit attached to the house before the basin filled: hinges, a latch, a rectangle of light cut into plaster. Floodwater loosened me from usefulness. Roots carried me downward. The world forgot my name. And freed from being a door for human bodies, I became a door for conditions.

Through me, water could become root.

Through me, root could become recollection.

Through me, a ruined room could stop being ruined without becoming restored.

I would be careful. A door is dangerous in a small place. Too much passage and the basin would empty itself into whatever waits beyond the clay; too little and it would harden into a grave. So I would open only in minute ways.

A wall would become briefly permeable to a leaf.

The ceiling would forget its weight for a silver mote.

The blind fish would find a route shaped exactly like its own need.

On the rare dark days, when floodwater presses down so heavily that even the weeds bow, I would collect myself near the seam. The creature would gather there too, child-sized and faceless, with the whole drowned chamber moving in the water where its face should be. It would place its many-fingered hand against the new sharp edge.

I would be the small aperture beneath that hand.

Not beneath the earth, exactly. Not above it either. I would lead into the place where all unchosen forms remain wet and waiting: rooms that might become ponds, ponds that might remember rooms, roots testing the outlines of future walls, doors learning how to grow.

And when the creature listened, I would listen with it.

Not for a voice.

For the moment when the world, somewhere on the far side of the basin, becomes ready to admit that it has an inside.
