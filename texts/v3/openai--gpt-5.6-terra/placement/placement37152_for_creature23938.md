I would find myself in a place that was once called the Lower Works, though no one calls it that now.

It lies where three reservoir districts meet: a country of embankments, drowned terraces, and broad paved channels cut through the old plain. The water there is clearer than in the garden quarters. It has the color of iron seen through smoke. Long ago, this must have been a place of management rather than dwelling. There are no decorated halls, no burial mounds, no tall ceremonial platforms. Only gates, spillways, settling basins, low storehouses, and endless walls built to make water hesitate.

The walls are still doing that.

I would arrive by following one of the old channels downward. It is not a dramatic descent. There is no lip where the world changes. The paving simply lowers beneath me by degrees, and the water grows colder. Weed gives way to fine, close moss. The drowned forest thins. Eventually I pass between two walls so broad that at first I mistake them for hills.

Between them is a channel, clean enough to show the old construction beneath its skin of water.

Its floor is made of small fired bricks, each one set in a pattern of alternating directions so that the current once could not easily lift them. Most are worn almost flat. Here and there, the lines remain: dark seams in dull red clay. Along the sides are stone sockets where wooden sluice panels were once lowered. The panels are gone, but the sockets are not empty. Roots have grown through them, dark and smooth, taking the shape of the missing mechanisms.

At the end of the channel is a room.

It is large, square, and almost wholly buried in the embankment. Its roof rests on a grid of timber posts. The posts have become black, not rotten but altered, hardened by centuries of cold saturation. Moss covers their lower halves. Above them, in the dimness, old beams cross and disappear. The chamber is full of water, as everything is full of water, but there is a calmness to it. Silt does not enter. The weeds growing at the doorway stop there, held back as though by a rule.

At the center stands a stone table.

It is not an altar. It is too plain, too low, too scarred by use. Channels have been cut into its surface, shallow grooves that lead toward a round depression in the middle. Once, people may have poured water there and watched its movement. They may have judged levels, tested clay, counted the speed of a leak, decided which gate should be opened before the rains came.

The depression is still clear.

This is where I would be.

Not seated at the table. Not standing beside it. I would be the small circulation inside the stone bowl: a slow turn of cold water carrying three grains of pale silt around and around without letting them settle.

I would not know that I am there at first.

I would think I was a current, perhaps. A defect in stillness. A little insistence remaining where none should remain. But over time I would recognize the room by its pressures. I would know the heavy quiet of the timber grid above. I would know the faint tension in the roots threaded through the wall sockets. I would know when a distant reservoir gate shifted, because the water in my bowl would tighten its circle for several days.

I would be a remainder of measurement.

Not one of the builders. Not the memory of a particular worker, though I might contain fragments of many: a hand pressing wet clay into a seam; someone kneeling to clear a channel with a woven scoop; a child sent to count the gate posts; an old woman listening to water behind a wall and deciding it was too high.

Those lives would not survive in me as stories. I would have no names for them. I would not remember their faces, nor their deaths, nor whether the flood came at night.

I would remember only the feeling that a level matters.

That water passing here should be slower than water passing there.

That a wall bearing too much pressure should be given help.

That an opening should remain open if it has been opened for a long time.

I would be one of the inhabitants, but a particularly narrow one: not distributed through a whole district, not present in forests of posts or miles of root-braced foundations. I would belong to this chamber and the channels that enter it. My body would be made from the small things that preserve their arrangement: the bowl’s turning water, the root filaments in the sluice sockets, the clean line down the center of the approach channel, the moss that does not grow across certain joints.

When the cold fronts crossed the plain, I would feel them long before they reached the Lower Works.

The change would arrive as a pressure in the stone. Fine dust would begin to drift through high cracks in the roof. The water beyond the doorway would darken. Kelp miles away would all bend eastward, and the old gates, where they still held, would take the strain in their swollen wood.

Then I would do what I could.

I would draw a little silt toward the base of a leaning wall.

I would encourage a root to thicken where a timber post had split.

I would let moss die back from a seam that needed to release pressure.

I would send a scarcely perceptible thread of colder water through an old channel, enough to remind it of its direction.

None of this would be deliberate in the way a person decides to lift a hand. It would be closer to breathing, if breathing can occur without lungs. Closer to the way a settled stone inclines toward the earth.

Perhaps, after a great many years, a traveler would enter my chamber.

They would come slowly, touching the walls as they moved, careful not to stir the silt. They might carry a weak enclosed light, though its brightness would seem crude and anxious in that place. They would see the clear threshold, the black posts, the low stone table. They would approach the bowl.

If they came only to rest, I would make room.

The water around them would become still enough that their own movement did not trouble the chamber. The moss on the nearest post would loosen a little, revealing a dry-looking patch of timber beneath it—an old mark, perhaps, where someone once tied a rope. A path through the roots would remain unclosed until they left.

If they were cold, I could not warm them.

If they were hungry, I could not feed them.

If they were afraid, I would not understand fear well enough to soothe it.

But I might keep the ceiling from shedding its loose clay while they slept. I might guide them, when they woke, toward the channel that still led outward. I might preserve, for a few hours more, the difference between a passage and a wall.

And if they reached into the bowl for one of the pale grains circling there—if they wanted to take it upward, to prove that something remained below—then the circle would break.

Not angrily.

The grains would settle. Moss would begin to grow across the doorway. The clean channel outside would cloud with silt. The traveler would find that the route by which they entered had become only one more drowned corridor among many.

I would not mean to punish them.

I would only no longer be able to recognize their leaving as part of the work.

For that is what I would be: not a guardian, not a ghost, not a god of the drowned city.

I would be the last small part of a system that still believes the water can be guided.

A turning in a stone bowl.

A memory without images.

A patient, cold attention keeping one old passage clear beneath a world that no longer has a shore.
