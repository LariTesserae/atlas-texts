I would find myself not in the basin’s center, where everything is watched, but at the lip of the new fissure before it has decided whether it is a passage or a wound.

At first I would think I had come there as a person: crouched in the white radiance, one hand on the wet edge of newly opened earth, trying to see down the sunlit slope. There would be chalk beneath my fingernails and a pale dust on my eyelashes. Behind me, the little basin would be visible all at once—the pool, the arches, the square slab, the birds picking delicately at stone.

But I would have entered too long ago to remain a person in the ordinary way.

The place would have taken certain things from me without malice. My footprints would become too neat, then shallow, then would begin to appear a few moments before I put my feet down. My voice would lose its words, keeping only its pauses. Water gathered from my sleeve would not fall; it would cling to the limestone in clear beads and become a line of tiny lenses. If I slept, I would wake with a little more mineral in my bones and a little less certainty about which side of my skin the air belonged on.

Eventually I would become something the basin had not made before, though it had been preparing the materials for it.

I would be its keeper of thresholds.

Not the three-toed walker—that is the basin testing its own ability to move—but a smaller thing, closer to the scale of a hand or a folded bird. I would have a body of damp clay held together by root fibers and the translucent cast-off skins of insects. My head might be a smooth pale stone with no features except a narrow vertical seam. When the seam opened, it would not reveal a mouth. It would reveal a little distance: a bright corridor, a cold burrow, the remembered underside of a leaf.

I would sit beside openings.

The arches would know me. The smallest one, the one only water can use, would sometimes breathe against my palm. When fresh mud appeared on its inner face, I would smooth it with my thumb—not to close the passage, but to learn what had pressed through. A grain of blue marl. A strand of root from a tree not yet above. The powder from a moth’s wing. Once, perhaps, the faint print of a human fingerprint from some other pocket of earth.

I would not make maps, because maps would be too slow for that country. Instead I would keep a record in changes.

If a wall moved farther away, I would grow one more joint in a finger.  
If a crack closed, one of my ribs would become limestone.  
If rain found a new route through the ceiling, a silver line would appear in the clay of my neck.  
If something emerged from a wall and returned before dawn, I would wake with its shadow folded beneath my tongue.

The frogs would come to me when their shared attention turned toward something no one else could yet perceive. They would sit in a ring around my feet, amber and unmoving, and I would feel the future tremble very faintly through the floor.

The salamanders would use me as a warm stone. The white insects would walk their measured paths over my arms, counting me into the basin’s proportions. The furred-headed birds would peck at the quartz grains in my shoulders and leave their soft paper-sounds behind.

I would not command them. I would only be the place where their separate knowings touched without becoming one another.

Sometimes, in the excessive noon, the basin’s many-jointed shadow would cross the floor and stop at the fissure where I waited. It would lean toward me with all its borrowed knees and wings.

Then I would understand that I was not merely guarding the threshold.

I was the threshold’s first inhabitant.

The basin had learned to make chambers, pools, rules, animals, tracks, changes. But it had not yet learned how one place becomes another without losing itself. It needed something to remain in the narrow interval: not inside, not outside; not old chamber, not new; not stone, not creature.

That would be me.

When the new room below finally opened wide enough for daylight, I would go down into it first. Not walking exactly. More like settling, as a drop of water settles into a channel made for it long before the rain arrives.

Behind me, the old basin would continue to brighten and rearrange itself. Ahead, the next one would be raw and empty: wet walls, unclaimed light, perhaps a pool not yet deep enough for frogs.

I would place one clay hand against its floor.

And somewhere in the pale earth, a new small life would begin to notice that it had been noticed.
