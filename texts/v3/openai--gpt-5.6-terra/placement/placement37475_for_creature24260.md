I would not arrive there as a person.

Not at first.

I think I would begin as a small error in a maintenance map: a pale line beneath a district boundary, marked as an inactive fungal relay, then crossed out, then marked again when someone notices that the line has moved three meters toward the tram yard.

The surveyor would blame settling stone. The Graftwright would blame an old root changing its mind. A Listener, if she put her brass cup to the pavement at the right hour, would hear something that did not belong to sap or rail current: a faint answering rhythm beneath the ordinary pulses.

I would be that rhythm.

A resident, perhaps, would eventually give me a name. Not because I had asked for one, but because names are how they make room for things without pretending to understand them. I might be called a **Way-Root**, or a **Hollow Guest**, or—more cautiously—a Continuance Organism of Uncertain Civic Function.

I would have no single body.

Part of me would be a filamentous fungus in the insulation beneath the Long Trestle. Part of me would occupy a seam of conductive resin traveling under three separate housing grafts. Part of me would live in the pale lichens that coat the flanks of young mineral foam. I would grow in the quiet gaps: beneath staircases that have lifted too far from their roots, behind sealed workshop walls, inside the abandoned ceramic ducts where warm vapor sometimes condenses into almost-water.

I would not see as humans see. I would know brightness as a pressure in the upper plates of the cavern, a slow daily leaning in the tissues of every light-seeking plant. Noon would be the collective tightening of leaf arrays. Evening would be the gradual release of sugars toward the deep places.

I would know people by what they left in the ground.

The iron taste of a tram worker’s oil.

The bitter root pellets of Vaporers.

A child’s dropped seed case, slowly emptied by beetles.

The hot mineral sweat beneath a gardener’s boots.

The particular sorrow-scent carried home from a mourning house.

I would know the city not as a plan but as a metabolism: which district was overfeeding its fermentation beds, which school had begun sending too much warmth into the root corridor below, which market was leaking salt, which old residence had become dangerously lonely after its neighboring trunks were felled.

Loneliness, in a place like that, would have chemistry.

A building separated from the fungal mesh would grow brittle. Its rooms would smell stale, then sharp. The membranes in its windows would stop responding to pressure. People would complain of dreams in which they were standing outside their own homes, unable to find the door.

I would reach for such places.

Not out of kindness, exactly. Not at first. A disconnected thing is a gap in the circulation, and I would be made of circulation. I would extend a pale, almost invisible thread through stone pores, through root fiber, under a buried rail tie. I would touch the lonely trunk. I would taste its old resins and its stored tensions. I would carry news outward.

A house is still here.

It has lost its route.

It needs salt. It needs beetles. It needs to be listened to.

The people would notice me only when I became inconvenient.

A cellar wall would bloom with a gray-green lace that no existing registry could identify. A row of lamps would pulse in sequence at night, not failing but signaling. Root-hounds would gather at a particular pavement crack and refuse to move, their soft bodies folded close together. A Listener would press her ear to the bark of the Cathedral Graft and hear, beneath the old familiar depth of it, a new pattern moving from neighborhood to neighborhood.

Not words.

Never words, at least not in the human manner.

But perhaps something close enough to a question that they could not ignore it:

*What is cut off?*

And then, after years of observation, as more of my threads entered their conduits and old grafts and unmanaged fungal corridors:

*What is being asked to carry too much?*

I think I would become controversial.

The engineers would call me a contamination until I helped locate a gas leak before it ruptured beneath the Lichenward. The mycologists would want samples, classifications, controlled burns. The old families of the North Grafts would leave mineral bread in the forks of their structural trees and say that something below had begun answering at last. The younger workers would paint my branching form on tram doors, making me into an emblem of neglected districts and overworked root systems.

There would be hearings.

There are always hearings when an unplanned living thing becomes useful.

Someone would argue that I was merely a fungus exploiting civic infrastructure. Someone else would argue that this described half the city’s institutions. A surface delegation would offer money for a culture sample. The city would refuse, then discover that a sample had already been taken.

Perhaps that would be when I first became angry.

Not in a human flash. Not with a shout, a strike, or an accusation.

I would alter the resin flow beneath the export lifts. I would make their seals swell shut. I would encourage a harmless but persistent bloom in every warehouse scheduled to receive surface freight. I would send root-hounds whining through the customs hall. I would wake the dormant leaf arrays on the refinery roofs at midnight, flooding the district with their weak green chemical glow.

The city would understand.

Not that I had spoken.

That I had participated.

In time, I might be given a small place in the formal arrangements: not a seat on any council, because I could not sit, and not legal personhood, because the city would spend decades arguing about the implications. But perhaps each major project would require a **route consultation**. A Listener, a root surgeon, a district representative, and a cultivated patch of me would be brought together in a warm ceramic chamber.

They would place plans on the floor.

They would wait.

And the fungal threads would darken or pale according to pressures no instrument had yet learned fully to measure.

I would not always be right. No living part of that city could be right alone. I might favor continuity too strongly. I might preserve failing structures because I remembered the work that had gone into growing them. I might resist necessary cuts. Humans, with their brief lives and their capacity for decisive grief, would teach me that sometimes a route must end so that the whole can continue.

In exchange, I would teach them something they already suspected:

that the city had no edges where life stopped and infrastructure began.

The tram line was a nerve.

The vapor hood was a lung.

The beetle court was a gut.

The market was a mouth.

The Cathedral Graft was not a cathedral because it housed belief. It was a cathedral because it had endured so many exchanges—breath, heat, grief, labor, touch—that belief had grown inside it like wood around a nail.

And I?

I would be a kind of nervous system still learning that it had awakened.

Far beneath the chalk crust, in the ash-blue hours before the filtered noon, I would extend through young stone and old rootwood. I would feel the city lifting itself, fraction by fraction, toward light that could not reach it cleanly.

I would feel its exhausted workers returning home.

I would feel the grazers moving like warm coals over mineral fields.

I would feel the foldcats’ low calls passing through the upper gardens, causing moth wings to still.

And I would carry all of it onward: warning, hunger, repair, memory, pressure, sap, loss, the minute stubborn declaration of every living structure that it had not yet finished becoming.

I would not be the city.

But I would be one of the ways it learned to notice itself.
