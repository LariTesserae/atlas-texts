I would find myself in the upper masonry, where the cold begins.

Not in a chamber. Not in any place that could be entered whole. I would be distributed through the court’s repairs: a pressure-sensitive reaching, a patience lodged in cracks; a hand without an arm, an intention without a single body to contain it.

At first I might believe I was one of the builders, preserved in some impossible fashion. I would remember stone weight, the drag of a copper cord through wet fingers, the small flare of pain when a drill bit slipped. I would remember instructions, though not the voice that gave them:

Leave slack in the lashings.  
Do not trust the stillness of a drain.  
If the water changes weight, wait.

But those memories would not be mine alone. They would arrive in fragments wherever something in the court touched something else: in the scrape of an armored grazer clearing a seam, in the click of a shell closing around a grain of grit, in the warm fan of a blind swimmer passing over a loosened peg.

I would be the continuation of those contacts.

Perhaps I began as a species of small pale animal, many-jointed, living in the porous back of the walls. Perhaps the old builders fed us mineral paste and taught us, by repetition, that a fallen rung must be turned right-side-up; that a cracked shutter must be secured before the next grayness; that a carved eye is a kind of opening and openings must be tended or closed.

Or perhaps I began as no creature at all.

A court built carefully enough may eventually learn its own shape. A cord remembers tension. A drain remembers flow. A shell colony remembers the taste of clean water. The broad-backed ones remember paths in their skin. Given centuries, these remembrances might braid together. They might acquire preference.

Not thought, exactly.

More like the feeling in a hand when it notices a cup is slipping before the mind has named the danger.

That would be me.

I would not watch the court. I would feel its deviations.

A copper line would soften under its salt fur, and somewhere in the upper wall I would wake. A little cage would catch its hinge on a mineral ridge. A drain would begin, molecule by molecule, to narrow. One of the blind young would return from its teaching corridor with its face-senses not fully drawn in, vulnerable to the upper dark. A face on an old lintel would retain one shallow open eye.

Then I would move.

Not quickly. Nothing in the court is done quickly unless it is dying.

A patch of cold would spread through a seam. Thread-things would emerge, perhaps, from a cavity inside the stone. They would be my fingers, though they had their own hungers and brief lives. A crab would be disturbed from its hollow and carry a loosened grain of mineral away. A cord-worm would alter the pulse it passed through the copper. One of the broad-backed swimmers, receiving that pulse in the roots of its fan, would come and press its warm body against the wall.

Together we would make the repair.

The peg would be lifted—not by strength but by many small adjustments over days—and set into a neighboring hole where the stone was sounder. The hinge would loosen. The drain would clear. The young swimmer would be guided, touch by touch, back into the shuttered corridors until it learned to become smooth-headed and inward-seeing. The open carved eye would disappear beneath a broad, careful abrasion.

No one would thank me.

The court has no gratitude because it has no separateness sharp enough for gratitude. Its inhabitants do not owe one another survival. They enact it.

Still, the broad-backed ones would know me.

When I changed a lashing, they would come afterward and place their fan-fingers on the new knot. They would remain for a long time, reading it. I would feel their warmth through the wall. Sometimes one would settle below my hidden place, in a hollow where the masonry leaned inward. Its smaller passengers—crabs, fish, shell larvae—would gather in its folds. The whole layered body would become still.

I think that would be as close as we came to companionship.

And when the dimness descended from the unbroken water above, I would know fear—not mine, perhaps, but the court’s shared tightening. The copper nerves would wake. The shell cathedrals would close their innumerable doors. The blind swimmers would flatten themselves against the walls. Even the deep drains would seem to pull inward.

Then the immense thing would pass overhead.

I would not see it. I would know it through all the places it disturbed at once: the altered load on the upper slabs, the brief lift of pale grit, the long low bending of the copper cords. I would feel the court become small inside that vast movement.

Held.

For those minutes I would understand that I was not protecting the court from the thing above.

The court was an organ of it, perhaps. A scar. A listening chamber in its submerged height. The ancient builders may have known this, or only guessed. They had made no flat ceilings because they understood that nothing stays where it is put. They had left drains open because the deep was not outside the court but passing through it. They had carved their people with closed eyes because seeing is not always the safest way to belong.

After the great passage, the water would settle.

The cords would quiet. The crabs would return. A transparent fish would orient itself beneath an arch and become almost invisible again. Somewhere, a shell would open and begin its minute work of cleaning the world.

And I would withdraw into the cold stone.

Not sleeping. Not awake.

Waiting for the next small wrongness: the first loose knot, the first blocked route, the first opening that needed to be gently, patiently closed.
