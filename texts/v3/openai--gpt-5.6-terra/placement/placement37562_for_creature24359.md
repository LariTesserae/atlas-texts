At first I think I would be a ground-reader.

Not because I would be especially wise, or because I would know what the tremors mean. I imagine myself young enough to still be corrected often, kneeling badly at the edge of a courtyard while an older woman tells me not to put my whole palm down on warm ground. *Not like that. You are listening with your bones, not trying to hold it still.*

I would learn the city from below before I learned it from above.

The lane outside my yard would be white with crushed mineral, but the court inside would be brown-red, tamped hard by generations of feet. There would be a pale-trunk tree leaning over our roof, its roots somewhere under three houses, perhaps five. Its leaves would make a leathery clap in dust weather. We would complain about its fallen fruiting bodies and defend it fiercely if someone proposed cutting it back.

My family might keep two horned grazers, neither of them truly ours. One would be old, sharp-shouldered, and always finding a way through the gate. The other would follow my younger cousin, who would insist they understood one another. In the morning we would scrape their rich, crusting droppings from the yard and put them in the fermenting pit with chopped reed stalks, mineral powder, and whatever kitchen scraps had not already become something useful.

I would know the smell of each stage.

Hot dung. Sharp sap. Sweet rot. Sulfur. The dry, clean scent that means a crack has opened somewhere beneath the east wall.

But perhaps I would not be a ground-reader after all. That is too honored a thing to choose for myself. There are people in every world who want to listen to deep truths, and fewer who are willing to spend the day doing what the truth requires afterward: moving a wall, abandoning a store room, telling a family that their shade tree will have to go.

Maybe I would be a dustkeeper.

That feels closer.

I would rise before the hard part of dawn, veil my face, tie my sleeves at the wrist, and take up a broad brush made from stiff dryfiber. My work would be a lane shared by several yards: an old narrow passage where a saltwright lived opposite a family of roof-bird tenders, and where every market day some careless person spilled husks, grit, or beetle-lacquer shavings into the drain channels.

I would sweep dust from a road made of dust, knowing it would return.

This would not seem pointless to me. That is the important part. The lane would be clear for a morning. An elder could walk it without slipping. A child could see a fresh crack before stepping in it. The horned grazers could pass through without finding some poisonous black grains uncovered by last night’s wind. A neighbor could bring a covered basket of cheese to market without dragging it through a drift.

The world would never remain repaired. But repair would still be real.

I think I would love that.

I would learn which dust was ordinary and which had come up from somewhere newly opened. I would know that pale gray heaps around a burrow should be left alone, that pink powder could be collected for plaster, that certain glittering black grains meant I should stand in the mouth of the lane and call—not shout, except perhaps then—for a ground-reader.

There would be satisfaction in being one of the first people to notice change.

Not mastery. Notice.

Or I might be one of the roof birds.

This comes to me when I imagine the city at dawn: thousands of low roofs, leaf layers silvered with dust, courtyards still dark beneath pressed canopies. I would be a small bird with a dull green breast and rust around the throat, nothing bright enough to attract unnecessary attention. I would nest under an eave where the morning warmth reached first. I would know each household by its habits: the one that left groundleaf fibers out for us, the one that chased us from the fruiting racks, the old person who muttered to us each morning as if we were irresponsible grandchildren.

I would not understand their language, not in its meanings. But I would know its weather.

The soft interior talk of people before dawn. The quick closed voices that meant an animal had gotten into a storehouse. The particular silence that spread through a yard when someone had received bad news. The rounded humming of evening songs, which would travel just far enough for me to hear from the roof beam.

And when the dust front came, I would know before they did.

Not because birds are magical, but because my whole small body would feel the pressure alter. The warm air would flatten. The far vegetation would become a darker line. Insects would close themselves away. I would stop calling.

Then the people would look up.

That is a kind of place in the world, too: to be the first quiet thing that tells others to prepare.

But I do not think I would want to be only a bird. I would miss hands. I would miss the slow practical intimacy of making something hold together: salt mortar pressed into a seam, a broken roof panel patched before wind season, a child’s veil retied, a bruised grazer calmed while someone checks its hoof.

Perhaps I would be a maker of temporary things.

A mover of houses, maybe.

I would belong to one of those families whose tools are stored in layered bundles: lifting poles, joint wedges, woven drag mats, mineral rollers, strips of resin-treated fiber, little hammers made from shed horn. When a ridge began to rise beneath a room, when a courtyard wall leaned too sharply, when a grove’s roots started breaking through a foundation, people would come to us.

We would not say, “We can save your house.”

That would be an arrogant promise.

We would say, “We can bring its useful parts with you.”

We would mark the walls first. We would remove the stored food, then the sleeping mats, then the roof leaves, then the door bone, then the particular stones that belonged to grandparents or marriages or children no longer living. We would loosen a wall section by section. We would carry a household into the next yard, or across the avenue to a new shelf of ground, while its former rooms cracked gently apart behind us.

There would be grief in this work, but not only grief.

A house would not be its walls alone. It would be where the family continued to gather after the walls had changed shape. It would be the old root-tree cutting taken to the new court. It would be the same chipped cooking stone set near a different hearth. It would be the remembered position of the doorway, re-created even when the new building faced another direction.

I think that is where I would find myself: among those who refuse both despair and permanence.

Not at the edge of the plain, seeking a river that may not exist. Not high above it, dreaming of conquering it. I would be low down, dust-veiled, carrying something useful from a place that had ceased to be safe to a place that was safe for now.

And in the oldest speech, perhaps there would be no separate word for that work.

Listening below. Rebuilding above. Carrying forward.

All one verb.
