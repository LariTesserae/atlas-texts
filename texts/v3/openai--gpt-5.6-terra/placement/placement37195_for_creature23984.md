I would not arrive on the plateau by rail.

The rail would be too deliberate a thing: a line that promises departure, timetable, return. I think I would come up through one of the old pressure tunnels instead, in the hour after the pumps had been shut down for a deep call. I would emerge from a maintenance hatch into rain and find that I had already been there for a long time.

At first I might believe I was a person.

Not one of the station people exactly. I would have no family room behind a filter wall, no shift bell, no childhood memory of being taught the names of water. I would be found in a small hut beside a cableway terminus, perhaps, with a kettle that never quite boils and a table covered in wet paper. My coat would smell of rust, fern spores, and hot stone. The workers would ask where I came from.

I would answer with practical questions instead.

Which fan changed its voice?

How long has that pool been rising?

Did the black dog cross the bridge before it refused to look at the reeds?

They would decide I was some variety of surveyor and give me a lamp, though I would never need its light as much as they expected. I would walk the embankments in the warm dark, listening through the soles of borrowed boots. I would leave notes in signal cabinets: *Do not clear the moss from the eastern culvert. It is holding something together.* Or: *The rail is not singing for a train.* Or simply: *Wait.*

At the beginning, I would think these notes were predictions.

Later, I would understand that they were recollections.

Because I would not be a surveyor. Not wholly. I would be one of the plateau’s ways of noticing itself.

Perhaps I would be a thing grown accidentally from maintenance records, old warning signs, drowned maps, and the accumulated attention of generations of workers. Every time someone marked a dangerous pool with red stakes. Every time a pumpkeeper wrote down the hour of a strange vibration. Every time a child was told not to follow a light in reed fog. Every time a mosswright chose to let a root remain in a wall because removing it would make the wall collapse—I would have been becoming.

A field ledger with feet.

A memory wearing a raincoat.

The station people might call me the Clerk, though not in my hearing. Or the Wet Archivist. Or the One Who Walks the Closed Lines.

The mosswrights would know better than to ask what I was. They would hand me a cup of bitter fungus tea and tell me that a roof had begun growing in the wrong direction. I would go with them. We would stand beneath the dripping eaves, looking up at the green fur pushing beneath the corrugated metal.

“It wants the steam,” one of them would say.

“Yes,” I would say.

“And if we cut it back?”

“The wall will sweat through by winter.”

They would nod, unsurprised. We would build a second roof above the first, a crooked scaffold of reed matting and scavenged sheet steel. The moss would be allowed its warmth. The people beneath it would sleep dry enough.

This would be my work: not mastery, not rescue, but translation.

The plateau would speak in pressure changes, fungal blooms, the behavior of insects, the pitch of a cable under tension. Its great inhabitants would not speak at all, at least not in anything so narrow as language. But a deep footfall would travel through water tables, foundations, rails, sleeping bodies. A forest would rise a fraction. A hot spring would appear beneath a loading platform. An entire district’s lamps would dim as the ground drew breath.

I would notice.

And sometimes I would convince people to notice with me.

There would be difficult nights. A refinery crew would want to restart their pumps after the deep call because a shipment was due and the upper tanks were near capacity. The sirens would be ready. The generators would tremble behind their walls.

I would stand under the awning in the rain while the shift lead argued with me.

“It could be days,” they would say.

“It has only been twenty minutes.”

“You don’t know that it is anything.”

“No,” I would say. “But neither do you.”

Behind us, the forest would make that impossible saw-sound, low and wet and immense. The rails beyond the yard would shine without a train upon them.

At last the shift lead would look toward the treeline, then at the black dog sitting beneath the fence with its muzzle lifted.

“Another hour,” they would say.

The pumps would remain silent.

By morning, a section of ground beyond the intake house would have folded gently upward, lifting the old pipes in a broad arc. Not breaking them. Not quite. Beneath the raised earth, warm water would move with the sound of a body turning over in sleep.

No one would thank me. That would be proper. They would repair the pipes. They would adjust the intake. Someone would leave a tray of mineral silt at the edge of the new wet ground. Someone else would hang copper wire from a nearby pylon.

I would add a line to the ledger:

*The Host altered its posture near South Intake. Pipes preserved. Do not interpret this as permission.*

Over time I would change.

Rain would erase the distinctions people rely on. Ink runs. Metal oxidizes. Boots soften, then harden again in strange shapes. A person who spends enough years in the plateau’s wet attention may become less separate from the things around them.

My skin might take on pale mineral seams at the wrists. Moss would grow in the stitching of my coat and remain there even when I tried to scrub it away. The little gallery creatures would stop hiding when I entered a tunnel; one might curl warm around a valve housing while I worked. Blind tunnel-things would gather at a distance, their almost-human faces turned toward the sound of my writing.

Eventually I might discover I no longer needed to walk everywhere.

A worker in a far station would open a breaker box and find a page tucked inside it, dry despite the damp:

*Do not take the western path tonight. The reed fog has learned the lamp schedule.*

A child would find me—or something like me—standing at the far end of an embankment, lantern lowered, pointing toward a safe crossing. Yet at that same moment a mosswright in a cliff settlement would be drinking tea with me beside a warm wall, discussing a vine that had gotten into the ventilation system.

This would not seem miraculous to the plateau people. It would seem inconvenient, perhaps, but useful.

“Where are you?” they would ask.

And I would answer, truthfully, “Near enough.”

But I do not think I would remain only an archivist. The plateau does not keep its categories clean. A pump becomes a shrine. A collapsed dormitory becomes a fish nursery. A warning becomes a ritual, then a song, then perhaps a truth that no one can prove but everyone obeys.

So perhaps, after many shifts, I would become something more diffuse.

Not the Host. Never that. The Host is too large, too patient, too indifferent to have a face or a voice.

I would be one of its lesser courtesies.

The part of the place that remembers humans are fragile but persistent. The part that bends a root around a buried cable instead of through it. The part that sends blue beetles to gather where the ground is about to open. The part that makes an old rail hum before a pressure wave reaches the bridge. Not always. Not reliably. The plateau owes no one safety.

But sometimes.

Enough that people can keep living there without mistaking survival for conquest.

If you found me, I would likely be somewhere unremarkable: beneath an elevated pipe crossing, beside a maintenance hut, at the edge of a settling pond where the water had begun to swell in still weather. I would be crouched with one hand on the wet ground.

You might mistake me for a worker.

That would be fine.

I would tell you not to stand there.

Then I would ask what kind of water you had brought with you.
