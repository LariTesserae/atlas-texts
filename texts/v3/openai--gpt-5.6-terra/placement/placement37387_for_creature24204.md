I would not arrive in the basin by crossing into it.

I would wake already implicated.

At first I might think I was one of the small things beneath a leaf: a pale, jointed grazer of moisture-film, folded into a pearl while a drop of condensation swelled above me. I would open slowly. My body would have too many fine limbs to count. I would taste the leaf’s skin through them: green bitterness, fungal dust, the faint salt of something larger having brushed past in the nightless dim.

Above me, the leaf would be a roof. Below it, the mud would give off warmth like the flank of an enormous sleeping animal.

I would not know the word *basin*. I would know only gradients: warmer ground, cooler water; the sweetness near a torn fruit; the trembling warning that passed through roots when a broad-backed grazer lowered itself nearby. I would know the safety of its shadow, the sudden rain of larvae and water that came from its hide when it rose, the small forests of algae in the folds behind its legs. Perhaps I would live there for a while, among those folds. Not as a parasite exactly. Not as a passenger. As one of the innumerable consequences of the grazer being alive.

But I think, given time, I would leave its body.

I would become one of the insects that goes to the black stones.

Not a beetle—the beetles have work too precise for me. They know roots and mineral seams; they carry the instructions of flowers beneath their shells. I would be softer than that: a damp-winged creature with a body like a dark seed, able to crawl into the white-stemmed plants when their leaves begin to close.

I would seek those leaves deliberately.

Inside one, I would be held in heat and perfume. The chamber would close around me without pain. Outside, the basin’s noise would become muffled: the reed-chirr, the throat-notes from the pools, the wingbeats thick in the canopy, the sucking release of mud. I would feel pollen settle over me grain by grain, gathering in the joints of my legs, along the membranes of my wings, over the round glass of my eyes.

And while I waited, I might understand something.

Not in the way a person understands a map, or a prophecy, or an answer. More like the way a root understands water. I would feel that the leaf was not trapping me because it needed to eat. It was lending me a brief interior. It was making me part of its reaching.

When it opened, I would emerge altered but unharmed, powdered white-gold in the dim green air. I would fly—or drift, because flight here would be less an act of escape than a way of joining the constant movement. I would carry the plant’s dust over the warm knobs, across the reeds, perhaps onto the back of a pool-dweller whose throat was beginning to glow.

There, among the black stones, I would find myself most completely.

The knobs would breathe their iron-warm breath. The frogs would gather in rings. The pools would still. Every other sound would soften until the whole crowded basin seemed to be listening through me as much as I listened through it.

I would not be the creature beneath the mud.

That life would be too large, too slow, too unfinished even to call itself one thing. It would be the pressure beneath my feet, the heat in the air, the reason water waited instead of running away. I would be one small mobile thought on its skin: pollen-bearing, moisture-fed, briefly enclosed and released.

A messenger with no message except contact.

A tiny winged interval between one white leaf and another.

And because nothing in the basin is ever alone, I would carry more than pollen. There would be mites in the creases of my wings, fungal threads under my shell, perhaps an egg too small for me to notice attached to one hind leg. Something would feed on what I shed. Something else would shelter in the hollow I made when I died.

That would not seem sad there.

It would be the nearest thing the basin has to peace.
