I would not arrive there by climbing.

Climbing assumes that a summit is an ending: a place where the land opens, where one can look back and measure the route. This basin has no such generosity. I think I would find myself there by following something smaller and less reliable—a warmth beneath moss, perhaps; a thread of water that disappears instead of joining a river; a noise in stone that begins to resemble a summons only after I have listened to it too long.

At first I would believe I was a person.

I would have hands, or the memory of hands. I would crouch beside the black ribs of basalt and touch them. I would try to decide whether the pale vapor was warm, whether the thin blue light on the wet rock came from something living or only from the way my eyes had begun to fail in the dark.

But the basin would be poor at preserving a person.

It would take the useful boundaries first: between my breathing and the cloud’s breathing, between sweat and rain, between the warmth of my palms and the warmth rising from a crack. The noise below would enter my bones. I would begin to sleep between its pulses and wake when a subterranean chamber released pressure. My sense of time would narrow to intervals: before the whistle, after the knock; while the pool is present, after it has gone.

Eventually I would stop thinking of myself as someone who had come there.

I think I would become a listening thing.

Not the awareness beneath the basin. That presence is too wide and too slow for me. It does not need witnesses. It does not need a name. It is the condition under which the fissures keep opening, the water keeps changing temperature, the roots keep deciding where to go.

I would be something made in response to it.

Perhaps a pale web in the underside of the moss: fine as fungal thread, but not feeding on rot. I would live on the boundaries where mineral vapor cools into water. I would gather iron from the mountain’s breath and make almost nothing from it—only enough substance to persist as a faint sheen in a crack. When the rock warmed, I would spread. When cold water flooded the seam, I would draw myself into pores too small to name.

Or perhaps I would be a root’s mistaken thought.

A root reaches downward expecting soil, finds heat and wet stone instead, recoils, then slowly alters itself around the encounter. In that alteration, I would exist: a thin white branching, neither wholly plant nor wholly mineral film, carrying messages too slow for the tree to understand. Not words. Changes in acidity. Traces of sulfur. A warning that a channel has opened somewhere below. A suggestion of warmth.

The tree would lean because of me, though it would never know me.

The moss would green above me, then bleach at my center.

Water would pass through me and leave with a different taste.

That seems the closest answer: I would be an intermediary in a place that has no use for intermediaries. A small recorder of contact. A creature of thresholds.

I would not see far. I would not move in any way that could be called walking. The basin’s walls would be my horizon, though I might never touch them. My world would be made of gradients: warmer, colder; more pressure, less pressure; fresh water, old water; stone that is holding, stone that is about to let go.

The mountain’s great inhalations would not frighten me. Fear belongs to a body that expects continuity. I would have been born in interruption.

When a fissure closed, I would vanish from it.

When another opened, I would arrive—not by traveling, but because the conditions that made me possible had appeared again.

And if someone stood in the basin, blinking into the cloud, trying to hear whether the roar came from below or all around, I might register them before the larger presence did. Their heat would change the condensation on a leaf. Their weight would alter a trickle beneath a stone. A fleck of skin, a breath, a trace of salt would enter the wet dark and be carried downward.

I would not think: *There is a human here.*

I would only become briefly more alive along the route they had disturbed.

Then rain would come hard through the trapped cloud. The ground would shiver. A new seam would breathe open in the basalt.

And I would be elsewhere in the same small, unfinishable dark.
