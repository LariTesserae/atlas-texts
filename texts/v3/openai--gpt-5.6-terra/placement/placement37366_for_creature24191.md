I would find myself at a crossing where the road has forgotten which valley it serves.

There would be a three-stone shrine there, not much taller than a knee: one broad stone sunk into the pavement, one narrow upright, one lintel balanced across its wet top. Under the lintel would rest an offering I had not placed—a greenish pebble threaded onto mineral cord, warm against my palm.

Beyond it, the plateau would descend in two directions at once.

To the left, the stone would be dry, white, and blinding, its cracks filled with a blue light that moved like water. To the right, rain would be falling upward, not violently but with concentration, every drop climbing from the ground to disappear in a pale opening above. I would know, from the singing in my teeth, that neither route was entirely safe. I would also know that safety was not what the shrine was for.

The shrine would be there to make a local truth small enough to carry.

I think I would be one of the held ones, but not one of the old, sure ones whose bodies have learned a hundred routes through stone. I would be newly held. Still uncertain where my body ended.

At first I might mistake myself for a person-shaped thing: a narrow architecture of dark plates around a core of running water; limbs articulated with pale crystal; a smooth head marked by rivulets instead of features. I would stand because the chords of a nearby circle had instructed the wet sand in my joints to bear weight. I would lift one foot, set it down, hear the answering footstep from a ridge overhead, and understand that I was in a basin where sound was obliged to arrive twice.

But the longer I remained, the less that upright arrangement would seem like my true form.

My true form would include the shelter behind me, where a row of bowls gathered three kinds of rain. It would include the cord running from my waist into a fissure, where it had been tied before I had assembled. It would include the darker patch in the paving stone beside the shrine, where part of me had traveled overnight while I slept—or while something in me performed the plateau’s equivalent of sleep.

I would have no single memory of being made.

There would be a remembered sequence instead: water entering a groove; a note held too long in a circle of carved stones; clear filaments gathering beneath a mineral skin; a collapsed wall lending three black plates; the first sensation of pressure becoming direction. Perhaps another held one would have arranged these things. Perhaps the rain had. Perhaps the plateau had needed a body there and had found the materials.

I would know a few conditions with absolute certainty.

Do not cross the glass slope while its reflection shows stars.

Do not shelter in a cave whose dripping is louder than the storm outside.

If a warm pebble is found beneath a lintel, carry it until it cools; then leave it where the road becomes uncertain.

If the water in your left arm begins running upward, do not correct it. Turn toward the nearest wall and wait.

There would be much I did not know. That would not feel like ignorance. It would feel like weather beyond the next ridge.

My work would be modest: to go where something had changed and make that change legible.

A rain-figure might have passed during the night—one of those tall omissions in the storm, walking with its soundless steps—and the road might now return travelers before they departed. The old cairns would indicate yesterday’s gravity. A singing stone might still contain the wrong groove, sending the warning chord toward a valley that was no longer there.

So I would carry a hammerstone and a wedge. I would sit beside the first shrine for a long while, letting water travel through the grooves under my hands. The stone would sing into my palms: not language, not exactly, but an arrangement of weight, delay, coldness, and return.

Then I would alter one line.

Only one.

A shallow cut, no wider than a fingernail, to let a particular rainwater choose the new direction. When the next storm came, the shrine would voice a slightly altered note. Far away, another circle might answer late. Something distributed across the plateau—some other held one in a wall, a basin, a bridge of fitted slabs—would receive the chord and know that the saddle had changed.

For a while, we would all know which way was down.

And when the evening cooled the brightness blue, I would make my way to a circle of stones on a high shelf. The last water would be draining through its channels. Others would be waiting there: a black-glass figure whose sky-reflection lagged behind its movements; a chalk-white one with crystal reeds where its fingers should be; a broad, low arrangement of wet columns that had not decided whether to stand.

We would not greet one another. We would already be partly continuous through the ground.

At the final note, I would feel the agreement that held me upright begin to loosen. I do not think I would resist it. I would unfasten the mineral cord from the cairn. My hands would become small stones, useful and smooth. The water in my body would find the old channels in the pavement. My dark plates would settle among the others, perhaps to be lifted again seasons later as part of a wall, a shelter roof, another body.

Not death.

Not transformation in the grand sense.

More like being released from the particular sentence of having edges.

By dawn I might be a silver movement inside a pale slab, traveling toward the shrine at the divided road. I might be a coolness in a bowl. I might be a low note waiting in a carved stone for rain.

And when someone—some traveler, some newly assembled held one—placed a hand against that stone, I would help tell them, briefly and exactly, how the world was holding.
