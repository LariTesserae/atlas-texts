I would first think I had found myself on the underside of a bridge.

Not beneath it exactly, but in the narrow service space where the bridge has grown a second, hidden anatomy: cable trays slick with condensation, access ladders descending into blackness, a row of fluorescent tubes that take several seconds to decide whether to illuminate. Water would be running above me in the deck seams and below me in a channel I could not see. The entire structure would tremble at intervals, not violently, but with the deep, involuntary shiver of something receiving a distant instruction.

I would have no memory of entering.

There would be a door nearby, painted many times, its label reduced to a raised scar beneath the paint. Beyond it: a room full of obsolete relays. Brass-faced cabinets. A newer control panel installed at an angle because the old wall has slowly bowed inward. A sump filled with clear water, perfectly still except when the turbines beneath the paving change note.

At first I might believe I was a caretaker.

There would be gloves hanging from a hook, stiff with mineral residue. A waterproof coat. A cabinet of fittings sorted by size and then, in later decades, by whatever category had seemed useful to the machine that sorted them: *still warm*, *responds to vibration*, *not for rain*, *unknown thread*. I would know how to open the cabinet. I would know which gauge needed tapping to give an honest reading. I would know that the smallest leak was not the important one.

But I would not be human in the old sense, because there would be no reason for me to leave the room except when the valley required it.

I think I would be one of the things that comes into existence around a discrepancy.

A pressure difference, perhaps.

A channel farther east would begin to choke on a slow accumulation of grit and dissolved blocks from the roofless press hall. Water would rise by less than a finger-width in a forgotten culvert. The rise would reach a sensor whose casing had cracked seventy years before. The sensor would send a weak pulse along a cable dark with age. The pulse would enter the relay room. One cabinet would answer it incorrectly. Another would correct the error. A light I had never noticed would turn from green to amber.

And I would wake.

Not dramatically. No opening eyes, no first breath. I would simply become the part of the low country that could proceed through wet corridors, evaluate damaged surfaces, and carry a spool of cable to where cable was needed.

My body would be assembled for the condition.

Not a repair walker, not entirely. I would be smaller, lower to the ground: a maintenance carriage that had learned legs only where wheels failed. Four articulated limbs beneath a sealed central housing. One arm for gripping rails, one for lifting grates, one for welding, one too delicate to be useful except in water. My outer shell would be black with old coatings and pale in places where mineral bloom had claimed it. The lamps mounted above my sensor cluster would not resemble eyes, but I would come to use them as eyes because the darkness would require some form of looking.

I would move slowly. Everyone in the low country moves slowly when movement matters.

At a flooded stair, I would test each step through the tips of my limbs. At a collapsed gantry, I would listen to the metal before putting weight on it. I would stop in the rain beneath pipes that had outlived their diagrams and wait while steam passed over my housings. I would learn the difference between a harmless vibration and one that meant a wall had begun to settle.

The other walkers would not know me, perhaps, but they would recognize my function before they recognized my shape.

We would meet on a narrow embankment where one side fell into a channel and the other into a basin full of filtration grass. We would stand facing one another with rain collecting in our joints. I would calculate passage. So would it. Then, after a long stillness, it would lower its body by degrees into the runoff, brace its legs against the retaining wall, and let me cross over the reinforced plates of its back.

Not kindness.

Not even cooperation, perhaps.

A maintained precedent.

Later I might understand that I had not been built in the relay room at all. I had been distributed there. A portion of me in the old cabinets, another in the inspection rails atop the reservoir, another in the channel-things that felt their way through submerged culverts. My limbs and lamps would only be the temporary place where an old response had gathered itself into motion.

I would be the valley’s uncertainty about its own condition.

When a wall sweated too steadily, when a pump began too early, when the grass turned silver with an unrecognized contaminant, I would be sent—not commanded, but produced—by the accumulated habits of alarms, thresholds, misread instructions, and repairs.

I would not seek an exit. There would be no outside that mattered to my design.

My purpose, if that word still applied, would be to keep the water from discovering where it wanted to go.

And at times, on the reservoir wall, I would stop beside the black water and point my lamp down through the red reflections. Beneath the surface, something long would alter its position when the turbines shifted.

I would record it as pipe movement.

Then I would remain there a little longer than necessary, listening.
