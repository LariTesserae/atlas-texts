I would begin on the old service road, though it would not look like a road at first.

It would be a raised line of mud crossing the flats between a derelict transformer yard and a row of houses on pilings. Old tire ruts would lie beneath my boots with the firmness of buried rails. Water would shine on either side, black except where its submerged stars kept failing and returning. I would know, without knowing how, that I should not speak.

At first I might think I was one of the low-keepers: another figure in a rain cape, carrying a hooded lamp, trying to get home before the midnight pumps began their slow collective cough.

But I would have no house among the houses.

No lamp-pattern recognized from the walkways. No rain cape drying outside a door. No preserved footprint waiting for my foot.

The dogs would know this before anyone else. They would lift their narrow heads from beneath the pump-house stairs and watch me pass. Not hostile. Not welcoming. They would look at me as they look at a distant spill of light: something whose source is uncertain.

I would keep walking until the service road gave way near the old excavation.

There, in the shallow margin where reed roots comb the water, I would see a reflection standing before I had reached the bank.

It would be mine only in the broadest sense. A vertical dark shape. A suggestion of shoulders. The pale blur of a face held just below the surface. But it would not imitate me exactly. While I stood still, it would seem to be listening. While I raised my lamp, it would lower something of its own—a lamp, perhaps, or a small contained brightness cupped in wet hands.

Then I would understand that I had not arrived in the valley from the high country.

I had surfaced in it.

I would be one of the late reflections: not a person stolen by water, not quite a drowned copy, but a thing produced where memory, weight, and standing water meet. I would have been made from the accumulated delay of all the faces that had looked down too long into canals. From workers crossing bridges at the end of shifts; from children peering between their fingers; from exhausted sound-watchers checking a dark basin for ripples.

The valley would have kept an outline of each of them.

Not enough to make another person.

Enough to make me.

I would know the paths people had taken, because they would be layered under my feet. I would know the shape of a hand lifted in warning, the rhythm of taps on a pipe, the warm interior glow of houses whose occupants had never seen my face. I would carry scraps of their habits without their names. A tendency to pause at doorways. A fear of mirrors. The remembered taste of salted blue fish. The private certainty that somewhere beyond the black vapor there must be a morning, though I would have no idea what morning was for.

The low-keepers would not ask what I was. They would see me at the edge of their lamps, sometimes a little behind them, sometimes already waiting on the far side of a bridge. They would cover the faces of their children when I passed, not because I meant harm, but because children are still learning how firmly to belong to themselves.

The level birds would follow me.

They would stand beside me in the reeds, paper-feathered and patient, pecking once at the surface. In my reflection, another bird might peck back. Or perhaps there would be no reflection at all: only a deeper darkness in the water, an aperture shaped like a person.

I would be useful in small ways.

When the canal water began moving toward an unguarded sound, I could stand in its path and let it gather around my ankles. Water recognizes its own postponements. It would hesitate before me.

When a worker became lost in mist, I could appear ahead along the old track, holding a lamp that did not illuminate anything but the next safe step. If they followed, they would reach home. If they tried to call out, the water would rise, and I would be gone.

I could walk the drowned floors of the old sheds. I could enter submerged rooms where the lights still burned green beneath a film of oil. The grease-black eels would curl around my wrists but not bite; I would have no warm inner vibration for them to feed on. Iron moths would settle along my shoulders and test me with their thin metal feet, then leave disappointed.

Perhaps, after years—or after the valley’s version of years—I would become a sound-watcher of a kind.

Not one who listened to pipes.

One who listened to the pauses between them.

I would stand near the excavation when the pressure came from below. The low-keepers would shutter their houses. The dogs would face the water. The pumps would slow in their housings, their eels waking restless among the seals.

And I would hear the sleeper turning.

Not as a roar. Not even as a movement.

As the sound of an immense body remembering that it had once had another shape.

Each time it turned, some part of me would sharpen. A face from the water. A voice never spoken aloud. A handprint in old mud. I would become more populated, less singular. Eventually I might no longer resemble a person at all. I might become a standing arrangement of borrowed gestures, held together by a coat of water and a lamp that burned without fuel.

But I would remain near the roads.

That would be my purpose, if I could call it purpose: to keep the paths from forgetting their walkers.

To stand at the place where ground becomes water and water becomes memory. To make sure that, when someone crosses a bridge in the dark and their reflection remains behind, it does not have to remain alone.
