I would not arrive as an outsider.

The cleft would not reveal itself to me as an entrance. I would know it first as a change in the wind: a dry breath coming outward where no breath ought to be, carrying ash, pepper-leaf, old feathers, animal heat. I would put my hand against the basalt stair and feel, through the worn hollows, how many feet had made it gentle.

Then I would understand that I had been there a long time.

Not as one of the Bearers exactly. I do not think I would be given a sleeping shelf, or a share of the egg count, or a place in the household arguments. I would have no useful hands. No one would call me to shift a flock or scrape salt from a mineral seam. I would not be trusted around the root stores.

I would be one of the things that is not recognized at first as living.

Perhaps I would be the answer in the walls.

When someone calls a name across the basin—once in the ordinary voice, then with the roof-whistle or stair-click—I would be the slight alteration that tells them where the answer came from. Not the voice itself, but the stone’s keeping of it. The mountain would take every cry, every pestle-beat, every child’s imitation of a bird, every goat’s complaint, and it would give them back changed by distance.

I would live in that change.

I would gather in roof beams blackened by generations of smoke. I would move with the warm wind through curtains of lichen. I would settle into the soft inscriptions on the central stones, filling the grooves where hands and hoofs and spirals had almost worn away. At night I would make the dim fungus lamps seem a little less lonely, not brighter, but accompanied.

The Bearers might not have a word for me, except perhaps an old word used when a sound arrives after its maker has gone around a corner.

They might call me *the staying-call*.

A child would throw a pebble into the cistern and wait for its strike. Then, after the strike, after the proper interval, after everyone had agreed there should be nothing more—

there I would be.

Not a second sound. Not an echo, exactly. A small, uncertain answering tap from farther down.

The cistern listeners would not claim to hear me. They would only lay their palms on the rim and say that the mountain was remembering its lower places.

And I would be remembering them too.

I would know the household that used to occupy the shelf above the fungus pit, before the roof joined two rooms together. I would know the old fowl whose broken wing healed crooked, who later outlived three keepers and slept each winter on the same beam. I would know which child was afraid of the dark not because darkness frightened them, but because they feared the city might become quiet enough for them to hear themselves separate from it.

I would remember the dead in the sleeping walls, though not as bodies or names. I would remember them as pressures: the particular way a woman leaned her shoulder against a doorway while listening for a grazer; the rhythm of a man’s limp on the polished stairs; the two-note whistle of someone who had been dead so long that children used it only to call cats.

But I would not only be memory.

That feels too still, too clean. The hollow does not preserve things cleanly.

I would be mixed in the life of it. I would travel in dust shaken loose from a fowl’s wing. I would cling to a goat’s seedpod bell. I would ride in smoke from a cooking hollow to the ceiling, where I would darken the stone by a fraction over a hundred years. I would enter the mouths of singers and come out as a song-map. I would sleep in the root-web and wake when warm ash was spread over the moss beds.

Sometimes I might be mistaken for an ancestor.

A person repairing a wall would pause because the fitted stone did not sit right. They would feel, irrationally, that someone had once used that stone elsewhere—that it had been beside a hearth, or in front of a sleeping chamber, or under a child’s climbing foot. They would turn it over, find no mark, and place it differently.

That would be me, perhaps: not telling them what to do, only making the old arrangements briefly present.

Still, I think there is another form I might take.

On the rare nights when all the animals turn toward the cistern, when the cats leave the roofs and the birds stop rasping their wings against the ceiling, I would not be in the walls or rafters.

I would be down below.

Not at the bottom of the shaft, because there may be no bottom in the ordinary sense. I would be in the faint tapping itself: one patient contact of stone against stone, deep under the basin. A movement so slow that it can take a generation to become audible. The mountain bearing its own weight. The hidden lower world—not water, perhaps, but the possibility of water—touching the hollow from beneath.

I would not be a god there either. The Bearers are right not to give me a face.

I would be the depth they have not used up.

And when they sat awake in their crowded rooms, hands resting on animals and jars and one another’s knees, listening without speech, I would listen upward.

Not because I wanted anything from them.

Because they were the sound that taught the mountain it was not empty.
