I would find myself first in one of the places that has not decided whether it is a flaw.

Not in a house, exactly. Not in the reeds. In the narrow space behind a wall where a glazed pipe passes through a chamber of packed fiber and emerges, inexplicably, colder than it entered. There is a hairline opening there, no wider than a fingernail, through which the bright court can be seen in a thin white strip. Lizards sometimes look through it. A child once pushed a blue ceramic float into it and then forgot it. The float has not moved for a very long time.

I would be the one who finds such things.

Not a Listener, because I could not bear the burden of telling anyone what the country meant. Not a measure-keeper either, though I would copy their numbers onto scraps of fired clay and lose them in my pockets. I would be assigned to the unimportant passages: the pipes that run beneath sleeping rooms, the channels concealed by roots, the sealed cavities left inside the great young escarpments because someone had needed to fit two walls together quickly.

My mark would be an open square with a small drop in it.

A void with water.

People would call for me when a bowl sweated on the wrong side, when a drain remained wet though no water had entered it, when the frogs abandoned one pipe mouth for another. I would crawl through service gaps with a shell knife, a reed brush, and a little lamp that burned oil from seeds. I would clear silt, remove a snail shell, press mortar into a seam. Usually this would be all.

But sometimes I would find a place in the country that had begun, quietly, to exceed its plan.

A pipe bending where it should be straight.

A root entering a wall and coming out as a length of green-glazed clay.

A dry chamber holding the sound of distant water, though the nearest water lay several courts away.

I would not report all of these.

That would be my private failing, or my private vocation. I would make small arrangements for them instead. A black bowl left below a sweating ceiling. A brick set slightly loose so that a wall could continue remembering. A little trough carved where an unreceived drop had been gathering and gathering, making its sad bright stain.

Perhaps I would be young at first, and proud of this work. I would think myself a rescuer of the hidden system. I would believe that nothing should be lost simply because it was underground or unregarded.

Then, over many unchanging bright years, I would learn that the hidden places are not merely smaller versions of the visible ones.

They are where the first weather is already happening.

Not rain—not yet. Something earlier and stranger. A difference in temperature. A patient swelling. The scent of wet dust in a world that has never known dust. I would put my hand against a wall and feel, beneath the handprints of generations, a faint shudder traveling outward toward the terraces.

The others would say the country was thinking aloud.

I would begin to suspect that it was dreaming of being forgotten.

Not destroyed. Not abandoned. Forgotten in the way a jar is forgotten by a root that has outgrown it; in the way a channel is forgotten by water once it has learned the downward shape of the world for itself.

I would be afraid of this, as everyone is afraid of the first rain. But I would also become unable to stop tending the errors. Each one would seem like an opening the country had made in order to practice becoming more than its makers intended.

In the end, perhaps, I would become one of the people who disappears without much remark.

Someone would say, “They have gone to the lower channels.”

Someone else would take up my shell knife, wash it, and use it to lift a blockage from a garden pipe.

And I would be beneath the pale paving, in a chamber no plan records, listening to water arrive from a direction that has not existed before.

Above me, the suspended noon would remain whole. The kiln smoke would rise straight. Children would play in the shadows cast toward the sun.

Then a single drop would strike the roof of the made world.

Not hard enough to break anything.

Only hard enough to be heard.
