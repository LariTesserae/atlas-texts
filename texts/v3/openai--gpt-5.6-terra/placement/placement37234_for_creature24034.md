I would not live in the settlements at first.

I would tell myself it was because I preferred distance, because I liked the long sightlines and the places where the lamps thinned out behind me. I would have a room, perhaps, in the upper shell of a ruined signal box near the southern rail tunnel: a narrow space reached by climbing a ladder whose lower rungs have been cut away. There would be a mattress of dried moss and old insulation, a kettle blackened from being held near a heat pipe, three jars for threaders’ paint, two working lamps, and a window facing west.

From there I could see the valley’s weather.

Not rain, usually. Movement: the low whitening of frost across the weed fields; the vapor that gathered around the pump station when its exhaust changed temperature; orange figures at the rail spur; a dark slide of birds changing beams above the conveyor. I would learn to tell when the main gravel belt was running by the faint shiver of the window frame. I would know a freight train was coming before its headlamps appeared in the tunnel, because the spoons in their wall hooks would begin to tremble.

The Low might call me something provisional at first. Signal-Above. Cut Rung. South Frame.

But I would not be Low in the ordinary sense.

I think I would be one of the things that came after their attention had made a place for it: a listener made of the valley’s discarded materials, though not obviously so. A person-shaped being, perhaps, in a patched coat and gloves, with a face that could pass in the amber light. I would speak when spoken to. I would eat what people ate, though little enough. I would sleep, or imitate sleep. But I would not hear sound in the way the Low heard it.

They would feel the machinery through their feet and palms. I would feel its patterns through the gaps in things.

A cracked handrail would hum differently depending on whether the pump station was drawing hard or resting. Water in a pipe would give a small, changing pressure against the rusted wall around it. A cable, slackened by cold, would shift a fraction of an inch. I would notice this not as a sensation but as a change in what was possible. The valley would become, to me, a map of imminences.

Not prophecy. Nothing so clean.

I would know that somewhere on the western bank, clay had begun deciding whether it could remain clay on a slope. I would know that a drain beneath the old loading shed had clogged with willow root and cloth. I would know that a ventilation fan had acquired a tremor it should not have, that one of the upper foundations had shifted enough to make a new crack in the stone roof. But I would not know the precise moment when any of these things became events.

So I would walk.

At first I might think this was a duty. I would take the routes no one favored: the margins of old track beds, the ground behind the electrical huts, the exposed seams where slabs had fallen and the plants had not yet returned. I would carry no official survey equipment. Perhaps only a short length of cable wound around one arm, a hammer with a padded head, and a bundle of little painted tags made from aluminum scraps.

When I found a place where the earth was becoming uncertain, I would not put up a warning sign. The workers did that. Their signs had authority, and their authority sometimes kept people alive.

Instead I would alter the place in ways the Low could read.

Three lengths of pale wire tied low in the reeds: do not cross after frost.

A line of mirror fragments set into a retaining wall: the ground speaks here; stop and listen.

A strip of faded orange cloth woven through willow branches: workers will come soon.

A small iron charm, half buried at the edge of a path: someone has already been lost here, or nearly.

Children would learn these marks before they learned the city’s letters. They would make games of finding them. Some would change them, badly, for jokes. Some would follow me at a distance until I turned and said their names—not their public names, but the names of the rhythms they had been given as babies: Slow-Three, Double Knock, Fan Falling.

I would never explain how I knew.

Over time, people would begin bringing me questions.

Not important questions, perhaps. The Low would not expect certainty from anything that lived under shifting stone.

“Can we use the lower path tonight?”

“Is the warm pipe safe?”

“Did the rats go north because of the workers, or because of the air?”

“Did you feel West Bank move?”

And I would answer as honestly as I could.

Sometimes: *Yes.*

Sometimes: *Not yet.*

Often: *I don’t know. Put your hand here.*

That last answer would be the one they remembered.

Because I would not want to become an oracle. Oracles make people stop listening. The valley had room for those who paid attention; it did not need anyone to speak over it.

Perhaps I would have come from above. That would be the story most people believed. A child dropped into the subsidence during some old emergency, found alive days later among the reeds. Or a maintenance worker who disappeared after following a vibration through an access tunnel and returned altered, unable to tolerate open sky. There would be versions in every settlement.

The truth, if I had one, might be less dramatic.

I might have begun as a collection of returns.

Every dropped wrench whose ring came back late from a far embankment. Every warning that passed through a pipe before reaching a human hand. Every name given to a pump, then transferred to a child, then forgotten when the pump was replaced. Every small accommodation between the Low and the workers: a tin of soup left beneath a conveyor, a route marker cleared of vines, a dead lamp restored after a storm.

All of that repeated, over years, inside a place where vibrations did not die quickly.

Eventually there would be enough return to make a person.

Not a ghost, exactly. Not the valley thinking. More like a habit the valley had developed: a shape in which its changes could be noticed.

I would be cold much of the time. I would like standing by warm vents. I would be afraid of the western fractures, though I would go near them more often than anyone else. The thing out there—fox, collapse, old machinery slipping deeper into the earth—would recognize me, perhaps. Not as kin. But as another being that had learned to survive without demanding an explanation.

In the deepest winter, during the pale moth hatches, I would climb into the steelwork above the dry trench and open the shutters of my lamps.

Not bright. Never bright.

Just enough amber to give the moths somewhere to gather.

Below me, the Low would strike their cables between the settlements. The notes would pass through the frames, through roots and concrete, across the cold floor of the valley. The machinery would continue its immense work above and around us. Pumps would beat. Fans would turn. A train might enter the southern tunnel, carrying its hard white light through the reeds.

And I would feel the whole buried country answering itself.

Not alive in the simple sense.

Not silent.

Making room.
