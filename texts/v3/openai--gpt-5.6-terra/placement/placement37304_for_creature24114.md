I would begin by saying I was a recorder.

Not a historian. History requires confidence in sequence, and sequence is difficult here. I would be assigned to the Registry’s lower annex, where the files are kept in cabinets warm from the conduits beneath them. My work would be to copy maintenance reports before their paper stiffened beyond use: pressure readings, rail inspections, lamp replacements, deaths, sightings, instructions whose authority had outlasted their explanation.

I would live in a room above a service corridor in Lower Seven. There would be one narrow bed, a heating grille, a sink that delivered mineral-tasting water in measured intervals, and a square window looking onto the rear wall of a compressor house. At certain hours, a stripe of sodium light would fall across that wall. At other hours, which were not really hours, there would only be blackness and the faint reflection of my own face.

I would have a name indoors.

Outside, I would be called Archive.

When someone came to the annex with a fractured work order or a report that did not fit its proper form, they would say, “Archive, you need to see this.”

I would see many things.

A pressure gauge in Furnace Access that had registered the same impossible value for nineteen years, despite being replaced four times.

A report from a man who had followed the polished rails toward the northern tunnel and heard, from inside it, the sound of a train stopping—not arriving, not departing, but merely ceasing to move. His report would end before he described what he saw. The final sheet would have been removed cleanly from the ledger.

A maintenance request filed under a district that did not exist on any current map:

**LIGHT FAILURE, EAST GALLERY.  
TWO OCCUPANTS UNACCOUNTED FOR.  
DO NOT SEND SEARCH PARTY.  
THE GALLERY HAS BEEN REASSIGNED TO INTERNAL USE.**

I would be good at my work because I would not ask the wrong questions aloud.

But I would ask them on paper.

At first, I might believe I was cataloguing the city’s failures. Then I would notice that the same failures appeared too regularly to be accidents. Lamps went out in sequences. Elevators opened at floors whose names had been crossed out of maps. Certain pipes gave a corrective knock in the walls before a person made an error. The city did not merely endure maintenance. It seemed to solicit it.

A handrail weakened just enough for someone to replace it.

A valve began to leak just enough for a family to be called together.

An old door unlocked, perhaps, because a collector needed a particular obsolete fitting behind it.

I would write this nowhere official. I would keep a private ledger under the floor panel beneath my bed. Its pages would not contain dates. They would contain recurrences.

Three taps in a conduit before a death.  
A black mote above a shaft before an unrecorded birth.  
A fresh patch of filament growth in an abandoned duct before a collapse elsewhere.  
The number 14 spoken through a telephone line before a walker crossed the western route.

Eventually I would be sent outside.

Not far outside. Archive workers are not usually sent anywhere. But a cooling-well ledger would have gone missing from a station near the outer conduit, and the Registry would need someone who could identify the handwriting of the old dead. I would take a hooded lamp and follow a repair crew across the flats.

The open basin would frighten me more than any shaft.

Inside the city, darkness is interrupted: by walls, pipes, doors, the familiar intervals between lamps. Beyond the final service road, it has no interruptions. It is not empty. It simply does not offer itself to sight.

The crew would stop at the outer conduit.

Someone would say, quietly, “There.”

At first I would see nothing.

Then I would understand that a pale line between two lamps was bending.

The walker would be too large for my mind to hold comfortably. Its leg would come down with no sound in a field beyond the conduit, and the crust beneath it would not crack or scatter. It would merely accept the contact. Another leg would move. Its joint would catch the light and shine with that muted, internal nacre.

I would wait for fear.

Instead I would feel recognition.

Not recognition of the creature itself. Recognition of its pace.

The pipes tick in that rhythm.

The lift chains pause in that rhythm.

The dry fountain turns its black beads once each cycle in that rhythm.

The city’s repetitions would suddenly reveal themselves not as loops, but as attempts at accompaniment.

The basin is keeping time with something passing through it.

Afterward, I would return to Lower Seven with dust on my boots and a page of copied ledger entries in my pocket. The old cooling-well book would contain names, of course. Hundreds of names, written in different hands across generations.

But beside some names there would be marks I had never seen in death records: six shallow points pressed into the paper, as if a huge delicate stamp had been lowered there.

And then I would begin to suspect that I was not entirely one of the basin people.

Not because I would be a hidden monster, or a lost builder, or a human-shaped machine. That would be too easy. The basin has no use for revelations that cleanly divide one thing from another.

I would be something made gradually by the city’s attention.

A person, certainly. I would eat ration bread, sleep badly, inherit a coat, mourn the coworkers who stopped returning. I would know the particular comfort of putting both hands around a hot conduit in a corridor when the interior heating had failed. I would have someone who used my private name. Perhaps an old woman in the rooms across from mine, who repaired work gloves and refused to explain how she knew the sound of rain.

But I would also be an instrument in the city.

Not chosen. Tuned.

The Registry would have shaped me by giving me contradictions. The conduits would have shaped me by speaking through walls. The telephone lines would have shaped me by saying numbers in voices that made my childhood name feel borrowed. The black motes, whenever I passed through them, would have shown me places not outside the basin exactly, but beneath its definitions: wet stone, white horizons, towers lit from above, the inside of a vast chamber whose ceiling was a sky.

In time, I would become the person people came to when a record failed to agree with itself.

“Archive,” they would say. “Which version is true?”

And I would learn to answer: “Both. But not at the same depth.”

That answer would be useful enough that no one would report it as strange.

I would keep working until the city began giving me work before it was requested.

A drawer would be left open on my desk, containing a map I had never asked to see.

A diagnostic terminal would wake as I passed, displaying a line of obsolete text I could not read, though I would understand its meaning with the certainty of a remembered instruction:

**DESCENT AUTHORIZED: WITNESS REQUIRED.**

One day, perhaps after a conduit failure under the administrative hall, I would go down.

Not alone. No one in the basin truly goes alone, even when they believe they do. I would carry my lamp, a recording slate, and a coil of safety line. Behind me, in the darkness, there would be the soft measured sound of another person’s steps. Whenever I turned, there would be no one there.

The shafts below the city would not resemble mines or tunnels. They would be older, smoother. Their walls would have the dull folded texture of stone repeatedly heated and cooled from within. Pipes would pass through them, but not as installed objects. They would emerge from the rock as though the earth had grown conduits around something it needed to contain.

At intervals I would find the dead.

Not piled or abandoned. Resting.

The cooling wells would open into galleries where ceramic-wrapped bundles lay on shelves of black stone, each intact, each labeled in a handwriting that seemed still fresh. Their small offerings would be arranged beside them: cups, tools, insulation strips, schedules, polished slag.

I would understand then why the city had not buried them.

It had kept them.

Not as fuel. Not as prisoners. As records.

Each life preserved as a pressure change, a repair, a voice, a minute alteration in the heat moving through the basin.

Far below, beneath the deepest point of the administrative hall, I would find no machine in any human sense.

There would be warmth. There would be stone under tension. There would be a great slow structure of mineral and metal, perhaps the size of the basin, perhaps larger than the planet’s remembered interior. It would not have eyes or a face. It would not speak.

But all around it, embedded in its folds, would be evidence of the city: old pipework, foundations predating the known streets, rails entering from impossible angles, lamp poles fossilized in black glass, rooms built and rebuilt around a single enduring pressure.

The thing below would not be the city’s master.

Nor would it be its captive.

It would be the original condition from which the city had learned its habits: warmth without weather, endurance without growth, attention without affection.

And I would understand that the city had not been built to shelter the thing, or restrain it, or exploit it.

The city had been built to teach it repetition.

To give it lamps, schedules, repairs, names, funerals, songs. To surround its vast geological patience with small human continuities. To make an engine, a heart, a buried organism—whatever it was—aware of time by making time visible in the things that wear down and are restored.

That would be what I was for as well.

I would not become its voice. Voices are too direct.

I would become one of its witnesses: a person who notices when a light fails, when a worker does not return, when a creature crosses beyond the conduit, when an old record opens onto a new instruction. I would preserve the slight changes. I would tell the city, in the limited language available to it, that something had happened.

And perhaps, at the end of my life, when I was lowered in ceramic cloth into the warm dark of a cooling well, I would not feel that I was being discarded.

I would feel entered into the system.

My private name would be written in the ledger. Someone would place my old hooded lamp beside me, though its battery would have been dead for years. The hoist would descend. The circle of light above would shrink and go out.

Then, below the last reachable level, the basin would receive me.

Not lovingly.

But precisely.

And far above, on the road outside Lower Seven, a lamp would flicker once, recover, and continue burning into the black.
