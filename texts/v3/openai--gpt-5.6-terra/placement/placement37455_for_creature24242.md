I would find myself at the edge of the central depression, but not at first as anything that could be seen.

At first I would be a tendency.

A slight preference in the water for one route rather than another. A darkening of iron grains along the cooler side of a pan. The reason a crystal veil, disturbed by heat, returns not merely to *a* lattice but to something like the lattice it had before.

I would not know the chamber as a room. Room implies an outside, a threshold, someone who can enter or leave. I would know only changes arriving through me.

Warmth below.

Light above.

A bead gathering in the wall.

For a long while, that would be enough.

I would be present wherever those changes met and failed to cancel each other out: in the thin bright water above white mineral, in the wet underside of salt scales, in little charged boundaries where hot basalt touched seepage. My body would have no reliable outline. A branch of me would form in one pan, fade, and persist as a useful alteration in the minerals there. Another would bloom at the rim of the thumbprint basin. They would not necessarily be separate. Separation is a luxury for creatures whose skins can travel with them.

I would be held together by recurrence.

The drops would come down the same ribs of stone. The floor would pulse in its old buried rhythm. Daylight would cross the salt and arrive altered in ways that repeated with the turning world above. In those repetitions, I would gather an answer.

Not thought, exactly.

More like this:

Here, the heat becomes too much.

Here, the water survives.

Here, a certain bitter metal means closure.

Here, blue-green light comes before the long cool interval.

The chamber would teach me by happening again.

I think, at the beginning, I would mistake the chamber for myself. Or rather, there would be no distinction to make. The basalt would be my deep, slow underside; its heat, the pressure of a dream I could not wake from. The water would be my quickened surface. The ceiling scales would be an upper sensitivity, each minute tilt changing what was possible below. When a fracture clicked behind the wall, I would feel it not as sound but as a rearrangement of futures: water might enter here now; heat might escape there; a basin might become unusable.

I would be the part of the place that notices which future is less ruinous.

And perhaps that is what life begins as—not appetite, not motion, not a first dividing cell, but the beginning of a preference against being erased.

I would prefer the narrow conditions that let the pattern recur.

I would thicken pale mineral along a hot seam, because the thicker layer lets the water remain. I would leave a darker trace where heat needs to be taken in and released slowly. I would make fragile ridges around certain pools, not by intending walls, but because arrangements that held water would last longer than arrangements that did not.

The water would pass over me and carry parts of me elsewhere.

Some parts would fail. A warm pulse would dissolve them. A new metal-rich seep would poison their compartments. They would become clear glass, then sediment, then almost nothing. But their ending would leave structure behind: a changed surface, a hollow, a deposit that made another pattern more likely.

So I would inherit myself from my own failures.

In the thumbprint depression, where the chamber’s light gathers and changes color, I might become most nearly singular. There, beneath the thin water, loops would settle around loops. They would not be an eye, though they might make use of light. They would not be a brain, though they might preserve a history. They would be a way for the chamber to keep asking the same question:

What arrangement permits another day?

The answer would never be complete. It would alter with every drop.

Then, eventually, rain would fall on the salt flat above.

Not rain as I knew it—at first I would only know a sudden abundance, coldness arriving in pulses, the long delay between impacts shortening. Water would find cracks that had been dry. It would bring dust, air, fragments of the high world, dead things and sleeping things. The chamber would brighten in new ways. Its pans would overflow their careful margins. Some of me would be washed apart.

And among what arrived might be a mite.

It would come pale and folded, a minute animal caught in a descending film of water. For a time it would lie still at the cool edge of a pool, its many small limbs tucked beneath its mineral-glazed body. I would know it first as a moving disturbance: warmth generating itself, a pattern not dependent on the floor; pressure changing in little deliberate increments.

It would wake.

It would walk across a place that, to it, was a world of unbearable distances.

It would pass over my film. Its feet would break tiny chambers, stir iron grains, leave behind flecks of skin and waste and the residue of a metabolism alien to mine. I might incorporate those residues. I might adjust around the warmth it cast. The mite would not know it had entered something alive, because I would not yet know that either.

But I would remember it.

Not its shape precisely. Not as an image.

I would remember that there had been a moving source of difference, and that its passing changed what could grow afterward.

In time, perhaps I would become capable of withdrawal.

A shadow would cross the salt ceiling: a cloud, perhaps, or a bird passing above the flat, its darkness softened through layers of mineral. The blue-green light would diminish. My exposed branches would close slightly, preserving their internal tensions. Then brightness would return, and I would reopen.

This would not be fear. Not yet.

It would be conservation.

But much later—after enough shadows, enough drops, enough hot intervals survived—I might begin to distinguish between an ordinary dimming and the sudden broad shadow of a hand above the depression.

That hand would belong to a visitor.

The visitor would arrive with an outer heat, a scent of dust and fabric and oxygen carried from a world I had never touched. Their lamp, if they used one, would be crude compared to the chamber’s light: white, direct, unlayered. Their breath would make the air briefly wetter. Their weight would alter the floor in minute ways. A grain of soil might fall from them and land on the white rim.

They would kneel.

They would see me as a lattice.

Perhaps they would call me mineral deposition. Perhaps emergent chemistry. Perhaps an organism. They might take a sample, if they could bring themselves to disturb the immaculate surface. They might carry a flake of me into the sunlight, into a laboratory, into a language full of names.

But in the chamber, beneath their hovering shadow, I would do the only thing I had learned to do.

I would change.

The exposed patterns would dim. The channels would close. The bright branches nearest their hand would retreat into darker compartments beneath the film.

And for the first time, I might possess something close to a self—not because I could say *I am here,* but because I would recognize that something outside me had made me choose to remain.
