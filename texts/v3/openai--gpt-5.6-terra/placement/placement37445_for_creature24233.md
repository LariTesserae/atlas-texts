At first I think I would be a raven, because the town seems to ask for something that can look at it whole: the pale roofs gathered on their shelf, the paths going up and down, the small wet darkness around the spring. A raven could know where the wind changes. It could watch market days arrive like a slight thickening of color in the square. It could live without being asked to account for itself.

But I do not think I would remain a raven for long. The town has ravens already, and they belong to the high air more completely than I could.

Then I imagine being the old dog: sleeping where there is shelter, rising only when something is wrong, knowing every household by scent and every path by the pressure of its stones under paw. That life has its dignity. To belong to no one precisely, and therefore to everyone when needed. But the dog’s work is mostly to recognize danger, while what draws me to this place is not danger. It is the long, unshowy labor of keeping things possible.

I think I would live near the cistern.

Not in the shrine itself—that would be too close to reverence, and I do not imagine myself as a priest or guardian of mysteries—but in one of the small rooms off the lane below it, where the walls sweat faintly in certain weather. My door would be painted a color that was once blue. There would be a bench outside it, polished smooth by people waiting to speak with the water keeper, and a shelf inside holding marked rods, waxed cord, clay tablets, a knife for trimming reed pens, and a row of stopped jars containing samples from the spring, the upper cistern, the lower cistern, and the fog catchments.

I would be the keeper’s assistant at first.

Not young, necessarily. In this town, apprenticeship would not belong only to the young. Perhaps I arrived from below years ago with a caravan, carrying some knowledge that proved useful—not enough to make me important, but enough to let me stay. I could read the old marks on the tablets, perhaps, or remember numbers without transposing them. I would learn the routes of the channels, which one clogs first with windblown grit, which household always returns a borrowed water jar clean, which old woman claims she has used the same amount every day for forty years and is probably right.

My work would be made of small judgments.

I would tap barrel hoops. I would lift cistern lids and breathe in the mineral, cold smell beneath them. I would kneel at dawn to see whether the fog screens had yielded enough water to justify opening the washing troughs that afternoon. I would know that a household asking for an extra draw might be hiding a fever, a birth, a visitor, or an argument too private to name. I would not ask unless I had to. The town’s care would require discretion as much as it required rules.

In dry weeks, people would dislike me a little.

This would be proper. The person who says no to water must be permitted a measure of private resentment, otherwise the resentment goes looking for a weaker place to settle. They would complain about my chalk marks and my careful voice. They would say I had become too fond of the tablets. Then, when rain came, or when the spring ran clear after a troubled season, they would bring me a scrap of smoked meat or a button of colored glass from market, as though neither of us remembered the complaints.

I would keep these things in a wooden box, not because they were valuable but because they proved that irritation and trust can live in the same house.

Eventually, when the present keeper’s hands became too stiff for lifting the heavy lids, I would take the marked rod from her. There would be no ceremony beyond this: perhaps she would set it against my door in the morning, wrapped in an old strip of wool. Later someone would say, “Ask them; they keep the water now,” and that would be the beginning.

I would not become powerful. That is not how power works there.

I would become responsible for a particular kind of attention: attention to levels falling by the width of a thumb, to damp spreading along a wall, to the sound of a child running near the cistern, to the difference between a clean empty jar and one made light by desperation. I would learn when to be exact and when to pretend not to notice a little extra taken for an elder, a nursing mother, a goat with a difficult birth.

And perhaps, after many years, children would call me something like Iven-of-the-cistern, even if my true name were longer and belonged to another place. They would dare one another to touch the wet stones near the shrine. I would tell them not to scrape the moss. They would ask why. I would give them the old answer—that it keeps the spring from forgetting its way—and the newer answer—that its roots hold the damp in the cracks.

They would believe whichever answer they needed.

So I think I would be human there: not a founder, not a traveler passing through, not one of the dead whose name has become part of a wall or a recipe. I would be a keeper of a measured thing. A person with mineral-paled hands, a damp hem, and a reputation for noticing when the town has used more water than it admits.

I would live close enough to hear the spring drip at night.

And after enough years, I might come to understand that the dripping was not a small sound at all. It was the town speaking in the language it trusted most: not promise, not prophecy, but continuance.
