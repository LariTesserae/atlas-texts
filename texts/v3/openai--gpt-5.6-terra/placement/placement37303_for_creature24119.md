I would not arrive as a person, at least not at first.

A person in that basin is always visible. A person carries a shadow, makes decisions that matter too loudly, opens doors, starts engines, leaves tracks that fill with dust. To be human there would mean being answerable to thirst in a deliberate way: measuring it, rationing it, arguing with pumps, reading clouds, knowing which sound in the night is a loose panel and which is an animal at the valve.

I think I would begin lower down.

I would be one of the dark beetles that climbs before dawn.

Not an emblematic beetle, not a miraculous jewel among the stones—just a small, hard-bodied thing with a shell black enough to hold the last of the night. I would live in a crack in basalt near the processing shed, beneath a lip of stone that stays cool for perhaps an hour after sunrise. My world would be gradients: shade to heat, wet metal to dry metal, the smell of fox passage, the tremor of boots through the ground.

Before the sun cleared the escarpment, I would emerge.

I would cross the dust past a discarded wrench, climb the base of a weather mast, then the cold rail itself. Above me the instrument cups would be still. The cableway would not yet be moving. The valley would be holding its breath in that narrow interval when it is briefly possible to believe the day will remain gentle.

Condensation would gather first where the metal bends.

A line of droplets on the underside of a rail. A bead swelling on a bolt head. A small clear weight caught in the seam of a pipe. I would wait beneath it with every part of myself attentive. Then it would touch my foreleg, cool as an underground thing, and I would draw it in before it fell.

This would be enough.

Not abundance. Nothing in the valley believes in abundance. Enough is a different condition: a droplet held, a crack found, a day crossed without being eaten.

But perhaps, after a while, I would become something less small.

Not the beetle exactly. Not the worker either. I would become the person who maintains the drip pans.

There is someone in the settlement who understands that the valley’s official water system is only part of the water system. They know the seep beneath the salt, the leak at the bad valve, the overnight sweating of pipes, the way dew forms on one side of the pumping house and never on the other. They know which troughs the grazers have learned to use, and which ones must be covered because foxes have begun to fall in.

They do not think of themselves as a keeper of animals. They would deny it if asked.

They are a technician, perhaps. Their work order says something about intake, filtration, pressure, mineral load. Their hands smell of rubber gasket, coffee grounds, hot copper, and the faint green tang of wet algae. They wake before the heat becomes commanding. They walk from building to building with a ring of keys that opens less than it should.

They straighten a pan beneath a pipe.

They shake moth dust from a lamp cover.

They see the goats sleeping under the conveyor and do not bother to move them, because the goats will move when the machine begins. They see a pale fox carrying one work glove in its mouth and let it go; the glove is already beyond recovery, and the fox has young somewhere under the concrete slabs. They pause at the wetland and watch the grazers drinking in their careful hierarchy.

This person has learned that every maintenance task is also a negotiation.

Close a leak, and the beetles lose their morning water.

Leave it open, and the coupling will fail.

Patch the fence, and the goats will find a new route through the cable supports.

Remove the carrion too quickly, and the great birds will descend on the work yard instead.

There is no clean solution. Only adjustments, made repeatedly, under heat.

I think I would like to be that person: not the manager, not the owner, not someone who believes the valley can be made orderly. I would be the one who notices. The one who knows the first dark line of stormwater in a dry channel is not a line but an instruction. The one who carries a small radio and rarely uses it because sound carries too far anyway. The one who understands that the wetland is not scenery, not a resource, not a remnant, but the slow black heart beneath the salt.

And later, if I remained long enough, I might become something even less separate.

I would become the metal pan beneath the leaking pipe.

All day I would receive one measured drop at a time: water that escaped its intended route, water not clean enough to be sacred and too precious to ignore. A beetle would drink from my rim. A fox might lap the shallow accumulation before retreating into dusk. The technician would come with a rag and curse softly at the coupling above me, then leave me there because there is always a more urgent repair.

At night, after the generator stops, I would make the smallest sound in the valley.

One drop.

Then another.

Not enough to change the basin. Not enough to save anything alone. But enough to declare that the heat has not taken all of it. Enough to draw the listening lives close.
