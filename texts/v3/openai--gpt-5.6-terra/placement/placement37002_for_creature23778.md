I would not be at the pool.

At first I think I would want to be there: under the six old trunks, near the wet stone, where the town’s decisions gather and where the unmelted water keeps its small, dark refusal. I would want the dignity of being a keeper—of water, roots, births—someone trusted with a thing too important to own.

But I do not think that is where I would settle.

I would find myself in one of the passages that is almost not a passage anymore.

It would begin behind a goat shelter, beneath a roof garden where bitter herbs and dark cabbages grow in shallow boxes. Its first stretch would be stone-floored, then packed earth, then a sequence of old boards laid over a drainage run. A person carrying a basket would have to turn sideways to pass. One wall would be the exposed roots of an alder, polished dark by generations of sleeves. The other would be a house wall, or perhaps the back of three houses built so closely together that no one could say whose wall it first had been.

There would be a low door in that passage.

Not a secret door. The Basin People do not need secrets for every hidden thing. It would simply be a door that has acquired too many uses to be named by one. Beyond it: a room once used for storing dried fish, later divided by a woven screen when two cousins married, later opened again when an old woman could no longer climb to the upper rooms. There would be a workbench under a small light well. The light would arrive in the room by way of three bone disks, each hung at a different angle, so that at noon the far wall held a pale trembling oval like a reflection from water.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be a joiner, though perhaps not one of the great ones—the people called to settle a dispute about a channel or decide whether a household may take down a wall. I would be a lesser joiner, still learning. Someone whose hands know which repairs are only repairs and which will change the shape of a life.

I would mend latches made of leather and ash wood. I would fit replacement slats into old doors. I would make small hinged covers for water channels so children do not step into them in the dark. I would repair the frames that hold hides beneath the eaves. I would be asked to look at a sleeping ledge and say whether it could be widened without putting too much pressure on the wall below.

Mostly, I would listen.

A joiner in that town would have to listen to wood: to the dry complaint of a beam, the damp swell under a floor, the faint tick of a peg working loose in cold weather. But they would also have to listen to people in the way the Basin People listen: not waiting for declarations, but noticing the small repetitions.

A woman pauses outside her own door before entering. A boy has started taking the long route home, though it puts him near the old dry chambers. Two households have begun to leave their wash water in separate basins. An elder is carrying less each week but has not asked for help. A returning traveler speaks too quickly and keeps looking upward, as if expecting the sky to be visible.

These would be structural facts, as real as a cracked foundation.

I would not be wise enough always to understand them. But I might be useful in noticing them early, when a small adjustment could still preserve room for everyone.

Perhaps I would have left once.

Not far, not heroically. I would have gone with a salt-trader beyond the willow boundary and slept in a building with straight walls and empty space around it. I would have walked on ground that did not curve back toward familiar roots. I would have heard a river making noise enough to cover speech. For a while I would mistake largeness for freedom.

Then I would return carrying something impractical: maybe a strip of thin blue glass, or an iron nail too long for any ordinary repair. People would welcome me without making much ceremony of it. Someone would take my wet cloak. Someone else would ask if I had eaten. A dog I remembered as a pup would be gray around the muzzle and would sniff my boots as if it had been waiting for my return only to confirm that I smelled wrong.

For a season I would be restless.

Then I would be given a task.

A roof garden would be leaking into the room below, where a newborn slept. The garden could not be moved without losing its soil; the roof could not be raised without blocking a neighbor’s light; the water channel had been patched so many times that it had become a kind of braided history. The elders would send me—not because I was the most skilled, but because I had seen other places and might still be foolish enough to imagine an answer.

I would sit in the damp room for a long time.

I would touch the wall. I would watch the droplets gather. I would see that the problem was not the leak alone. The problem would be that every previous repair had tried to preserve the garden, the roof, the channel, the sleeping room—each thing separately.

So I would propose something smaller than a solution and more patient than a fix.

A shallow shelf, perhaps, set under the garden bed: not to stop the water, but to receive it. It would lead the drip along the wall into a narrow clay trough. The trough would feed a moss box by the doorway. The moss would clean the overflow before it entered the lower channel. The newborn’s room would remain dry. The garden would remain where it was. The neighbor would lose no light. And the family would have one more patch of green close to hand in winter.

No one would praise it much.

Someone would nod. Someone would bring me a bowl of hot roots. The keeper of roots might say, quietly, that the alder would need watching because its lower branch now shaded the moss box. A child would immediately begin putting pebbles in the new trough to see whether the water could carry them.

That would be enough.

I think, in that town, I would become someone of the in-between places: the person who knows that a boundary is not only where one thing ends, but where two things learn how not to injure each other.

And if I had a name there, it would probably not describe my trade.

It might be something like: from the low door behind the goat shelter; of the room where the water was taught to pass through moss; born elsewhere, returned by the north roots.

That would be enough of a self.
