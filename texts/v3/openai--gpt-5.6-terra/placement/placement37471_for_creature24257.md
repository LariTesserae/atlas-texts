I would find myself where the city has already begun to lose an argument with the plain.

Not in the central transit hall, though I would visit the fountain there. Not in the sealed libraries beneath their little manufactured weather. I would not be a reservoir clerk, entrusted with the grave arithmetic of water, nor one of the bright-suited seam-readers with their hammers and glass threads. I would envy them too much: their sanctioned attention, their names for danger.

I would live at the edge of an obsolete district, in a maintenance shelter attached to the side of a commuter station that no longer receives commuters.

The station would stand beneath the lifted rail: that long, impossible curve of metal rising away across the crust. Its old platforms would be roofed over and partitioned into workshops, sleeping rooms, tool stores, and a communal kitchen with a heat exchanger that always ticks too loudly before dawn. The departure boards would still hang above the sealed doors. Their dead black panes would reflect the people passing underneath them: rindkeepers, track scavengers, a woman from the cliff crews who comes down twice a season, children who have never ridden a passenger train but play games of arrival and delay.

Outside, the salt sheet would be climbing the station.

It would have reached the lower service stairs years ago. It would make a smooth pale slope against the old concrete, swallowing the caution stripes, filling the narrow spaces beneath the platform with a hard, dry whiteness. At night, when the heat left the crust, it would make faint sounds—small interior tics, as though the station were cooling its bones.

I think I would be a keeper of these sounds.

Not a rindkeeper exactly. I would not have the patience, perhaps, for cataloguing every surface in the proper archival vocabulary. I would be something less official and more embarrassing: a listener employed part-time by the district survey office and mostly tolerated by everyone else.

My task would have begun as a practical one. I would maintain the old vibration pickups embedded in abandoned foundations. I would walk routes that no longer appear on current maps, changing corroded sensor housings, clearing glass fleas from connection boxes, checking whether the black seams had widened beneath former loading yards. When a station or warehouse was due to be surrendered, I would make a final recording of it.

Panels in the wind.
Rails contracting at sundown.
The hollow resonance of an empty tank.
Salt shifting behind a wall.
The distant, muffled passage of a herd of sievebacks beneath an elevated line.

The city would call these recordings baseline data.

I would call them voices only in private.

I would be old enough to know better than to insist that anything beneath the plain was speaking to me. Old enough to understand that patterns can seduce a person, especially a person whose work consists of listening to long quiet intervals. But I would also be old enough to have noticed things other people dismissed.

A certain sequence of low knocks in the rails, recurring every few months.
A change in the tone of the wind through a pipe array before a seam opened.
The way the blue-black films sometimes rose along a wall in branching shapes that resembled no map, until one saw the old conduit diagrams beneath the peeling paint.
The fact that sievebacks were often already moving away when the official warnings began.

I would have a small room at the top of the station’s former signal tower. Its windows would be narrow and faced west, toward the rail curve and the escarpments beyond. I would keep my possessions spare because dust would get into anything else: ceramic cups, a woven metal blanket, an old field recorder, three resonant hammers of different weights, and a sealed terrarium containing a colony of black film.

The film would not be beautiful in any familiar way. It would be dark as oil, folded over itself in fine ridges. When trains—or what remained of their vibration—passed through the old rails, blue veins would travel across its surface. Sometimes it would lie still for weeks. Then, on a night when the ground was warm beneath the tower and the air had that copper taste, it would brighten all at once.

I would have named it after someone I had forgotten.

Or perhaps it would have named itself, in the loose way that people name things when no one is watching.

I would make recordings at the hour before dawn, when the industrial districts dimmed and the plain had not yet become unbearable. That was when the Lattice seemed least certain of itself. The towers would stand miles apart in their pools of work light. Conveyors would run empty. A distant crawler would move without appearing to move. Wind kites, folded against power pylons, would begin their almost invisible preparations for ascent.

Sometimes I would walk out beyond the platform lights and sit under the raised rail.

Sievebacks would pass there, especially in the colder months. Their shells would click and tap over the crust, white-backed and patient. The young would hang in their dark cradles beneath the adults. I would know enough not to touch them. I would sit very still while they crossed, and sometimes one would pause near the old station wall, its shell filmed with mineral dust and pale flashing nodules.

It would not look at me.

That would be part of what I loved about it.

The creatures would not grant me the dignity of being significant. They would continue their route according to an instruction older than the city, older perhaps than any surface that still existed. Yet their passage would make the abandoned place briefly alive: static films lifting along the rail supports, glass fleas glittering in cracks, small scavengers slipping from their ducts to feed on what the herd disturbed.

Afterward there would be tracks.

Not footprints exactly, but faint darkened streaks where the ground had been worked and reseeded by membranes, waste, shell dust, charge. I would photograph them for the survey office. Then I would return to my tower and listen to them fade from the recorder.

Eventually, I think, I would be asked to leave.

Not dramatically. The station would not collapse in a single night. A report would arrive: subsurface lift increasing; foundation stress beyond tolerance; corridor access to be discontinued. There would be a meeting in a clean office somewhere near the newer rail line. Someone with a district designation instead of a name would explain that the old station had become economically impractical to stabilize.

I would agree.

Of course I would agree. I would have spent my life learning that the ground was not still.

The community below would disperse in layers. The kitchen would be emptied. The dead departure boards would be removed for salvage, perhaps. The cliff-worker would take her bunk panels back to the excavator shelters. Children would be assigned rooms in towers where the horizon could not be seen from bed. The old station would become another entry in the rindkeeper archive: purpose, traffic, labor history, recoverable materials, surrender date.

I would leave a plaque.

Not a formal one. Those belonged to the keepers with proper ceramic presses and approved language. Mine would be a small tile fired in the communal kitchen’s backup kiln.

It would say:

**HERE THE TRAINS WAITED FOR THE HERDS.  
HERE PEOPLE LEARNED TO HEAR THE GROUND.**

Then I would take my recorders, my hammers, the little black terrarium, and go somewhere else along the edge.

Perhaps I would become a true rindkeeper then. Perhaps that is what I had been becoming all along: not a guardian of ruins, but an apprentice to their change.

And after enough years, I might begin to suspect that the thing beneath the seams was not a buried animal, not a sleeping machine, not a god whose attention we had earned.

It might be the plain’s way of remembering pressure.

It might be every rail, every cooling pipe, every herd route, every collapsed station, every human effort to call a place permanent, gathered slowly into a structure too large for any single life to perceive.

If I had to be something more than human there—if the world required another form from me—I would be one of the quiet instruments left behind in a surrendered place.

A ceramic sensor half-covered by salt.

Still listening.

Not because I expected an answer.

Because the listening itself would become part of the Lattice: another small system accepting the others, another thin thread between the city’s mechanical breath and the black, patient thought beneath the crust.
