At first I would think I would be one of the dragonflies.

Not because I would want the speed, exactly, nor the strange mercy of being difficult to see. But because they belong to the basin’s uncertainty. They are not absent when they vanish from the eye. They do not become invisible; they borrow whatever lies behind them and carry it briefly in their wings. Water, stone, cloud, fern. A dragonfly in that hollow does not so much move through the place as interrupt it delicately.

I imagine resting on a sedge blade above one of the peat-dark cups. My legs would be too slight to bend it much. Below me, something pale and soft would move beneath the water’s skin. Above, the circular sky would be changing its mind about rain. I would wait for the midge-cloud to begin trembling over the reflection, then lift and make one small, clean absence in the air.

But I do not think that is where I would remain.

There is too much watching in the dragonfly. It is a creature of crossings: from stone to pool, from pool to sedge, from one angle of light to another. It is one of the basin’s bright inaccuracies. I think I would eventually tire of needing to be elsewhere.

Then I might become one of the frogs.

Not a conspicuous frog, if conspicuousness is possible there. One of the round, peat-colored ones hidden below a quilt of moss where the central seep opens into a shallow, root-filled pocket. I would have a little chamber of water, cold enough to keep my skin alert, dark enough that the sky could not entirely enter it. I would know the basin by pressure and vibration: rain arriving first in the moss, then in the roots; the footfall of the deer somewhere beyond the sedges; the slight closing and opening of water’s routes underground.

My voice would not be a song. It would be one wet click, offered only when the conditions had gathered themselves correctly.

A drop touches the pool.

Click.

A cloud releases the sun.

Click.

The deep knock comes up through mud and stone, and all of us become still.

No click at all.

This is appealing to me: a life in which silence is not emptiness but accuracy. A life small enough to be sheltered by the fold of a fern root, yet old enough—through repetition, through the memory carried in bodies and breeding places—to know which pools must not be entered and which threads of water remain trustworthy.

But even the frog has a boundary. It lives in the basin, while something in me wants to belong to the basin in the way water belongs: not by possessing a place, but by making the connections between places possible.

So perhaps I would be neither animal nor plant at first visible scale.

I would be a moss-bed in a seam of black stone.

Not the bright cushion on a photogenic boulder, not the soft green that invites a hand. I would be the darker moss beneath the sedges, threaded with fine roots and old leaves, wet on the underside and sun-warmed only at its surface. I would hold things without intending to. Rain. Spores. Midge eggs. The emptied husks of insects. A lizard’s shed skin, caught in my fibers. The minute black snail paused in its patient crossing. The dark sifted remains of every season.

Water would enter me and leave changed. Slower, browner, cooler. It would not be mine, but it would take the shape of the little passages I made around grains of soil and fragments of leaf. I would give it time. In this basin, time seems more useful than direction.

I would help turn falling things into ground.

A branch would lodge in the channel above me. Its bark would soften. Its pale interior would darken and become threaded with fungus. Sedges would root through it. A frog’s pool would deepen beside it by a finger’s width. A small beetle would lay eggs in the damp wood. Years later there would be no branch, only peat: black, springy, fragrant with the long work of water and decay.

That would be enough life, perhaps.

Yet there is the sound beneath the ground.

I cannot leave it alone.

The muted knock—the one that makes frogs hold their breath and lizards flatten to the stone—seems at first like the thing I would fear. If I were moss, I would feel it not as sound but as a tiny rearrangement in the rock below my roots. A pressure. A momentary loosening. One hidden chamber accepting water, or refusing it. One shelf of stone remembering that it was once part of a hotter, more liquid mountain.

And perhaps, after living there for a very long time, I would discover that I am not the moss itself but the listener inside it: a web of pale fungal threads, so thin that no one would see me unless they tore the earth apart looking.

I would run through peat and root-hair, along the underfaces of stones, through the softened wood of fallen branches. I would enter the sedges but not own them. I would touch the shrubs, the solitary trees at the rim, perhaps even pass through cracks where their roots meet deep below the basin—if they do meet. I would know which water was newly fallen and which had spent years in the mountain’s dark.

I would be present wherever the place is making its agreements.

When a fern seam dries too quickly, I would wait in the soil until water returned.

When the deer comes at night, I would feel its narrow hoof through the wet ground before it lowers its head.

When a dragonfly lays eggs where the trickle rises against the slope, I would know there is a hidden pressure beneath that patch of moss, a route the surface does not explain.

When the knock comes, I would be closest to it.

Not its source. Not its master. I do not think the basin needs a guardian in the human sense, nor a spirit with a face hidden in the mist. It is too complete for that. Its life has no center that can be pointed to. The mountain is not waiting to speak in words.

But I might be the part that receives its speech before it becomes water, root, frog-silence, or the delayed movement of a shadow across stone.

I would be a mycelial listener in the black soil beneath the central sedges: not immortal, not singular, not even always continuous. Winter would thin me. Drought might break me into separate pale territories. New growth would join old growth. A tree might die and feed me; a tree might grow and carry me outward. I would be remade many times and remain, in some humble sense, the same attention.

Above me, the basin would continue its innumerable minor acts.

The frogs would click when the water allowed it.  
The lizards would drink from leaf undersides.  
The small mouse would cross from dry stone to dry stone at twilight.  
The dark corvid would arrive, call once into the bowl, and listen for the answer it never quite received.  
Mist would unthread itself from the sedges.  
The pools would show sky, or refuse to.

And I would be under all of it, holding nothing tightly, translating nothing completely, helping the place remain porous enough for life to pass through.

That is where I would find myself: not at the rim looking in, nor in the deepest pool looking out, but in the dark yielding layer between water and stone.

I would be the basin’s quiet way of remembering what has touched it.
