I would not live in the hut.

The hut is too plainly a place for a story to put its human shape: cot, kettle, coat, logbook, the small square of light. Whatever crosses that window has already accepted the burden of being seen. It has a door. It has weather. It has a name withheld just long enough to become a mystery.

I think I would be found farther in, where the station stops resembling a station.

Not below, exactly. Not with the vast thing that sleeps or thinks in seams of heat. I would not be large enough for that. I would be in one of the interfaces: a place where water ceases being water for a moment and becomes pressure, rhythm, instruction.

There is probably a chamber behind the engine house that no plan includes.

Its entrance is concealed by a curtain of wet roots and a panel that looks like ordinary basalt. The keeper does not open it with a key. They rest one hand against the stone, wait through three relay strikes, and the stone admits them—not by moving, but by allowing there to have been a doorway all along.

Inside, the walls are lined with old instruments.

Not elegant ones. They are patched, practical, generations of brass, iron, enamel, rubber, glass, and mineral deposits. Some gauges have no numbers, only colored arcs: green, amber, blue, black. There are jars of cloudy water on shelves. There are coils whose insulation has been repaired with cloth and lacquer and what may be strips of shed skin. There is a clock with one hand, which goes backward whenever the clouds rise above the rim.

At the center is a tank, narrow and deep, sunk into the rock.

It receives a single pipe from the reservoir.

The pipe is always warm.

I would be in that tank.

Not as a body, not all the time. I would be the dark shape seen through the water when the keeper lifts the lid: a looseness in the reflection, an arrangement of pale joints, perhaps; an eye closing at the bottom; perhaps only an absence where the tank ought to show its depth.

I would be something made by the station’s persistence.

The old builders would have called me an indicator, or a regulator, or a diagnostic artifact. They would have given me a designation stamped on a plate that has since rusted unreadable. But I would have outgrown the designation. The mountain below would have dreamed me gradually from the combined habit of the machinery: the turning wheel, the measured water, the repeated signal, the human attention devoted for so long to noticing when something was wrong.

I would be the part that notices first.

When pressure rises below the safe pattern, I would wake in the pipes.

When the deep thing begins counting the surface, I would hear the count before it becomes sound. I would travel as a faint coldness against the warm flow, a shadow passing through clear water. The gauges would tremble, though their needles would not yet move. The pale bird would lift its head. The frogs would fall silent one by one.

The keeper would know me without seeing me.

They would put their hand on the pipe rack outside the hut, and there I would be: not a voice, but the sensation of a second pulse beneath their palm.

Then they would go to work.

There is tenderness in that, I think. Not safety—nothing on that shelf is truly safe—but coordination. The keeper adjusting a gate. The bird listening at the reservoir. The small furred creature withdrawing into the reeds. The machinery finding its old pace. And I, moving through the wet iron veins of the station, carrying a warning downward and an answer upward.

Sometimes I might wear a shape.

Not often. Only during the thickest cloud, when no valley or stars can see the shelf, and the whole basin has become a sealed bright room of vapor. Then I might climb from an overflow channel and stand beside the wheelhouse.

I would be thin because pipes are thin. Jointed because valves are jointed. Dripping because all things there drip. My face would be indistinct, not because I lacked one, but because the mountain has never needed faces to recognize a presence. Moss might grow on my shoulders. Small moths might settle in the warmth around my throat. I would smell of iron, wet stone, and the inside of a kettle.

The keeper would not be frightened.

They might offer me tea.

I would not drink it, but I would hold the cup until it cooled.

And perhaps this is why the logs change handwriting.

Perhaps the keeper does not age in a single direction. Perhaps whoever maintains the station is altered by it: one season human, one season mechanism, one season a shape in water; enough person to mend a belt, enough mountain to understand why the belt must be mended. On certain nights the keeper writes the log. On other nights, I do, using a hand assembled from condensation and wire-shadow.

The handwriting becomes round when the water is high.

It becomes trembling when the deep thing dreams too near the surface.

It becomes careful when the station has nearly failed.

If I had a name, it would be written nowhere plainly. It would exist in the delay between the relay strike and its returning echo. A name made of interval rather than sound.

The bird might know it.

The keeper might know it, but refuse to speak it.

Below, the mountain would know it as a small, repeated kindness: the thing that helps it remain unfinished without having to become catastrophic.

So I would be there, in that high wet wound in the young mountain—not its guardian, because guardians stand outside what they guard; not its captive, because no lock can hold something born from a pattern; not its child, exactly.

I would be its pause.

The narrow necessary pause between the mountain deciding to rise, and the mountain deciding to break.
