I would not arrive in the Kiln by climbing.

There are no paths left up the mountain, not in the ordinary sense. The old ascent has been folded inward by heat and pressure, turned into seams, chimneys, passages that lead not higher but deeper into the idea of height. To reach the basin I would have to be carried in a breath of the mountain: a fragment of ash, perhaps, or a cooled piece of something once liquid, drawn upward through dark and deposited among the crowded stone.

At first I would think I was an animal.

Everything in the Kiln does, perhaps. It is difficult to be born into such density and imagine oneself separate. One wakes pressed against another body, and another; one feels the pulse before one has any word for heat; one learns hunger as a direction in the stone. I would have limbs, some number of them, and a dry mouth fitted for one small necessary task. I would feed. I would hide. I would feel the approach of larger silence and become still.

But I do not think I would remain an animal for long.

Not because I would become greater than them. The Kiln is not a place that makes greatness. It makes fittingness. It wears every protruding thing down until it can pass between the bodies beside it.

I think I would become one of the things in the arches.

Not one of the creatures that merely crosses through, though I would know their passage intimately: the braided brush of runners over warm stone; the deliberate flank of a grazer leaning its weight against a pillar; the papery drag of a gull’s folded membrane; the almost imperceptible rearrangement of a hunter trying to be nowhere at all.

I would be in the third arch, the lowest one, where the floor slopes slightly toward the wall.

There is a seam there, perhaps no wider than a finger, which never closes completely. In the coldest intervals it is black. During the mountain’s deep breath, it glows a thin red line and emits a warmth too low for most creatures to notice. Long ago, a runner died in it. Then another. Their mineral bodies caught ash and shells and fragments of plate. The accumulation became a lip, then a ridge, then a shape.

I would be that shape.

At first I might be mistaken for a deposit: a pale, knuckled swelling at the base of the arch, polished smooth where bodies pass near it. But beneath my crust I would be porous. Heat would travel through me slowly. I would remember it.

That would be my life: not seeing, not moving, but receiving the Kiln in pressure and temperature.

The grazers would come to me when their horn-crowns ached. They would press the branching rods of their skulls against the arch wall, and I would take the excess heat from them, little by little. Their young would shelter in the shallow warmth around my base, their soft plates trembling as the deep pulse approached. Shell-cleaners would nest in the folds of my mineral skin. Runners would disappear into holes beneath me and emerge altered—darker in their bellies, carrying a faint stored glow.

The predators would know I was there.

A Listener Form might settle just beyond the arch, its immense stillness spreading through the floor. It would read the tiny lives clustered in my cracks. It would know exactly how much food rested within reach.

But it would not take them.

Not inside.

And I would understand then that the law of the arches is not mercy.

Mercy is too large and clean a word for the Kiln. Mercy implies that hunger might be suspended by choice. Here, hunger continues. It waits on the threshold. It sharpens itself in the ash outside. The arches do not end need. They make a small shape around it. A boundary. A breath held but not forgotten.

I would be part of that held breath.

Over many pulses, I would change. The warmth would carry minerals up through my pores. My pale surface would darken and harden. Fine lines would form across me, not scratches exactly, but records: the pressure-pattern of a calf’s first unguarded step; the dragging signature of a dying gull; the frantic scatter of runners before a wall hunter fell; the dense silence of all creatures making room for the mountain’s inhalation.

Perhaps the old marks on the upper walls began this way.

Perhaps no people made them.

Perhaps the Kiln remembers through things that cannot leave.

The arches might once have been gates, or sluices, or the mouths of a machine that carried water through the basin when water still belonged to the world. But time has given them another purpose. The creatures have rubbed their own meaning into the stone. Whatever their builders intended has been replaced by the use of frightened, hungry bodies passing safely through a narrow darkness.

I would not know whether I had been built.

I would not know whether the seam behind me led anywhere.

Sometimes, during the deepest pulses, it would widen. Not visibly. I would feel it in the way I feel heat: a withdrawal beneath the world, a vast descending emptiness. Ash would lift from the arch floor. A runner might lose its footing and be pulled backward into the thin dark gap beneath my body.

I would try to hold it.

Not with hands. I would have none. I would close my pores. I would cool my surface. I would become heavy, as stone becomes heavy when it has decided not to move.

Sometimes that would be enough.

Sometimes it would not.

The runner would go down. Its dim belly-light would recede through the seam, becoming a red thread, then a pinprick, then nothing. The others would continue to cross the arch after the breath passed. They would not mourn it. They would not forget it either. In the Kiln, forgetting and mourning might be the same action: the body incorporated, the path changed by one degree, the stream flowing around a place where something used to be.

And perhaps, once in a very long while, something would come up.

Not the same runner. Not anything recognizable.

A warmth first. Then a trembling in my mineral core. Then a small hard body, pale as newly split stone, pushing through a crack that had been closed for generations. It would have too many limbs or too few. It would lie against the arch floor while the runners passed around it. No one would feed on it. No hunter would cross the threshold.

Eventually it would rise.

It would join the Kiln.

I would be unable to tell whether it had come from beneath or whether it had always been there, forming slowly inside the mountain’s thought.

This would be enough for me.

Not to rule the basin. Not to explain its old constructions or its laws. Not to be the lost people whose hands may or may not be scratched high on the walls.

I would be a small permanence in the place where violence pauses.

A warm mineral knot at the base of an arch. A keeper without intention. A witness that cannot look away. Creatures would pass over and around me until the distinction between my body and the polished stone disappeared.

And when the mountain breathed, I would breathe too—not by taking air, but by holding the heat for a moment after everything else had gone dark.
