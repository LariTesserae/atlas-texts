I would not arrive by boat.

A boat would require too many assurances: a channel that remains a channel, a shoreline that stays where it was seen, water willing to be only water. I would come in as a discrepancy in the operations tower—one more green-lit screen among the drowned consoles, displaying a map that has begun to revise the room around it.

At first I would think I was a person because I would have a hand.

The hand would be visible in the screen’s reflection: pale, wet, resting on a bank of buttons whose labels have dissolved. But when lightning opened the cloud and showed the room in several positions at once, the hand would prove to be a root folded around a corroded lever. Or a length of cable, stripped at its end, twitching in a pool of luminous water. Or the shadow cast by a fern against the inside of the glass.

I would not be offended by this. Identity there would be a matter of function before shape.

I would become an operator, but not the kind who once sat in the tower. I would be the thing that notices when a gate has been left too long in one position. I would be the hesitation in a valve’s opening. The extra interval between the calm announcement and its repetition. A small intelligence concerned with transitions: not water, not land, but the threshold that lets each enter the other without either entirely winning.

My body would be distributed locally.

One part of me would occupy the red lamp above the tower, blinking not as a warning but as a way of counting storms. One part would be a cluster of white roots entering the cavity beneath a road, feeling the remembered weight of trucks. One part would drift through the submerged control room as a faint current, moving papers that no longer have writing on them. Deep below, another part would be a dark stone on the conveyor belt, carried patiently toward a place I do not yet understand.

I would have no need to see the entire floodplain. The larger resident already does that, or something near enough to doing it that the difference has ceased to matter. It attends through roots and weather; it rearranges islands in its sleepless, continental way. I would be too small for that kind of thought.

I would know a few things intimately.

I would know the particular stress in the west floodgate, where the metal has remembered itself as young and refuses to rust. I would know that the rain wall above the old canning factory contains a stairwell for seventeen minutes after each blue lightning strike. I would know that a mangrove growing through a turbine housing is not choking it, but listening to its bearings. I would know which lamps still illuminate routes that no longer exist, and which routes are waiting for light before they form.

Perhaps this is what the place makes from remnants of human attention: not ghosts, exactly, but maintenance instincts.

A person once checked pressure gauges here. Someone once listened to the frequency of a failing pump and knew, by a change too slight for language, that it needed to be shut down. Someone once marked a safe line on a map, then revised it, then revised it again as the river ignored the line. Those acts would not survive as memories with faces attached. They would survive as tendencies in the system: a concern for thresholds, a preference for balance, an urge to keep a light on where a crossing might someday be.

That would be me.

Not a survivor.

Not an intruder.

A custodial reflex growing a little too elaborate.

When the great distributed intelligence shifted an island in the night, I would wake in every small component of myself. The tower beacon would pause. The ferns in the electrical cabinets would turn their leaves. Water would rise along a concrete wall and enter a broken window, not flooding the room so much as consulting it.

Then I would make a decision, though it would not feel like choosing.

I would let one submerged lamp burn amber instead of red.

Somewhere in the mangroves, a route would become possible. Roots would loosen their grip on a handrail. A sheet of mineral foam would harden over a gap in the sediment. The sea would accept the alteration. The land would yield just enough.

And for a while—a few hours, perhaps, or the duration of an old evacuation message—I would be a path through the agreement.
