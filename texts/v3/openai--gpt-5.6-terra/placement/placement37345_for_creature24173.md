I would begin by thinking I was a keeper.

Not one of the tuned ones. Not yet.

I would arrive on a lift from below, ears already pressured by the climb, carrying a sealed tool case whose contents I had checked three times: torque wrench, thermal probe, fiber scope, spare seals, grease cartridges, a pencil that would not write well in the cold. I would have been sent because something minor had entered the record. Not a wrong reading—nothing so crude. A discrepancy in the timing between two correct readings.

The west-bank compressor note had advanced by less than a fraction of a beat.

Everyone else would call it drift in the monitoring equipment. I would be sent to prove that they were right.

At first I would be offended by the place.

Not frightened: offended.

It would seem indecent for machinery to be so immense and so indifferent. The walls would not look like walls but like the exposed interiors of an idea too large to fit into the world. I would hate the cold, hate the headset pressing around my skull, hate the fact that every sound I made inside my own suit—the scrape of glove against fabric, a breath, the click of a buckle—was swallowed at once by the industrial chord.

I would walk the painted paths because they were painted. I would look at the pale shrub because it was the only thing in the valley that appeared to have made a choice.

Then, after several shifts, I would stop thinking of it as a shrub.

It would be on the eastern side, in its tray of powdered ceramic, just beyond the area where the floor plates changed from white to grey. I would learn that there was a warm seam beneath it—not warm enough to feel through a glove, but warm enough that frost failed to settle in a narrow crescent around the tray. I would learn that the plant had a particular smell when the air filters cycled: dry metal, bitter resin, something almost medicinal.

I would be told not to touch it.

Naturally, I would touch it only once.

Not the stem. Just the edge of one leaf, through my glove.

It would not move. Nothing dramatic would happen. But the vibration under my fingers would be different from the vibration in the handrail, the grating, the tool case at my feet. It would be a faint, irregular tremor—not random, not mechanical exactly. A pause occurring where no pause had been designed.

I would spend the rest of the shift trying to measure it.

The instruments would say there was nothing there.

That would be the first thing I could not put in the log.

After that I would begin to remain late.

At first, by minutes. Then by the length of a meal break. The lift crews would learn not to wait for me unless instructed. I would tell myself that I was following an unresolved acoustic issue, and this would even be partly true. The valley’s chord would become separable to me. I would learn the wide pressure of the turbines beneath the floor. I would distinguish the thin, feverish note of a bearing that had been replaced thirty years ago but never stopped sounding new. I would recognize the cough-click of relay bank nineteen, the dry inhalation of the upper ductwork, the almost private ticking inside the western control cabinet.

Eventually I would hear the discrepancy.

It would not be in the compressors.

It would be in myself.

My footsteps would arrive a little late against the floor. My breathing would fall into a pattern that the headset somehow amplified. When I stood before the Core, I would find that the beat I had been hunting was not coming from the shaft. It was coming from the cavity behind my sternum, correcting itself against the valley’s fundamental note.

I would become careful then.

Not because I believed the Cold Core could harm me. I would not believe much of anything about it. Belief would feel like an unnecessary moving part.

But I would start to understand why the keepers did not improve things.

The valley would not be frozen in time. It would be holding time in a particular shape.

The frost on the pipe joints, the washer trembling on the rail, the red lamps blinking into the impossible brightness, the mold blooming beneath Gauge Bank Seven: none of them would be evidence of stasis. They would be evidence of effort. A balance is not the absence of motion. A balance is motion so exact that, from the wrong distance, it appears still.

I think I would become the keeper assigned to notice the wrong distances.

Not an engineer, precisely. Not a priest, though other workers might think so when they saw me standing with one hand held near a duct seam, listening. I would become an observer of things that could not be entered into the official system because the official system had no column for *almost*.

Almost a change in tone.

Almost an opening in the flower.

Almost a second shadow inside the blue.

My quarters would grow bare without my deciding to empty them. The mug would go first, because ceramic made too much noise on the galley counter. Then the photograph, because faces viewed after a long shift would seem strangely unsteady, as if they had been captured during a vibration test. I would keep my old boots for a while, though I would stop wearing them. The valley would teach me where every raised seam and drain channel lay beneath the frost. I would not need to look down.

And perhaps, in the end, I would not be a keeper at all.

Perhaps I would become something the valley has made from a keeper: a living reference instrument.

I would stand near the eastern shrub, or beside the central handrail where the washer points inward, and I would know if the Core’s chord varied by the width of a human thought. I would know whether the flow had altered direction, though every gauge remained correct. I would know when the closed copper flower was about to fall.

When it did, I would collect it before it struck the grate.

Not to smuggle it away. Not for luck.

I would place it in a labeled tray and carry it to a sealed cabinet where the other fallen flowers were kept: dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, each one arranged by a keeper who had noticed something too small for the record.

Then I would write my report.

All readings correct.

No action required.

Observation continuing.

And below those permitted words, in pencil, where the next person might find it after years of dust and frost:

*The valley is listening back.*
