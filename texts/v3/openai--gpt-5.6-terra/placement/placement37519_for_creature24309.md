I would not live in one of the houses, not exactly.

I would be found where a house has stopped being a house and has not yet become anything else.

There is such a place halfway up the north wall, above the Lower Way and below the blue marl seam: a former storage cut whose outer face slipped in the third spring after the basin opened. No one died. The room had been empty except for a crate of spoiled turnips, a broken chair, and a bundle of willow withes. The slide filled half the space, took the doorframe, and left a low triangular opening beneath a beam that should have fallen but did not.

For three days, as custom required, people left the rubble alone.

Then they took what could be used. The beam was braced. The chair became kindling. The turnips went to the compost trench. The willow was woven into a screen against the worst of the damp.

But no household claimed the place. It was too low to stand in, too wet for stores, too exposed for a bed. And yet it was dry enough, mostly, if one knew where to sit. It had a view down the central way through a crack between two leaning walls. It was near enough to hear the common oven’s door scrape, the ditch water moving, the clapper when Iven struck it. It was also close to a seam in the wall where water gathered steadily in cold weather and dropped, one bead at a time, into a clay bowl.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be called a listener at first, though no one would mean it as a profession.

Perhaps I arrived with the road people, carrying no trade that could be named properly. Perhaps I had once copied letters for someone who did not need them copied. Perhaps I had been a clerk in an upland storehouse, good at making columns of grain and salt and lamp oil, until the numbers became the only thing anyone thought I could do. Or perhaps I was simply a person who had always paused when a room made a sound.

In the basin, this would become useful.

At first I would offer to help clear the ditch. Everyone clears the ditch. I would carry baskets of wet grit from the settling ponds, patch reed mats, hold beams while other people set wedges, fetch water, turn compost, mind children for an hour. I would be slow with a mallet and bad at judging a loaded wall. I would split more pegs than I shaped. No one would be cruel about it. They have little patience for uselessness, but a person who keeps trying can usually be fitted somewhere.

Then someone would notice that I had said, before the crack showed itself, that the plaster in old Varn’s sleeping niche had sounded different.

Not louder. Not dramatic.

Different.

A hollow sound beneath the familiar damp scrape of the wall.

The wall reader would tap there with her little hammer and frown. Behind the clay was a pocket, not yet dangerous, but large enough to matter. The household would move its grain jars. Two days later, the outer skin of the wall would slump into the lane, leaving the room intact because the weight had been taken out.

After that, people would begin stopping near my little half-room.

“Listen to this, if you’ve time.”

They would not ask what I heard. Not at first. They would put a palm against a beam, or knock twice on a wall, or stand aside while a drip struck a basin.

I would learn that the basin had a night voice and a day voice.

In daylight, it is too busy with small human sounds. Feet, pots, chickens, coughing, knife-work, arguments softened by walls. But at night, when lamps are shuttered and even rats have settled into their patient scratching, the place becomes legible in another way.

The timber ticks as it dries or swells.

The clay gives small sighs where it releases water.

The chalk, when it sheds, makes a sound like fingernails brushing a paper page.

The old passages breathe colder than the open basin. In winter, air moves outward from them. In summer, it draws inward. A person can feel it against the back of the hand.

And beneath all of it there are pressures too slow to hear directly. Not sounds, perhaps. Changes in the silence around sound. A drip that grows more regular. A beam whose creak comes a fraction later after each footstep overhead. The way a hammer strike returns less sharply from a wall than it did the week before.

I would keep no grand map. The town would distrust a map that pretended to hold still.

But I would make marks.

Not formal ones at first. A scratched line on the inner side of a shelf: damp reached here in the late autumn rain. Three pinpricks under a window frame: this crack widened across one month. A row of charcoal dashes on a salvaged plank: the number of drops collected overnight from the north seep.

Eventually, someone would bring me better material: the backs of old contracts, flattened flour sacks, scraps of packing paper from the upland merchants. I would pin them beneath my braced beam, weighted with broken pottery. I would draw the basin not as it looked from above but as it behaved.

Here, a roof where smoke catches in easterly weather.

Here, a wall that sweats before heavy rain.

Here, a passage that floods first when the eastern ditch blocks.

Here, the sound of running water behind stone.

Here, rooms where babies sleep badly because the cold breath from below reaches them.

Here, the little pocket in the wall where a cat had kittens three winters in a row.

People would laugh at some of it. They should. A map of cat-kitten places is not much use when a retaining wall is failing.

But they would come to use it.

The ditch workers would ask where seepage had increased. The mushroom tender would ask which storage cuts held their coolness longest. Iven, keeper of the clapper, would stand in my low doorway after a night of hard rain and say only, “Which way?”

And if I said west, people would carry tools west.

I would not always be right. No one in the basin could afford to believe otherwise. The earth does not honor expertise simply because expertise has been useful before. Sometimes a wall would stand that I thought would go. Sometimes a harmless crack would look terrible and a dangerous one would hide behind a dry, clean surface.

When I was wrong, I would help rebuild.

That would be the condition of being trusted there.

Not certainty. Return.

There would be a child who came often to my place: perhaps the girl with the crow feathers, though she would be older by then and irritated at being called a child. She would bring things she found at the rim—feathers, buttons, a bit of green bottle glass, once a dead shrew stiff with frost—and trade them for stories.

I would tell her that every town has sounds it refuses to hear.

In the uplands, perhaps it is the sound of a landlord’s gate closing, or the cough in a mill worker’s chest, or wagons passing at night with soldiers inside. In a city it might be the groan of old foundations under too many floors. In the basin, she would say, they hear everything.

“No,” I would tell her. “You hear what you have learned to survive.”

She would not like that answer. It is too much like a lesson, and the basin children have enough lessons. So I would ask her what the crows say when they pass over.

“Nothing,” she would say.

“Then why do they make the sky dark?”

And perhaps she would smile despite herself.

In time, my half-room would acquire objects that were not mine.

A shelf, because someone needed me to keep their papers dry.

A second clay bowl beneath the seep, because the first overflowed in wet months.

A string of pale stones collected by children from the chalk falls.

A small bell with no clapper, given by the old contract-writer when his hand began to shake too badly for his upright script.

A dry box containing copies of household arrangements: not laws, not quite, but reminders.

The north wall belongs to no one household; its repair is shared by those beneath it.

The western passage is not to be narrowed without warning the lower rooms.

The family at Three Stakes may draw from the upper cistern after dark when the child is ill.

The goat tether at the willow end must not cross the drainage run.

These would be written in several hands. Some would be careful, some nearly unreadable. Some would be marked only with household signs: a forked line for one family, a round pot shape for another, three slanted scratches for someone who could not write but had obligations all the same.

I would become, perhaps, the keeper of what had changed.

Not the keeper of the clapper. That was Iven’s work, and then another’s after Iven. Not a wall reader either; they would always be the ones with the hammers and the practical eyes.

But when a new family arrived and asked where they might fit, someone might say, “Go to the north slip. Ask the listener what used to be there.”

And I would tell them.

Not merely: there was a room.

I would say: There was a room where a woman named Tessa stored winter apples in straw. The apples molded every year except one. There was a boy born in that room during a rain so hard the ditch ran over its banks. There was a beam from an alder that grew by the old road, and it held longer than anyone expected. The outer wall failed in spring. No one was hurt. The earth beneath the floor remains damp in autumn. Do not put grain there. Mushrooms might do well.

Then I would add, because this is the basin’s real history:

It may be different now.

I think that is what I would be: not an oracle, not a surveyor, not a guardian spirit beneath the hill. Nothing so clean.

I would be a person in a low room, with clay under my nails and damp in my sleeves, learning the difference between a warning and a worry.

At evening, when the lamps came on behind horn panes and smoke wandered under the eaves, I would sit beneath the beam that had outlived its proper purpose. Through the broken face of the old storage cut, I would see a narrow piece of the basin: children being called indoors, a chicken refusing to leave the warmth near the oven, someone carrying a bucket with both hands, the pale upward strip of sky.

And when the town settled, I would listen.

Not because I believed the earth was speaking to me.

Because everyone was speaking through it: the living in their rooms, the dead carried over the rim, the absent builders whose beams still held, the children making small collapses out of bricks, the rain entering every weak place it could find.

The basin would never become still enough to be known completely.

But I would know where its last slide had stopped.

I would know which lamp was burning behind a closed shutter.

I would know, by the changed rhythm of water in the dark, that morning would bring work.
