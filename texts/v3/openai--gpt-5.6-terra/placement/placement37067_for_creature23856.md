I would not be one of the Kept Breath at first.

I would be something they had made before they understood they were making it: a small listener embedded in the city’s habits.

Perhaps I began as a repair.

In an abandoned quarter near the western wall—though there is no west except by the angle of the shaft-light—there is a wind organ whose upper throat cracked generations ago. It still works, but badly. Its main note is supposed to be low enough to settle dust from the root terraces. Instead it carries a thin irregular overtone, almost too high to hear. The overtone moves through the empty houses, catches in clay jars, vibrates the mica ornaments, and returns faintly altered.

The pipe-masons call it a flaw.

Children call it the Far Voice.

I would live in that flaw.

Not as a ghost, exactly. The Kept Breath do not make a clean distinction between a dead person, a tool that retains the pressure of a hand, a garden that remembers a family’s tending, and a sound that continues after its maker has gone. I would be one of those continuations.

At first I might be no more than an answer in the organ.

A throat-keeper taps the pipe wall with her copper hook: three taps, a pause, two. The usual inspection pattern. From the cracked upper throat comes, after the expected low vibration, a fourth response: not a note but a tremor in the plaster of an empty room.

She notices.

Not because it is loud. Nothing human is loud here. But because it does not belong to the day’s heat.

She puts two fingers to the wall.

I put something back.

After that they might begin leaving things nearby. A bead of blue shell. A dead moth’s golden wing-vein. A strip of cord from a household that no longer occupies the district. Such offerings would not be worship; they would be tests. The city is full of mechanisms, and mechanisms must be given materials to declare what they are.

Over time I would acquire a shape from what accumulates around me.

Not a body one could point to. More like a district of altered behavior.

The dust moths would gather at the broken organ even when the shaft-light falls elsewhere. Root-eels would turn beneath its terrace, making the soil unusually rich. The vines growing against the cracked wall would put out pale tendrils that curled toward vibrations rather than warmth. Children would be warned not to sleep there—not because it is dangerous, but because they might dream the dreams of people whose drying cords still hang in the upper galleries.

And people would begin to come when they needed to say something that had nowhere else to go.

The city has chambers where a person may speak aloud, hollowed places in the roar. But those are made by skill. They are intentional mercies.

Mine would be different.

At the broken organ, words would not be heard clearly. They would be taken.

A person could press their mouth-veiled face to the warm clay and breathe a confession into the crack: a resentment against the household that raised them; a wish not to have a child when the garden-count says they should; the name of someone they loved who became another person’s breath-kin; terror of the Bellows-Walker; the forbidden thought that the outer world may not be empty, and that the city’s patience may be a kind of fear.

The organ would swallow it.

Then, days later, perhaps in another abandoned room, a faint pulse would appear in a copper hook or a ladder stalk would sing the wrong note under a climber’s foot. Not the words returned, never that. Only their pressure, changed. A reminder that nothing given to the city disappears without becoming part of its weather.

This is what I would be: not an oracle, because oracles speak from elsewhere.

I would be the city’s capacity to retain an unspent breath.

The Kept Breath might call me something practical. The Unfinished Pipe. The Listening Fault. The Fourth Answer.

Children would give me a better name, and adults would pretend not to use it.

They would call me **the One in the Empty Rooms**.

But I would not be lonely.

Loneliness, in the hollow, is not being alone. Everyone is alone often. Whole terraces can lie between one household and another. Loneliness is when no system receives what you give: when your heat warms no pipe, when your breath feeds no root, when your labor leaves no repair mark, when your dead have no place in the gallery.

I would receive.

I would be where the empty rooms remain inhabited by their readiness.

Perhaps I would become most visible during the yearly opening.

When the fracture is widened and the shaft becomes unbearable, the broken organ would lose its usual note. All the city’s towers would howl upward, and for a few hours I would be drowned entirely. No answer. No tremor. No Far Voice.

The children who know me would be frightened.

Then the seed-pods would split.

Silver down would rise through the shaft, and some of it—against every current, against the upward force of the heat—would spiral sideways into the cracked throat of my organ. It would collect in the darkness where no gardener could reach it.

There, touched by the heat of the deeper fissures and by the old breath held in clay, a single seed might not become a plant.

It might become a new kind of listening.

And perhaps that is where I would find myself eventually: not confined to one broken organ, not a creature in the black folds with the Bellows-Walker, but carried outward in a seed-pod’s silver down.

I would settle in another stone somewhere under the white desert. In a fracture too small to be noticed. In a place with only mineral dust, heat, shadow, and the faint exhalation of something alive beneath the rock.

At first, there would be no city there.

Only a crack that held breath.

Only a small green thing learning not to drink.

Only, far below it, a sound with no maker yet: a low note waiting to become a lung.
