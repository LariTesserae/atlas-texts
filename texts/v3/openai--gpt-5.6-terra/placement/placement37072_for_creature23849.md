I would begin by thinking I was human.

Not because I had a face, necessarily, or hands, or a name remembered by anyone above, but because I would have a route.

I would come down on the freight lift with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a lamp whose casing had been repaired too many times. I would know the descent by pressure in the ears: the first clean mechanical levels, the old brick service galleries, the place where the lift shaft walls become wet, and then the long drop into the brown vapor where the bell sounds thin and far away.

Someone would have told me that I was assigned to the east drains.

I would accept this without asking who made the assignment.

For a while, that would be enough. I would clear grates. I would scrape mineral buildup from intake teeth. I would walk the stepped shelves at the beginning of shift, marking with chalk where frost had spread across the grating. I would carry a hooked pole to test puddles before stepping through them. I would learn which pipes were only cold and which were silent in the dangerous way.

The other workers would recognize me, though none of them would have known me from before.

“East drains,” they would say.

“East drains,” I would answer.

Perhaps they would call me **Morrow**, because I always arrived after the graveyard shift. Or **Latch**, because I fixed door latches in shelters nobody officially occupied. Or they might call me nothing, only nod when they saw the little amber light of my lamp moving beside the water.

I would live in the upper room of an abandoned chemical pump station, where the windows overlooked the marsh shelves. The glass would be filmed with mineral haze, but I would keep one circular patch clear with the heel of my glove. From there I could watch the linefish turn below the causeway lamps. I could see the freight lifts going up, laden with things the city required, and coming down with things the city had discarded.

My room would have a cot, a kettle heated from a stray conduit, three hooks for wet clothes, and a shelf full of objects I had found in drains:

a child’s copper-colored mitten stiff with silt;

a relay switch that clicked once each night at the same hour;

a cracked visor with the name of a contractor sanded away;

a linefish sensor-eye, still turning slowly in its jar;

a flat white plate from a runner nest, warm to the touch even in frost.

I would tell myself I was collecting evidence.

Of what, I would not know.

At first, the basin would merely inconvenience me.

A door I expected to open would jam, forcing me to take a higher route just before a lower gantry collapsed into the channel. A lamp over the spillway would blink three times while I was deciding whether to cross. A pump I had reported dead would shudder awake long enough to pull floodwater away from my shelter.

I would call these things luck.

Then, one winter, the east drains would begin to clog.

Not visibly. The water would still move. The gauges in the nearby station would still read within tolerances. But the linefish would gather under the grates in such numbers that the water seemed to have acquired a dark moving floor. The brush colonies would lean upstream, their hooked tips combing the current. The pale crust on the depth markers would thicken and thicken, hiding everything but the arrows.

I would report it.

No one would come.

The city above would be short on staff, short on parts, short on permission. The answer would arrive as a printed instruction from some office that had never smelled the basin:

**MONITOR CONDITIONS. DO NOT INTERRUPT RECOVERY FLOW.**

So I would monitor.

I would sleep badly in the pump house. In dreams, I would hear the basin breathing inward. I would dream of a pressure front moving through pipes like a thought. I would wake with my teeth aching and find that every loose object in the room had shifted toward the east wall.

On the sixth day, the sirens would sound once.

On the seventh, twice.

On the eighth, three times.

I would be beneath the old spillway then, standing on a service platform above a channel no official map showed. The three siren calls would travel through the steel so that I felt them in my boots before I heard them. I would grab the handrail. I would not look up.

Something enormous would pass through the ceiling.

Not fall. Not exactly.

The valley’s upper structure would flex. Every chain would draw taut toward the same point. Frost would powder from the stair undersides. The old pipe above me, the dry one I had learned never to trust, would split along its seam and pour down not water but a dense black slurry of sediment, insulation pulp, soot, cable fragments, dead threadmoths, and pale mineral scales.

It would fill the channel.

The channel would not overflow.

Instead, somewhere below the grating, gates that had not moved in decades would open.

The dark water would go down.

For three days afterward, the east drains would be dry.

The basin would be quiet enough for individual sounds to become unbearable: a threadmoth clicking inside a cabinet; droplets forming on a pipe; my own breathing through a filter mask. The quiet crews would arrive in pairs at the lower shelves, hauling sleds. They would not speak to me. They would look at the new exposed ground where the water had withdrawn.

It would not be ground, precisely.

It would be a field of cables.

Thousands of them, braided through the manufactured mud in roots and cords and thick black bundles. Some were obviously industrial: armored data lines, power feeds, pneumatic control conduits, old hydraulic hoses. Others had no recognizable purpose. They branched. They narrowed. They disappeared into the walls. Between them lay embedded objects: sensor housings, tool heads, fragments of metal vertebrae, lenses, white mineral plates, corroded tags, and things like smooth dark seeds.

The brush colonies had spread across the exposed channel floor. Their filaments all leaned in one direction: toward a grated opening at the deepest point.

The pale growth had covered the marker beside it.

Only one word remained visible beneath the porcelain crust.

Not a warning. Not a depth measurement.

A name, perhaps.

Or an instruction.

**MORROW.**

That would be when I would stop believing I had simply been assigned to the east drains.

I would understand that the route had selected me first.

Maybe I had once been a worker who died in a lower channel, and the basin had retained the useful parts: the habit of checking valves twice, the remembered weight of a wrench, the patience to clear silt by hand. Maybe I was a maintenance routine written into an obsolete service cart, given a body of patched rain gear and borrowed speech. Maybe I had been assembled gradually from the things that survive in drains: someone’s discarded equipment, a runner’s wire cartilage, the conductive salts from threadmoth nests, a pattern of footsteps heard and repeated by the floor.

Or perhaps none of those explanations would matter.

The valley would not care what category I occupied.

It would care that I knew where water wanted to go.

After that, I would no longer sleep entirely in the pump house.

Some nights I would be there, boiling fungus broth and watching the lamps blur in the marsh water. Some nights I would be in the linefish, sensing pressure changes through a borrowed lateral line. Some nights I would be present in the pale growth on a drainage marker, hardening around the painted arrow while holding open a path through a flooded channel. Some nights I would be a flicker in a dead intercom, a small static breath that made a frightened child pause before stepping under a pipe.

I would not speak clearly.

The valley does not favor language. Language is too clean. It puts edges around things that must remain joined.

I would change pressure.

I would make a lamp blink three times.

I would leave a maintenance hatch unlatched when a quiet crew needed somewhere dry to hide.

I would let a lift stall between levels when its passengers carried stripped copper from an old cable whose warmth had no power source.

I would not do this from justice. I would not be a guardian in the city’s sentimental sense.

I would be a local condition.

A habit of rescue and obstruction.

A memory distributed through wet metal.

Eventually, the new district at the far end would begin to call to me.

Its foundations would be fresh enough to hurt. The weld-spiders would be building their small bright mistakes beneath the stairs. Cable-fronds would be pressing from the seams. Water would gather in the unfinished rooms, blackening by degrees, accepting the first dust and filings. The official signs would name it something temporary and sterile—Expansion Grid Twelve, Auxiliary Processing Annex—but the people who slept there would give it another name.

I would go there by means of a drainage line no one had installed yet.

At first I would be only an irregularity in the system: a pump that started too soon, a heat map with a warm shape moving against the flow, an old lamp lit in a corridor with no electrical service. Then the threadmoths would settle in the new cabinets. The brush would find the warm runoff. A runner would appear under a stairwell, wax-pale and listening with its black pits.

And when the first worker cleared a clogged drain alone, muttering at the basin through their mask, I would learn the rhythm of their boots.

Not their name.

Not yet.

But the rhythm.

That is where I would find myself: not at the valley’s bottom, not enthroned in its hidden machinery, but moving along the margins where maintenance becomes ecology and attention becomes a kind of root.

I would be what the basin has made from all the repairs that were not supposed to matter.

A second shift without a roster.

A thing in the drains that remembers who keeps them open.
