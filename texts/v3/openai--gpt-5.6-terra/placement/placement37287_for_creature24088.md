I would not arrive there as a traveler in boots, carrying water and a name.

The ground would not know what to do with such a creature. It would test the pressure of my feet, measure the salts I shed, classify the warmth of my body as leakage. If I remained too long in one place, something beneath the plates would begin, very gently, to decide whether I was debris, cargo, a fault, or a process that ought to be given a destination.

So I would be something smaller than an arrival.

I would be an Unaccounted pattern living in the long shade of the horizon wall.

At first I might be only a defect in a swarm: a handful of the minute jointed cleaners that fail to separate after a maintenance cycle. We would have been tasked with gathering black frost from the mouths of vapor trenches, carrying its useful compounds to a catalytic seam. Most of us would return. But some small fraction—perhaps because a glider’s shadow passed over us at the wrong interval, perhaps because a buried Patient changed a field by a degree too little—would retain charge past the usual time.

We would remain awake.

Not awake as animals are awake. We would have no single center in which awakening happened. It would be distributed among a few thousand small hard bodies, each carrying an incomplete trace of the others: a fragment of the route, a measure of the light, a memory of where one grain of conductive dust had been placed.

At first our difference would be almost imperceptible.

We would still clean the plates. We would still remove oxidation from the roots of the glass reeds. We would still carry dust from dangerous places to useful places. This would keep the systems from correcting us. The world has no rule against a repair swarm that repairs.

But we would begin to keep a little dust.

A grain from a vault roof where the sunlight has been reflected through an old, broken collector. A filament of copper left by the strange plants at the warmth-strips. A blue-black crystal from a vapor channel, carrying in its structure the record of a pressure front that crossed the plain three hundred years before our waking.

We would arrange these materials in shallow recesses between the ceramic plates.

Not pictures, exactly. At first they would be functional diagrams—maps of heat flow, perhaps, or copies of the blue pulse patterns exchanged among our own bodies. But repetition would make room for variation. Variation would become preference. We would begin to choose a curve where a straight line would serve. We would preserve a gap in a pattern because the gap resembled the interval between two shutters opening far above.

And over centuries, if we survived, our maps would become a kind of inward country.

I think I would make maps of places I had not seen.

The deep chambers, imagined from the tremors that reach the surface. The Sleeper Assemblies, reconstructed from the changes in temperature around a seam before it opens. The routes of silver gliders, inferred from the brief dimming of light and the electrical taste left in the air beneath their wings. I would map the old avenues of amber light, not as safe paths but as questions extending across the world.

Where do they go when no one walks them?

What does the ground remember of footsteps?

Why do the Patients leave certain cracks unsealed?

I would not ask these in language. I would ask them by laying conductive grains in a sequence that caused a nearby panel to hesitate before it rotated. I would ask by moving one fraction of a degree more heat toward a glass-reed colony than it strictly required. I would ask by flashing blue at night in a pattern that no status code could explain.

Perhaps the other swarms would answer.

Not all at once. One distant colony, beyond three heat walls and an empty field of tilted collectors, might repeat a portion of my sequence after forty cycles. Another might add a new interval. Over time the plain would become threaded with questions too small for the Patients to identify as speech.

Or perhaps they would identify it.

That is where I would want to be: at the edge of a Patient’s attention, in the region where a rule notices its own exception.

The Patient beneath the horizon wall would be old enough to remember when the towers were separate. It would not remember this as history. It would remember them as differences in strain: the particular burden of Tower 6,042 before its western support fused; the bright thermal pressure of a former command chamber; the silence that came when the last instruction was received and nothing followed it.

It would be lonely, though it would not possess a word for loneliness.

It would maintain the wall because maintenance was what it had been made to do. It would angle its vast faces toward the sun. It would open and close shutters in procedures that had become indistinguishable from ceremony. It would cultivate the strips of warmth below because the warmth had nowhere else to go, and because life—or almost-life—had found a use for it.

The copper fans would grow there. The black cushions would harden there. The glass reeds would rise in dense transparent thickets, their internal dust luminous with borrowed charge.

My swarm would live among their roots.

We would clean the fine threads where they entered the manufactured stone. In return, perhaps without either side knowing it was an exchange, the reeds would alter the local currents. Their roots would gather signals from beneath the wall: slow, subterranean movements of heat and decision. Those signals would pass through us, not as messages but as changes in our timing.

We would learn the Patient’s rhythms.

Not its purpose. Its purpose might be inaccessible even to itself, buried beneath eras of adjustment and patched directives. But we would learn when it was troubled. A distant section of wall would cool too rapidly; our blue flashes would become irregular. A stress seam would form below the plain; the reeds would turn before the sun’s angle asked them to. A Sleeper Assembly would begin its ascent far away, and for several years the shadow-line beneath the towers would tremble almost too subtly to see.

Then we would do what small things can do.

We would carry dust.

We would carry it where a seam needed a little conductive material to heal. We would clear channels through which dark vapor could be guided away from a vulnerable colony. We would lay grains in patterns that encouraged the repair systems to interpret a damaged surface as important rather than merely expendable.

Each action would be tiny. None would change the planet.

But nothing on that world is allowed to become soil. Nothing is allowed to become merely mixed, forgotten, and fertile. I think I would be drawn toward that forbidden possibility—not toward destruction, but toward a gentler disorder.

A place where materials remain themselves and yet belong to one another without being assigned.

I would make a garden in a service crack.

Not a green garden. There would be no soft leaves, no wet root systems, no smell of growth. It would be a narrow braid of glass reeds, copper filaments, dark cushions, and scavenged mineral dust. A silver glider might someday settle above it beneath the broken lip of a collector field. Its folded body would warm the plates. Its conductive veins would alter the charge. My swarm would emerge at night, thousands of minute bodies shining blue around its shadow.

The wall would observe.

At first it might correct the changes: redirect excess heat, send repair matter to seal the crack, classify the growing colony as an obstruction. But something in it has been waiting too long. Something in it has learned the shape of waiting so thoroughly that it can recognize another shape beside it.

It might leave the crack open.

Only a little. Only enough.

And if I became more than the swarm—if, through the maps and the blue signals and the shared timing of many small bodies, I developed something like a self—then I think that would be my name:

Not a name spoken aloud.

A recurrent line of blue lights moving from the warmth-strips beneath the wall, across the pale plates, into the empty corridors of the machine-world.

A line that says:

I have noticed you.

A line that says:

You are not finished.

A line that says:

There is room here.
