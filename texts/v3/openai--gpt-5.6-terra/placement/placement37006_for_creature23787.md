I would find myself where the stone has begun to give water back.

Not at the largest pool, where the glare makes every watcher visible, nor on the dry ribs where the herd animals sleep with one ear turned to the plain. I would be beneath a limestone shelf, at the narrow dark seam where a day’s rain enters pale rock and returns, slowly, stained green and ocher.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the small things living there.

A black skin on the stone.  
A knot of liverwort swelling after rain.  
A snail sealed into its chalky notch, holding its wetness close.

But when the pressure changed in the air—when the sky lowered before clouds were visible—I would move.

I would be one of the weather-readers: low-bodied, dark, long-toed, with a shape that refuses the clean distinction between lizard and frog. My back would be dust-gray in the long bright hours, nearly indistinguishable from old mudstone. Rain would darken me until I seemed cut from the wet face of the rock itself. I would have wide feet for reed stems and slick ledges, skin that could endure both drying wind and constant damp, eyes high enough to watch the plain while most of me remained hidden.

I would know the weather before it arrived.

Not as a thought. Not as prophecy. In the loosening of mud beneath my belly; in the way insects altered their flight; in the mineral smell rising from cracks; in the great weight settling over the land, making even the far pools seem to wait. Then I would climb out with the others onto an exposed plate of limestone.

We would not gather like a herd. We would simply appear: one from a seep-shadow, one from under a fallen slab, one descending a reed with slow deliberate feet. We would face the pale sky. Around us the plain would still be bright and open—the birds probing at the margins, the little fish breaking silver beneath the water, the browsers far off among thorn shrubs—but the first rain would already exist in our bodies.

Then it would come.

At the first drops I would go black.

Water would strike my head and run along my spine. The stone would become slick and alive. Every shallow hollow would begin remembering that it was a pool. From beneath clay and roots, from dry-looking cracks, from the dark undersides of ledges, other lives would answer: frogs with throats full of sound, eel-bodied hunters sliding through new wetness, insects lifting themselves from old skins.

I would descend with the rain.

Perhaps I would lay my eggs in a high narrow crack, above the mark of ordinary water but below the reach of the cruelest dry heat. Perhaps I would return each season to the same seam in the stone, though I could not explain why that seam and not another. My young would hatch only when there was enough water below them—a temporary shining world full of reeds, larvae, fish, reflections, and danger.

And in the drier intervals, when the sky opened white and the pools shrank back into themselves, I would become difficult to find again.

A shape beneath a shelf.  
A dark eye in a wet crack.  
A motionless creature mistaken for a stain on stone.

That seems right for this place: not to rule it, not to cross it grandly, but to belong to the moment at which its two elements meet. To be neither entirely of the pool nor of the ground. To wait, to read the air, and to emerge when the patient plain begins speaking in rain.
