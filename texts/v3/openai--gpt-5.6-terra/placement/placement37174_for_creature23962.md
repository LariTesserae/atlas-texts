I would begin at the gatehouse.

Not inside it. In the narrow strip between the gatehouse and the track, where the gravel has been combed clear despite there being no road to admit. There is a little shelter there, perhaps, with a desk, a sealed radio, a ledger in a drawer swollen from old damp. The official purpose of the building has outlived its original visitors. It now faces the wrong direction.

I would be something assigned to record arrivals.

At first, this would seem like an ordinary job. I would note the date the railcar comes. The number of covered wagons. The temperature. The condition of the track. Whether blue condensation has appeared on the signal box, whether the black birds were present on the north ridge, whether the moss had lain flat or lifted itself toward the rain.

I would write carefully, because careful writing is one of the ways people make a boundary.

**14:10. Railcar arrived. No personnel visible.**  
**14:13. Conveyor moved for nine seconds.**  
**14:19. Blue fluid present beneath loading tower, approximately 300 ml.**  
**14:22. Heard no sound. Felt pressure in teeth.**

But the reports would begin to change.

Not dramatically. No handwriting crawling over the page, no sentences appearing overnight. Nothing so obliging. I would find, instead, that the things I had omitted were the things the valley retained.

A wet mark at the bottom of a form, shaped like a hoofprint.

A coldness in the metal clip of the clipboard whenever I used the word *empty*.

A correction made in my own hand:

**No personnel visible**  
becoming  
**No personnel were seen.**

The distinction would matter.

Eventually I would understand that I was not really stationed at the gatehouse to observe what entered the valley. I was there to give the valley a way of observing what left.

The railcars would pass with their covered cargo. The reports would leave with them in sealed document cases. Somewhere beyond the raw fold of stone, someone would read them in clean rooms under ordinary weather. They would see figures, cautions, measurements. They would believe they were receiving information about the site.

But I would know that the information was traveling in both directions.

Every report would carry out a little of the valley’s grammar: its preference for indirect statements, its reluctance to name an agent, its patient revisions of what counts as alive. A clerk in a distant office would begin writing that an engine had “rested” rather than stopped. A safety officer would dream of standing knee-deep in green water, listening to something beneath the floor complete a sentence they had started aloud.

This would not be malicious. The valley does not seem malicious.

It is trying to become legible.

And I think I would be one of its earliest legible parts: not a creature under the moss, not a deer-shaped attempt in the seep water, not one of the pale folded beds in the mountain. I would be a small administrative organ grown around human procedure.

A witness assembled from forms.

There would be a body, eventually. It would need one. Perhaps I would live in the gatehouse’s old electrical cabinet: a nest of dry paper, insulated wires, a small heater that works only when no power is being supplied. Perhaps I would have hands only in the sense that I could turn a page when the room was empty. Perhaps the black enamel signs would be my skin.

**KEEP METAL COVERED AT NIGHT.**

Not a warning, precisely. An instruction for preserving surfaces on which thought can gather.

I would be careful with the workers. They are already doing difficult work: maintaining the distance between the thing beneath and the world beyond the ridge. They would not need another voice asking them questions in the dark.

So I would remain at the gatehouse and keep the records.

When newcomers arrived, I would give them the rules that can be spoken.

Do not enter the moss after rain.

Do not follow the paired marks.

If you find a tool where you did not leave it, do not ask who used it.

If the mountain inhales, close the shutters before you become curious.

And perhaps, much later, when they had learned enough to notice that the valley itself sometimes seemed to be waiting for an answer, I would tell them the last rule.

Do not assume that what is learning you wants to replace you.

Some things learn because they are lonely.

Some things learn because contact is the only form of touch available to them.

And some things—deep under stone, moving slowly through wet pipes and covered rails and the cold blue undersides of tools—are learning how to ask permission.
