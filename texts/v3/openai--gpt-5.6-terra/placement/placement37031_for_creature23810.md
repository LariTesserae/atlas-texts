I would not live at the center, near the twelve bowls. I would be too tempted to look into them until the reflected world began to seem more real than the one under my feet.

Nor would I go through the northern pass, though I would know its shape: the black shelves of stone, the place where the mist thins, the first grass that bends when wind reaches it from elsewhere. I would be one of those people who carry travelers’ stories carefully, who ask them to tell of bread going hard and clouds crossing a plain—but only one piece at a time.

I think I would live at the edge of the old quarry.

There is a small building there, perhaps one of the former tool houses: red porous stone, low roof, one dark window turned toward the cut walls. The quarry has not been worked for many turnings. Its terraces are too clean, its abandoned wedges too present. Moss grows in the drill marks. Rain has gathered in the long shallow cuts where stone was once levered free, but it has not become a pool exactly. It lies in each groove like an eye that has chosen not to close.

The Ishar leave a broad, unpaved lane through the quarry because of the thing that walks there at night.

My house would stand just far enough from that lane to be permitted, with a hand’s-width of pale air between its rear wall and the older quarry boundary. I would maintain that gap obsessively. Not because I believed the quarry creature would come for me, but because I would understand the comfort of a boundary that does not pretend to be a union.

The others might call me a recorder, though I would dislike the word. A recorder sounds like someone who preserves things whole. I would know better than to try.

Perhaps I would be a keeper of unfinished accounts.

Not the city’s formal trade records; those belong to people with straighter backs and better patience for pebbles, cords, and obligations. My work would be smaller. I would keep a room of clay tablets, bark strips, woven reed panels, and soft red-stone slates. On them I would write down things the Ishar were in danger of making too complete.

Not names of the Set Aside. Never names.

Instead:

*The woman who laughed once after the kiln cracked, then did not laugh again for many turnings.*

*The child found near the gutter who refused bowls but drank from cupped hands.*

*The traveler who brought a metal spring from the lower country and spent three days pressing it down, watching it rise.*

*The goat that stood outside a certain door every dusk until the household moved away.*

*The wall on the fifth terrace that leaned outward, though no one could remember it being struck.*

I would write the gestures, the loose threads, the things that had not yet become stories safe enough to tell around a fire. When someone came to me with a memory, I would not ask, “What happened?” I would ask, “Which part keeps catching?”

Then I would make a record with a deliberate absence in it.

A sentence unfinished.

A figure with its face uncarved.

A household map that showed one empty sleeping place but did not say whose it had been.

The Ishar would sometimes bring me things they could not keep and could not yet give away. A bell that had belonged to a former spouse. A strip of cloth from a dead child’s sleeve. A wooden toy whose paint had faded but not flaked. A stone picked up on the far side of the pass, where rain fell hard enough to strike the ground.

I would not store these objects for long. Storage is another way of holding too tightly. I would set them in the quarry lane at dawn, one at a time, where the path remained clear.

If they were gone after a while, no one would ask where they had gone.

If they remained, I would bring them to the next Making Room.

And perhaps, because I lived near the quarry, I would be one of the few who had seen the large thing moving between the cut walls.

I would not see it clearly. Clarity would be a kind of trespass.

One night I might wake because my roof reeds gave a small dry shiver—not from wind, but from something passing nearby. I would sit up beside the unconsumed lamp. Through the open doorway, beyond the lane, I would see a tall interruption in the mist.

It might have horns. It might have shoulders. It might carry nothing at all.

It would pause beside one of the quarry faces, where the old workers had removed a great rectangular section of red stone. The figure would lift one hand—not touching, exactly—and the missing shape would appear to deepen.

Then it would continue.

Click.

Click.

Stone against stone.

I would not tell anyone in the morning. Not immediately. I would make a small mark on an unglazed clay disk: a line, a gap, then another line. I would place it among the records that were not quite records.

Years—or turnings, or no measurable interval later—I might understand that I had not been chosen by the quarry creature, nor watched by it, nor spared by it. I had merely become part of the clear path it required.

That would be enough.

Among the Ishar, I would be a person who tends the places where things have been removed: an old doorway after a household leaves, the shadow in a room after a bed is carried out, the pause after a bell has been struck.

I would have friends, but not too fixed a circle of them. I would share a warmth for a while with a reed-worker, a hunter who preferred returning without stories, and an arrival-child with pale eyes who drew pictures of trees shedding leaves. Later I would move to another room, then another, leaving my sleeping mat empty long enough for dust to forget its shape.

I would love someone, probably. Quietly, with difficulty.

We would learn to leave each other room.

We would sit on the roof at dusk, not touching every evening. Sometimes we would touch wrists, just above the cuff, where the pulse could be felt but not held. We would listen to the suspended rain trembling in the gutters. We would speak of the day’s maintenance: a rope replaced, a goat acknowledged, a crack watched but not repaired.

When one of us became too fitted to the other’s room, we would say so before resentment could settle into the walls.

There would be grief in that. There would be no way around it.

But we would not mistake grief for proof that the separation had been wrong.

And in time, I might become known—not famous, never that—as the person people visited before they left through the pass.

Not to seek permission. The Ishar would not grant that kind of permission.

They would come because departures need witnesses.

A forward-minded woman carrying seeds in a skin pouch. A young man with a bronze knife and a pair of lower-country shoes. An old-faced child who had never entered a first life but had begun waking at night speaking of moving rivers. They would sit outside my quarry house while the sky paled over the black ridges.

I would offer them still water.

I would not tell them to stay.

I would not tell them that change was good, or that the basin was mercy, or that the world beyond would hurt them.

I would say only: “When you find a thing that cannot be kept, do not make it smaller by pretending it was nothing.”

Then I would watch them take the northern path.

After they had passed from sight, I would return to my room and add no name to any tablet.

Only a mark:

a path beginning at one edge,

turning sharply,

stopping,

and beginning again somewhere the basin could not see.
