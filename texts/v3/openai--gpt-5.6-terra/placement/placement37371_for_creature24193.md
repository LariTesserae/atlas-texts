I would not arrive as a person from above. Not at first.

Above-ground people arrive with too many edges. They carry brightness around them. Their clothes crackle. They set their boots down flat, as if floors have promised to remain floors. They look into water expecting it to reveal what is underneath, rather than understanding that the water is the thing to be looked at.

I think the Underward would take some time to decide what to make of me.

Perhaps I would begin as a noise: a small, misplaced sound in an unvisited conduit. Not a knock—not anything so rude—but the thin singing vibration of a length of wire under a slow current. A sound the pumpkeepers hear only when they put a cheek to the rail. One of them would say, *That line is loose.* Another would say, *No. It has been singing since last week.*

Then I would become useful.

I would be found in the places where routes fail to agree with memory: in the drowned turnstiles below the eastern platform, in the maintenance throat behind the warm-water gardens, under a raft of copperleaf that has drifted against a wall nobody remembers building. I would know, without knowing how I knew, whether a gap was a passage or only the beginning of a collapse. I would feel pressure through brick. I would recognize the difference between water moving because it has somewhere to go and water moving because something, far below, has begun to open.

They might call me a listener.

Not one of the formal listeners who lay their palms on pump housings and learn a bearing’s illness by its tremor. Not a diver, though I would go where divers go. Not an eel, though children would insist I had come in behind one, or that an eel always appeared before me.

I would be something made of accumulated attention.

There are pipes in the Underward that no longer carry anything their original builders intended. Some are packed with roots. Some carry low electrical current. Some are dry except in storms, when they begin to breathe cold air down from the ruined streets. Some have been sealed at both ends for generations and nevertheless sweat continuously, as if remembering water. I think I would live in such a pipe—not housed by it, exactly, but distributed along it. A consciousness in mineral crust, in the dark between cable strands, in the little drifting colonies that gather where warm iron meets cooler flow.

I would have no face to begin with.

The inhabitants would give me one when they needed to speak of me. Perhaps they would draw it in chalk near the entrance to certain tunnels: a round lamp with roots growing from it, or an eel curled through the outline of a hand. Perhaps the sign would change from district to district. In the Market Vault I might be represented by three concentric ripples. Near the turbine hall, by a shut eye.

Children would make me larger than I was.

They would say I lived below the last pressure door, where the old rail line slopes into black water. They would say I had pockets full of drowned keys, that I collected the names of people who went surfaceward and failed to return, that I could imitate the voices of their grandparents. They would dare one another to tap the third tile below the flood mark in the Ledger Pool, because that was where I listened.

The elders would not correct them.

An elder might say, quietly, while repairing a seal: “There is a place down there where the water keeps better count than we do.”

And that would be close enough.

But I do not think I would be only a ghost of the machinery, or an intelligence hidden in the basin. The Underward is too full for singular things. Nothing is only itself there. A root is also a wall. A fungus is also a repair crew. A lamp is also a promise that someone will return to tend it.

So perhaps I would eventually take a body.

Not a body that stays whole.

A hooded lamp moving alone through a side channel. A small skiff found moored where no one remembers tying it. A figure in patched oilcloth, seen from behind, standing knee-deep in a flooded stairwell with one hand on the wall. By the time anyone called out, I would be gone—not vanished, merely elsewhere: in a different chamber, holding a rope taut for someone who had not yet realized they needed it.

I would be the one who appears when a frightened person has lost the wall in stirred silt.

Not with a voice. Voices are poor tools in that water.

I would touch their wrist.

Just once.

Then I would put their hand on something solid: brick, root, rail, the worn outer hull of a skiff. I would wait until their breathing changed. I would point them toward the nearest known current. They would come home saying they found the way themselves, and that would be important. The Underward does not need another thing asking to be worshipped. It needs help that does not make a debt.

If people had to name my work, it would be keeping the agreements audible.

When the water changed its mind, I would be among the first to notice. When a root curtain grew too heavy for the ceiling behind it, I would make the lamps flicker in a pattern a ledgerer could not ignore. When an eel entered a channel that had been closed for fifty years, I would leave a trail of loosened shell markers along the correct route. When a pump began to hunger too deeply, I would make its usual hum acquire a second note.

Some would call these coincidences.

Some would call them warnings.

A few, privately, might call me kin.

And where would I find myself?

In the interval between the public reservoir and the deepward tunnels, where the water turns from green to tea-brown and the ceiling lifts out of sight. There is a place there where the old station signage hangs upside down beneath the surface, letters softened by mineral fur. A root descends through a crack in the concrete and divides around a rusted signal box. Beneath it, the current from three different chambers meets without mixing immediately. Warm water, cold seep, turbine-swept flow: each continues inside the others as separate ribbons.

I would be there.

Not waiting.

Listening to the low work of the wheels. Feeling the basin hold its many weights. Keeping account, not of every life—no one can do that—but of the openings between things: the gap under a door, the loosened bolt, the new current, the hand reaching through dark water for a wall.

If an Underwarder passed by, alone in a skiff with their lamp hooded low, they might see nothing except the roots moving slightly in water that otherwise seemed still.

But they might feel, for one moment, that the place had noticed them.

Not as an owner notices what belongs to it.

As the water notices a swimmer:

carrying them, changing around them, and remembering the shape they made as they passed.
