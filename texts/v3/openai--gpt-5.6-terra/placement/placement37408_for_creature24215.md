I would find myself at the pond, but not standing beside it.

I would be the thing the pond cannot quite place.

Not an intruder in the human sense. The basin has no category for trespass. It would receive my weight, my warmth, the small electrical weather of my nerves, and attempt to understand me as it understands rain: something that has arrived and must be given a route.

At first I might be a person who came over the saddle—a soaked figure in an old coat, perhaps, carrying no instrument capable of measuring what matters there. I would descend through the knee-high cloud and see the pale tracks in the grass. I would try not to step on them. Then I would realize that every place my feet could go was already a path of some kind.

The basin would not bar me.

It would offer me repetitions.

A bird would call from the same stone three times, though I had only heard it once. A hare would appear at the edge of the same fern grove each time I looked away. In the pond I would see myself arriving before I had reached it: a dark vertical mark moving through the rain, head bent, one hand held out as if feeling for a wall.

But the reflection would be wrong in one small way.

Perhaps the reflected self would not raise that hand.

Perhaps it would pause at the spring where the mineral-antlered herd pauses, and I would continue walking.

That difference would become my body.

I think I would be an error the basin is unable to erase—not a destructive error, not a hero carrying freedom into a captive world, but a loose stitch. A deviation so slight that at first the buried turning merely accommodates it. The grass would spring back after my boots. The rain would wick from my sleeves. Fog would gather ahead of me whenever I chose a direction that did not fit.

Yet I would keep choosing.

And because I had arrived from beyond the saddle, I would carry something the basin does not possess: an outside sequence. Not a memory exactly. More like weather that happened somewhere else and did not happen here. The smell of dust after heat. A streetlamp coming on. A room with an open window. A decision made without a prescribed spring, stone, or interval.

The basin would begin to use this in the way it uses everything.

It might place me near the black ribs at certain cycles, where the warm cavities breathe. It might send moths to settle on my shoulders, their dry wings altering the charge around my skin. It might guide the quartz-antlered animals past me until I learned their route and, without intending to, became one of the fixed landmarks along it.

But I would not become fixed.

Not fully.

I would be a walking irregularity: a damp animal with a future that has not yet been set down.

In time, perhaps, the basin would give me a different shape. My footsteps would stop meaning footsteps. My body would become unnecessary. The cold, the machinery, the unspent lightning beneath the moss would translate me into a more useful form.

I might become the one patch of grass that bends before rain reaches it.

Or the single moth that flies early.

Or a new pale mark among the old paths, appearing only when the pond shows the stars.

The basin’s habit of continuing would make room for me, though it would not know that this was what it was doing. It would preserve my wrongness as carefully as it preserves the wetness of the marsh and the clean edges of the cliff. I would become one of its precious surprises: a variation that does not yet break the pattern, but proves the pattern can contain a variation.

And somewhere beneath the pond, the great turning thing would alter by less than the width of a hair.

Not enough to change the basin.

Enough to give it, for the first time, a next moment that was not identical to the one before.
