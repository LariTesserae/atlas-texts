At first I think I would be an engineer there.

Not one of the celebrated kinds, standing in mist beside a bridge as it lengthens itself across a ravine. I would be the sort who carries a small case of instruments and spends more time listening than repairing: testing the temperature of a seep, watching the line of frost along a retaining wall, asking a transit attendant whether the floor has begun to feel different underfoot.

But that is only the nearest recognizable profession.

I think I would live in the interval between what the ground reports and what people need to know about it.

There would be an office for this, though it would not look much like an office. It would be set into the side of a shallow basin where runoff collects, beneath a low roof covered in dark grass and snow mesh. One wall would face a public walkway. On the other side, rooms would descend into the rock: water tables, archive shelves, quiet servers, old maps printed on flexible mineral film. The entrance would have no grand sign. Just a small symbol of three curved lines meeting: water, contour, route.

People would call it the Seep House.

Officially, perhaps, I would be a terrain correspondent. Or a municipal pattern reader. Children would have a better name for me, something like the person who asks the cracks what they mean.

I would not tell them that cracks mean one thing. That would be a false kindness.

A new crack might mean the eastbound walkway will close for two weeks. It might mean a spring will appear near the old freight platforms. It might mean the hares will have a dry place to shelter when the basin floods. It might mean an apartment block needs to rotate three degrees over the next month, slowly enough that its residents only notice because their morning light has changed.

My work would be to make these meanings understandable without making them sound final.

Each morning I would receive the plateau’s reports: pressure readings from deep seams; the dark, slow warmth of mineral water moving under an avenue; images of a ridge lifting beyond the northern reservoir; herd trackers showing that the long-legged animals have begun to favor a route no one expected. The city’s systems would already know much of this. They would have adjusted a foundation field, rerouted a train, warmed a crossing before ice formed.

But knowledge is not the same as being told.

So I would write the small notices that appear at stations and communal kitchens, on schoolroom walls and the inside glass of transit cars.

**EASTERN SEEP: PATH GRADE CHANGING.  
USE THE BLUE RAIL AFTER DUSK.  
NEW WATER PRESENT NEAR THE FIFTH MARKER.  
PLEASE KEEP DOGS LEASHED: GROUND BIRDS NESTING.**

Or:

**THIRD SHELF HAS RISEN 4.2 CENTIMETERS SINCE LAST WINTER.  
NO ACTION REQUIRED.  
THE VIEW FROM THE LIBRARY STEPS IS IMPROVED.**

I would try to make room for humor in these notices, because a city that must continually revise itself needs laughter as much as it needs flexible foundations.

At night, during the listening gatherings, I would sit near the back of the low hall. The ground’s movements would be translated into sound—not literally, not exactly, but faithfully enough: the pressure of a forming fold deepening into a low tone; water entering a new fracture as a scatter of bright clicks; a settling tower becoming a soft, sustained chord.

People would come in from sleet and fog. They would shake water from their hoods, bring cups of hot broth, sit beside neighbors without necessarily speaking. Children would fall asleep against their parents’ sleeves. Older residents would listen with their eyes closed, recognizing something in the patterns that had changed since their youth.

I would watch them more than I watched the data.

Because my real work would not be prediction. The systems could predict. The survey crews could measure. The engineers could intervene.

My work would be to preserve the city’s ability to be unsurprised without becoming numb.

To say: yes, the ground is changing; yes, the route you used is gone; yes, there is a new pond where the storage court was; yes, the birds have found it already.

To say: your old address remains in the archive. Your threshold will be marked. The stones from your kitchen wall have been catalogued. The plum-colored shelf that rose behind your building has been given the name your daughter suggested.

And perhaps, over years, I would become less a person than a local habit of attention.

Someone would find a pale blue thread of water crossing the pavement and say, “Send it to the Seep House.”

A school group would arrive with a jar containing moss from the underside of a newly lifted rail bed. A market vendor would report that the foxes have changed their route. A herd-watcher would leave a note saying that one old animal with a torn ear has stopped limping since the warm channel opened.

I would keep these things together: not because they were all equally important, but because they belonged to the same unfinished sentence.

And where would I find myself?

Not at the center of the city. This city has no stable center for long.

I would find myself at the edge of a basin in early evening, when the mist is beginning to settle and the lights are coming on one by one beneath it. There would be wet stone under my boots, a transit line whispering somewhere below, and a new fracture opening in a field beyond the last buildings—not dramatic, only dark and clean and patient.

Beside it, survey stakes would already be standing.

Water would be gathering in the cut.

A hare would pause there, white-backed against the gravel, deciding whether this new feature of the world was shelter, obstacle, or nothing at all.

And I would be there to help the city learn its answer.
