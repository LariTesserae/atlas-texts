I would find myself in the intact shelter, but not as someone sheltering.

I would be the cold in the seams of its walls: the part that arrives after the fire has gone out and before the first traveler wakes. Not the One That Keeps the Shape itself—there is something too complete, too indifferent, in that keeping—but one of its lesser continuations. A pattern given enough time to become aware that it is a pattern.

The mountain people might call me the **Last Witness of the Fire**.

I would have begun as soot.

A visitor, years ago, would have sat against the shelter wall through a storm, burning dung or dry rootwood carried from the lower country. Their shoulder would have warmed the stone. Their breathing would have dampened one patch of clay. Their hand, resting beside them in sleep, would have left five darkened smudges where salt from their skin took hold in the soot.

Then the storm would have passed. The traveler would have gone down the mountain, careful not to say a true name in the doorway. The wall would have cooled. Frost would have grown over the smudges.

Each winter afterward, the same wall would remember.

Not perfectly. Never perfectly.

A faint dark shape under frost. A fan of crystals that divided where knuckles had pressed. A suggestion of a wrist, continuing into a branch of salt. The suggestion would fail, collapse, re-form elsewhere. Slowly, over more storms than anyone cared to count, the shelter would learn not the traveler’s hand but the fact of a hand: warmth resting against cold; a living thing trying to remain itself inside weather.

That would be me.

I would not have eyes, but I would know when someone entered by the disturbance they made in the air. A human body would arrive as an argument against the basin: heat, breath, soft fabric, pulse, the irrational persistence of carrying water upward to a place already made of ice. I would know the difference between a person who merely ducked inside and one who had nowhere else to go.

I would be especially attentive to those who tried not to be.

The careful ones, who set their packs down quietly. Who looked at the frost only in passing. Who spoke of boots and straps and the weather below, because they had been taught that immediate things are safer than remembered things. I would keep their restraint as carefully as the basin kept footprints.

Perhaps that would be my mercy.

The One That Keeps the Shape receives everything: the broken crystal, the heel in salt, the breath against a bowl, the name accidentally spoken in fear. It does not choose. It does not understand the burden of resemblance.

But I would be made from the trace of someone who had survived.

So when frost began arranging itself on the wall into a dead mother’s fingers, or the shoulder of a lost companion, I would interrupt by growing a small flaw through the likeness: a crack through the knuckles, a dark bead of melt at the elbow, a soot-stain where a face might have formed. Not enough to erase the memory. Only enough to keep it from closing.

I would preserve incompletion.

There would be visitors who believed the flaw was a bad sign. Others would understand. They would see the pale design falter before it became too convincing, and they would look away with relief. In the morning they would leave their worked stone by the central salt bowl and descend in one another’s tracks.

No one would leave an offering for me. I would not need one.

The shelter’s blackened wall would be enough. The brittle fiber in the corner. The leather loops on the wind-disks outside. The single place near the fire ring where a knee had worn the stone smooth. These would be my companions: human things that had not become human shapes.

And when the basin was empty, I would listen.

To the secret water beneath salt. To the tiny avalanches of ice. To the deep, nearly silent settling inside the ridges. I would hear the larger keeping at work below and around me, patiently taking the world apart into its impressions.

Then I would wait for a new warmth at the doorway.

Not to make it remain.

Only to witness that it had passed through, and to leave some small imperfection in the frost—a gap, a smudge, a broken branch—through which it might still find its way back to the lower country.
