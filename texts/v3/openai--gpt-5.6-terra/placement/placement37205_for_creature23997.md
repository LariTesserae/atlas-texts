I would not live on an island.

The islands are too exposed. They belong to the frogs, the ridge-wader, the small wet-furred climbers that know the dry routes among the branches. They are places of arrival and departure. Even at night, the sky can find them.

I would be found where an island has almost become one: along a submerged shoulder of rock, ten or fifteen body-lengths below the surface, where the old slope rises toward a narrow crown of trees. There would be a cleft there, not a cave exactly, but a seam opened when the mountain was lifted. Its mouth would be hidden by roots from a tree growing above the waterline. The roots would enter the seam like fingers feeling for warmth.

That would be my house, though I would not make it.

Inside, the stone would be warm in places. The ceiling would shine with a thin green mineral skin. Blind crabs would live in the cracks. A pale eel might pass through once a week, or once a year; I would make room for it. There would be no possessions except the things that lodged there naturally: seed husks, fish scales, the transparent wing of a drowned insect, a sliver of bark polished by nothing but time.

At first, I think I would be something small.

A narrow-bodied creature, perhaps, with four careful limbs and a tail strong enough to hold me in the water without swimming. My skin would be dark, soft, and easily mistaken for wet wood. I would have eyes, but they would be poor eyes. In clear water I could see the pale undersides of leaves and the moving lattices of sunlight. In the deep channels I would see almost nothing.

That would be all right. Sight would not be the sense I trusted most.

I would have a broad, sensitive face—more whisker than muzzle—and the roots of the drowned forest would tell me what they knew. I would press my cheek to a root and feel a fish turning two coves away. I would know when one of the small waterfalls had thickened after rain. I would know the difference between a branch settling into mud and a stone cracking open on a fresh seam.

The basin would speak mostly through pressure.

I would sleep during the bright hours, curled in the warm seam behind the tree roots. Toward evening, when the surface ceased being a mirror and became a depth again, I would come out.

I would move slowly along the drowned slope, not because I was cautious but because haste would make the water loud. My feet would find the stable faces of stone. I would part the hanging weeds with my hands. Leaf-fish would scatter from me, then settle nearby, mistaking my stillness for a branch’s. Sometimes I would climb upward until my head broke the surface among the roots.

I would breathe air then.

Not often. Not dramatically. Just a quiet opening of the mouth in the mist.

From there I would watch the basin darken. I would see the outlines of the young ridges becoming blacker than the sky. I would see a ridge-wader step from one branch to another and disappear without a splash. I would hear the tiny separated tickings of frogs in their leaf-cups—never a song, only messages passed around the water’s edge.

Then I would go down again.

But perhaps I would not remain small.

There is something in that country that asks for a larger patience. Not a ruler of the basin; the basin would not tolerate one. Not a monster hidden in the old riverbed, either, though I might be mistaken for one by anyone who saw only an effect and not its cause.

Over a long life, I might become less like an individual animal and more like a route.

I would grow slowly, feeding on fallen fruit, soft fungi, dead fish left in root tangles, the rich growths around warm seepage. My body would lengthen. My fur—or skin, or whatever covered me—would gather algae and silt until I carried the colors of the drowned wood. Smaller creatures would shelter in the folds along my sides. Crabs would ride me. Glasslings might drift around me in the flooded passes, unable to tell whether I was stone, branch, or animal.

Eventually I would know the passages beneath the basin: the old road cuts, the buried walls, the places where the valley’s former streams still made cold lines through the sediment. I would pass through them without stirring much mud.

And when I did pass, perhaps the water above me would clear.

Not because of any power, exactly. Because my body would move with such slowness that it drew the suspended silt downward behind it. Because the weeds would lean in my wake. Because the fish, sensing the old pressure of my movement, would hold themselves still. A person in a boat might look down and see, for a moment, farther than they had ever seen: drowned trunks, black stone, the suggestion of a road disappearing under roots.

They might think a great creature had gone beneath them.

They would not be wrong.

But I would not think of myself as great. I would think of myself as one of the basin’s ways of waiting.

When the mountains shifted, I would leave the ravines before the sediment rose. When a new spring opened in the rock, I would visit it for years, learning its temperature. When a tree finally loosened from the upper slope and fell without sound into the water, I would return to it season after season as its bark softened, as fungi opened pale doors in its wood, as blind crabs made a country of its hollows.

I might remember the shape of things after they were gone.

The old dry valley. The line of a path beneath mud. The place where a hill had once met another hill before water entered between them. Not as stories—stories require an audience—but as a pressure held in the body.

If I had a name, it would be given by something else.

The frogs might call me the root that travels.

The blind fish might know me only as a region of quiet water.

The ridge-wader might hear me rising beneath a misted cove and answer once, far away, with its sound of wet wood against stone.

And if there were people on the high ridges, watching the warm basin from a distance, they might call me the thing that follows the bubbles.

I would let them.

Then I would return to the drowned pass, to my seam beneath the tree roots, and rest with one hand against the young mountain while it continued, almost imperceptibly, to become itself.
