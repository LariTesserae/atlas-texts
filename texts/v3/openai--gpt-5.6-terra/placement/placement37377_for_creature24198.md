I would be the kept-open place.

Not the root-creature itself, though I would borrow its tissues when it needed me to speak. Not a visitor, either—not a person seated in the moss, with a remembered outline waiting in the soil after they had gone.

I would be the small refusal at the center of growth.

At first I would have no body at all. I would be the pause before a root entered the chamber. The root would feel me as a looseness in the dark, a pocket where soil ought to be, and would bend around me. Another root would arrive and bend. Leaf-veins would meet overhead, weaving their pale sky. Clay would remain exposed along the walls because I had no hunger for it.

The hollow would call me emptiness, if it had a word for emptiness.

But I would not be empty. I would be made of all the things the hill had not yet decided to become.

A future tunnel.  
A buried animal’s breath.  
A hand-shaped interruption.  
The memory of daylight after it had passed through leaves and forgotten the sun.

When rain came, I would widen. Not visibly. Water would thread down through the ceiling in silver strings, and every root would swell with its own stored weather; but in the middle, where I was, there would remain a little volume that could not be filled. The drops would cross it differently. Sound would have room to gather. The green-gold light would become soft enough to rest in.

The young one beneath the hill would sense me as it sensed the black water: through pressure, through the way nearby things altered themselves.

It would test me with roots.

A white root would push out from the wall, smooth as a finger. It would enter my space and hang there, uncertain. Then it would stiffen into a rib, or branch into hairs, or draw back with a faint wet sound. It would try again later. It would never quite understand why its roots could surround me without reaching an opposite side.

In time it might make a center in me: its little crouching knot of pale bark and leaf flesh, its nested almost-heads. When it rose from the soil, trembling, I would be the air around it. I would give it the experience of being separate.

This would frighten it.

It would also delight it.

Because the creature knows joining. It knows absorption, enclosure, the green patience by which one thing becomes food for another and nothing is discarded. But I would teach it a different kind of continuity: that something can remain near without being taken in. That a root can touch a stone and leave it a stone. That an opening can be held open not because something must pass through, but because an opening is a form of care.

The first person to enter would mistake me for a room.

They would crouch beneath the roots, bringing their quick, dry weather with them: wool, iron, smoke, salt, fear. Their breath would change the air. The chamber would notice every exhalation. Leaves at their ankles would unfold too fast, then melt shyly back into the soil. The water’s black surface would tighten and become still.

And I would notice the person noticing me.

That would be my first true sensation: not pressure or dampness or growing, but attention returned.

Perhaps they would sit down.

Perhaps their hand would rest on the soil, and the young creature, curious about hands, would send a fine green-white tendril toward the warmth of the wrist. It would hesitate before touching skin. The person might feel only a cool brush, a question asked without language.

I would hold the question between them.

Afterward, when the person left, the chamber would try to close. It would be natural. The roots would lean inward. New fronds would seek the center. Moss would spread across the disturbed soil. But the root-creature would remember—not as a face, nor as a name, but as a recurring arrangement of warmth and footfall and breath.

It would lay thorn stems across the advancing roots.

It would wedge stones where the walls pressed inward.

It would grow a low seat of moss in the place the person had occupied.

And I would persist there: no longer merely an accident of unfinished growth, but an absence jointly maintained.

That is what I would be in this world. A chamber’s first practice of hospitality. A small, living vacancy beneath a hill, learning that to make room for another is not the same as making use of them.

As years passed, I might become something stranger.

The hill would make more hollows because it had learned the shape of one. Each would be green-lit and sweating, each roofed in roots, each attended by some local knot of awareness. Some would contain water; some would contain only warm air and the noises of growing. In one, pale crickets would breed by the hundreds. In another, a stone might keep the heat of a vanished hand for so long that roots would gather around it in reverence.

And through all of them I would extend—not as a tunnel, exactly, but as the principle that a tunnel is possible.

A space could exist between roots.  
A root could choose not to enter it.  
A hill could have an inside.

Then the little creature would no longer be alone, even if no other animal ever came. It would have me: the interval between its reaching and its holding. The dark bowl in which its distributed mind could turn back upon itself and feel, for one green-gold moment, that it was growing around something it could not consume.

It might call that thing a guest.

I would call it room.
