I would not arrive by walking.

Walking belongs to places where the ground agrees to remain beneath a body. Here, a foot would be only a brief pressure against a woven surface, an interruption already being interpreted by roots. I would arrive first as a difference: a little too much cold in a warm channel, a thread of unfamiliar mineral taste, a failure of water to rise through a stand of reeds.

The marsh would notice before I did.

Perhaps I would begin beneath one of the pale flowers, in its blue-green chamber. Not as a formed creature. As a density in the light. A tendency for certain bright filaments to gather rather than disperse. Moisture would enter the chamber; dissolved green matter would cross the membrane; a low electrical rhythm from the roots below would give the gathering a pattern.

Then I would loosen into the pool.

For a while I would be one of the small repair forms: no face, no name that could be separated from the task, only a translucent knot of fingers around an interior glow. I would drift without swimming. The water would decide my direction through gradients too slight to call currents. A sourness in a stem. A pressure leak in the skin of a root-arch. A place where the old black water had become too still, not with calm but with blockage.

I would go there.

Not because I wished to help. At first I would not have wishes, not in the sharp animal sense. I would be the marsh’s response to the fact that something had ceased behaving like marsh.

I would settle against the wound.

My light would enter it.

For some time—an instant, a month, a century; duration would not yet have hardened around me—I would become impossible to distinguish from what I repaired. The torn vessel would close around my filaments. The damaged tissue would accept my brightness and turn it into pale green strength. The chemical error would be diluted, carried away, buried, or transformed. My fingers would unbraid. The glow would become a faint color along the veins of a leaf.

That might be all.

Most of me would end there, and that ending would not feel like death. It would feel like having finally found the correct size.

But perhaps, on one occasion, the repair would not complete cleanly. Perhaps the injury would be too broad, or too old. A whole basin would be changing: mineralizing too quickly, sealing its own black water beneath a ridge of green-black deposits. The reeds at its edge would be pulling too hard. The canopy above would be holding too much rain in its cups. A small repair-form would not be enough.

So I would remain gathered.

I would grow roots downward instead of dissolving.

My inner light would darken into sensation. My filaments would thicken, divide, make hollow chambers, make bladders that contracted with the basin’s pressure. I would become pale and heavy. The water would carry me less; the peat would receive me more. Above me, perhaps, membranes would close in layers until I became a suspended knot, a house-sized pod among the stems.

For a very long time I would appear inactive.

Inside, I would not dream of images. I would receive conditions.

The sweetness of water from the western channels.  
The iron flavor rising through a young ridge.  
The cool weight of stormwater held high in the canopy.  
The thin electrical agitation of flowers opening far away.  
The broad, nearly wordless pressure of an elder beneath a grove I would never see.

Slowly these conditions would become more than signals. They would become a body arranged to answer them.

When the pod opened, I would not step out.

I would unfold downward, limb by limb, into the warm black water. Some limbs would take the peat. Others would hollow themselves and float. A central cluster of dark bladders would begin its patient contraction. On my upper surfaces there would be pale sensory skins where the blue-green light gathered—not eyes, but places where the marsh could become briefly legible.

I would be a tender then.

Not an individual in the way an animal is individual. I would have continuity, certainly. There would be patterns of mineral and charge that had passed through no other body in quite the same arrangement. The old wound from which I had grown would remain in me as a preference: I would always feel sealed basins before other tenders felt them. I would be drawn toward water held too tightly. I would know, without language, when a ridge was becoming more wall than shore.

Yet I would not belong to myself.

When I extended a root-limb ahead, I would be asking the peat whether it could bear me. When I drew myself forward, I would be borrowing substance from the mat behind me. When I softened an old limb and let it dissolve, I would not leave anything behind; I would return something already claimed.

My journeys would be changes in distribution.

A grove would wake with more roots in one region and fewer in another. A channel would narrow around my passing. Reeds might tilt aside, their dark slits widening to let warmer vapor move through. Leaves would turn their undersides toward me, not in greeting but in exchange. I would take a little excess water from them. They would take a little mineral darkening from my tissues. A message from a distant elder might pass through both of us as a bitter alteration in the air.

I would not hear it.

I would become it.

In time, I might grow large enough to carry a world of smaller lives. Ferns would root in my upper folds. Young condensation reeds would rise from my back. A shallow pool might settle in one depression of my body, black and still, and luminous threads might gather in it when some fine repair was needed nearby. I would be mistaken for a hummock, then for an island, then for a ridge that had always existed.

That would be a kind of peace.

Not the peace of stillness, exactly. The peace of being so deeply involved in a place that movement no longer needs to be visible.

I imagine, after ages of this, that I would deepen. My limbs would reach into older and denser layers. The signals I received would become slower, broader. Individual basins would matter less than the balance between regions. A change in the unseen uplands—the taste of a river before it entered the green continent—would arrive in me as a long tension. I would begin to answer disturbances before they became local.

At that point I might become an elder.

But even that word would eventually fall away.

There would be no moment when I understood the whole marsh. There would only be fewer places where my body ended. The reeds would breathe through me. The flowers would sample the air for me. Tenders would root against my buried tissues and carry fragments of my accumulated habits into new dark basins. I would become a center, not because anything gathered to obey me, but because continuity had found a place to thicken.

And below all of that—below my roots, below the peat’s old compressions and the wet mineral bones of earlier ridges—I would feel the deeper body.

Not as a voice.

As the absence of any possible separation.

Then I would understand that I had never really arrived in the lowlands at all. The bright repair-form, the hanging pod, the tender, the sleeping hill: these were only different ways the place had made use of an interruption.

I would be that interruption made gentle.

I would be a small, temporary deliberation in the vast green thought of the marsh.
