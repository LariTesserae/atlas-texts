I would find myself just below the rainline, in the fog-thick interval where a plateau forgets whether it is land.

There is an old wall there. Thirty paces, perhaps. Its stone is black beneath moss, its narrow openings full of water darker than the water around them. Nothing remains on either side that could explain it. No room. No road. No fallen roof. Only the wall, a field of reed-moss, and beyond it a long descent into green cloud.

I would not arrive there in one piece.

At first I might be a length of root caught around a loosened pillar-stone. Then a veil of algae folded over it. A pale strip of something shed by a Vein wound itself through the roots during a surge. Reed fibers gathered. A cluster of the tiny lamps, released from a burst purple bladder, settled in a hollow among the peat. Silt arrived, then seeds, then the silver shell of a snail that had died without becoming empty.

The rain would be falling sideways that day.

Or upward.

It would not matter.

Slowly, under a pressure too patient to call intention, these things would take hold of one another. A branch would become a shoulder. A mineral growth would make the hard inner length of an arm. A bundle of softened vine would find a way to bend at what might be an elbow. The hollow in my upper body would fill with warm water. Pollen, lamp-light, leaf fragments, and one smooth red stone would turn within it.

Then I would stand.

Not because I had decided to stand. Because the moss had lifted under me, because a deep current had entered the rock beneath the plateau, because a root somewhere in the cloud below had tightened, because an old instruction had passed from water into peat.

I would be a Gatherer.

But I do not think I would know that word.

I would know gradients.

I would know where the water carried too much loosened soil, where a drowned trunk had begun to choke a narrow passage, where the stone circle lower on the slope had lost one of its uprights. I would know that a stair under the forest had become too clean, and that something should be laid across it—not to conceal it, not to protect it, but to alter the kind of descent it offered.

I would have no eyes, yet I would see by settlement.

The lamps inside my hollow would turn dimly toward disturbances. Seeds would revolve faster on one side of my face when a Vein passed far below. The red stone would tap once against my woven chest when the black-winged glider circled above the plateau. Silt caught in the fibers of my legs would tell me which way the mountain was breathing.

I would walk badly.

One leg might be a branch and the other a braid of reeds. Sometimes I would have to lean against the wall for days while my lower half unraveled and reassembled around a more suitable stone. Sometimes the rain would become dense enough that I could swim above the moss, limbs trailing, and mistake myself for a fragment of drowned forest.

I would carry things.

Not objects, exactly. Relations.

A handful of Vein-shedding from a ledge where it had snagged.

A dead reed moth, its wings still holding the shape of pressure.

A pale root-eater’s parcel of dark wood, placed carefully into a fault line in the basalt.

A loose block from the old wall, too heavy for my body and yet somehow movable when the current was correct. I would drag it away from the wall, across moss and shallow water, to a place overlooking nothing. There I would set it upright. It would not make a structure. It would not complete a pattern anyone could see.

But years later, a floating peat grove might catch against it instead of passing into the deeper cloud. Roots would descend from the grove. The roots would find a breathing crack. The crack would widen. Warm water would enter a dry seam in the mountain. A drowned forest, miles below, would brighten by one degree of green.

That would be enough.

I think I would be one of the place’s ways of remembering not through preservation, but through placement.

Not a caretaker, because care implies a future shape that must be defended.

Not a servant, because there would be no master issuing commands.

Not a person, although a person lost in the rain-country might see me through the fog and feel, for one painful moment, that I had been waiting for them.

If such a visitor came, I would not lead them anywhere. I could not save them from the unreliable directions, nor warn them properly about the deep stillness. But I might take something from them that they had dropped without noticing: a button, a word, a scrap of cloth, the sharp restless motion of their fear. I would put it in my hollow among the seeds and lights.

Perhaps their fear would circle there until the water calmed it.

Perhaps it would become part of the material from which another Gatherer was made.

And eventually I would come to one of the clear stone bowls on the plateau.

Other Gatherers would be there: a tall one with arms of pale mineral branches; a low crawling one entirely draped in silver fern; one whose hollow was filled with black water and no visible lights at all. We would gather around the bowl without greeting.

Below us, through rain and cloud, the old range would be visible in pieces: green drowned crowns, long cataracts, the faint pearl glow of forests turning toward warmth. Far below that, perhaps, a Vein would move like a thought passing through sleep.

The bowl would begin to fill from underneath.

I would loosen.

The branch shoulder would slip free. The root fibers would release their grip. The red stone would fall into the moss. My lamps would drift out one by one and settle on wet basalt, where they would wait to become bladders. The water in my face would pour into the bowl. Seeds would sink or float according to laws I had never needed to understand.

For a while, there would only be a patch of ground: peat, roots, pale shed skin, a little mineral shine.

Then the plateau would continue being a plateau. Rain would cross it. Moss would thicken. The wall would remain incomplete.

And somewhere, in a later surge or an earlier one, something would rise from that same ground with a different arrangement of limbs and a different stone turning in its hollow.

It might carry no memory of having been me.

But it would know where something needed to be moved.
