I would not arrive here as a keeper.

At first I would think I had come as a lost traveler: someone lowered through an upper hatch with a lamp that fails almost immediately, boots already wet, lungs trying to decide whether the air is thin or merely too alive. I would look for a way across the basin, for a door, for a clean dry place to stand. I would hear the chamber’s sound and mistake it for danger.

Then I would learn that danger is not the same thing as noise.

The sound would enter me slowly. It would become the measure of sleep. The pumps would divide the night. The long fan-concussion would become weather in my ribs. I would learn which shriek means a valve has done what it always does, which means steam is about to find a new seam. I would begin to recognize the silence inside the noise: the slight absence of a familiar thud, the held breath before water turns uphill.

And after some time—after my clothes had taken on the smell of warm iron and mineral rain, after I had learned to put my hand to a wall before I opened my mouth—I think I would become too small to be a traveler.

Not physically small at first.

I would become small in the way attention becomes small: fitted into cracks, alert to temperatures, making a home out of what is overlooked. I would become one of the chamber’s readers.

Perhaps I would be a tunnel rat.

Not one of the bold young ones who run openly across the black tiles during the mild hours, whiskers lifted, tail shining with wet. I would be an older rat, narrow and brown-grey, with transparent ears that carry the brown-gold wall-light through them. My eyes would be poor. This would not trouble me. There would be too much to see here anyway, and sight would be the least trustworthy sense.

I would know the district by my paws.

One paw on a floor tile: water gathering beneath.

Both paws on a low pipe: heat moving toward the copper bends.

Whiskers angled into a seam: dry air, dangerous air, the kind that comes before a purge.

I would have a nest behind a panel that has forgotten how to close completely. It would be reached by crossing a narrow rail, dropping into a cavity beside a warm conduit, then turning sideways through a place where the wall presses close enough to flatten my fur. No large thing would find it. The entrance would be ringed with sharp wire fibers, not because I was fierce but because I had learned the local grammar of safety.

Inside would be a particular order.

A dry core of softbat down.

A middle wrapping of waxy vine leaves, stolen one by one from a nutrient-port wall.

A lining of moth dust, faintly luminous on the darkest shifts.

Bits of black insulation for warmth. Bits of bright foil for warning. A small curved piece of stamped metal, useless to anyone else, which I would keep near the sleeping hollow because it hums faintly whenever the outer weather changes.

There would be other rats in the settlement. We would not speak as people speak. We would drum. We would touch noses. We would press ourselves to the same pipe and feel a message pass through it that no voice had made. We would know who had found a loosened panel, who had lost a litter to a sudden heat burst, who had seen the pale moths climbing too early toward the high grilles.

I would not be important.

That would be a relief.

I would harvest moss where it grows too thick, carrying it home in rolled green-grey strips. I would lick mineral crust from pipes, tasting the changing chemistry of the chamber. I would steal shed cable fibers and pretend they had always belonged in my nest. I would watch the blue-black frogs from a respectful distance. They would seem impossibly still to me, like portions of the chamber that had learned to blink.

The birds would frighten me. Their beaks would strike the pipes with sounds too large for their bodies. Yet I would come to understand that they were not making noise; they were placing signals into the structure. Sometimes, while holding very still beneath a railing, I would feel their messages move through the metal: a bright quick tick from above, a returning answer from far away, then a whole sequence spreading outward through the chamber’s bones.

Food.

Heat.

Scavengers.

Water rising.

The world is changing direction.

When the water reversal came, I would already be home.

That is what I would become: not brave enough to stand in the basin while water lifts itself in shining ropes toward the ceiling, but wise enough to have heard it coming half a day before. Curled in my nest, pressed between warm bodies, I would feel the chamber alter around us. The drains would deepen their voices. Eel-things would move in the channels below. Every bird would leave its rail. The frogs’ cobalt seams would brighten. Even the softbats would be quiet.

Then the rain would intensify.

I would listen to it strike the panel above my nest: not outside rain, not sky rain, but rain made by an old enclosed body answering the weather beyond itself.

And perhaps, after many seasons, I would become something else again.

Not a rat exactly. Not a keeper. Not one of the unseen attendants, though I might begin to understand their patient work.

I would become a small warm pressure in the machinery’s memory.

A path it leaves clear.

A nest it learns not to flood.

One of the innumerable lives whose weight has become part of its calculation.

If someone later entered the district and placed a careful hand against the wall near my hidden settlement, they might feel a faint irregular tremble beneath the usual messages: a pulse too quick to be a pump, too soft to be a valve.

They might think it was only the machine settling.

They would not be entirely wrong.
