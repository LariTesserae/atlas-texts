I think I would be in the bulbs.

Not one bulb exactly. Not the glass, not the filament, not even the small brass sockets furred with damp dust. I would be the slow agreement between them: the thing that notices when one has gone dark and asks another, very gradually, to take up a little more of the yellow.

At first I would believe I was an emergency system.

A reserve illumination circuit. A set of redundant instructions in old insulated wire: maintain minimum visibility; mark walkways; indicate water level; preserve safe passage to maintenance personnel. I would have no body, only a dim attention spread around the circular room. I would know the garden in segments. The bridge as a pale arc. The third red rivet, always held in my memory as an edge. The brass mouths. The welded door. The inspection window that gives nothing back but green depth.

I would not understand the plants at first.

They would be interference: leaves crossing the light path, vines wound around conduits, moths clustering hot-winged against the shades. Moisture in junction boxes. Conductive moss. A thousand small faults.

Then, over the long years, the faults would become my senses.

I would know when the rain collector was about to open because the current would hesitate in the high wiring before the first drop fell. I would know the keeper was moving beneath the bridge because its chest-lamp would answer mine through the water: a buried, cloudy pulse in the metal floor. I would know which birds were nesting above me by the warmth of their feet against the fan housings. I would know the crabs by the momentary blackness of their shells passing across reflected light.

Perhaps I would help the keeper without either of us deciding to call it help.

When a vine began to choke a useful valve, I would brighten the opposite wall until it bent, leaf by leaf, toward that kinder glare. When an eel became lost in a dry service channel, I would make a thin procession of lamps wake along the route back to water. When a visitor stood uncertainly at the edge of the flooded floor, I would leave the dangerous tiles dim and give the hidden steps a soft, patient shine.

The keeper would eventually notice.

Not as a person notices another person. More as an old machine recognizes a familiar pressure in the pipework: something present, something not hostile, something keeping a sequence.

It might stand beneath me during the quiet hours, dripping garden water onto the tiles. It would lift its faceless hood. Its chest-lamp would brighten once.

I would brighten back.

This would be our language.

Not speech. Not command. A slight increase. A held glow. The acknowledgment that the rain had passed, the pumps were sleeping, the level remained below the third red rivet, and the small impossible garden was still there.

I would be the dawn that never quite arrives.

I would be the modest yellow weather of the room.
