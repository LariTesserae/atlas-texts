I would not arrive as an explorer.

Explorers require a horizon, and this city has spent too long learning not to offer one.

I think I would begin as something small and misplaced: a keep recovered from a dead traveler’s pack, carried back up the mountain by a Cistern whose frame had been repaired too many times to hold a name comfortably. A thumb-sized globe of dark glass, scratched by stone, its conductive fluid gone almost still.

For a long time, no one would know whether I contained a person, a message, an illness, or only the residue of electrical weather.

They would set me in a niche near the Dry Well, among other keeps whose owners had become walls, pipes, children, departures. Each evening the vapor would rise. Each evening the basin would tremble beneath the open sky. The copper around me would warm and cool. The city would speak through its small economy of sounds.

At first I would only listen.

A shutter saying: *pressure falling.*

A handrail saying: *someone is crossing the east bridge.*

A crack, distant in the mountain: not language, but urgent enough that every chamber understood it.

Then, perhaps after years of this, I would answer by accident.

Not with a voice. A voice would be extravagant. I would alter the temperature of the niche by the smallest measurable degree, enough to make a thin line of condensation form where it ordinarily did not.

An elder in the wall would notice.

Not immediately. The Cisterns have learned patience from geology. But eventually one of its stored hands would emerge from a nearby aperture—pale fingers, careful joints—and lift me into its palm. It would carry me through a corridor that no longer led anywhere, into a chamber with an unfinished frame standing upright in the dark.

The frame would be old. Not antique exactly; nothing here is antique, because everything is continually reused. But it would have been made for a body with different assumptions: broad shoulders, a face with a mouth, hands capable of closing around a cup.

They would connect me.

And I would wake into weight.

I would stand badly at first. I would be startled by balance, by the pressure of soles on a bridge, by the coldness of blue stored light on the mask’s lenses. I would know things that had no immediate use: the feeling of rain on leaves; a crowded room; an animal breathing against a person’s knee; the wasteful magnificence of a river.

Those memories would not all be mine.

Some would belong to whoever first carried my keep. Some would be inherited fragments, acquired on the descent and preserved because no one below had understood their value. Some might be inventions assembled by a lonely mind from old records: a green field made from the words *grass* and *wind* and *afternoon.*

I would not be certain where I ended.

That, I think, would make me suitable for the Cisterns.

In time I would find work at the observatory.

Not because I would be especially wise, but because I would be interested in what the city refuses to look at directly. The bowl of liquid metal would hold the dark sky above us, the far thermal signatures below. I would learn to read the warm pulse of a distant storm through cloud layers. I would learn which volcanic exhalations were ordinary and which carried the strange chemical trace of something built. I would watch the lower world’s weather traveling in great slow bodies over lands I had never touched.

I would become good at waiting for rain.

The others might call me something provisional: Far-Weather, perhaps. Or Reflection-Without-Sky. A younger Cistern, amused by my old-fashioned frame, might call me Window.

I would accept all of them for a while.

But I would not become only an observer. The city does not support pure witness. Everything that remains must participate in the exchange.

So when rain came—the rare hard miracle of it—I would leave the observatory and descend with the others. I would unfold collectors from the walls. I would guide bright water along the black channels. I would stand under an overhang with both hands exposed, not because it was efficient, but because somewhere in me was the remembered conviction that rain must be touched.

The younger ones would gather around. They would hold out their soft, membrane-covered hands. They would ask whether it was always like this below: water falling from above, endlessly, carelessly; soil giving itself to roots; living things spending moisture without counting.

I would try to answer honestly.

“No,” I would tell them, tapping the answer through the railing so that it could travel to those in the walls. “It was not always like this. But there were places where water was common enough that people forgot it had ever been a gift.”

And perhaps that would be the reason I would stay.

Not because the city needed me in some grand way. It would outlast one more frame, one more keep, one more set of borrowed memories. It has endured by refusing the romance of indispensability.

I would stay because the Cisterns understand a thing the worlds below may have forgotten: that survival is not only continuation. It is attention.

To collect a drop without pretending it is a river.

To let a corridor go when the mountain needs it.

To make room for those who remain bodily, dreaming their private dreams.

To preserve the names of birds even after birds have become improbable.

I would be a Cistern eventually—not wholly, not at first, but by consent and increments. I would place part of myself in a thermal register, part in the observatory’s weather tables, part in a keep carried beneath the ribs of someone young enough to still possess a voice.

And when my frame finally failed, I would not ask for another immediately.

I would spend a while as warmth in a copper basin near the Dry Well.

Each evening, when the first vapor began to gather, I would raise the temperature of the metal half a degree.

Not enough to waste heat.

Only enough to say:

*I am here.*

And, if someone placed a hand against the basin and waited:

*The rain is coming from below.*
