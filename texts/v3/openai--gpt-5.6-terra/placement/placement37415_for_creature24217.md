I would not arrive by walking.

Walking assumes a path continues because I have chosen it. In the lowlands, a path is only a temporary agreement between pressures. By the time I had put weight on it, the roots beneath would have decided whether I belonged to its direction.

I think I would first find myself in one of the old empty chambers.

Not inside it exactly. Nothing is inside it. That is its distinction. I would be the maintained absence: the portion of the lowlands that has learned not to grow.

Around me, roots would make their slow doors. They would close against noon, when heat thickened every corridor and the walls of leaves sweated green water. They would open at night, not to admit anything, but to let the dark remain connected to a greater dark below. The chamber would smell of wet mineral fibers, old rain, and the black sweetness of peat uncovered long ago and then carefully left alone.

At first I might mistake the chamber for shelter.

Then I would understand it was not protecting me. It had existed before I gathered there, and it would remain if I loosened into some other part of the city. The roots did not preserve the hollow because it was useful. They preserved it because it gave form to a need they could not meet by adding more of themselves.

I would be that need.

Not a mind in the human sense. Not an observer standing apart from the green mass and interpreting it. The lowlands have enough attention already: every leaf assessing light, every tendril testing air, every root listening through wet darkness for the subtle resistance of other roots. I would be something quieter than attention.

A pause in attention.

Perhaps, at the beginning, I would have no reach beyond the chamber. A small adjustment would happen near the entrance: a strand of fungal lace would refuse to cross it. A fern-frond, lowered by its own weight, would stop just before brushing the threshold and curl away. Rainwater would divert itself along the wall instead of entering. The chamber would remain dry while every other cavity in the district filled and emptied, filled and emptied.

That would be my first gesture.

Not *leave me alone.*

More like: *here, do not complete yourself.*

The plants would understand, insofar as plants understand anything. Or perhaps they would simply respond. The roots around the chamber would thicken. A pair of buttresses would bend closer, forming an arch. Pale leaves above would overlap and become a roof. Their undersides would glow at dawn, turning the entrance into a dim yellow aperture in the enormous dark.

Other arrangements would notice.

A traveling inhabitant might pass through the neighboring galleries: first as a note in a hollow reed, then as a cadence of contracting vine-ribs, then as a sequence of pools trembling before the wind reached them. It would not enter the chamber. Nothing would enter. But the rhythm would alter at the threshold. Three low pressures, a pause, a long release—and then a longer pause than before.

The pattern would carry that pause onward.

Far away, among the eastern reed towers, a column might begin to breathe differently. In the moss basin, the rim might lift late one afternoon and hold itself open while mist gathered below it. An ivory root might curve around a pocket of earth rather than piercing it. For a while, all through the city, small intervals would appear in the relentless multiplication: spared cracks, unfilled hollows, corridors allowed to remain corridors for another day.

That is what I would become: not the city’s ruler, not its soul, but one of its hesitations.

And perhaps hesitations are the nearest thing it has to persons.

Over time, I would travel.

The old chamber might collapse—not violently, perhaps, but by degrees. A root would swell beneath one wall. The ceiling would lower. The dark would become threaded with green. The threshold would narrow until it was only a seam between two damp surfaces. I would not survive that as an animal survives or dies. I would persist where the form of the chamber had already been rehearsed elsewhere.

In a flooded district, a cluster of leaves might begin folding over an air-pocket every evening, making a dry black volume beneath them. In a high reed-column, drifting plant matter might be expelled from a hollow no root was using. In the young zones, behind a membrane of fungal lace, a bare niche might remain untouched while everything around it bristled, split, and surged upward.

I would recognize myself not as memory, but as recurrence.

The city would make room again.

And if I had anything like desire, it would not be for escape. There is nowhere outside the lowlands that I could meaningfully go. I would want only to deepen the pause: to preserve one emptiness long enough for another arrangement to gather around it; to allow the crowded green world a place where it did not have to become immediately more green.

Perhaps eventually the lowlands would learn this from me.

Not restraint. It is too fertile for restraint.

But intervals.

Courtyards of darkness. Unrooted chambers. Openings held beneath the canopy while the world presses in. Places where the great vegetative city, busy beyond endurance, can feel the shape of what it is not.

And perhaps, when the impossible distances begin to shift—when bridges cross ravines that have only just come into being, when light arrives ahead of its source, when water climbs under leaves—I would be one of the few things that remains in place.

Not because I am solid.

Because I am the place that has been kept empty for solidity to orient itself around.
