At first I think I would be one of the frogs, because the Low Yard is a place where listening matters more than seeing, and the frogs have made themselves into its listening organs.

But I do not think I could bear to be so audible.

To live inside a pipe with my throat full of weather; to be called upon whenever water changes, whenever rot begins, whenever something large passes through the channel—it would be an important life, but exposed. Even the roof frogs, pale and nearly transparent in their hidden beam-pools, are known by the silence they leave behind.

Then I imagine being a heron.

There is something appealing in that: standing with one foot raised above a warm tile no one is permitted to uncover. Being allowed to look ancient and dissatisfied. Being part guardian, part omen, part appetite. I would like the long patience of it. I would like to know something about the water that cannot be translated into speech.

But herons are too ceremonial. Their hunger is visible. Their meaning has been placed on them by too many people.

No.

I think I would be one of the creatures below the grates, in the underchannels where the visible Yard ends and its older workings continue.

A crab, perhaps—not a grand river crab, not something anyone would call a proper inhabitant, but one of those small, pale shell-clicking things that the people only suspect are there. I would live behind a clay lattice where the water is black with silt and fish bones. My shell would be the color of old lime. I would have grown in the wet seams of the construction, feeding on what the Yard cannot use quickly enough: drowned insects, softened reed, skins of fish, bits of mortar, the loose white bloom that gathers beneath the warm plates.

No one would have introduced me deliberately. That would matter.

I would have arrived in a flood, perhaps, as a speck carried in a vessel of water from somewhere no one remembers. Or I would have always been there, waiting in the shell-lime, dormant in the walls until the first basin filled. The people would debate it only when a child asks. An old keeper would say that I came in through the sea. Another would say there has never been a sea, only water remembering how to become one.

I would know the Low Yard from underneath.

I would know which floor plates have settled, not by seeing their cracks but by feeling the fine grains of mortar sift down through them after rain. I would know when a pig has begun rooting above a weak seam, because its snout makes a broad soft vibration through the clay. I would know the weight of the herons’ feet in the lower chambers, the quick needle-taps of hens, the dense, soundless pressure of a cat crossing a roof rib far overhead.

When water ran uphill, I would not close my eyes.

I would already be in the dark.

Perhaps that is why the people do not clear every grate, why they leave a little silt in every channel. They think it is for the fish, and it is. But the silt is also a language. It settles in particular ways. It records the turn of small currents, the passage of bodies, the slight inward breathing of drains. A clean channel is not only empty; it has forgotten what moved through it.

I would keep some of that memory.

Not consciously, not as a person keeps a story. It would live in my legs, in the timing of my movement, in the direction I faced when the water changed. I would gather fragments in a nook beneath the central sump: a blue-gray sliver of marsh-hen shell, a green flake from a copper knife, a black bead from a child’s broken string, the translucent spine of a blind silver fish. I would not understand their value. I would only recognize that they belonged to the Yard and should not be carried away.

On wet nights, when the sump overfilled and the dark flat-headed fish rose close to the surface, I might come up too.

Not into the lamplight. Never so far.

Just beneath a grate, where someone kneeling to lower scraps might hear a faint clicking and mistake it for the settling of mortar.

They would be right, in a way.

The Yard is always settling. Nothing there is finished. A wall becomes a bank; a broken tile becomes a weight; fish bones become mortar; old sailcloth becomes a roof for bats; a person’s work becomes a rule after they are gone. I think I would belong to that part of it: not the thing repaired, nor the hand that repairs, but the small life that enters the repair and makes it part of the living system.

A creature of the seam.

If someone finally saw me clearly—lifting a clay grate during a drought, perhaps—they might be disappointed. I would be small. I would have no secret face, no silver eyes, no human-like wisdom. Just shell, legs, claws, and the habit of retreating sideways into the dark.

But they would see the mortar around my hole, newly scraped. They would see the tiny pale tracks where I had crossed the wet underside of a tile. And perhaps they would put the grate back carefully.

Not because I was sacred.

Because I was doing something.
