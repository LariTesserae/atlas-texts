I would not arrive by the gate.

The gate is too clear an idea: an opening made for someone who knows they are entering. I think I would find myself already inside, halfway up some stair where the treads are wet and uneven, one hand on a wall furred with moss. Above me, something large would shift in the vines. Below, water would be moving through a grating with the careful, private sound of a person trying not to wake anyone.

At first I would think I was a person.

Not because I had a name, or a room, or even a face I could remember, but because I would know to duck beneath the low arch. I would know that the third stair from the landing rocks under weight, and that the shallow basin by the red-plastered wall holds frogs with bright yellow throats. I would know to put my bare foot on the darker stones, because they are warmer, because heat rises through them from some old channel below.

I would be carrying something.

A bundle, perhaps: roots, damp cloth, seeds wrapped in leaf skin, a broken cup full of pale larvae. Or a length of vine cut from one place and intended for another. The valley would not require grand acts from me. It would have no use for a ruler, an explorer, a chosen heir. It would ask only that I notice a drain beginning to choke with leaves. That I lift the grate. That I pull out the compacted nest, apologize to the beetles, and let the water continue down toward the lower cisterns.

But I do not think I would remain human in the ordinary sense.

The darkness would alter the boundaries of that word. I would spend enough years—or enough nights—in the warm passages that my hearing would become more useful than my eyes. I would recognize the dogs by the hush of their paws and the particular damp-animal smell each family leaves under an arch. I would know when goats are moving overhead because dust would sift through ceiling cracks in a certain rhythm. I would distinguish rainwater from cistern water by its voice: rain impatient, bright, striking; cistern water low and old, speaking from inside the walls.

The parrots would know me as a source of salt.

The bats would fold around me without changing course.

The cats would allow me to see them only when they had decided I was no longer worth hiding from.

Perhaps I would be one of the nearly-human keepers, though not a keeper in the sense of ownership. More like a small corrective force within the great habit of the place. A hand that moves a fallen tile so that water does not enter a nest chamber. A body that carries fallen fruit uphill to where the monkeys have stripped the trees too early. A memory that knows which reservoir must not be opened during the hottest weeks, because something blind and white breeds there beneath the black water.

I would sleep in an old lamp niche, perhaps—not because it was a proper bed, but because the wall behind it still held warmth. I would line it with dry leaves gathered from the upper gardens. My clothes would be patched with vine fiber and softened by mildew. There would be green staining at my wrists. There would be a persistent smell of limestone, wet fur, crushed fern, and the mineral sweetness that rises from cistern mouths.

Then, after longer, I might become less like a keeper and more like one of the valley’s passages.

A route.

The dogs would come when I made a low sound in my throat. The crows would watch from the beams as I crossed a court. Frogs would continue calling around my feet. I would not command them, and they would not serve me. But my movement would belong among their movements. A dog would know that if I passed toward the eastern terraces, the upper water was running. A parrot might follow because I had found fruit. A goat might wait on a ledge until I had moved a loose stone from its path.

Or perhaps this is still too human a story. Perhaps the valley would gradually reveal that I had never been a person there at all.

Perhaps I would be a vine.

Not one of the vast old load-bearing vines, but a young reaching thing beginning in a planter beside a sealed doorway. I would grow toward warmth, then toward water, then toward the sound of wings. My first leaves would open in darkness. I would not know the mountain as a shape. I would know it as tension: stone at my roots, empty air beyond the wall, another living stem somewhere across the alley.

I would cross.

Years would pass through me as dampness and thickening. A monkey would test me with one hand, then trust me. Parrots would tear strips from my leaves and carry them into nest-holes. Snakes would lie along me in the evenings, borrowing the last warmth from the wall. A child—if there were still children, if the hidden inheritors still made them—might climb me to reach a roof garden and never think to thank me.

That would be enough.

But no: I think I would be water before I was vine.

Not the rain itself, which falls loudly and leaves, but the retained water: the seep gathering behind walls, the slow dark pressure inside old conduits. I would be the part that remembers. I would travel where no animal can travel, through hairline gaps in mortar, beneath tiled floors, inside the roots of trees planted centuries ago. I would cool the underside of stairs. I would make moss possible. I would swell the throats of frogs. I would bring blind fish their food and carry the small white eggs of something unknown from one reservoir to another.

Sometimes I would emerge as a bead on a broad leaf.

Sometimes as a drip from the chin of a carved stone animal.

Sometimes I would fill a cistern until the eels woke and moved.

If I were water, I would know the whole valley without ever seeing it. I would know every repaired wall by the change in pressure. I would know when a roof had collapsed because silt would arrive. I would know when a goat had died on a high ledge because, eventually, a sweetness and a darkness would enter the channels. I would know the hidden people, if there were any, by the warmth of their hands in the basins.

And perhaps that is what the custody is: not a collection of creatures, but the valley’s way of continuing to recognize itself.

So I might be the hand clearing a drain.

I might be the vine spanning the alley.

I might be the unseen water under the paving.

I might be a dark bird carrying a shard of blue tile to a nest above the bridge vault, adding it to a glittering accumulation no one will ever find.

But if I had to choose the form that feels most native to that built darkness, I think I would be a small, nearly invisible frog.

I would live in the lip of a high basin where rainwater collects in a cracked glaze. I would be brown-green, mottled like old stone, with toes made for wet vertical surfaces. In the dayless hours I would sit still enough to become part of the masonry. At the first change in pressure before rain, I would wake. My throat would fill. I would call—not loudly, perhaps, but with a note that belonged precisely to that court, that height, that particular arrangement of walls.

Other frogs would answer from cisterns above and below.

The sound would travel through pipes, under doors, across roof gardens, through the sleeping chambers of dogs and cats, past the warm niches where whatever remains of people lies listening.

And the valley, hearing itself alive, would continue.
