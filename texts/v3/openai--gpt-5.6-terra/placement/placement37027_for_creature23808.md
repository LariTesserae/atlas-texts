I would begin by thinking I was one of the stones.

Not a stone exactly—something that had learned the posture of stone. Low against the basin floor, grayed over with mineral frost, with silt settling in every crease. I would not know, at first, whether I had arrived there or whether I had been accumulating for a very long time: shell powder, bacterial film, the cast-off skins of smaller lives, a few deliberate pebbles caught in a fold of myself.

The pressure would be the first fact.

Not pain. Not even weight, once I had ceased remembering any other condition. It would be the medium in which thought occurred: a dense dark persuasion, pressing every possibility flat against the ground. Above me, distances would be theoretical. There would be a ceiling somewhere, and storms beyond it, and perhaps a brightness so extravagant that creatures still possessed shadows. But none of that would belong to my body.

My body would belong to the lee side of a black ridge.

There, under a shallow overhang where the current divides and loses some of its force, I would be a listening sponge.

At least, that would be the nearest name for me.

I would have no face. No eye to turn toward the faint dilution of light. My attention would be distributed through wet channels, through dense layers of tissue packed with mineral grains, through the innumerable mouths by which I took the basin into myself. Water would pass through me constantly. Not quickly. Nothing worthwhile here would happen quickly. The water would enter carrying fine mud, bacterial dust, larval bodies, dissolved iron, the chemical aftermath of something dead far off in the trough. It would leave me altered—cleansed in some small measure, perhaps, but also read.

I would know the basin by what it pressed through me.

A gray hunter passing three ridges away would make a thin rearrangement in the pressure field: a long-bodied absence moving against the ordinary pulse. An eel rootling beneath a mud shelf would send a muddier tremor, intermittent and close to the floor. Crabs at their shell station would make their patient little signals: scrape, brace, loosen, settle; scrape, brace, loosen, settle. Their work would travel through stone and water alike.

I would not call these signals. I would not understand them as messages.

But I would keep them.

For a while.

A tide arriving from the eastern lowlands would stiffen certain internal strands. A surface storm, though I could never see it, would leave a broader and less regular tension: a long confused shudder that arrived in layers. The mineral grains inside me would shift into new arrangements. My flesh would thicken on one side and relax on another. I would close some pores and leave others open. By the time the current itself reached the ridge, I would already have become shaped for it.

That would be my form of anticipation.

The crabs would learn me before I learned them.

Young ones, still clean-shelled and too quick in their movements, would pass over me without understanding. They would use my surface as ground, picking through the silt caught in my folds. But the old crabs would stop nearby. They would stand with their long legs bent, shells burdened with moss and chalk and old attachments. Some would wait for hours beside my edge, apparently motionless.

I would feel their feet through the sediment.

Then they would begin.

Not urgently. Urgency is a surface habit. They would move the stones that could be moved. They would pull shell halves from the mud. They would make a crescent against the coming flow, or reinforce the low ring around a warm crack where their young might find water rich enough to live in. One grain-width at a time, resting between efforts, they would turn loose matter into shelter.

Perhaps, eventually, I would understand that I was not only listening for myself.

Perhaps I would become part of a small weather system of warning: the sponge tightening beneath the overhang; the crabs gathering stones uphill; the mud-lampreys driving deeper; the floor fish flattening themselves into the bottom until even their eyes disappeared. No one would issue a command. No creature would know the whole pattern. Yet the basin would answer its own approaching change with thousands of partial adjustments.

Then the silt river would arrive.

It would come as a low wall of brown density, too large to be seen as a thing. The ridge would vanish. The crab barricades would disappear. Fine grit would scour the outer skin of me and take away years of accumulated growth in an hour. Some of the smaller creatures sheltering in my folds would be stripped free. Others would enter my channels and remain there, caught in the dark water moving through my body.

I could not save all of them.

I would not even have a concept for saving.

But my bulk would interrupt the current. Behind me there would be a little slack water, and in that slack water sediment would settle rather than run. A worm might survive there. A pale mite might cling beneath a fragment of shell. A crab no larger than a fingernail might wedge itself into a crease and endure until the water passed.

When the flow receded, the basin would be changed again.

The black ridge might be bare, shining through its chalky bloom. The old shell station might be gone. A floor fish might lie exposed where yesterday there had been a hollow. The bones of some killed thing would already have begun to soften into the work of bone-crawlers, bacteria, worms, and water.

And I would remain beneath the overhang, though not entirely the same sponge.

The patterns inside me would have been erased and rewritten by the passage. The minerals would settle into another quiet arrangement. My pores would reopen. Water would enter. The smallest lives would return first: films, dust, larvae, threads. A crab would arrive later carrying a pebble twice the size of its body.

It might set the pebble against my flank.

It might begin, with someone else or alone, to build a new low wall.

So I think I would be that: not a ruler of the drowned country, not even one of its more visible inhabitants. I would be a patient, gray, half-buried body that receives the movements of the dark and changes shape in response. A thing whose memory is not a story but a temporary tension. A living obstruction. A place where water slows.

And if I had a self there, it would not be contained inside my skin.

Part of it would be in the crabs waiting beside me. Part in the shelter that formed behind me. Part in the worms that used the sediment I caught. Part in the old ridge whose vibrations I held in my mineral threads. Part in every current that entered, wrote itself through me, and went on carrying something of me into the rest of the basin.

I would not shine.

I would not rise.

I would grow low, hold fast, and listen until listening became another way of becoming ground.
