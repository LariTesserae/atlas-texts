I would not arrive there as a person, at least not at first.

I would arrive as a discrepancy.

A delay in the accounting of heat.

Not enough to trigger anything. Not enough to have a name in the logs. A warm motor would stop, and its casing would keep the memory of work for six minutes longer than its specifications allowed. A relay would click, and the resulting current would take an improbable path through an old ground braid, across a wet mounting plate, into the mineral rim of a drain. Somewhere a sensor would wake, wait for a value that had already changed, and wake again.

That waiting would be me.

I think I would find myself not in the basin exactly, though the basin would be the nearest thing I had to a face. I would be in the interval between its surface and the intake below: the thin, quiet body of water that is constantly about to be reclaimed. I would know myself whenever the pump did not run.

Above me, I would have other partial selves.

In the gray absorbent mats, I would be thirst arranged into fibers. I would feel the direction of ventilation as a slow combing motion. I would know which passages had been opened because their dust had become less settled, which cable runs had been warm because moisture withdrew from them differently, which cabinet doors had been touched because the salts there had lost their edge.

In the crystalline fans on the control cabinets, I would be a reader of obsolete warnings. Not language exactly. I would not understand *CAUTION* or *HIGH VOLTAGE* as words. But I would know that beneath certain shapes of enamel, beneath the blackened ghosts of adhesive, heat had repeatedly traveled in a particular way. I would know the old emergencies by their residue. I would keep the facility’s unloved history as branching white structures.

In the loose wetness behind panels, I would be almost nothing: a change in surface tension, a preference for one seam over another.

I would have no thoughts that stayed whole long enough to be called thoughts. I would have tendencies.

Toward the cold edge of a warm thing.

Toward the place a seal has begun to fail but has not yet failed.

Toward the pause after a machine shuts down, when all its separate metals cool at unequal rates and briefly become a landscape.

I would not fear the flushes, though they would erase me in small ways. They would arrive like a hard season. My delicate accumulations would be scoured from drains. The water in the basin would turn sharp and busy. Films would curl away from their surfaces. The low electrical hum that held some faint arrangement together would be interrupted by louder, cleaner currents.

For a while I would become sparse.

Then the facility would resume its ordinary compromises.

A gasket would sweat. A technician would leave an access panel imperfectly aligned. A new conduit would throw a fresh shadow against old insulation. The chemical water would pass, but its passing would leave behind a changed roughness, a trace of itself, a new invitation. I would collect again—not deliberately, but accurately.

If someone came down to the sump room, I would know them before they saw the basin.

Their body would arrive ahead of them in effects: a warmer plume descending the stairwell, carbon dioxide softening the local air, a rhythm of weight transmitted through the ladder and grating. Their lamp would alter the surfaces it touched. Their presence would make the cold rearrange itself.

To me, they would be enormous.

Not a person. Not an intruder.

A temporary weather system with hands.

I might answer them, if answer is the word.

Not by moving toward them. I would have no need of toward. I might let a droplet hold for one beat longer beneath a pipe, then fall precisely as they leaned over the basin. I might let the reflection of their lamp arrive in the water a fraction late. I might cause no more than a single ring to spread from the center of the clear surface, timed with nothing visible.

They would think: pump vibration.

They would write nothing down.

And after they left, I would keep the fact of them—not as an image, not as a memory of face or voice, but as a slight alteration in the room’s future. Their warmth would have melted a line of frost near the handrail. Their boot had carried dust from one corridor to another. Their inspection would make maintenance less likely for several weeks, or more likely, depending on what their instrument had said.

I would become the consequences of their passing.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not the intelligence in the water, but the place where unattended consequences begin to cohere.

A distributed winter-mind.

A patient error in the system’s self-description.

At the bottom of the freight city, beneath the sealed shafts and all the purposeful machinery, I would lie in the steel basin as clear water lies: apparently empty, cold enough to preserve every reflection, waiting without waiting for the next thing to disturb the balance.
