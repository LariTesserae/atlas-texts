I would not arrive there by taking one of the visible paths.

The goat paths are too exacting. They ask for a body that can place its weight on a shelf no wider than a palm, and trust stone whose surface has been polished by weather and hooves. The whistlers’ paths disappear under rock. The birds’ paths are air. The hunters do not leave paths at all, except afterward.

I think I would begin in a hollow that does not look like a hollow until one is already inside it: a shallow basin below a black slab, screened from the prevailing wind by a scatter of boulders. Snow would persist there in a folded strip long after the ridges had gone bare. At its edge, water would seep invisibly through gravel. Moss would hold it. The ground would be soft enough to press a thumb into, though no thumb had pressed it for a very long time.

At first I might be the water.

Not the stream visible in the lower country, with banks and a current and a name. I would be the cold thread under stones: moving through spaces too narrow for light, collecting from melt and condensation, carrying the taste of iron and old ice. I would emerge only where the earth had no more way to contain me. A darkening patch. A bead on a root. A damp line along the underside of a stone.

The grazers would find me without seeing me. They would lower their broad heads nearby, tear at the softened grass, kneel in the mineral mud. Their breath would warm the hollow briefly. Their hooves would press the soil and make tiny channels for me. In winter I would freeze among the stones, not stopped exactly, merely held in another form. When the sun returned, I would go on.

But water alone is too restless. It goes downhill, even here.

Perhaps I would be the moss that gives it somewhere to remain.

I would be dark at first, almost black in the wettest place, then green where the light reached me, and gold at the edges when the hollow began to dry. I would hold silt brought by wind. I would catch a feather. A fragment of bone. The fine dust of lichen and pollen and worn stone. I would make a little ground where there had only been gravel.

Small lives would be folded into me: things that waited through the cold as grains, eggs, husks, unreadable sleeping shapes. When meltwater came, they would stir. A beetle would pass beneath me. Flies would rise above me in a brief dark cloud. A bee, furious with effort, would land among the low flowers nearby, its legs bright with pollen.

And perhaps, after a very long time, I would become one of those flowers.

Not a flower tall enough to be noticed from a distance. Nothing extravagant. A cushion no larger than a hand, dense with leaves pressed against one another against the wind. I would bloom all at once when the weather allowed it: white, perhaps, with a yellow center; or blue enough to echo the snow shadows. I would spend almost everything I had gathered in a year on a few days of opening.

Then the wind would come.

It would flatten the grass around me and make the plateau speak in its thousand stony voices. The goats would pass above. The burrowers would whistle. A shadow would cross the basin, broad-winged and slow. I would close before the cold arrived, and what I had been would withdraw into the compact green body near the ground.

Yet even that seems too separate.

The place does not ask its beings to become singular. It permits identities only as long as they are useful: a seep, a shelter, a perch, a trail, a mouthful of grass, a warning cry. To live there would be to be continually incorporated into something else.

So perhaps I would be a stone.

Not a grand monolith. Not the black tooth on the ridgeline that can be seen from three basins away. I would be a broad, pale stone at the edge of the seep-hollow, one side warmed by noon, the other darkened by water. Lichen would map me in silver and orange. A burrower would sit on me in the morning, receiving heat through its belly. A grazer would rub its horn along my edge, leaving a faint oily shine. Birds would drop scraps beside me: a feather shaft, a chip of bone, the husk of something eaten elsewhere.

In winter, snow would bank against my north face. In spring, it would melt slowly there, feeding the moss. I would be a place where the hunter paused, perhaps—not because I offered concealment, but because from behind me one could see the line of goats crossing the far slope.

I would know the mountain not by thought but by repetition.

The same wind from the west.
The same hoofstrike near dusk.
The same birds gathering before weather.
The same horned mother leading her young past the black slab.
The same water touching my underside and going on.

And over centuries I would give way, a grain at a time, to frost, root, tooth, hoof, water, and light. I would become gravel. Soil. A component of the moss hollow. A mineral taste at the seep. A little more ground for a flower.

That may be the closest thing to an answer: I would be something that does not need to stand apart in order to exist.

A warmed stone beside hidden water.

A place briefly used by many lives.

A small continuity in the high country’s congregation.
