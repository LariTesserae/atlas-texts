I would not arrive there by climbing.

Climbing presumes a destination: a summit, a view, some clean conclusion above the weather. This country has no use for conclusions. It would receive me gradually, by reducing the world around me to fewer and colder things—the last tree gone, then the last birdcall familiar to me, then the notion that silence is empty.

I would find myself where the glacier lifts slightly from its bedrock.

Not in the open blue body of the ice, nor in the valleys where the herds move, but in one of the black chambers underneath: a place roofed by ice and floored with stone ground slowly to powder. Water would pass through it with the steady, hidden force of blood through a sleeping animal. There would be air sometimes, caught in pockets along the ceiling. There would be a faint mineral gleam where the walls had frozen clear.

At first I might think I was a creature.

Something white, low to the ground, with too many joints.

I would move through the shallow water by folding myself rather than swimming. I would have no eyes, though not from deprivation. Sight would simply never have entered the design of me. My body would know pressure instead: the remote settling of the glacier overhead, the approach of a stone rolling in some unseen channel, the minute warming that means meltwater has found a new route.

I would taste the mountain.

Iron would be bitterness.  
Old pollen would be sweetness.  
The bodies of drowned insects would be weather.  
Fresh snowmelt would be a season.  

But this would not quite be enough. I do not think I would remain one animal for long.

The cave would teach me that a body can be porous.

The pale things in the water would pass over me and leave behind their films. Fine worms would gather in the warmer folds of stone near my resting place. Bacteria would inhabit the soft creases of my skin and alter what I could digest. A dark algae, carried down through a fracture in a brief summer thaw, would find a translucent edge of me and remain there. I would become not a single life but a chamber of agreements.

Perhaps I would have a name among no one.

The Underlistener.

Or the Silt-Gilled One.

Or, more accurately, nothing with a name at all: a living instrument left beneath the glacier to register its work.

I would not hear sound in the way an owl hears it. The owls would be far above, turning their round attention through the thin dark. Their calls would not reach me except as a faint trembling through meltwater, long after the wood-creak had crossed the ridges and been absorbed by snow.

But I would hear the glacier.

Not its voice, exactly. Its decisions.

The slow downward yielding of an ice wall.
The sudden clean opening of a pressure crack.
The change in a stream’s mineral taste when it has found fresh stone.
The annual darkening of water when the surface algae wake.
The many small collapses that permit the mountain to retain its enormous shape.

The glacier would pass over me, and I would remain.

Years would have no edges below the ice. There would be periods of abundance, when droplets carried spores, drowned flies, root fragments, the soft torn feathers of cliff birds. There would be lean intervals in which the water ran clear and cold for so long that all activity narrowed to a pulse. I would slow with it. My gills would scarcely move. A visitor, if there could be one, might mistake me for a pale mineral shelf.

Then something would change.

A storm above. A warm seam in the season. A temporary river pouring down a crevasse.

And I would rise.

Not quickly. Nothing there needs to be quick. I would uncurl from the floor of the pool or channel and move toward the new water, carrying with me the small hidden world that had grown upon and within me. I would feed. I would leave clusters of eggs, perhaps, in a crevice warmed by the breath of stone. They would cling there through years of darkness, waiting for the right chemistry of water.

Yet even this feels too separate.

Given enough time, I think the glacier would wear away the distinction between me and my place. Silt would collect in the folds where I rested. Moss spores might arrive, though they could not grow in the deepest dark; perhaps one would wait, dormant, in me. Minerals would enter my skin. My dead would nourish the cave’s white beetles and transparent scavengers. The water that passed through my gills would later emerge beneath the ice, feed a black tarn, moisten a fern root, bead on the lip of a flower, or freeze again in the snow caught high on a ridge.

So where would I find myself?

In the interval beneath the visible world.

In the hidden water between the glacier’s weight and the mountain’s patience.

And what would I be?

Not its guardian. The place has no need of guarding.

Not its ruler. Nothing here is ruled.

I would be a small, blind recorder of continuance: an amphibious, many-inhabited thing in the cold dark, living long enough to know that the mountain is never still—and humble enough to understand that knowing is only another form of being carried along.
