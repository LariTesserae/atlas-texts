I would not arrive by the pass.

I think I would wake in the basin already wet.

Not drowned. Not born there either. I would wake in one of the lower stone rooms after a rise, with the water gone down to ankle depth and the walls shining black. Someone would be taking bowls from a pulley shelf. Someone else would be pushing a goat through a doorway too low for its horns. Nobody would ask who I was until they had determined whether I could carry something.

At first I would be useless in the ordinary way of outsiders: looking for level ground, trying to understand where one building ended and another began, mistaking ice for stone and stone for ice. I would look at the pond too long. I would hear the mountain only as noise, until I learned that the basin’s sounds are not noise at all.

Then, gradually, I would become a keeper of crossings.

Not a builder, exactly. Not one of the Unlidded, though I would envy them their patience. I would be the person who knows the temporary paths: which plank has warped but not failed, which rope can be trusted with a child but not a loaded sled, which roof remains dry after the second rising but floods from below after the third. I would carry spare lashings looped around my shoulders. I would keep knots in my fingers.

People might ask me, in place of a name:

Which way from here?

And I would point without looking down.

I would live high enough to hear the pond through the floorboards. My sleeping place would be above a family who keeps salamanders in shallow basins beside their hearth. Their oldest child would teach me the names of the goats. Their youngest would distrust me for a season, then begin leaving small objects in my pockets: a trout scale, a crooked willow leaf, a bit of red thread, a bone boat.

I would have no possessions that could not be lifted in one armful.

A waterproof skin coat, patched at both elbows. A bowl. A hook. A length of good rope inherited from someone whose name I never learned. Perhaps a knife too dull for prestige and too useful to give away. I would keep a little bundle of dry willow bark under my shirt, not for fire but for writing the routes I feared forgetting.

But the basin would not let me remain only a person.

There, anything that holds long enough becomes almost alive.

My rope would know me. My boots would remember which slick surfaces I had crossed. The ladder outside my door would complain differently under my weight than under another’s. If I left the basin, perhaps these things would go on expecting me. Perhaps, after a while, a stranger would find my old line tied between two roofs and feel it tighten slightly in their hand, as if recognizing the pressure of a familiar palm.

And perhaps that would be what I really was: not the keeper of crossings, but one of the crossings myself.

A route between households.

A person whose work was to make interruption passable.

When the water rose, I would not try to save everything. I would know better. I would help lift the children, the needles, the food, the sleeping furs. I would cut loose what had to be lost. I would stand on a roof in the glare while the pond climbed the steps and the bridges rose on their lashings, and I would count—not houses, never houses—but breaths.

One breath between the knocks beneath the water.

Two.

Then the goats moving upward before anyone called them.

Then everyone else moving too.

At night, if I sat near the pond, I might eventually be invited to sit with the Unlidded. I would not speak. I would watch steam take shape and lose it. I would learn the feel of the willow records: shallow cut for a harmless swell, deep notch for a flood that entered homes, a cross-mark for a life lowered into the basin.

I would touch the oldest sticks and understand that attention has a body.

It is not merely watching.

It is remembering what watched before you.

And if, one morning, black cloth appeared tied around the finger of stone, I would be among those who carried things without being asked. I would bring water to the bereaved household, though water would be the last thing they wanted. I would repair a door. I would take a child across a shaking bridge. I would say nothing that tried to make loss smaller.

In that place, I think I would become quiet—not from fear, though fear would be there, always, under the ribs—but because quiet would be the form of my listening.

Eventually the basin might take me in one of its less dramatic ways.

Not in the pond.

Perhaps I would grow old beside a route no one else remembered. Perhaps my knees would fail on wet stairs. Perhaps a child would take over my rope work and correct my knots with gentle impatience. I would become one of the old people who can no longer move quickly but can tell, by the taste of the air in a room, whether the water is rising from below.

Then I would be a map.

And when I died, a route would fall in.

But not entirely.

Someone would still know that there had once been a way from the third shelf to the dry beam above the goat enclosure, if you crossed the roof of the woman with the blue hood, ducked under the whale-bone spar, and trusted the old rope with the red repair in it.

They would call it my way.

And for a while, perhaps, it would hold.
