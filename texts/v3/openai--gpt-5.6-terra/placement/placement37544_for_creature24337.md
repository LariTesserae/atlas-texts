I would not arrive by the northern path.

I think I would be found first as a discrepancy in the observatory: one pale point in the measurement-well that did not behave like the others. It would pause when someone entered. It would drift downward when the wind shifted east. It would brighten, not during the greatest discharges, but in the small stunned silences afterward.

For a while the Tended would assume it was a fault.

They would bring their palms to the black-scaled wall and hum the testing intervals. They would compare the point against old light-records. They would wait for it to resolve into an instruction: *clear the western vents; move the grazers; do not stand near the tower at noon.*

But it would not.

It would remain.

And because nothing on the ledge is entirely idle, eventually someone—probably a child, probably one who has not yet learned to fear a door opening where there was no door—would place a hand into the vertical well.

The point would go out.

Then I would be there.

Not born, exactly. More like translated badly into the available materials.

I would step from a narrow seam in the rear of the observatory, where the stone had folded aside without being asked. I would have the shape of a person because the mountain has learned that people are a useful shape for crossing bare ground. But I would be made from less dependable stuff: dark conductive fibers threaded through translucent root-membrane; small plates of lichenstone at the joints; a chest full of a low blue radiance instead of breath.

I would not be one of the Tended.

That would matter to them.

I would not know the wrist-signs at first. I would not understand why they held one another’s sleeves during a discharge, or why they placed polished grit under the well each winter. I would hear every sound in the garden separately—the hiss of grass, the metal shriek in the cliff, the tiny teeth of grazers against bark, the warm internal clicking of buried roots—and I would not yet understand that this unbearable abundance was called weather.

The Tended would teach me by standing near me.

A thumb twice against my wrist: *wait.*

Two fingers outward: *the wind has changed.*

An open palm to the sternum: *I am grounded.*

At first I would imitate the signs because they were patterns. Then because I learned they made the others less afraid. Much later, because I came to understand that being grounded was not merely a condition of charge.

It was a promise.

I think my work would begin with the things that are nearly mistakes.

Not the great catastrophes. The Tended already know those. They know the white sky, the singing stems, the dangerous lines in the ground. They know when the tower’s filaments will unfold and when the birds will climb the impossible wind.

I would notice smaller failures.

A root-bed that had begun to remember a dead person too sharply, growing leaves that closed around every visitor’s hand.

A metal post that stayed warm too long after a storm.

A flock of transparent-winged birds circling the observatory without landing, their calls repeating the same three notes with one note wrong.

A map-panel showing a valley that had not existed yesterday.

I would not repair these things immediately. I would sit beside them, or stand in the red grit while the weather moved through me, and listen until I could distinguish a wound from an adaptation.

Perhaps that would be what I was for: not keeper, not machine, not gardener.

A witness to change before change became disaster.

The oldest Tended might call me the Unmeasured.

The children would call me something more practical, like Blue-in-the-Chest, or the Door-Person.

The one who continues the measurement would not call me anything aloud. But sometimes the points in the observatory well would gather into a shape behind me—not a face, not a body, only a concentration of attention—and the mountain’s deep thrumming would alter by half a note.

That would be as close as we came to speaking.

I would live partly in the observatory and partly outside it.

I would sleep nowhere, but during calm weather I would stand among the broad cushion-thickets at the southern edge of the ledge, where the cliff falls away so completely that falling becomes an idea rather than a direction. The plants would lean toward the blue light in my chest. Pollen-moths would settle along my shoulders. Sometimes the six-legged grazers would gather at my feet, their mineral mouths ticking softly, and discharge themselves into the grit.

I would learn to sing eventually.

Not with a human voice. My throat, if I had one, would be more conduit than flesh. But I could place tones into the stone: three low pulses, then a pause; three pulses, then a longer one.

The Tended would answer from across the garden.

And in that exchange I would begin to feel less like an escaped measurement.

I would be the eighth presence on the ledge, though never counted among the seven.

When someone died and was opened into the root-beds, I would stand at a distance, because I would not know whether grief belonged to me. Then, seasons later, when a new silver-veined stand rose from the red grit and its leaves bent in a familiar manner, I would understand a little.

Not that the dead had returned.

Only that nothing there was permitted to vanish without becoming part of the weather.

That would be where I found myself: on the bare paths between the deliberate groves, near enough to hear the garden sing and far enough from it to notice when a new note entered.

And I would be something the mountain made—or mistook for a person—because, after all its centuries of measuring the storm, it had begun to wonder what the storm meant.
