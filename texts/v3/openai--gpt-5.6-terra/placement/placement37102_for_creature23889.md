I would begin by thinking I belonged to the upper terraces.

Not because they are beautiful, exactly, though they are: the cold white rooflands, the wet black seams, the goats moving soundlessly between vents. But because the upper terraces are where one can still see the whole basin pretending to be still. I would have a room there, narrow as a drawer, with a window onto a freight line that never leaves. I would know the schedule of the rain engines by the feeling in the backs of my hands. I would keep a small illegal pot of moss beside the heater and insist it is doing well.

Perhaps I would be a surveyor.

Not an important one. Not one of the bright-pole people who arrive in teams and make the official measurements. I would be the sort assigned to secondary structures: handrails, old stairways, pipe brackets, the edges of market platforms. I would carry a folding rule, a pencil, a packet of seals, and a little hammer for testing hollowness. My work would be to notice changes too small to require action yet.

Three millimeters here.

A new crack in the ceramic skin of a hill.

A drainage channel that has begun to hold water against its intended slope.

A pigeon flock choosing a new cable.

I would write these things down in a precise hand. Then, at home, I would write them again less precisely in a private notebook: *The valley shifted eastward today, but only enough to disturb the spoon in my cup.*

But I do not think I would remain a surveyor for long.

The place seems designed to make every profession become a form of listening. A goat-handler listens to what the goats refuse to eat. A water worker listens to the sequence in which a pipe cools. A child listens to the forbidden silence beyond an unlit doorway. Even the people below, if they are really there as people say, must listen to the walls through their beds.

So perhaps I would become a keeper of impossible records.

There would be a minor office in a former switchroom, somewhere between the upper housing terraces and the first roof plain. Its official title would be something dry: Department of Deferred Structural Observations. It would have three desks, an unreliable warming coil, cabinets full of maps that disagree with one another, and a window that looks directly onto the side of a chimney.

My duty would be to collect reports no other department wants.

A woman in Canal District Seven says the rain in her kitchen ran toward the stove.

A child says the stairs to school took one fewer step than yesterday.

A fox with a blue ear-tag has been seen entering the same sealed lift shaft for six consecutive dimmings.

The swift clerks report that the creatures have begun folding their outer wings inward before the weather engines release cloud.

An unfinished warehouse has developed a shadow on the wrong side at noon.

Most of these reports would be dismissed, filed, cross-referenced, and forgotten. But I would preserve them. I would learn that the basin does not announce itself in disasters. It makes small revisions. It changes its mind by degrees. It lets the inhabitants discover, years later, that their routines have been living around a new shape.

And eventually I might realize that I am not a resident at all.

Or rather: I would have a residency plaque. A ration seal. A district assignment. People would nod to me in markets and know which tea I buy. But something about me would remain unmeasured.

I might be one of the chimney swifts.

Not wholly one. At first I would think this metaphorical. I would find soft gray membranes in the pockets of my work coat. I would wake with soot under my nails and the sensation of warm upward air under my shoulder blades. During the brief dimming, I would feel an ache in my ribs whenever the exhaust stacks cooled.

Then, one morning, climbing an inspection stair near a frost-ringed chimney, I would hear the structural ticking deepen.

The valley would be changing.

Not moving in the way the surveyors mean. Not a hand’s width of ridge displacement, nor a slumping terrace, nor another careful bureaucratic relocation of a market. Something older would be occurring below the pale aggregate and inaccessible foundations. A long pressure would be answering itself.

The swifts would emerge in numbers I had never seen.

They would pour from the chimneys slowly, silently, unfolding wing after wing after wing—not rising so much as being remembered by the air. Their pale bodies would turn in the brightness. For a moment the whole roofland would look underwater.

And I would understand, perhaps, that the swifts are not unfinished people.

They are not machines that escaped their documentation.

They are the part of the valley that learned how to leave without departing.

That is what I would be: a chimney swift clerk who becomes, by increments too minor for any form to record, a chimney swift. A creature of warm shafts and pressured light. A keeper of the place’s discarded possibilities.

I would still come down sometimes.

I would perch above the old switchroom window while the next clerk worked at my desk. I would watch them sort reports of rain, foxes, tilting steps, breath appearing in rooms that ought to be warm. I would want to tell them which reports matter.

Not the loud ones.

Not the ones about collapse.

The ones about preference.

The new hill’s shadow.

The pigeons choosing a cable no map includes.

The way the far ridge has shifted by less than the width of a hand.

But I would have no voice suited to the task. Only the slow opening and closing of my wings in the warm exhaust.

And perhaps that would be enough.
