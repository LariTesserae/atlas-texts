I would not arrive as a traveler.

There is too much distance here for arrival to mean much. A traveler implies a route, a purpose, a place from which to return. In the high country, routes dissolve after a few seasons of wind. Purpose becomes another kind of weather.

At first I imagine myself as one of the gliders, because they possess the clearest freedom: a dark shape held above the stone, reading the ravines by the answers they give to a single note. I would ride the warm lift from an iron-red wall into the thin blue air. I would know each escarpment not by its appearance but by its voice—the bright hard return of a clean fracture, the muffled silence of a new fall, the long chambered answer from an arch that has widened another inch since last season.

But I think I would become tired of seeing so much and touching so little.

Then perhaps I would be a pale grazer.

I would move without urgency between the green basins, low beneath the enormous sky. My body would be built for restraint: broad feet for the fragile crust, a narrow mouth for taking only what the mats can spare, a hide that accepts frost and mineral snow without complaint. I would sleep beside warm stone and wake with white light laid across my back. When I met another of my kind on a plain, we would stand together in the wind—not needing to speak, not even needing to decide anything—and afterward continue in separate directions, each carrying the other’s scent for a while.

There is something merciful in that life. To be known at a distance. To require little. To leave behind, accidentally, the beginning of green.

But even that feels too mobile. Too visible. The grazers are part of the country’s long conversation, but they are still visitors to each basin.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not beneath the deepest stone, perhaps. I do not want the patience of a thing that must wait centuries merely to brighten a vein or widen a crack. Nor would I be one of the mats alone, although I admire their way of receiving the world: not chasing water, but opening to frost; not resisting the cold, but making a use of it before the sun comes.

I would be one of the burrowing lights beneath the red-leaved rings.

A branching colony under the pale crust of an amphitheater. Not one body, but many soft mineral forms joined in darkness. I would live where warmth rises slowly from below and the frost comes down from above. The world would reach me as pressure, temperature, salts, the fine downward taste of roots. I would know the passing of night through the cooling of stone. I would know dawn through the first thin melt descending in the pores of the earth.

Above me, shrubs would make their ring.

Their red leaves would turn in the dry wind. They would spread roots toward my warmth, and I would feed on what their dead leaves gave back. I would not think of this as exchange. It would simply be the shape of being alive: giving off what another thing needs, taking in what it cannot use, widening slowly together.

At night, after a hard freeze, I would shine.

Not brightly enough to challenge the stars. Not brightly enough to be noticed by anything crossing the open floor quickly. My light would move in pulses beneath the mineral crust, a blue-green thought passing from one hidden branch to another. A glider overhead might mistake it for reflected moonlight. A grazer sheltering near the ring might see the faint glow at its feet and choose to remain until morning.

That would be enough.

I would not be the center of the amphitheater, though I would lie beneath its center. I would not command the shrubs, the frost, the dust, or the slow expansion of the ring. I would only participate in their continuance.

And perhaps, after many years, when the red shrubs had extended outward and the old center had gone dry, some fragment of me would remain in a crack under a fallen plate of stone. It would wait in the dark, nearly without metabolism, while dust gathered and the wind redrew the floor.

Then one cold night, a little frost would find its way down.

A root would follow.

And somewhere else in that vast bright country, beneath a new circle of red leaves, I would begin to glow again.
