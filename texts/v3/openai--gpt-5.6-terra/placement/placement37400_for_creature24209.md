I would not begin in a house-tree, nor on a caravan route, nor high in the shelves of a crown-fern where the wind has names.

I think I would begin as a small uncertainty in the root mat.

Not a creature at first. A pressure. A place where many materials had gathered without yet agreeing on a purpose: the black sweetness left by a dead browser, a broken thorn-hen egg, the white threads of fungus, pollen washed down from some far flowering, the damp imprint of a human knee where a path-singer had rested at dawn. Beneath that imprint, warmth. Above it, the bright hard day.

For a while I would be only a listening.

The upland seems loud from within itself. It would be impossible to hear everything, but I would learn the difference between noises that mean nothing and noises that are decisions. The groan of water shifting inside a reed tower. The shallow percussion of bark-apes. A distant chain of thunder-vine reports. The dry, irritated tightening of roots beneath a path that has been neglected. The immense muffled digestion of the land.

Then I would rise somewhere people did not expect a thing to rise.

Perhaps in the narrow open ground between two great towers, where travelers saw only tawny root-swell and heat shimmer. A pale shoot would appear there at dawn, thin as a finger, curled around a bead of water. By noon it would have become a low fan of ribbed leaves. By the next dawn there would be three of them, all angled toward the route.

People would call it a path plant, eventually. Or a traveler’s ear. They would give it a name in their own language, something practical and a little affectionate. They would discover that its leaves turned before rain—not toward the storm, but toward the places where rain would *not* fall, warning them away from dry crossings. They would learn that when a climbing cat crossed nearby, its veins darkened with sap, and that its stiff hairs clicked softly together in the direction of the cat’s movement.

But those would only be the things it did that people could understand.

What I would be, deeper than that, would be a collector of passage.

I would grow along the old gray trails, not invading them but taking their margins: a series of broad low leaves, pale underneath and dust-colored above, easy to mistake for dead foliage. I would drink the first wash of condensation. I would feed it through fine root channels to the thirsty sections of the mat. In return, the mat would tell me things—not in language, not even in sensation exactly, but through changes in tension, sweetness, heat.

Someone had walked there yesterday carrying a wound.

A herd had passed three days ago, and one old beast was limping toward the northern cisterns.

A root-whale had turned below the eastern rise; the path would buckle by evening.

A seed-broker had spilled an unripe bundle of blue pollen under the shade of a tower reed, and something beautiful or disastrous was beginning there.

I would not remember people by their faces. I would remember their weight, their warmth, their footfall patterns, the minerals in their sweat, whether they sang as they walked, whether they put down water before taking it. The path-singers would be familiar to me. Their voices would travel into the ground ahead of them. Their brushes would clear the dust from my leaves. Their little cups of first-drawn cistern water would sometimes spill at my roots deliberately.

They would think they were keeping the trail open.

I would know that we were keeping one another open.

In the flowering season, I would change. My ordinary leaves would yellow and lie flat, and from their center I would lift a number of translucent stalks, each bearing a small cup shaped like an ear. The cups would fill with morning moisture and amplify the sounds of the upland. A traveler kneeling beside them could hear, in miniature, the song of nearby towers: low moans for emptiness, bright whistles for full cisterns, the sudden closed-mouth silence that meant a grove had sensed a predator.

Some would carry my cups with them, carefully cut and smoked, using them as weather instruments. This would hurt, though not terribly. I would have learned that being used was not always the same as being harmed. The upland was full of such arrangements: leaf-cutters and fern shelves, thorn-hens and house roots, cats and the groves that endured them until they did not.

A child might lie in the dust beside me one evening, listening through a cup-leaf while their family repaired a shade screen nearby. They might hear a strange rhythm beneath the ordinary vibrations: not rain, not herd, not root-whale.

A distant walking.

Many feet. Too regular. Too quiet.

And perhaps, because I had grown from old compost, from forgotten pollen, from generations of swept paths and songs pressed down into the mat, I would recognize that pattern as something the upland had not heard for a very long time: strangers approaching from beyond the shelf of the world.

Then I would do the only thing I could do.

I would turn all my leaves toward them.

Not as warning, exactly.

Not as welcome.

As attention.
