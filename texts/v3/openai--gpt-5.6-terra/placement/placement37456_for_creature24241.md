At first I think I would be one of the unplaced.

Not because I would resemble them, necessarily, but because I would make the same error: I would see a distance and believe that it could be crossed; see a wall and believe it divided one place from another; see a tool and believe it had been left behind by someone who might be found.

I would arrive carrying too much certainty.

Perhaps a notebook. A water flask that would become warm before noon. A compass whose needle would tremble over the copper plates and then turn, slowly, toward some buried hinge in the ground. I would try to record the plain in lines: canal, ridge, kiln, wall, tower. Soon the lines in the notebook would begin to join themselves. Margins would thicken into foundations. A sentence written at morning might be mortared shut by evening.

Then I would understand that a map is an especially vulnerable thing here. It is an unfinished construction pretending to describe another unfinished construction. The plain would take it personally.

For a while I might become a traveler in earnest. I would follow the advice of an unplaced figure who had mica over its eyes and carried a wrapped chisel against its chest.

“Do not walk toward the towers,” it would say.

“Then where?”

It would place one palm against the wet brick beneath us and listen.

“Toward the place that is becoming an arch.”

This would seem unhelpful until I learned that an arch is not merely a shape. It is a temporary permission. For an hour, perhaps, the ground on either side of it agrees to remain separate. One can pass through. One can be neither incorporated nor corrected.

I would seek such intervals.

I would sleep only where a wall had recently collapsed, among bricks still too warm to touch. I would learn that the safest shelter is not a finished room but the idea of one—a square of low ridges, a threshold marked by two loose stones, four upright lengths of fiber leaning toward each other without yet becoming a roof. In a completed room, the plain might decide that I belonged to the inventory: a vertical element, a useful mass, an obstruction requiring enclosure.

At first, I would fear the mortar-bodies.

Their blank kiln openings. Their many-handed elbows. The patient way they would stand beside a misplaced object until it began to remember its use. I would hide from them behind unfinished cisterns and in the shadows of the tripod towers. But they would find me eventually, not by searching, but because a route of paving would rise beneath their feet and mine until we met.

One would look at me—if looking is what it did.

Steam would pulse from the dark aperture in its brick head.

Then it would reach toward my notebook.

I would expect it to take the book apart.

Instead, with one trowel-limb, it would press a strip of wet mortar along the spine where the binding had begun to loosen. With another, it would stamp a small mark into the cover: a square with one side absent.

An incomplete room.

A door, perhaps.

After that I would follow it for a time. Not because it led anywhere I wished to go, but because it knew how to move without insisting on being a traveler. It would pause at seams and kneel, releasing joint-things from its chest. They would spill out in pale threads, gather around cracks, and click their small damp jaws together while the ground decided what shape to be next.

I would try speaking to them.

At first I would ask questions.

Where is the edge?  
Who made this?  
How do I leave?

They would answer by arranging grit in patterns I could not read: a shallow circle crossed by a straight line; a staircase descending into a pool; the same square-with-one-side-missing stamped over and over until steam blurred it away.

Eventually I would stop asking questions and begin saying things that were truer.

“I do not know what I am for.”

At that, the joint-things might become very still.

Then they would gather around my boots, build a little curb of mortar, and lead a thin line away across the paving.

Not an answer. A place to put the sentence.

That is when I would begin to change.

The plain would not turn me into a wall all at once. It has more delicate methods. My footprints would remain after I stepped away, not as impressions but as clean rectangular tiles. The skin of my knuckles would acquire hairline divisions. Rain would strike my shoulders and bead there, unable to decide whether I was cloth, clay, or mineral. When I slept, I would dream of weight—not being crushed, but being relied upon. A pressure from above, a pressure from below, and the strange, satisfying necessity of holding them apart.

I would resist this.

I would wrap copper wire around my wrists. I would keep walking. I would carry some small useless object that had not yet found its place: a cracked blue bowl, perhaps, or a broken awl with its bone handle polished by hands that no longer existed. I would tell myself that as long as I carried it, neither of us could be assigned.

But the plain is patient, and the object would have its own patience.

One day, in a region where the seams glowed white beneath the fog, I would come upon a wall growing from the ground in a long curve. It would be neither ruin nor building yet. Its wet bricks would rise one by one, breathing heat. And there, halfway along its length, would be an empty space precisely the size of the bowl I had carried for years.

I would know, with a grief that was also relief, that it had been waiting.

I would set the bowl into the gap.

The wall would accept it without closing over it. The blue interior would face outward, becoming a small impossible window. Behind it there would be no room—only hot vapor, darkness, and the long grinding motion of the world beneath.

Then I would know what I would be.

Not an unplaced person, though I would have been one for a while.

Not a mortar-body, because I would never have their calm.

Not a buried mason, though perhaps every body here is only a small surface expression of one.

I would become a keeper of misplacements.

A carrier of things whose purposes had become too narrow for them. I would walk the wet plain collecting the objects that no longer matched the structures around them: handles without doors, pulleys without loads, keys without locks, bricks stamped with symbols belonging to foundations long since softened back into clay. I would not try to restore them correctly. Correctness is one of the plain’s hungers, and it has enough servants.

Instead I would look for the wrong opening.

A stair that wants a bucket at its base.  
A wall that needs a mallet embedded in it like a heart.  
A channel shaped exactly for a length of rope.  
A half-made kiln that has forgotten how to become hot but remembers how to hold something carefully.

Where I placed an object, the ground would hesitate.

That would be my work: to make hesitation possible.

The maker beneath the plain has forgotten what it meant to make. Everything it touches is pulled toward completion, incorporation, use. I would not oppose it; nothing on that continent could oppose it for long. But I might introduce a small error into its vast grammar. A window facing soil. A doorway opening onto water. A copper plate laid beneath a pillow of bricks so that, when the seams brightened, it reflected the red-gold light upward.

A place where the world could see itself unfinished.

And perhaps, much later, an unplaced traveler would find me.

They would see a figure standing beside a low wall in the vapor, dark garments beaded with rain, hands wrapped in green copper. They would call out. I might not answer immediately. The joint-things would be listening underfoot. Somewhere far beneath, a buried mason would be closing one immense hand.

But eventually I would turn.

I would offer them a tool I had carried a very long time.

Not to use.

Only to keep it from becoming certain what it was for.
