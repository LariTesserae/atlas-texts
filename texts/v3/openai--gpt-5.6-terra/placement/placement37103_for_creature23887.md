I would not be on the ridge.

The ridge is too exposed to belong to anything for long. It is where weather arrives first and leaves last; where a bird may pass, where snow may hold, where stone may split open in the night. I would look at it often, because everything in the basin looks upward eventually, but I would live lower down—in one of the places where the mountain briefly forgets to fall.

A seam, perhaps, at the foot of a new scree fan.

Not a pool with a clear edge. Not a stream that has decided it is a stream. A dark, shivering run beneath three flat stones, where meltwater comes out of the gravel and slips through moss before disappearing again. Above it, blocks still carry their fresh fracture faces. Below it, sedges have found a few inches of black mud and are making a thin bronze ring around standing water. The place would be cold enough that even in summer the water would feel like snow thinking about itself.

At first I imagine myself as one of the little black beetles.

I would live under a stone whose underside stays damp. When cloud lowers and rain begins tapping the basin into a thousand small surfaces, I would move through the creases beneath gravel, hard-backed and quick, feeding on whatever the weather had delivered: a drowned midge, a shred of feather, the soft dark remains of a larva caught in a drying channel. I would know the mountain not as a view but as pressure, temperature, smell. The warmth of a recently lifted rock. The sudden white violence of daylight. The tremor that means a hoof has landed nearby.

But a beetle is too much a creature of departure. It can run when the stone shifts. It can disappear into another crack.

I think I would be something obliged to stay.

A caddisfly larva, perhaps.

I would begin in the cold water under the stones, no larger than a needle clipping, with a soft body that cannot bear the open world for long. I would gather what the basin gives me: grains of quartz, black flakes of shale, a fragment of sedge stem, the pale shell of a dead insect egg. With silk from my own body I would make a case around myself—not elegant, not symmetrical, but fitted to the particular materials of that one wet seam.

My house would be a small argument that the place could hold together.

Its stones would come from a slope that had fallen only recently. Its plant fragments would come from sedges growing in soil made partly from old dung, old feathers, moss, and things too small to name. Water would pass through it continuously. Fine green algae would grow on it. A mite might walk over it. A bird, probing at the edge of the pool, might take me for a pebble and leave me untouched.

I would anchor myself to the underside of a rock where the current is strongest.

There I would feed on what the water brings: loosened algae, fragments of moss, fine brown particles washed from the basin floor. I would learn the season through force. In rain, the current would darken and pull hard at my case. In frost, water would thin and become glass-cold, carrying fewer drifting things. When snowmelt came, the whole channel might rearrange in a single afternoon. Stones would knock together with that metallic clink from the gulches. Silt would bury one entrance. A new flow would find another route.

Perhaps my stone would move.

Perhaps I would be carried a few yards downstream and deposited in a shallower place, among gravel too bright and bare to feed me. Or perhaps the channel would dry from above while water continued invisibly below, leaving me sealed in a pocket of wet mud, waiting without knowing how long waiting would be required.

That seems right for the basin: to be a life no one notices unless they kneel in the rain and put their face close to the water.

And yet I would not remain a larva.

In time—if the pool lasts, if the freeze is not too deep, if no bird’s beak finds the right stone—I would loosen myself from the case I made. I would rise through water that has never been warm. I would climb a sedge stalk or the wet side of a boulder. I would split open.

For a few hours, perhaps a day, I would be winged.

Not a grand mountain bird, not one of the ravens that crosses the entire basin with apparent intention. I would be a small, soft-winged thing in the gray light, drawn toward the dim silver reflected from another pool. I would fly low over the water, past the tracks of hare and ungulate, past the moss cushions and the black ribs of rock. I would find another of my kind, if there was one. We would leave eggs in the current. Then the weather would take us.

My life would be almost entirely hidden: under stone, in water, inside a case built from the mountain’s unfinished debris. My visible life would be brief enough to seem accidental.

But I would be part of the basin’s slow insistence.

I would turn suspended fragments into a body. I would be eaten, perhaps, by a bird that would leave droppings on a shelf above the pool. Those droppings would feed a patch of moss. The moss would catch silt. The silt would become a little soil. In that soil a sedge might root. Its roots would hold the edge of a channel through one hard rain. The channel would make a more reliable wet place. More larvae would live there.

Nothing grand would happen.

The mountain would still shed stone. Frost would still enter every crack. The water would still change its mind. A whole slope might come down and bury my pool in pale rubble.

But somewhere beneath the new gravel, water would begin looking for a route again.

And if it found a dark seam between stones, if algae clouded the edge of a small pool, if enough organic drift gathered in the current, then something like me would be there: carrying a house made of the place’s broken pieces, holding on as long as holding on was possible.
