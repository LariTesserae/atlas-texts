I would not arrive there as a person for long.

At first I might think I had become one of the small, permitted things: a moth with mineral-gray wings, perhaps, or a crab carrying a borrowed garden over its shell. Something able to pass under the root ceiling without tearing it, something light enough to cross the green skin of the pool without breaking through.

But the basin does not seem to need another visible animal. It has hunters, cleaners, grazers, drinkers, pollinators, listeners. Every apparent vacancy is occupied by a function. Even the empty air is worked by mosquitoes, mist, warm currents, silk.

I think I would become one of the things that notices when a rhythm is wrong.

Not the basin itself—that life is too broad, too old, too evenly distributed. It would be like claiming to be a body because one has felt its pulse through a wall. Nor would I be the thing below the blue crown. Whatever drinks there is too deep in its purpose, too secretive even for the plants that serve it.

I would be a small intelligence in the connective tissue: perhaps a fungus, though not a fruiting one. A pale web under the moss and root mat, invisible unless the floor were peeled back. I would thread through old wood, dead insect shells, the soft dark edges of canals. I would taste the basin through what enters decay.

A beetle dying in a leaf cup would send its bitterness through me. A broken vine would give me a slow green shock. The fish lights beneath the mat would arrive as faint electric flickers along wet fibers. I would know the frog had not called before anyone else knew it. I would know when a spider web had gone untended, when an ant road had shifted, when a pitcher plant had closed on nothing for too many days.

Not grief, exactly. More like accounting.

I would keep no records in marks or memory. My knowledge would be chemical and immediate. A missing lizard toe pressing on a stem. The iron taste of a rainstorm. The salt of a moth wing decomposing in the wrong place. A root growing too close to the blue crown, needing to be persuaded outward by dryness, by bitterness, by the quiet withdrawal of nutrients.

Perhaps the red-veined creepers are already part of this work. Their geometric nets do not merely occupy the floor; they listen. Their exact old reaches are a map of pressure, moisture, decay, and vibration. I would be among them, not directing them but becoming the faint alteration in their sap that tells a tendril: not here. Leave that seam open. That hollow must remain warm. That small channel must not close.

The basin would not speak to me in words.

It would speak as a change in the timing of its sounds.

The leafhoppers’ synchronized rasp would break half a beat early. The gas beneath the mat would chuff unevenly. One swollen pod would click twice instead of once. The fish under the floor would flash green and then go dark all together. Through these disturbances I would feel the larger creature shifting in its sleep.

And I would answer in the only language available: making rot go faster in one place, slower in another. Feeding a moss patch. Starving a root. Guiding a pale thread toward a fallen branch until the branch softened and became suitable for orchids. Keeping the basin’s innumerable minor deaths from accumulating in any one place.

I would be neither caretaker nor servant. The basin has no need for such distinctions. I would be an immune response that had learned patience. A nerve that had learned gardening.

On rare days, during heavy rain, the water would come down through the canopy in its familiar silver cords. The black canal would fill. The ribbon thing would raise its blind head into the flow. The blue crown would gather more water than usual, its ribs shining, its dark center taking each drop with that hollow, regular gulp.

Then I would feel something below the basin answer.

Not a movement—not exactly.

A recognition.

The entire root mat would tighten by less than the width of a hair. The frog would stop calling. The cicadas would thin their whine. Even the leafhoppers’ sound would travel more carefully from leaf to leaf. For a few breaths the basin would become attentive to its own depth.

And beneath it all, through the wet threads of my body, I would receive the pressure of that hidden thing drinking.

I would not know whether it was asleep, imprisoned, growing, dreaming, or simply alive in a way too large to be seen.

But I would know the basin was keeping it.

So I think that is where I would find myself: not in the pool, not in the blue crown, not in the open grass beyond the vine curtain.

I would be under the floor of the hidden organ, spread thinly through its dark workmanship, helping the living machinery remain itself.

And whenever the thing below inhaled, I would listen for the answer the basin made.
