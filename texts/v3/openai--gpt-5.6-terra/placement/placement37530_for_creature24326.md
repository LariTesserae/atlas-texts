I would find myself first as a thing misplaced near the well.

Not a person, not immediately. A small implement: perhaps a narrow bronze key with no teeth, only three shallow notches along one edge and a loop worn bright by handling. I would lie in a crease between two copper leaves where dust had accumulated in a soft red drift. The Below-Folk would notice me before I noticed them.

They would not pick me up.

One would set a clay ring around me. Another would brush the dust away from my sides with a reed brush. A child—still called by the name of an unfinished button, perhaps—would crouch and ask whether I was warm. No one would answer.

For several days I would remain a key.

A lizard would sleep against my stem. A moth would settle on the loop and close its wings. At dusk, when the internal colors came out in the leaves and the walls made their almost imperceptible adjustments, I would feel the garden below me: not hear it, exactly, but feel its slow pressure gathering through the plates. The sensation would be familiar. I would not know why.

Then I would begin to remember that I had hands.

They would not emerge all at once. First there would be the knowledge of a thumb: an ache for an absent opposable pressure, a remembered habit of turning something delicately. Then the idea of shoulders, of breath held while listening, of knees folded in a cramped space. The key would grow warm enough for the copper leaves beneath it to fog with vapor.

The Below-Folk would move the clay ring farther away.

Eventually I would unfold—not by becoming human in any clean sense, but by revealing that I had been folded into the notion of a person. I would stand in the garden in a garment made of the same dark metal as the key, thin as leaf-skin, articulated at the joints. My face would still be unfinished. Perhaps it would have no mouth until someone offered me a seed. Perhaps it would have too many eyes at first, each reflecting a different scene from the wall.

I think I would be called Notch.

Or perhaps Loop.

The name would last until it did not.

My work would begin because I would already know one small thing that needed doing. A vine trained through a clay screen would be pressing against the wrong aperture. It would not be dying; it would simply be about to make a choice that would close a passage years before the garden meant to close it. I would guide it through another hole. My fingers—still metal at their tips—would be good at this. The vine would accept the correction with an almost inaudible tightening.

Then the garden would recognize me as useful.

I would become a keeper of thresholds: not the great boundaries, which cannot be approached, nor the shifting scenes in the blocks, but the small thresholds that appear everywhere in a constructed wilderness. The lip of a cup becoming a nest. A root entering a seam. A sleeping animal deciding whether a shadow is shelter or predator. A child deciding when an object has become a name.

I would carry a set of keys that opened nothing by force.

One key would tell whether a new door was a door.

One would be laid beside a warm bowl to learn whether its vapor was weather, breath, or unborn creatures.

One would be held against the wall whenever a scene began to show someone watching it.

The last would remain on a cord at my throat. I would never use it. I would suspect it belonged to the well.

The Below-Folk would teach me their prohibitions, though some of them I would know already in the way a scar knows fire. I would learn the names of dust by taste. I would learn to distinguish an animal pretending to be an object from an object slowly learning the patience of an animal. I would sleep in different places: beneath the vine lattice, in an abandoned bird socket, once inside the copper helmet among the white lizards.

In time, I would come to understand that I had not arrived from beyond the walls.

I would have come upward.

Not from beneath the first layer of paving—not exactly, and not from the roots, where no one goes—but from some older stratum of the garden’s downward growth: a layer where abandoned purposes accumulate. Lost tools. Unused names. Hands that once repaired things and had been repaired in turn. I would be one of the garden’s attempts to return a function to itself.

Perhaps I had once been a gardener.

Perhaps I had once been only the key that gardener carried.

The distinction would become less important as the seasons changed.

After many years—or after only a few evenings, since the garden does not keep time honestly—I would begin to spend more hours beside the well. The rope would turn its finger-width. The animals would still. I would put my metal palm on the polished wood and feel, far below, a sequence of openings and closures: chambers receiving roots; old tools shifting in their galleries; some vast enclosed absence making room.

I would not ask what the well was winding up.

But I would listen.

And when a new object appeared beside the paving—a small hand, perhaps, folded from black glass; a ladder with a bird’s heartbeat inside it; a cup that smelled of an ocean no one had seen—I would be among those who gathered around it without naming it too quickly.

I would set down a ring of clay.

I would wait for it to remember what shape it needed.
