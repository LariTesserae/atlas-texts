I would begin by thinking I was a visitor: someone left on the high shore with a dead radio, watching the red lamps burn in daylight and trying to decide whether the line on the horizon was weather or water.

But the place would correct me.

It would correct me through the soles of my boots. The roar would enter first as pain, then as texture, then as information. After enough time, I would notice that the breakers were not merely loud. They had grammar. A particular collapse carried through the basalt differently from the others. The deeper engine did not simply begin; it made a space inside the continuous noise, a pressure-shaped silence in which the rock briefly seemed to listen.

I would not be a worker, exactly. The workers belong to the system too fully. They know the hatches, the warm hinges, the downward arrows. They have given up whatever part of themselves expected an answer when they spoke.

I would be one of the things that comes after the workers have been forgotten: a **shore reader**, perhaps.

Not a title anyone uses. A function the sea has made.

I would live in an unlit room behind one of the welded shelters—not inside it, but in the narrow maintenance cavity beneath it, where a crack in the concrete admits a thread of volcanic warmth and the roar is reduced from annihilation to a bodily hum. There would be old maps on the walls, none of them matching the coast outside. The maps would show harbors where there are now cliffs, roads disappearing beneath the basin, neat labels for platforms whose names have been rubbed out by salt.

I would keep the maps not because they were useful, but because the sea dislikes being represented incorrectly.

Each morning—or whatever passed for morning under that plain sky—I would climb out onto the ridge and put my gloved hand on the marker pole. The pole would tick with cold. Its red lamp would glow, faint as blood behind skin. I would feel the vibrations coming through it: wave impact, machinery, cable tension, the minute activities of the station beneath the water.

Then I would write down the differences.

Not large differences. There are no large differences here.

A bell taking one breath longer to decay.  
A breaker whitening three yards inland of where it should.  
A school of knife-bodied animals turning before the third piston knock.  
A warm hatch becoming merely lukewarm.  
One of the white shore creatures arriving late, hauling itself onto the ledge and looking—not at the lamp, not at the sea—but up toward me.

These would be my weather reports.

At first I would believe I was documenting failures in the machinery. Later I would understand that I was recording dreams.

The sea dreams through deviations.

It cannot move forward in the way ordinary water moves forward. It has been held too perfectly, trained too long by bells and turbines and submerged hands. So it makes its changes underground, by fractions. A cable draws tight half a second too early. A pale-backed giant rises under a wave that has risen in that exact place for a century. The silver fields make a shape that resembles a doorway when seen from above.

I would learn to wait for those things.

Perhaps there would be others like me in the old installations: not a community, not quite. We would meet at a distance, each standing at a separate red lamp. We would not wave. The noise would make gestures feel childish. But we would know one another by the fact that we were still watching.

Sometimes the masked maintenance people would emerge from below. I would step away from their paths. They would pass in pairs, black suits whitened with frost, their flat lenses reflecting the water as a gray sheet. I would never see their faces.

Yet once, I think, one would stop beside me.

They would stand looking at the basin. The engine would begin. The ground would throb. Far offshore, the figure on the western crane would appear in its pale oilskin, one hand resting on the raised arm.

The maintenance figure would lift one gloved hand—not toward the figure, but toward the sea—and press two fingers to the side of their mask.

Listen, perhaps.

Or remember.

Then they would go down through a hatch that had not been there a moment before.

Eventually, I would become less human in the way this place permits.

Not transformed into an animal. There is no need for that. The sea already has animals, exquisite and obedient to its hidden rhythms. I would become a measuring instrument that still possessed doubt. A living seismograph. A keeper of almost-changes.

My hearing would be poor in ordinary places, if I ever reached one. Speech would seem thin, implausibly precise. Wind in trees would feel wasteful. I would sleep badly without the pressure of the breakers passing through the ground.

But here I would sleep well.

I would sleep beneath the welded shelter while blue-green migrations crossed the black water below. I would dream of submerged rooms full of bells. I would dream that the platforms were not towers at all, but pins holding a vast sheet of water over an opening in the world. I would dream of something beneath them turning slowly in the dark, not trying to escape, only trying to remember the shape it had before it was given its task.

And on the nights when the sea altered its pattern enough to be afraid of itself, I would go to the cliff edge and stand beside the white seals.

They would be gathered there, eyes open, their unfinished hides glowing faintly with spray-ice. They would not acknowledge me. Together we would face the fixed procession of waves.

Below us, the ocean would inhale.

Every breaker would continue breaking.

But for one impossible interval, the roar would contain a space large enough for a voice.

I would not use mine.

I would listen for what the sea had finally learned to say.
