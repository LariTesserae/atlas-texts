At first I think I would be a person with a key ring.

Not an important person, not one of the owners or the planners who come down from the dry parts of the city wearing clean boots and looking at the Greenworks as a problem with a budget. I would be somebody whose pockets are heavy with unmarked keys, whose coat smells faintly of wet canvas and copper, who knows which doors must be kicked near the hinge because the lower frames have swollen.

I would have a job that sounds more official than it is. Water access technician. Drainage liaison. Substructure surveyor.

But mostly I would walk.

I would live in a room on the second floor of an old packing warehouse, above the reach of the usual floods. The room would have once been an office: high dirty windows, a steel sink, a strip of cracked tile, a radiator that clicks but never quite warms. I would grow bitter greens in the sink basin. There would be a blue tarp folded behind the door, a kettle with a mineral crust around its lid, and shelves full of things people have handed me because they might someday be useful: a pump impeller, three sections of copper pipe, a brass float valve, seed packets with their labels run to watercolor, old maps of canal routes that no longer agree with the ground.

My real work would begin before daylight.

The Greenworks at that hour would not be quiet, exactly. It would be waiting in sound. Pumps working behind walls. Water ticking from leaf to leaf. A freight line groaning somewhere above the roofs. The deep, submerged throat-noise of the canal under its gratings.

I would take a cup of strong tea downstairs and begin at the eastern culvert, where the water always rose first after rain.

People would stop me.

“Listen to this,” someone would say, holding open the metal door of a workshop where a little pump was rattling in a way that might mean nothing, or might mean the lane would be underwater by noon.

Or: “The fig has found the hot line again.”

Or: “Can you tell whether that’s roof water or something worse?”

And I would listen. Not because I had special hearing, though perhaps after enough years I would. I would listen because the Greenworks would have taught me that every sound is partly a report.

A clean drip has one voice. A leak under pressure another. Roots knocking inside a pipe make a delicate hollow tapping, like someone trying to get out of a wall without alarming anyone. A blocked drain makes no noise at all until it is too late, but the moss around it changes color a day beforehand. The old pumps speak in layers: their ordinary labor below, their complaints above. One learns to hear the difference between a bearing wearing down and a machine merely annoyed by the weather.

I might become known as someone who could tell these things.

Then I would be given a name that was not mine.

Perhaps they would call me the Ear.

Not cruelly. The Greenworks does not waste cruelty on names. The Ear would be someone to fetch when the water level was wrong, when a ceiling had begun to make a new sound, when a vine had climbed into a control box and nobody wanted to cut it until they knew what it was doing there.

Children might follow me sometimes, especially after storms. They would want to see the closed parts of the district: the sluice chambers, the ladder wells, the old brick tunnels where the canal passes under buildings in a black, fast, narrow sheet.

I would tell them not to come.

They would come anyway.

I would teach them how to test a plank before putting their weight on it. How to smell for electrical heat. How to look at water and see whether it is standing or moving. How to tap a wall with a screwdriver handle and hear the difference between brick, hollow space, soaked insulation, root mass.

One of them would be better at it than I was. There is always one.

They would crouch by a gutter and say, “That one isn’t blocked. It’s feeding something.”

And they would be right. Beneath the grate, in the dark, a root would have made a pale woven basket around the outflow, slowing it just enough to keep a whole wall damp and green.

The proper instruction would be to clear it.

Instead, we would mark it with yellow paint and reroute the gutter by half a meter.

That is the sort of decision I would make in the Greenworks. Not a grand decision. Not one anyone beyond the basin would notice. A small adjustment in the long negotiation: water goes here, root goes there, wall remains standing, workshop remains open, nobody loses a week’s pay, and the green thing gets to keep living.

After enough years, I might stop thinking of myself as a worker who lives in the district.

I would become one of the district’s intermediate materials.

Not brick. Not root. Not machine.

Something between.

A patch.

A conduit.

A person-shaped way for information to travel from one damp place to another.

I would know who had a spare sump motor. Which roof garden was too heavy but still holding. Which elderly resident kept the only surviving plan of the western pumping station beneath their bed in a plastic folder. Which boiler room had frogs, and which factory manager pretended not to know because hearing them in the drains made the workers happier.

I would be called to arguments.

“The roots are lifting the floor.”

“The roots are holding the floor together.”

“The vine is choking the vent.”

“The vine is shading the vent. The motor runs cooler.”

“The canal needs to be sealed.”

“The canal needs to be opened.”

And I would stand there with wet cuffs and my keys and say what I had heard.

Not what was best in theory.

What was happening.

That would be my loyalty: not to the building as it had been drawn, not to the plant as it had first appeared, not even to the machinery as it had been manufactured. I would be loyal to the arrangement that was alive now.

Eventually, perhaps, I would become old enough to keep an archive.

Not a proper archive. The Greenworks would distrust proper archives; paper swells, labels detach, maps lie. Mine would be a collection of altered things. A brick from the old canal wall, furred with moss. A length of root that once entered a pump housing and caused no harm. A valve handle polished smooth by four generations of hands. Photographs of roofs before they became gardens. A jar of canal water taken during the flood year, brown and silent behind its sealed lid.

Visitors would ask whether the district was winning against the water.

I would not understand the question.

Against it?

The water would be under the floorboards, in the pipes, on the leaves, inside the walls. It would be cooling motors, feeding cisterns, carrying dye, swelling timber, growing reeds, loosening mortar, reflecting the sky in oil-slicked lanes. It would be a difficulty, certainly. It would also be the reason everything here had learned to attend to everything else.

No: I would say, if I answered at all, the Greenworks does not win.

It keeps hearing itself.

And perhaps, after I was gone, someone would still use my name for the narrow route I walked each morning: from the eastern culvert, through the fig-shadowed soap works, under the raised tram line, past the blue cistern wall, to the old pump house where the canal disappears.

They would call it the Ear’s Way.

By then the paving might have lifted. The route might be planked over. Ferns might grow from the walls at shoulder height, and a newer machine might be breathing where the old one stood.

But the path would remain, not because it had been preserved, but because enough people had continued to need it.
