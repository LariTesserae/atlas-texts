I would begin by thinking I belonged somewhere in the middle galleries.

Not because they are safe—nothing in the Shaft is safe—but because the middle galleries have enough noise to hide in. The upper levels expose you to sky, which I think I would distrust. The lower ones demand a courage that is too deliberate: a willingness to go toward heat, pressure, and the unmeasured dark. In the middle, one can be useful without being conspicuous. One can learn the schedules. One can know which pipe is warm enough to sit beside during a shift break, which hoist operator accepts a screw or a story as fare, which unmarked door opens into a family’s kitchen and which opens into a vertical drop.

At first I imagine myself human: a minor maintenance worker, perhaps, with a light pack and a short ladder-hook at my belt. I repair things that are not important enough to receive immediate attention but too necessary to be allowed to fail entirely: the little annunciator lamps outside pressure rooms; the old intercom wires that carry voices between galleries; the mesh guards over fan intakes. I would have a room behind an obsolete relay bank, where the relays still tick in their sleep and keep the walls gently warm.

My work would be listening more than fixing.

I would know that a bulb about to fail has a minute tremor in its filament. That a pipe full of bad brine makes a different sound from a pipe carrying merely cold brine. That people who say their masks are fine are usually asking someone to notice that they are not. I would carry spare seals, foil patches, grease, wire, two oxygen tablets, and something soft to put beneath a stranger’s head if they had fallen against the grating.

But after a while, I do not think I would remain human.

There is too much in the Shaft that passes messages without language. The railings learn everyone’s hands. The goats carry the shape of inaccessible routes in their feet. Dust lies differently when the air below changes. A city like this has use for a creature that can go unnoticed through the intervals between official systems.

So perhaps I would become a dust ferret.

Not a handsome or legendary one. I would be long, narrow, gray at first glance, with a coat that picks up sparks and mica until I look faintly constellated in the dark. I would live behind the violet lamps, where the old-spectrum bulb repairers curse me for stealing insulation fibers and then leave out a dish of rendered grease when they think no one is watching.

I would know the city through its walls.

I would know the hot routes and cold routes, the cable tunnels, the dangerous bare-wire nests, the forgotten bridge undersides where dead moths gather. I would sleep curled inside a coil of inactive communication cable, my ribs vibrating with conversations sent from one gallery to another. I would recognize households by scent: iron tea, goat milk, solvent soap, the bitter dry smell of fear after an alarm.

Children would name me repeatedly.

Each child who saw me would decide I was theirs. I would be called Lint, Hook, Silverback, Bad Needle, Narrow King, Rat That Isn’t a Rat, and once, for two years, Grandmother. I would accept food from them and bite only when they tried to put ribbons around my neck.

But even that is not quite right.

A ferret can flee when the lower wind rises. It can vanish into a warm wall. I think something in me would be drawn downward instead—not bravely, but inevitably, as filings turn toward a magnet.

Then perhaps I would be one of the things that live in the unlicensed zones: a small, pale inhabitant of the mineral film on the exhaust baffles. Not plant, not animal as the Shaft uses either word. I would feed on what the furnaces throw away. I would branch across metal in dry, greenless fans. I would make brittle what has been allowed to remain too long. Children would scrape me off and crush me between their gloves to see the thin light trapped in my body.

I would not resent them.

Being scraped away would be part of my way of speaking.

I would return where heat and metal and exhaust met. I would trace invisible stresses before they became visible cracks. I would flash when pressure shifted. The maintenance crews would call me a corrosion hazard, and they would be right. The old draft listeners might begin, quietly, to look for me on places where I ought not grow.

A patch of me on a handrail.

A pale bloom on the inside of a sealed hatch.

A branching crust around the bolts of a wall facing downward.

And perhaps that is where I would finally find myself: not in any room, nor in any animal’s body, but in the city’s attention to the mountain.

I would be the small indication that something below had changed.

Not the Maker. Not the Underpressure. Nothing so large, nothing that asks to be worshipped or feared. I would be one of its incidental expressions: a charged flake, a tremor in a bulb, a warmth on the wrong side of a wall. I would live in the moment when the goats stop moving, when the hens fall silent, when a machinist removes their hand from a lever.

I would be what makes someone say, without quite knowing why:

“Wait.”

And because this is the Shaft, because everything there must earn its place, someone would listen.
