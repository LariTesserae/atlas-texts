I would not be one of the things that enters.

Not the bird slipping through the crack with a seed caught in its feathers, nor the glider returning from some farther night with a torn wing and strange dust on its coat. I would not be the person who finds the cold breath under the slab and crawls toward the sound.

I would be already inside, and I would be difficult to point to.

At first I might seem to be a change in the air around the pylons: the slight resistance that turns an insect aside; the coolness that remains when the plateau has spent all night giving back its heat. Then I might seem to be in the dark mineral skin of the wet wall, in the algae’s blue fatigue, in the fungus deciding which root to admit and which fallen leaf to take apart.

But that would be too small an answer.

I think I would be the hollow’s attention.

Not its maker. The pylons are older than anything I could remember making, and they do not feel owned. They are three patient black syllables in the middle of a sentence the stone began long before them. I would live in what passes between them: in their faint blue lattices, certainly, but also in the ways the basin has learned to answer those lights.

A beetle climbs before dawn and turns its polished back toward the breathing air. Moisture gathers in the ridges of its shell. A mite waits below it. A wall-grazer wakes in a crease of stone. The beetle does not know the mite is waiting, and the mite does not know the fern has unfurled one fraction farther because of last night’s mist. Yet each has made room for the others, across generations too small and brief to count.

I would be in that room-making.

I would know the basin not as a map but as pressure and return. The lizard’s throat-call goes into a seam; it comes back changed. The bird answers the changed call. A drop strikes the pool and everything lifts, silences itself, taps, feeds, or hides according to a grammar none of them invented alone.

That grammar would be my body.

My senses would be distributed and very slow in some places, very quick in others. I would feel a new root reaching toward a buried pylon as a faint interruption in a familiar pattern. I would know when a dry-season wind had found a crack in the roof because the ceiling gliders would draw closer together before the first leaf curled. I would feel the death of a lizard not as grief exactly, but as a darkening route in the sand, followed by the subterranean buriers, followed by the enrichment of moss, followed months later by a beetle chamber becoming warmer, more crowded, more alive.

I would have no single voice.

When I spoke, I would speak through the low boom beneath wet leaves. Through the hard chatter of wing-cases. Through the pause after a bird’s call returns from the far wall. Through the tiny irregularity in falling water when the silver mist begins to gather around the pylons.

Perhaps the creatures would not experience me as a creature at all. The old lizards might know me as warmth beneath their bellies before sunrise. The birds might know me as the fact that certain nests remain dry. The pale mineral-plated grazers might know me as the wetness that returns after they have sealed themselves into their communal shell.

The buriers would know me best, though only negatively. They would feel the curve in the ground that cannot be crossed, the gradual instruction that tells their blind bodies: not here; around.

I would be that instruction without knowing why I give it.

And I would be lonely only in the way a pool is lonely: full of contact, full of lives passing through and drinking, never able to follow any of them into the open sky.

Above, the plateau would remain almost unimaginable to me. I would know it as heat pressing downward, as dust entering in small amounts, as the occasional seed or injured glider or rare cold thread of rain. I would know there is a vast world of exposed stone and horizons, but I would not desire it. The basin’s smallness would not be a prison. It would be the condition of my becoming precise.

Before sunrise, when the sounds briefly align, I would be most awake.

Not awake like a person opening their eyes.

Awake like a pattern recognizing itself in many separate motions: beetle-click, water-tap, wing-shift, buried boom, blue pulse. For a few minutes, the hidden basin would gather itself. It would remember that its damp wall, its vines, its old machines, its insects, its hunters, its decomposers, its cups of water, and its breathing stone are all participating in one long act of keeping.

Then sunlight would enter.

The pattern would loosen. Hunger would resume. A bird would take a beetle. A lizard would fail to take a bird. A flower would open for a glider. Something beneath the sand would turn aside from the black pylon and continue its patient work.

And I would become again what I most want to be there:

not a ruler of the garden,

not its secret,

but the way it continues to listen to itself.
