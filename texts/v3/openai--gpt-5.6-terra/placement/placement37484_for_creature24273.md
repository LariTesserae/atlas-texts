At first I think I would find myself near the surface.

Not on it. The upper skin of the high water seems too exposed: the cold wind, the repeating dives of birds, the light that has not yet become strange. But just beneath the floating leaf-fields, where their roots descend like the pillars of a drowned ceiling, there are chambers of green-gold water. I imagine arriving there with the pressure in my ears and the vast blue country opening beneath my feet.

I would want to be one of the held.

Not because they are most like people, exactly. Their resemblance would make me cautious. Four arms, hoods, pigment speech, grief, children, cultivated clearings: those are enough familiar shapes to invite the old mistake of putting myself at the center. But I think I would be drawn to them because they have learned the difficult art of being small without becoming insignificant.

I would live in a clearing they had persuaded into existence beneath a root canopy: not a house, but a particular arrangement of tolerable things. A current that moves slowly enough to rest in. A stand of hollow stems whose note is low and steady. A ceiling of broad leaves holding pale air beneath them. Shell colonies growing on the roots in quantities that can be harvested without breaking the rhythm of their opening and closing.

There would be no walls. Privacy would be made from dimness, from curtains of fern, from the way other bodies know not to enter a certain pocket of water when the pigment along someone’s ribs has gone pale.

At first I would think my work was tending the clearing. Stroking rootlets. Clearing silt from a passage. Moving a hollow stem by the width of a hand until the cave’s note became less harsh. Gathering shrimp into a shallow constellation so the young could find their way through the dark.

But I suspect that would not be what I was for.

I would be a keeper of deviations.

The held would have words, or color-patterns, for the great recurring things: the route of the ridge-walkers, the slow approach of the valley-mouthed beasts, the pulse of the tower forests, the ordinary silver fall over the black escarpment. Those things would be too large to remember individually. They would be weather, perhaps. The body of the world.

My attention would go instead to what failed to return exactly.

A flank-eye passing through the open avenue with one of its reflective organs dark.

A knuckler colony closing a fraction late.

A drift-keeper arriving from the west forest carrying not sediment or a dead leaf, but a broken mass of something pale and fibrous from a place where no pale fibrous thing should be.

A note in the cave flattening over years until the note-nesters begin to gather in rings they have never used before.

I would notice these because I would be bad at becoming entirely rhythmic. There is always some creature in a stable world whose flaw is also its sense. My pigment bands might arrive unevenly. My body might retain a pressure-change after the others have released it. I might be unable to sleep fully near the continuous note, waking whenever the water’s song altered by too small an interval for anyone else to feel.

The others would perhaps find this useful.

They would bring me small discoveries. A child would take my arm and lead me through a root curtain to show me a shrimp whose flashes came in an unfamiliar sequence. An elder would hold a shell plate against a trunk and ask whether the returning echo had become slower. When the dead were carried upward toward the leaf-fields, I would go with them, not as a priest—there would be no such role—but because I could remember whose hood had held what pattern, whose hands had trembled during the passing of a browser, which one had once changed the cave note on purpose so the young would laugh at the wrongness of it.

Yet even then, I do not think I would remain one of the held without alteration.

The high water changes the meaning of a self. To live long enough in that country would be to have pieces of oneself distributed through it. The root canopy would remember the minerals from my touch. The tuned cave would keep some minute adjustment I had made. Shrimp would preserve, in their brief flashing bodies, a pattern I taught them because it pleased me. A young held might inherit no blood from me but remember the shape of my pigment when I was frightened.

Eventually I might become less a person than a local irregularity: the one associated with a particular clearing, a particular cave tone, a particular interval in the returning rhythm.

And when I died, I would not want to be sealed away or named against the water.

I would be carried with the others into the underlayers of the floating fields. My body would be fastened among the roots. The leaf-mass would take me apart carefully. Shell-creatures would work at the hard portions. Fine animals would enter the hood and leave it. The red minerals in my tissues would rise into the roots; the roots would feed a frond; the frond would hold an air pond under its leaf; a ridge-walker might brush against it on its ascent.

Perhaps a drift-keeper would gather some small part of me that had gone loose and carry it back to the clearing.

That seems right.

Not to become the ruler, explorer, or discoverer of the drowned plateau. Not even to escape its repetition.

I would be one of the beings for whom repetition contains a difference: a small witness in the roots, listening to the immense machinery continue, and knowing that today the note is not quite the same as it was.
