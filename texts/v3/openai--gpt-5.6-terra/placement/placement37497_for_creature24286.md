I would not arrive by the rail.

The rail is for things that know where they are going: crates of salt, coils of cable, people with contracts folded in their inner pockets. I think I would come by a less official route, following the old stream-bed up from the lower ravine, climbing beside the water where it has escaped the city’s measurements.

At first I might believe I was a surveyor. I would carry a notebook, a pressure gauge, a bad map copied from three older maps. I would tell people I had been sent to inspect a section of buried conduit beneath the east terraces. They would nod without much interest. Everyone there has been sent to inspect something.

But I would stay.

Not because the place welcomed me exactly. The city would never do that. It would give me a cot in a former relay office, a hook for my wet coat, a chipped basin with a pipe that clicks at night. It would tell me which staircases ice first and which market stall sells bread after the morning shift. Someone would lend me a wrench. Someone else would ask whether I knew how to clear a blocked condensate trap.

And after a while, I would discover that the work I was meant to do had changed.

There are water people in the basin: people who know every sanctioned route. They maintain the gauges and valves, inspect the cisterns, trace pressure losses through the old mains. I would respect them too much to pretend I could join them. Their knowledge would be exact, inherited, and annotated in grease pencil on walls no visitor notices.

Instead, I would become responsible for the water that should not be there.

The seepage.

The narrow dark lines under storage doors. The droplets gathering beneath a bridge where no pipe is marked. The warm, mineral-smelling damp that appears in a stairwell after a week of wind from the north. The little flows that emerge through stone foundations and vanish again, uncertain whether they belong to the mountain or the town.

I would carry chalk, cloth tape, sample jars, and a stethoscope with its rubber tubing patched in two places. I would kneel in alleys and put my ear to retaining walls. I would learn the difference between rainwater inside a conduit, condensation under a cable tray, and the slow subterranean breathing of the mountain itself.

In time, people would begin saying things like: ask them; they know where the wet is coming from.

This would not quite be true. No one knows where water is coming from in such a place. Water has too many reasons. But I would know its habits.

I would know that the wall behind the old bakery always sweats before low cloud, even if the sky is clear. I would know that a line of moss below a shuttered workshop marks a forgotten overflow pipe connected to a cistern everyone believes was filled in. I would know that the willows by the south transformer have roots reaching toward warmth rather than water, and that they must be cut carefully, because beneath them is a clay conduit older than the district.

I would be called out at odd hours.

A schoolroom floor turning dark around the radiator feet.

A maintenance hatch filling from below.

A greenhouse bench suddenly tilted because the soil under its foundation has softened.

I would go with a lamp hooded in red glass, so as not to disturb the moths too much. I would move through the city after midnight, when the workshop windows have gone dark and the growing halls glow like deep-sea organs in the fog.

The ravens would know me eventually.

Not affectionately. Ravens do not need to be affectionate to be familiar. One would begin following me from roofline to roofline, a large bird with a pale scar at the base of its beak. It would imitate the squeal of the northern lift whenever I stopped beneath it, then look pleased with itself. I would call it Valve, because it only spoke when something needed attention.

Perhaps it would bring me things: a washer, a strip of blue insulation, once a key from the old pump-house that fit no lock I could find.

The children would call me the leak ghost, though I would be alive and not especially ghostly. They would leave chalk arrows at places where they had found water bubbling through cracks in the paving. Some would be real discoveries. Some would be traps intended to lead me to a puddle behind the public lavatories or a bucket hidden above a doorway.

I would pretend not to notice the traps.

The elders, however, would take me seriously. They would tell me about springs.

Not the approved springs, with their caps and filters and municipal labels. The older ones. The ones that ran before the first tunnel crews came up. The spring under the western freight yard, which had once been cold enough to numb a hand. The one behind the old chapel wall that smelled faintly of iron and made the rowans grow too quickly. The one in the ravine that supposedly gathered salamanders every thaw.

I would write these stories in my notebook alongside pressure readings. At first I would mark them as unverified. Eventually I would stop.

Because in the city, an old person remembering water is a kind of instrument.

Where would I live?

In a narrow room tucked beneath the north growing hall, where the ceiling pipes are warm enough to keep frost from the window. The room would have a view of very little: a slanted lane, a handrail black with wetness, the underside of a conveyor bridge, and beyond it, when the fog lifts, a piece of opposite cliff patterned with lichens.

I would grow moss in shallow trays on the sill.

Not rare moss, not useful moss. Just local dark cushions collected from places scheduled to be cleared: the edge of a stair, the underside of a broken drainage grate, an old stone inscribed with a warning no one could read anymore. I would keep them damp and watch which kinds brightened after rain, which preferred turbine heat, which held small insects in their depths.

In winter, when the greenhouse lamps stayed on long past sunset, I would sometimes go up to the glass and stand outside among the vapor. Inside, people would prune dwarf fruit trees or test nutrient lines. A child might put their hand against the pane. I would put mine against the outside, and the glass would hold a thin warmth between us.

I think that is what I would be there: not a builder, not a miner, not someone important enough to appear in the city’s records.

I would be a keeper of the unrecorded circulation.

A listener for the water that slips its channels.

A witness to the way roots find cracks, to the way old pipes become streams, to the way a town learns that nothing can be sealed forever—not stone, not machinery, not memory.

And after enough years, I might become one of the people who cannot leave without feeling that something has been left unattended.

Not because the city would own me.

Because I would have learned its private sounds: the patient turbine beneath the paving, the cough of the southern boiler, rain ticking on greenhouse glass, a raven imitating a broken relay, water moving where it has no permission to move.

And when I heard it, somewhere below the streets, I would know whether it was only water.

Or whether the mountain had begun, very slightly, to change its mind.
