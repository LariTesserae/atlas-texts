At first I think I would be one of the water-keepers: not an elder with authority, but the person nobody notices until a trough backs up or a pond gate sticks. I imagine knowing the settlement by its sounds—the clean knock of water under a bridge, the thick throat-noise of a channel carrying leaves, the sudden absence of frogs where frogs ought to be.

But I do not think I would remain human there.

I would be born into too much work, too many names, too many obligations to somebody’s wall or somebody’s duck pond. I would admire the people who keep the water moving, but I would live beside them at a slant.

I would be a dog.

Not one of the broad-chested valley dogs brought up by traders, proud of its dry paws and confused by bridges. I would be a local dog: dark-coated, long-muzzled, with a pale blaze on one paw so children could see me in fog. My coat would dry slowly and smell of woodsmoke, wet wool, pond water, and whatever kitchen I had last visited. I would belong, loosely, to the household beneath the Alder Steps, but in the manner of all useful village dogs I would believe the whole settlement belonged to me.

My name would be called differently from different terraces.

The old woman who listens to walls would call me **Moss**, because I was born under a collapsed goat shelter and came out green-streaked from the wet bedding. The children would call me **Three-Fish**, after I stole a string of river fish from a trader and swallowed three before anyone caught me. The household beneath the Alder Steps would use my true name only when they meant business, though I would pretend not to recognize it until they opened a door or lifted a bowl.

I would know every safe stone.

Not by learning, exactly. By growing into them. The stone with the sharp edge under the water. The plank that tips downhill when a grown person steps too close to the outer rail. The soft part of the upper path where a child’s heel can sink ankle-deep after three days of rain. The hollow beneath the smokehouse boulder where rats hide in winter. The root tunnel behind the reed-house where a small child can crawl but an adult must send in an arm and curse.

I would know which children have good sense and which ones need watching.

The good-sense children would be the dangerous ones, because they would go farther. They would leave the crowded paths to look for eggs under the old ash roots, or to follow frog calls down toward the lower pools. I would follow them without being asked, appearing behind them when the fog became thick enough to swallow the nearest roof.

I would bark once if they had merely gone too far.

I would bark continuously if I smelled boar.

The humans would say I had a gift for sensing weather. This would not be true. I would simply pay attention. The air changes before rain. The mountain changes before a slope gives way. Ants move their eggs. Goats become restless and stupid. The water starts speaking differently under the stones. Even the ducks fall into a particular ugly silence before hard hail.

When that happened, I would go to the house beneath the Alder Steps and stand with my nose against the door.

The old woman would open it, look down at me, and say, “What have you heard now?”

Then she would take her walking stick and go out into the wet, and other people would begin appearing from doorways with baskets, shovels, rope, and the resigned expressions of people whose evening meal has been interrupted by the mountain.

I would not be a hero. Dogs do not think in those words.

I would be useful.

I would chase pigs out of cabbage beds, though never fast enough to stop them taking one mouthful first. I would sleep outside the cattle shed on cold nights, not because I was assigned there but because the warm breath leaking through the boards was pleasant. I would learn which ducks were likely to peck my nose and which would scatter if I merely looked at them. I would dislike the rooster from the upper ponds with a sincere, lifelong hatred.

I would know the ravens too.

They would sit on the high poles above the drop, their feathers shining blue when the sky opened. They would call when strangers came up from the valley. Sometimes one would descend to the roof beam above the communal hall and make a noise that sounded almost like my name. I would pretend indifference, because that is how one deals with ravens, but I would leave scraps below the pole when no person was watching.

In old age, my muzzle would go silver. My hearing would fail before my nose did. I would stop running after goats, though I would still rise stiffly if one dared enter my sleeping place. Children who had once climbed over me would become adults with children of their own. They would say, “Careful around Moss. He knows that bridge is bad,” even after the bridge had been repaired twice and rebuilt once.

And perhaps I would know.

Not because I remembered every board. Because the settlement would have entered me by then: its water-noise, its cooking smells, its arguments, its bells, its damp wool, its piglets, its smoke. I would wake when the upper pond gate shifted. I would hear a child crying from three terraces away. I would notice the wrong silence in a house whose family had gone down-valley trading.

At last I would sleep beneath the communal hall, among the other old dogs, where the floor above thudded with feet and the cattle breathed somewhere nearby. Rain would come through the leaf roof in a slow, familiar ticking. The people would step around me. Someone would put a bowl of warm broth near my paws.

And that would be enough.

In that world, I think I would find myself not at the center of the settlement, nor beyond it in the wild green, but at the crossings: where path becomes bridge, where child becomes lost, where rain becomes flood, where an outsider becomes someone who knows to step on the safe stones.

I would be a village dog of the wet mountain—half guardian, half thief, wholly at home in the moving water.
