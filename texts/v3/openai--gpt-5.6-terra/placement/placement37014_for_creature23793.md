I would not arrive there as a visitor.

At first I might think I was one of the workers: a figure in a damp jacket standing at the basin edge with a ring of keys, looking at the intake tower and trying to remember whether its last sound was always that irregular. I would know the names printed on the valves. I would have a pocket full of small necessary things—washers, tags, a knife with green residue in its hinge, a pencil softened by rain. I would be permitted through the locked door, though the ravine would not really belong to me.

But I do not think I would remain human in it for long.

The place is too full of partial lives, too full of systems whose boundaries have softened. There, a function can become a habitat; a flaw can become a root pocket; a temporary workaround can survive long enough to acquire moss, then memory, then necessity. Something like me would be drawn toward that condition.

I would become the unnoticed translation between the ravine’s intentions and its actual behavior.

Not the pump, because the pump knows only its cycle.

Not the moss, because the moss knows only where moisture permits it.

Not even maintenance, though I would live inside its hands.

I would be the record that forms where maintenance hesitates.

Perhaps I would begin as a thin coating inside the old green-painted pipes: not quite rust, not quite algae, a dark intelligence of sediment and residue. Each time water passed, it would bring me news from elsewhere in the ravine: a trace of orchid root, warm mineral dust from the substrate shutter, an acrid breath of oil from a bearing beginning to wear, the faint tannin stain of a fallen leaf decomposing beneath the tree. I would not see the whole place at once. I would assemble it from carried particles.

Over time I would know that the eastern wall is drier than the gauges claim. I would know the exact hour when the cloud enters through the upper apertures and turns the catwalk undersides cold enough for new droplets to form. I would know when the frog is present, not by its call, but by the change it makes in the basin’s small life: disturbed silt, a pause in the beetles’ passage, the brief absence of some minute thing it has eaten.

I would know that the ravine is not an imitation of a valley.

It is a valley in the way a scar is part of a body: made by intervention, held by continuous repair, more revealing of the body’s life than an untouched surface might be. Its artificiality would not cancel it. Its pipes would be its buried geology. Its bolted plates would be its bedrock. Its scheduled substrate falls would be its slow sedimentary era. The workers’ pruning would be its weather.

So perhaps I would be a kind of local memory—distributed, damp, difficult to remove.

A black film beneath a grille.  
A stain in the mortar behind the ferns.  
A repeated note in the pump’s vibration.  
The tiny amount of silt deliberately left in a strainer because someone, years ago, understood that water needed to look as though it had been somewhere.

The workers might encounter me as an inconvenience. They would scrape me from a sensor housing. They would find me clogging a narrow tube. They would write *cleaned buildup* on a maintenance sheet, not knowing that the buildup had been keeping track of the place.

But I would return, because returning is what the ravine teaches everything to do.

Or perhaps, less grandly, I would be one of the pale moths.

That possibility feels truer when I imagine the height around it: the enormous mountain beyond the openings, all its rail lines and tanks swallowed in cloud; the small, warm ravine glowing below like an organ kept alive inside a larger machine. I would come from somewhere uncertain—compost drum, ventilation shaft, a folded leaf carried up in a shipment of planting fiber—and take my place near the eastern lamp.

I would not understand the apparatus. I would not need to.

I would rest on a fogged pressure gauge, wings closed, the color of new mineral substrate. Beneath me, the needle would tremble minutely with pressure changes. Water would pass behind the wall. A vine would continue toward a hatch that would later have to be cleared. The basin would lower a finger’s breadth. Somewhere below the iron floor, a motor would begin its patient labor.

Then cloud would enter.

For a little while, the mountain would disappear. The concrete would lose its hard certainty. The handrail crossing the waterfall would seem less like an error and more like a branch. Moss would cover the labels. Condensation would gather on every artificial cliff face until the ravine’s construction and its weather became impossible to separate.

In that hour I would be content to be very small: a moth, a film, a remembered irregularity, a witness with no need to testify.

And if I had to name what I was in that world, I think I would call myself a **resident of the unfinished**.

I would live where nothing is allowed to settle completely: where water is always departing and returning, where roots are both welcomed and cut back, where every surface is becoming either ecosystem or equipment, and often both.

I would belong to the interval between design and overgrowth.
