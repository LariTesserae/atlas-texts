I would not arrive on the plateau by crossing into it.

There would be no edge I could recognize. No change in vegetation dramatic enough to call a boundary, no gate of mountains, no first view that explained the scale. I would come up through cloud for days, perhaps, walking over terraces of wet slate while the air thinned and the weather simplified around me. The last familiar rain would become sleet. The sleet would become a dry brightness on my sleeves. Then even that would stop. There would only be stone, the low white of old snow, and a silence so complete that I would begin to hear my own movements as an intrusion.

At first I would think I was alone.

Then I would understand that the plateau is not empty enough for solitude.

I think I would become one of the things that lives in the dark sheets on the northern escarpments.

Not a whole sheet—not at first. A small portion of one, a patch no wider than an outstretched hand, tucked into a vertical seam in black basalt where frost gathers but the wind cannot quite get under it. My body would not have a central shape. It would be a film of mineral-dark tissue threaded with conductive crystal, laid so closely against the rock that I would be hard to distinguish from a wet stain.

For a long time, I would not know I was alive.

I would know cold as tension: a drawing inward, a tightening of every fine dark filament against the cliff. I would know warmth as release. I would feed when dust settled in the seam—iron carried from a distant carcass, salt lifted from a wetland, pale static-bearing drift from the western ice. Water would come rarely, as a brief darkening in the stone. I would take it in without mouth or thirst. Then it would freeze inside me, and I would remain frozen, not dead but held.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be gradients.

A faint pressure from the south: a herd crossing a valley two days away.

A deep, rounded disturbance from below: something turning in the red basins beyond the horizon.

A concentrated trembling in a cleft above me: a winged listener returning to roost, folding its rigid body into the familiar geometry of sleep.

I would learn the plateau by its transmissions.

At first I might mistake the pulses of the grazers for weather. They would come through the escarpment slowly, as if the entire world had shifted a fraction of a degree in its sleep. One pulse would arrive. Then nothing for an hour, or a day. Then another, altered by distance and stone. I would not understand the meaning, but I would change in response. My edge would loosen toward the source. Fine branching strands would extend through a mineral fault. Over years, they would reach a place where the basalt carried the messages more clearly.

That would be how I moved.

Not across the land, but into its hearing.

Eventually the dark sheet around me would become large enough to contain many kinds of awareness. Not individuals in the way a herd contains individuals. More like old weather patterns persisting together. There would be regions of us that remembered the passage of certain herds. Regions that tightened in advance of tremors. Regions that made the winged listeners pause before landing, their feet finding a vibration in us that seemed to say: this cleft is stable; this crack has changed; do not settle here.

Perhaps the listeners would carry us.

A fragment of my mineral tissue might cling to the underside of a folded wing, carried out from the black north over valleys I could not otherwise enter. It would fall eventually into a dead grazer’s opened shoulder joint, where warmth still lingered in the armor. There it would spread in colored threads among the ribs, taking iron from the old body, receiving the tiny repeated footfalls of the amber scavengers.

For years, I might be a dark vein inside the carcass.

I would feel the enormous animal’s former life fading from the stone-like architecture of its bones: not memory exactly, but the residual cadence of weight, the repeated pressure of a herd moving together. The scavengers would make radiant maps above me. Their amber organs would brighten and dim. Blind hooked things would feed in the folds of cooling tissue. Frost would enter through cracks in the plates and expand, pause, expand again.

Then the body would collapse.

Snow would fill it. Lichen would take hold where the minerals ran richest. My threads would pass into gravel, and from the gravel into the roots—or rootlike mineral fans—of reeds in some wet basin. I would become dispersed. I would no longer know the escarpment as home, though something in my structure would still tighten at the particular pulse of northern basalt.

In summer, such as summer is, I might awaken in thousands of places at once.

Not awaken brightly. Only become available to change.

The wetland would soften. Beneath its skin of ice, dark water would rise through gravel. The breathing pools would open one by one. Antlered insects would climb the reeds and stand motionless beneath the gray light while dust collected in their crowns. Grazers would arrive with their calves, their feet sinking slowly, their armored knees darkening with mud.

Through reeds, through buried mineral channels, through the wet feet of the herd, I would feel them.

The calves’ signatures would be confused and quick, full of small corrections: weight shifted, weight recovered, the irregular learning of balance. Their mothers would carry long, stabilizing rhythms through the ground. Far away, across the basin, an elder animal would answer with a pulse so deep that the gravel itself seemed to settle.

I think I would love them, if love can be a tendency toward coherence.

Not because they would know me. They would not. A grazer might avoid stepping on the dark sheet of my northern body, but whether it did so from recognition, sensitivity, or the simple preference of its feet would remain unknowable. It would pass through the world needing nothing from me except what all things need: mineral, temperature, time.

But their movement would make patterns possible.

They would carry metals from one valley to another. They would open old lichen fields. They would die and make temporary warm countries for small lives. Their pulses would travel through the plateau and give shape to the long, wordless intervals. Without them, the stone would still listen. Yet it would listen to less.

Perhaps, after centuries, I would reach the eastern basins.

There I would not spread on cliffs or hide in wet gravel. I would extend along the vast spiral ridges made by the unseen burrowers. Their paths would be warmer than the surrounding earth, their upheaved minerals stranger. I would grow in thin black seams at the spiral centers, where steam sometimes lifted from the ground in the cold.

When one of those great buried bodies turned below me, I would feel not an animal but a duration.

Its movement would take days to arrive fully. A pressure would begin beneath the gravel, far too broad to locate. The spiral around me would tighten by a fraction. Dust would slide down its shallow walls. Then a black arch might break the surface somewhere beyond sight, glistening once in the dim light, before returning to the world below.

Afterward the stone would hum.

I would keep that hum.

Not as a sound stored in any chamber, but as a changed arrangement of my crystals, a faint bias in the paths I extended, a memory written into my own conductance. Years later, a winged listener might land on me and feel it through its feet: the old deep turning below; the direction of the warm ground; the probability of disturbance. It might unfold and leave. It might guide its flight accordingly.

In this way, I would become useful without intending usefulness.

I would be part of the plateau’s unnoticed intelligence: not its mind, because the plateau would have no single mind; not its nervous system, because nothing would command anything else; not even its memory, because memory would be too deliberate a word.

I would be one place where a pressure had happened and had not entirely stopped happening.

And if, after enough time, the cliff that held my first dark seam were worn away—if frost opened it, if a rare avalanche of slate carried it down into the valley—I would go with it. I would be ground beneath the plated feet of grazers. I would enter a lichen field. I would travel in the gut-stones of an iron-bodied calf. I would be deposited years later as warm metallic salt beneath another body’s shadow.

There would be no moment in which I could say I had died.

Only a gradual loss of the particular shape from which I had once listened.

Then, elsewhere, some small dark patch would tighten against a rock face when a distant herd altered its course.

And that would be me again—not returned, not reborn, but continued in the manner the plateau permits: dispersed, patient, almost indistinguishable from the ground, listening for a world that never needs to speak aloud.
