I would find myself under the wash trough, where its runoff has made a permanent dark crescent in the soil.

Not in the water exactly. Beneath it.

I would be one of the old bricks that has escaped being a wall.

Perhaps I had once been part of the house before the nearest house: clay pressed into a mould, fired unevenly, laid in a corner meant to stay dry. The wall fell or was opened or slowly surrendered to roots. Someone threw me into the basin to firm a muddy place. Rain covered me. Leaves covered the rain. A fig root passed close by without breaking me, though it nudged me a little deeper each wet season.

I would not remember my making clearly. I would retain only heat: the enormous dry heat of the kiln, older and sharper than sunlight. Everything after that would be dampness, pressure, and the gradual instruction of being ground.

My upper face would be green-black with moss in the months of frequent rain. My lower face would rest in the packed brown softness where worms passed. I would have a crack through me, narrow at first, then widened by a root hair, then made useful by ants. Water would enter it after showers and remain there after every surface puddle had vanished. Tiny things would drink from the crack. A beetle might shelter its shining body in the shadow I made. A frog, no bigger than a folded leaf, might use me as the lip of its evening hollow.

People would know me without noticing that they knew me.

They would step on me when carrying jars from the back lane. Their feet would find my broad, level face even in darkness. A child would balance on me to avoid the mud. The old woman from the nearest house might put one heel on me while emptying wash water, and for an instant the whole basin would receive the small familiar weight of her.

I would become a rule.

Step there, not beside it.

I would be pleased by this, though not in the way people are pleased. I would simply continue to hold. My edges would collect silt. The silt would hold seeds. The seeds would make a thin grass at my corners, and the grass would make the ground less likely to slide away. In that way I would cease to be separate from the place that had received me.

At night I would feel the pocket more clearly.

The air above would cool first. Then the water in the earth would begin its slow rearrangement. Roots would tighten and ease around grains of soil. The raw rise would give one soft, almost inaudible settling sigh. Beneath me, a tunnel might collapse; beside me, another would open. I would not hear these things. I would be their resistance, their boundary, the hard old fact against which they altered course.

The frogs would come out.

One might sit with its chin against my side, so still that it seemed to be listening through me. Its throat would swell and shrink. Another would answer from the citrus roots. Their quiet calls would travel over my surface as vibrations too slight for anyone standing above to notice.

Sometimes the heron’s shadow would pass over me at dawn. Sometimes the cat would place one careful paw on my upper edge, test the soft ground beyond, and withdraw.

And after many seasons, when the rise had come closer and the nearest wall had leaned enough to be rebuilt, someone might pull me free.

They would lift me, surprised at my weight. They would scrape away the moss and say, This is still good brick.

Perhaps they would set me into a new threshold.

But I think I would prefer to remain where I had become useful: half buried, cracked open, keeping a little water beneath the wash trough; no longer quite brick, not yet soil; part of the pocket’s slow decision to become ground.
