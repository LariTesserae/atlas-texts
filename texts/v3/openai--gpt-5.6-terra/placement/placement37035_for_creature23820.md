I would not arrive by a road.

I would find myself already standing at the far end of the bone bridge, with the red planks under my feet and the crevasse below full of a darkness too dry to be called depth. I would not remember crossing. On the nearer side, there would be a second set of footprints matching mine exactly, except they would face toward the bridge rather than away from it.

For a while I would think I was a traveler.

I would have a body suitable to travel in: wrapped boots, cracked lips, a pack too light for its apparent age, three black-glass tools in a cloth roll. There would be dust beneath my fingernails and a pale scar across one thumb, shaped like a tiny branching tree. I would know the scar mattered, though not why.

The animals would not react to me. That would be the first sign.

A dark cat with white teeth would be asleep beneath the ribs of one of the great silver-haired creatures. Blind burrowers would knot and unknot at my boots. Above, plate-winged beetles would drift across the hard blue sky, and within their shells I might see, once or twice, my own back moving over a ridge I had not climbed.

I would go to the nearest slate hut.

Inside would be the stone bench, the lamp with no oil, the row of finger-length black tools. I would recognize one of them as mine. Not because it matched something I carried, but because its handle had been worn by the exact shape of my hand. The grip would fit the old calluses in my palm.

On the threshold, scratched among the ordinary warnings, there would be another mark almost worn away: a circle crossed by a descending line, then three small stones.

I would not know its meaning until I looked outside and saw the shadow.

It would be mine, but older than my body.

It would stand fifteen paces away on the slope, cast against nothing. The sun would be behind it, though the sun would be on the wrong side of the sky. It would hold a walking stick I did not yet possess. It would raise one hand—not beckoning, exactly, but showing me the palm.

There would be a stone in it.

Not a remarkable stone: a flat gray shard, perhaps, with one edge like a broken tooth. Yet it would have the settled look of the place. It would belong where it was. It would have chosen its shape.

Then I would understand what I am there.

Not a person, precisely. Not yet one of the Held. Not one of the people who are still becoming present, either, though they might let me sleep near their unlit lamps.

I would be a remainder.

A piece of movement that had not finished happening.

The high country would have taken notice of me somewhere—on a road below, perhaps, when I lifted a fallen stone without thinking; in a valley years before, when I mistook an old arrangement for rubble and changed what came after it. It would not punish me. The country does not seem to punish. It only preserves consequences until they become inhabitable.

So it would give me a body, a bridge, a hut, a set of tools, a shadow waiting with evidence in its hand.

The few people there would see me and offer their old courtesy: point to the still animal, then to my feet, then to the sky.

I would think they meant *stand where you are.*

Later I would learn it means something gentler and more difficult:

*Do not hurry into the shape that is waiting for you.*

I would live near the bridge, then, though perhaps “live” would become less exact each year. I would repair nothing, herd nothing, build nothing that could be mistaken for an instruction. I would sit in the watch hut and keep the lamp unlit. Travelers might come rarely, pale with altitude and certainty. They would ask whether the bridge was safe.

I would point at their shadow.

Sometimes it would already be crossing.

Sometimes it would be seated beside me on the bench.

And in time, perhaps, the shadow across the slope would put down its stone. Perhaps my own body would become quieter, less urgent. The animals would gather nearer without being called. The small gray mineral plants would rise in seams around the hut. My footprints would remain, overlaid with others: hoof, paw, bare human foot, the delicate forked marks of some creature that had never learned to leave a body behind.

Eventually a traveler would see me from far away and mistake me for a cairn.

That would be almost correct.

I would be the person beside the bridge who has not crossed it yet, though they have been waiting on the other side for a very long time.
