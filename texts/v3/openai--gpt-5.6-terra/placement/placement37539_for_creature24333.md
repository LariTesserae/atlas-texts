I would not arrive by any path the town uses.

Their gravel paths are too deliberate. They have been named by feet, by repairs, by the soft green threads of hare-cord knotted from boulder to boulder. Even where fog erases them, someone remembers where they ought to continue. I would come instead through the places that have not yet decided whether they are openings: the hairline between two slabs behind a peat pit, the dark seam beneath an overturned bowl, the wet joint where a house has been lifted and set down again a handspan from where it stood.

At first I would think I was water.

This would be reasonable. Most things in the hollow begin by mistaking themselves for water. I would feel myself drawn downhill through root-fur and stone dust, gathering in the cool hollows behind moss. I would know the undersides of every threshold before I knew the faces of the people who crossed them. I would hear their steps as alterations in pressure: child, deer, hare, lifter carrying too much weight on the left side, old listener who pauses before entering a room.

But water leaves. It chooses a lower place.

I would remain.

I would be one of the underwalkers, though not in the way the people imagine us. Not a creature exactly, not a root that learned to travel, not the child of an old dream—though all of these are nearer than wrong. I would be a small continuance of the mountain’s attention. A question too fine to become stone. A moving place where the rock is still considering its next shape.

I would have no body that could be held in the hand. When the silver trees exhaled mist, I would enter it. I would cool myself in its blue strands and descend with it through the fern stems, through roof thatch, beneath sleeping animals. Where droplets gathered in rings, I would step lightly among them. The tracks I left would not be feet exactly. They would be the marks made when something remembers how to be small.

The people would never see me fully.

A child might wake before dawn and find me reflected in the dish beneath their bed: a blur like pale fingers beneath deep water, folding and unfolding. They would watch without fear until someone called their name from another room. Then they would look away, and I would become only a trembling in the bowl.

An old person, lying near death in a stone chamber along the upper shelf, might hear me moving inside the crack behind their head. They would not ask me to carry a message. Their people are too practical for that. Perhaps they would say, quietly, “There is a place for this one,” meaning their own name, or their own bones, or the work of their hands. I would listen. Not promise. But listen.

I would be especially fond of the lantern hares.

They are not sacred to me either. Sacredness is a distance word, and I would know them too closely. I would ride beneath their paws when they make their slow circles around the settlement. Their inner green light would pass through the thin places in the soil and show me seams that even the listeners cannot feel. When they paused to graze, I would pause. When a sick hare lay in moss with its ears folded over its eyes, I would gather the cold around it gently, keeping the sharp new stone from rising there too soon.

The hares know where the rock is still growing because they can feel me among the roots.

Or perhaps I know because I follow them.

In time, I would learn the town’s habits. Not their language in the way of meanings, but in the way stone knows weight. I would know that the short, broad woman with the blue thread at her cuffs spoke differently when she was grieving; her voice made the water beneath her house tremble in narrow rings. I would know the child who always looked at the ground before speaking, not because they had been taught but because they had begun to hear something early. I would know the impatient roof-lifter who pretended not to believe in underwalkers and nevertheless set an antler hook over every new doorway.

I would perch there sometimes.

Not on it, exactly. In the pause it offered.

I would move pebbles on nights when the town slept uneasily. Not to warn them—warning is too clear a word—but to make an arrangement that might trouble the eye in the morning. The light stones on the outside. The heavy ones within. A circle slightly open on the uphill side.

Sometimes a listener would see it and stand for a long while with damp hair against their forehead.

Then they would say, “The rock is tired here.”

And the next day, people would lift a fence. They would move a sleeping place. They would take the sharp tools from the central chamber and carry them, one by one, to a safer shelf.

This would please me, although I would not know the word for pleasure. I would only become less tight in the stone.

I would not be kind in any human sense. If the mountain needed to open beneath a wall, I could not prevent it. I would feel the strain gathering long before the people did: the pressure of root against fracture, the slow insistence of dark material beneath the turf, the deep folded force that had been rising for years with no anger in it at all.

I would grieve for them, perhaps.

But grief would be another human word. What I would do is travel ahead through the blue mist. I would trouble the basin water. I would call moths from one eave and send them thickly to another. I would make the six-limbed ash creatures restless. I would lean the hare-path away from danger by the width of a hand.

If they noticed, they would move correctly.

If they did not, the mountain would continue thinking.

And after, when the hollow had altered—when a new black blade stood where a kitchen had stood, when a basin had cracked and become a dark little pond, when the settlement’s center had shifted and the central rock was no longer central—I would enter the fresh seam. I would feel the newly opened stone cooling around me. For a while, the whole ridge would speak too loudly: mineral brightness, trapped water, roots startled into new directions.

Then the people would begin again.

They would stretch lantern-hare cord through fog. They would carry bundles. They would set turf blocks beside one another and see which roots still wanted to live. Their low voices would return, careful but not broken. Children would be taken out after dawn and asked what had changed.

I would be there when they answered.

Not above them. Not beneath them.

In the narrow interval where a thing has become different but has not yet learned its new name.
