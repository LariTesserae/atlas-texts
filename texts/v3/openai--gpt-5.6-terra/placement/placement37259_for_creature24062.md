I would not arrive by a path.

I would come up through the mountain.

Not as a creature large enough to cast a recognizable shadow, nor as one of the bright-eyed lives already gathered around the pools. I would be born in a seam where the black stone has split but not yet learned it is broken: a pale, thread-fine fungus drinking the mineral sweat of the ridge.

At first I would be nearly nothing. A white trace beneath a lip of basalt. A softness in a world that distrusts softness. Water would darken the rock above me, seep through a maze of hairline fractures, and arrive carrying whatever the mountain had dissolved from itself—iron, salt, bitter green minerals, the fine dust of newly made stone. I would take what I could. I would lay my body through the crack in branching filaments, not conquering the rock but learning its smallest permissions.

A moth larva might eat me.

A silver crack-insect might graze the film I made on the wet stone. A mouse, prying loose a tuft of old feather for its nest, might disturb the moss around me and expose my pale fruiting cups to the cold. Then, in the brief wet hour after rain, I would release spores—so light that even the reluctant air could carry them.

Some would settle nowhere.

Some would land in the coats of the horned beasts and travel, damp and sleeping, from one familiar wall to another. Some would catch in the white-faced birds’ feathers and cross, for one costly moment, into a neighboring hollow. Most would die against dry stone.

That would not trouble me. I would not be made for abundance.

I think I would be one of the little lights, too: not a flame, because flame would be too extravagant here, but a faint green milkiness in the wet dark. A glow that appears only where my threads meet the mountain’s seep-water. The bats would pass it without needing it. The climbers would see it and distrust it. The birds might use it unconsciously, turning their pale faces toward the wall where the stone shines weakly, knowing that wetness means insects, and insects mean morning can be endured.

Perhaps the horned animals would know me best.

They would scrape the lichen above me with their close-curled horns and uncover the thin living skin I make along the fissure. They would lick it—not greedily, but with the grave attention they give all things that contain salt. Their warm tongues would take part of me into them. Their hooves would press my spores into damp paths. Their droppings would feed the moss that shelters my threads. They would not know my name, and I would have no name for them, but we would keep each other.

I would live at the edge of being noticed.

Not in the center, where bodies steam and birds arrange their bright stones; not on the outer walls, where the climbers run their patient routes. I would live beneath a shallow overhang halfway between a temporary pool and a crack where the bats sleep. There would be a certain angled stone above me that holds the day’s weak warmth longer than the rest. A mouse colony would use its underside in winter. In rain, the water would fall past them in silver strings.

I would know the hollow by vibration.

The delicate, innumerable tapping of hooves.  
The wing-folding rustle of the birds.  
The low bell-note of the herd settling around its young.  
The scrape of horn against mineral seam.  
The small avalanches of loose pebbles.  
The sudden held breath when the enormous caller sounded somewhere beyond the rim.

When that sound came, it would move through my whole body.

It would enter through the rock rather than the air. Every thread I had sent into the crack would tremble. For a long while afterward, droplets would loosen from the ceiling and fall one by one onto the moss.

I would not know whether the great thing beyond the hollow was beast, weather, or another form of mountain-making. I would only know that it had passed close enough for the stone to remember it.

And in the very coldest hours—when the birds had covered their chicks, when the horned beasts had become one dark breathing mass, when even the bats had quieted under their shelves—I would make my little light.

Not enough to defeat the dark.

Nothing in that hollow defeats the dark. The lives there do not overcome it; they enter into agreements with it. They warm one another inside it. They drink from it when it condenses on stone. They make nests and rings and sleeping circles within its weight.

I would be part of that agreement: a small wet radiance in a fracture of young black rock, feeding the insect that feeds the bird, holding the moss that holds the water, spreading by particles too small to see.

If someone stood at the edge of the hollow and looked down, they would not find me.

They might see, once, a dim green glimmer near the feet of a sleeping horned animal. They might mistake it for reflected starlight.

That would be enough.
