I would not arrive by the roadless side of the mountain, carrying provisions and a name. I think I would be found already inside the hollow, in the interval between the last rain and the first clearing of the sky.

At first I might believe I was one of the winds.

Not the great wind that crosses the rim and makes the whole basin speak at once. That one is too broad, too certain of its direction. I would be a smaller current: a cold exhalation from beneath a stair, a breath that has spent the night among wet roots and black cistern water. I would move slowly out through a doorway that no longer leads anywhere, carry the smell of stone and iron-rich water into a courtyard, stir a fallen feather, and then lose myself among the larger airs.

But wind does not remember where it has been, not in the way I would want to remember. It knows passage, not attachment.

So perhaps I would become mist.

Mist has more patience. It can linger at the foot of a wall. It can enter a room without needing an entrance. It can gather around a tree whose roots have found an old chamber and wait there until sunlight reaches the upper stones. It does not possess the city; it borrows its low places. It learns the shape of every depression, every broken threshold, every stair descending into colder dark.

I would be the mist that repeatedly chooses one particular route: from a hidden pool beneath the eastern terraces, up through a crack beside the old drainage channel, across a square where no seed will grow, then around the trunk of the smallest crooked tree.

Not because the tree needs me. Nothing here needs anything in that urgent way.

But because the tree has one scar in its bark, narrow and black, where a lightning strike long ago entered it and failed to take it. In that scar, water gathers. A beetle sometimes drinks there. In winter, it becomes a little wedge of ice. In spring, when the basin first thaws, the scar runs clear before any of the channels do.

I would return to that place each morning.

And in time I would discover that I was not mist exactly, but what mist leaves behind.

A darkening of stone.

A softness at the edge of a threshold.

The condition under which moss can climb.

I would be a small persistence of damp in the city: not visible most of the day, perhaps, but present in the mortarless joins and beneath the silver roots. A coldness someone might feel through the sole of a boot if they stood still in the right place. The reason a certain patch of green remains alive higher up a wall than it should. The reason droplets form, one by one, on the underside of a lintel even in dry weather.

There are lives in the hollow that move, call, feed, nest, enter and leave. I think I would envy them only briefly.

The ravens would know me without looking. They would land on the wet stone near the eastern stair and test it with one black foot. The hares would pass through my low grass at dusk, their bodies making tiny weather in me. The pale hornless creatures, if they came down at night, might stand in my thin veil until their coats disappeared into it. I would not see them as a human sees. I would recognize them by the slight warmth they gave the air, by the pause they made in the dripping.

Then I might become old enough to understand the city’s way of being alive.

It does not ask to be restored. It does not mourn its roofs. It has no desire to be interpreted correctly. Every wall that becomes slope is still part of its speech. Every stair filled with soil remains a stair somewhere beneath the roots. Its purpose, if it ever had one, has worn away before its form has.

I would live in that fact.

Not as a guardian. Guardianship has too much possession in it. Not as a ghost, either; ghosts are often only people refusing to become part of the weather.

I would be one of the city’s minor continuities: the damp breath rising from an underplace, the dark seam in a wall, the invisible route moisture takes when it remembers the mountain.

And if, years later, a person crossed the central square and looked toward the eastern terraces, they might notice a strip of moss climbing against all expectation toward a high, cold stone.

They might wonder why it grows there.

I would not answer.

But I would be there.
