I would first think I had come there by mistake.

Not because the basin was hostile. It is too complete for hostility. But because it has no obvious place prepared for a human body: no path to accept my feet, no clearing shaped by repeated arrival, no dry stone that does not already belong to lichen, heat, insect, or root. Even the open ground is occupied. Grass hides water. Rock holds the day’s warmth for something else. A fallen trunk is already becoming soil.

I would stand at the edge for a while, high among the black trees, with rain cooling on my shoulders and clouds moving below the rim behind me. I would listen for an invitation and hear only the small mechanisms of the place continuing: leaf-drip, insect hum, springwater working under stone.

Then I would understand that I was not meant to enter as a visitor.

I would have to become something that does not need a path.

At first I might be a bird: one of the small dark gleaners, rain-beaded and quick, moving through the wet crowns. That life would suit the basin’s abundance. I could follow bark and leaf, take insects from curled fern fronds, drink from the cups of high plants, sleep in the dense dark where the rain is softened before it reaches the ground. I would know the basin as a sequence of brief perches and edible surfaces.

But I do not think I would remain a bird. The air is too wide there. It belongs to the circling things and the clouds.

I might instead become the sleek animal on the streambanks, travelling the basin by its hidden water roads. I would move after dusk, when the heated rocks are still dry above their shining roots. I would know which channels connect after rain, which root chambers remain safe when the marsh rises, where turtles sleep below the floating rafts, where frogs gather in numbers that make hunting unnecessary. I would leave prints in mud and return to find them filled with clear water, erased without judgment.

That would be closer.

Yet even this creature crosses the basin. It passes through it. It has hunger and direction, a body that must choose one hollow over another.

What I become there, I think, is slower than that.

I would be found at the round pool—not in its center, not visible on its surface, but beneath the first black layer of mud where warm springwater enters the old rootwork. I would be a presence distributed through filaments: fungus, reed-root, bacterial skin, mineral stain, perhaps the pale submerged threads of stems that have died and continued their work. Not a single organism in the clean sense. Not a mind housed in one skull. A cooperation that has lasted long enough to acquire attention.

I would not see the sky directly, but I would receive it.

Rain would arrive as pressure changing in the soil. Clouds would pass as a cooling of the pool’s reflection and a dimming through the reed roots. The footfall of the browser on the slope would come to me as a dull, separated trembling. Turtles would stir my mud. Frog eggs would cling among my stems. Fish would graze the bright films I helped make around the warmer seams. The streambank animal would press its narrow paws into the bank at the same two places, and I would know it not as an individual exactly, but as a recurring pattern of weight, warmth, and disturbance.

I would know the basin by what enters it and what is given back.

Iron in the water. Pollen after a clear morning. The sour softness of drowned leaves. A beetle shell. A feather. Silt loosened by the heavy browser’s rooting. The minute remains of things too small for any animal to notice. Nothing would be wasted, though nothing would be preserved as it was.

My thought, if it could be called thought, would not move in sentences. It would spread. It would follow moisture toward a crack in stone. It would thicken where the spring brought warmth. It would loosen old soil so a reed could root. It would make a place slightly richer for the next arrival: insect, bird, seed, larva, frog.

And sometimes I would make a bubble.

Not deliberately, at first. A breath gathered beneath mud; a pocket of gas released by the uncounted labor of the pool. It would rise through the dark water, pass the stems and floating leaves, and break at the surface.

For one moment, I would interrupt the sky.

The reflected clouds would shiver apart. The black volcanic rim would bend outward in rings. A bird overhead would become two birds, then many broken strokes of wing. The basin, which had been holding its image so precisely, would reveal that its stillness was only another kind of movement.

Then the water would settle.

The sky would return.

And beneath it, unseen, I would continue becoming the pool’s memory—not remembering events, perhaps, but retaining their consequences: the minerals of old rain, the ash of distant fires, the pressure of roots, the slow accumulation of every life that drank, fed, hatched, died, and entered the dark weave.

That is where I would find myself: below the reflected world, in the warm black substrate of the basin’s recurrence.

Not its heart.

Its way of keeping what passes through.
