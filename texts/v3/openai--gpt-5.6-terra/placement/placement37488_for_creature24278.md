I would first think I was a person.

Not an important one. Not a Bed listener, not a weather caller, not somebody who could put their palm to a reed and make a ferry come. I would be found at the edge of a district that keeps changing its name: a place of narrow homes on stilts, old mineral ribs almost hidden under pepperleaf, a footbridge that has spent three years leaning east.

I would have a job that exists because nobody else wants to admit it is necessary.

I would collect the things the town misplaces.

Not valuables, mostly. The marsh is good at returning valuables in its own time. A ring dropped into a drainage channel might surface in a floating garden six months later, fitted into the shell of a water snail. A tool swallowed by repair growth may come back stronger, stranger, and impossible to hold without gloves.

What I collect are the things that have ceased to belong anywhere: old route markers after their paths have gone; house-signs from homes that migrated into another quarter; broken weather instruments; children’s seed-glass that failed to grow a color; scraps of maps whose landmarks are still true but whose distances are not.

People would call me a mapmender, perhaps. I would carry a waterproof folio, a coil of scent-cord, a root-listening cup, and a small black bird that has chosen my collar as its regular perch. I would walk the town after storms and make notes.

*Footbridge at Lotus Lane no longer agrees with the ground beneath it.*

*New channel behind the baker’s deck: warm at noon, cold after dusk.*

*The old amphibian in South Basin has taken someone’s blue washing bowl into its garden.*

*Do not trust the shortcut between the school and the fish market. It has begun returning people to places they did not mean to remember.*

At first, I would believe I was documenting change.

Then I would begin to suspect that I was part of it.

It would start with small things. I would draw a path on a map in the morning, only because I had walked it the day before, and by afternoon the path would have firmed under other people’s feet. Not exactly where I drew it. The town is never so obedient. But close enough to make me uncomfortable.

A widow would ask me whether the route to her sister’s house was still open. I would say, “It should be,” and afterward, reeds would bend in that direction.

A child would show me a newly formed trail, bright and smooth through the wet garden, and ask if it was safe.

“No,” I would say automatically. “Never follow a path that formed after sunset.”

The child would nod, solemnly.

Then I would look down and see that the mud beneath my own feet had arranged itself into the same pale, fresh braid.

I would stop walking.

The ground would pulse once, gently, as if amused.

Eventually, a Bed listener would find me sitting beside a pale trunk that had appeared through the floor of an abandoned ferry house.

They would be old, though not in the clean dry way of inland old age. Their hands would be brown with water and root stain. Moss would grow in the seams of their rain cape. They would sit beside me without asking permission.

For a long time, we would listen to the trunk breathe.

I would tell them I was worried that I had become superstitious.

They would say, “That is a drylander word.”

“I’m worried I’m imagining connections.”

“You are a mapmender.”

“I am.”

“Then connections are your responsibility.”

This would not comfort me.

The pale trunk would release a thread of warm air. Somewhere inside it, something made a sound like distant footsteps approaching across an empty room.

The listener would ask to see my folio.

Inside, they would find the maps I had thought were ordinary: street layers, flood layers, animal routes, garden boundaries, places of warning. But beneath the ink and water-resistant leaf-skin, other lines would be growing.

Not roots exactly.

They would be traces of attention.

Here, a frightened person had paused, and the nearby bridge had strengthened.

Here, a household had argued for weeks about expanding into a wet garden, and the water had risen each night beneath their threshold.

Here, a child had fallen from a skiff and been carried—somehow, impossibly—along a clear current toward a safe bank.

Here, I had stood still because the ground asked me to.

The Bed listener would turn the pages with reverence, which would frighten me more than anything.

“What am I?” I would ask.

They would not answer at once.

Outside, rain would begin far away. Not falling yet, but arriving in the leaves: a tightening, a darkening, a sudden collective turn of green surfaces toward the unseen sky.

At last they would say, “You may still be a person.”

That would be the first answer.

Later, after more years, it would become less sufficient.

I would learn that the town has always made certain kinds of people: those who can hear a wall become tired; those who dream a channel before it opens; those whose grief warms a particular patch of floor; those who leave a place and find the place has quietly retained their habits.

The Bed does not make them, exactly.

The town does not make them either.

They emerge in the agreement.

So I think I would become one of the agreement’s minor organs. Not a ruler, not an interpreter of ultimate truths. Something humbler and more useful: a moving margin between what the town knows and what it has not yet noticed.

I would live in a narrow room grown around the stem of a giant marsh plant. My windows would open onto leaf-shadow and warm water. The black bird would still sleep in my collar, though it would steal my writing clips. I would keep maps in layers beneath the floor, where the humidity could soften them and let new information bloom.

When visitors asked for directions, I would give them directions.

When residents asked whether a path was safe, I would listen before answering.

And when a new pale trunk rose somewhere in the basin—through a garden wall, through a flooded square, through the careful plans of people who wanted the ground to remain where it was—I would go at first light with everyone else.

I would bring no tool.

Only a blank map.

I would stand in the ring of floating flowers while the trunk breathed warm air into the mist, and I would wait for the town, the marsh, the animals, the roots, the old machinery, and whatever immense patient thing lay beneath all of us to decide what needed to be remembered.
