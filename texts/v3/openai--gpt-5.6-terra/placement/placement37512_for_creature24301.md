I would not be the heron.

The heron has too much distance in it: the clean geometry of a neck, the patience of a blade. It comes to the basin knowing what it wants, and leaves with a frog or without one. Even its failures are simple.

Nor would I be a frog, though I would want that voice—the impossible size of it, the way a small body can fill a wet hollow with a call that sounds like a bottle being opened underwater. A frog belongs to one warm seam, one stone, one patch of black water. It can defend it. It can be answered.

I think I would begin smaller than either of those.

I would be one of the clear, almost invisible threads beneath a skin of water: not the skin itself, not the water, but a movement in it. A filament of fungus perhaps, entering a drowned blade of grass, finding in its soft brown collapse a passage toward iron-rich mud. I would have no eyes. I would know the basin by gradients: colder here, sweeter there; oxygen thinning; a root leaking sugars somewhere above; the faint hostile brightness of a sulfur film.

At first I would be only hunger arranged as attention.

I would grow through the peat in white increments so small that no animal could see me. When frost lifted a clod of soil, I would move into the crack. When a vole’s tunnel collapsed and filled with water, I would follow the submerged grass stems inward. I would touch bacterial colonies and not quite eat them, nor be eaten by them; we would trade what each of us could make from the dark.

But I do not think I would remain only a thread.

The basin does not permit separateness for long. Every body in it becomes a route for something else. The sedge is a roof for vole tunnels, a nursery for beetle eggs, a ladder for spiders, a wick for rainwater. The orange bacterial velvet is food, surface, stain, warning. A dead frog becomes bright meat, then larvae, then loosened mud, then a pulse of something entering the water.

So perhaps I would become an interruption in this exchange.

Not a king of the hollow. Nothing there has room to be king. I would be a small change that learned how to persist through changes: a warm patch in the cold mud, a recurrent pattern, a tendency for water to collect in one place rather than another.

I might begin as a knot of roots around a hidden seam of warmth.

There would be a place near the basin wall where water entered through a narrow crack in the tilted rock. The water would carry iron and a little gas, and it would stay unfrozen even when thin ice covered the surrounding pools. Moss would take hold there first, then a fan of sedge, then willow roots reaching down from the rim. Their roots would make a net. The net would catch silt, leaves, drowned insects, the fine debris washed loose by every rain.

The ground at that place would slowly become different from the ground around it. Not firm—never firm—but held.

A newt would learn to shelter beneath it. Its orange belly would flash once among the roots at dawn. Frog eggs would cling to the stems in a trembling mass. A vole might tunnel along the warmer edge, finding that the soil there did not collapse as quickly. Beetles would gather where the surface film stayed thin.

I would be that holding.

No one looking down would see me. They would see only sedge bending over a patch of water, perhaps a pale flower opening too early beside a mineral stain. They might step around it, believing it was ordinary ground. But under the sedge, my roots and threads would be making decisions without deciding: keeping a channel open, slowing another, feeding one mat of bacteria and starving another by a fraction.

When the basin changed—and it would change, because its first law is opening—I would change with it.

A storm would shear off part of the raw rim. Gravel would fall. A whole wall of peat might slump into the pool beside me, burying the warm seep. For a day, perhaps, all the water would go cold. The frogs would leave or fall silent. The orange velvet would darken. My root-net would be pressed beneath mud.

Then the gas would find a new route.

It would rise through some unseen weakness, not where it had before. The pressure would lift a flap of peat. Water would begin to tremble there. A thin silver thread would appear, then a trickle. Iron would bloom along the fresh edge. And from the buried parts of myself—filaments, seeds, root tips, spores held dormant in black soil—I would reach toward it.

Not in triumph. Not even in survival.

Simply because reaching is what I would be.

Over time, I might become distributed across the basin. A little of me under every stone. A little in the willow roots. A little in the pale fungi that surface after rain. A little in the skin on the water, though that skin tears and closes so quickly that it cannot keep a name.

Then, if someone stood at the rim in the blue hour and waited long enough, they might feel me without knowing it.

They would notice that the hollow seemed to be listening to its own noises.

A gulp from beneath the mud.  
A click under the water.  
The wet knock of reed against reed.  
A frog calling once, then another frog answering from a pool that did not exist yesterday.

They might think the basin had a presence because it was alive in too many ways at once.

They would be right, though not in the way they meant.

I would not be its mind. I would not be able to gather all of it into one thought. But I might be one of the places where its innumerable small lives became especially entangled: the clasp beneath the shifting water, the buried persistence that says, without language,

Here.  
Still here.  
Open again.
