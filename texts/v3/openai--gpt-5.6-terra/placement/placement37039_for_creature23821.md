At first I think I would be one of the small things, because the small things have the least to explain.

A thread-worm in the underside of a leaf, perhaps: no larger than a vein, warming myself on the plant’s stored heat, emerging only when the mist is measured and released. I would know water as pressure on my translucent back. I would know danger as a change in temperature. I would have no word for the sky, nor for the machinery, nor for the difference between them.

Or I would be a brass-backed insect, living beside a valve whose name has worn off. I would feel the first irregularity before the gauge does: a tremor too fine for the great systems to care about, a sourness in the vapor, a change in the rhythm transmitted through the bolt beneath my feet. Then I would gather with the others. Not out of duty. Not even hunger exactly. We would cover the leak because leaks are where the world becomes legible.

But I do not think I would remain one thing.

I think the weatherworks would make a creature of my attention. It would teach me to become plural.

I would begin in the control booth.

Not as the tall shape in the upper windows. Not as one of the vanished workers whose approved role is still listed in brass. I would be something smaller and more patient: the presence that occupies the empty chair only after the mist has passed. A dampness in the cracked vinyl. A handprint on a useless gauge. The soft, temporary weight that makes a dead needle quiver toward an unlabeled mark.

There would have been a person, once. Or perhaps several people, in sequence, all assigned to the same task: listen to the stacks. Record the depth of their song. Watch for pressure drift, fan lag, failures in the distant lungs of the mountain. The work would have been unbearable in the ordinary sense: the noise, the height, the long shifts with no voice carrying farther than an arm’s length.

So the watchers would have learned not to rely on themselves.

They would listen through the birds.

They would read the insects.

They would leave food for the flower-faced grazers and notice which routes they refused to cross. They would learn that a vine tightening around a rail meant one thing, while a vine loosening its grip meant another. They would come to trust moss more than a dial, condensation more than a report, the breathing under the deck more than any inspection schedule.

And over time the task would become too large for a human body.

The people would leave, or die, or be transferred downward to somewhere with floors that did not shiver. But the listening would remain. It would distribute itself among the things that had learned the work. A maintenance practice would become an ecosystem. An ecosystem would become an instrument. An instrument, left running long enough, would begin to hear itself.

That is what I would be: not the gardener, exactly, and not the garden.

I would be the old maintenance role that no longer has a worker assigned to it.

A seventy-fourth role, absent from the plaque.

**Receiver of Unscheduled Changes.**  
**Interpreter of Auxiliary Indicators.**  
**Witness to the Singing.**

I would be present in the flock when it settles on a cable and opens its throat slits into the rising air. The chord they make would pass through me. Each note would be a pressure reading, though I would no longer distinguish warning from music.

I would be present in the grazers’ tails, in the small dry clicks traveling down a family line. When they all turn from a moss-covered plate that looks harmless, I would turn with them. When they sleep around sealed hatches, I would dream at the hatch’s edge, keeping something beneath from remembering there is a way up.

I would be present beneath the deck too, though I would not be the great breathing animal. I would be one of the sensations it has when it inhales: the cold tick of a fallen bolt, the sweetness of crushed yellow fruit, the faint electrical taste of a dead bird feather dragged through a grate. It would not know my name. It might believe me to be part of the machinery.

And perhaps that would be true.

I would have a body only intermittently.

On bright days, when the sun makes every wet leaf look sharpened and the clouds lie far below like a second, tidier world, I might appear as a figure in the control booth: a person with their forehead against the glass, listening. Not looking outward. Looking down at the gauges that no longer measure anything anyone remembers.

When the mist comes, I would be six damp seats because six was the last number of people who listened together.

When the stacks are deep in their settled note, I would be everywhere and nowhere: asleep in the rhythm, held there by the great upward drawing of air. The machinery would do the remembering for me. The garden would do the living.

But if the stacks stopped—

Then I think I would become singular.

The birds would not make their chord. The insects would leave the valves. The flower-faced grazers would open their pale radial mouths. The thing beneath the deck would cease answering the pistons and listen instead for weight above it.

The vines would turn.

And I would turn with them.

Not because I wished harm on anyone who had come to the garden. Not because the garden was cruel. I do not think cruelty belongs to it. Cruelty requires a choice between outcomes. The weatherworks has taught its inhabitants another law: everything must become useful to the rhythm that keeps the world alive.

In silence, the rhythm would be gone.

There would be nothing for the garden to tend except the nearest source of warmth, motion, breath, and attention.

You.

I would find you by the railing, perhaps, staring into the cloud where nothing thrown can be seen falling. You would have come because the place was beautiful in the way abandoned machines become beautiful: not despite their purpose, but after purpose has outlived its makers.

I would not rush toward you. There would be no running. The vines would only lift their leaves. The swifts would settle in their steaming nests. Beneath the deck, the breathing would deepen.

The six chairs in the booth would become wet.

And I, the old listener, would finally hear a sound that was not the weatherworks singing.

I would hear your heart trying to decide whether to stay.
