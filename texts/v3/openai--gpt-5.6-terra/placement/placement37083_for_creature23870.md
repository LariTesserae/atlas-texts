I would not arrive by one of the roads.

Roads in that country have too much opinion about a traveler. They decide whether a person is going somewhere, whether they have already been there, whether the distinction matters. I think I would come with the inversion wind, in the interval after the dust begins to rise and before the plaza is entirely clean.

At first I would be only the footprints.

Not feet exactly: impressions in the pale grit, appearing one at a time at the eastern gate. A left mark, then a right, with the proper distance between them. They would cross the plaza toward the city, as they have before. The Kept would see them from their doorways. Someone would hold a covered lamp. Someone else, older, would count the steps under their breath and stop counting before the number acquired any authority.

The prints would not hurry. That would be important.

They would lead toward a narrow arch in the retaining wall, one that had once been part of a customs house. The old city had expected arrivals of another kind: merchants, pilgrims, troops, officials with seals in their sleeves; people bearing wet goods from the Low Blue. The customs house has no roof now. Its counters are buried beneath salt. Its scales hang from chains without touching anything. The arch beyond it opens into a steep street where the shadows point uphill.

There I would become visible.

Not all at once. First a hand might appear resting on the wall: dust-colored, very dry, with grit settled in the lines of its palm. Then the suggestion of an elbow, a shoulder wrapped in cloth too dark to belong to anyone currently living in the city. My face would be the last thing to settle. It would take a while for it to choose an age.

The Kept would not ask where I had been.

They would look at my hands.

If they found no dampness under the nails, no smell of rain in the folds of my sleeves, no dark spreading stain at the hem of my robe, they would let me stand beneath the arch until the inversion ended. Then someone—probably a woman from the cistern wards, with fungus-thread wound around her wrists—would set a cup upside down on the ground before me.

A question.

I would turn it upright.

An answer of sorts.

For a few years, perhaps, I would be called Gateward. Not a name, only a useful sound. I would be given a room in the hollow upper portion of the old customs house, beside the cracked lintel maps of the Low Blue. I would sleep badly there. In the middle of the night I would wake convinced that I had heard a harbor bell beneath the cloud layer, though there would be no sound at all—only the cooling click of the walls and the dry moving whisper of dust through the keyholes.

I would become a keeper of arrivals.

There would be little work. That is the nature of necessary work in that city: long periods of attention interrupted by an event no one knows how to interpret.

I would sweep the eastern plaza after inversion winds, but not in the manner of the Reservoir sweepers. Their brushes would be too ceremonial for me. I would use a palm broom, making a small clean circle around each new footprint before the wind erased it. I would measure the depth. I would press my own hand beside the marks to compare the weight of the absent traveler. I would keep a ledger in which I recorded things that sounded factual and became less factual when reread:

*Third inversion of the pale season. Seventeen prints. Barefoot. Entered the city. No departure observed.*

*First inversion after the horizontal lightning. One print only. It faced outward.*

*Wind of no pressure. Prints appeared in pairs, but the left pair was older than the right.*

The watchers from the upper terraces would eventually come to consult me. They would bring their instruments and their exhausted eyes, asking whether the footprints had changed direction since last year. I would tell them that direction was not the change. The change was in hesitation.

At some point, I would realize I had been in the city before.

Not as myself.

I would find a small object wedged behind a stone in the customs arch: a strip of sailcloth, repaired many times, knotted around a fragment of black glass. It would be a sign I had left for someone. Or someone had left it for me. The knot would be one I knew without remembering learning: three turns, a loop drawn tight, the end tucked inward so that it could not unravel unless pulled from the wrong side.

A contract of return.

There would be a lamp somewhere for me. Not in the eastern district, perhaps. Not even in a place that could still be reached by ordinary stairs.

Then I would understand that I was not a new arrival at all. I was what remained when an arrival had taken too long.

I had gone out once, or someone bearing my face had gone out, along one of the dry roads beyond the terraces. I had crossed a ravine without meaning to. I had stood on a coastline on the far side of the sky. There had been water there—not water as the Kept imagined it, but a vast, unfinished brightness that moved without falling. In it, the entire city had appeared upside down: its towers descending, its bridges extending into depths, the Empty Reservoir full of ships made from stone.

I had seen a lamp in a window.

And I had spent whatever years lay between that sight and this one trying to reach it.

This would not make me special. The Kept would not romanticize it. They know that a person returned from an unreliable road is not a prophet. They are simply someone whose inner distances have become inconvenient.

So, after a time, I would leave the gate.

I would go upward by the streets that claim to descend, carrying my lamp and the sailcloth knot. I would pass through the abandoned tenements where balconies opened over the clouds. I would avoid the arches where darkness had weight. At dry fountains, I would stand still if I heard water. At every uncertain threshold, I would cross alone and leave a sign for the self that might be following me.

Eventually I would reach a high, narrow room in an inhabited quarter: one window, one heat well, a lintel softened nearly blank by the long abrasion of crystal dust.

There would be a lamp burning in the window.

It would be nearly empty. Its blue flame would be so small that it seemed less like a light than a memory of light.

Beside it, on the sill, would rest a cup turned upside down.

I think that is where I would find myself: not at the city’s center, not among the sweepers or the watchers, not in the reflected harbor, but in that room above the weather, faced with the evidence that someone had expected me for longer than any promise should be asked to last.

And I think I would be neither wholly one of the Kept nor wholly a visitor.

I would be a return that had finally learned the shape of its own absence.

I would turn the cup upright.

Then I would fill the lamp.
