I would not arrive in the Dry Garden as a person from below, with a pack and questions about water. I think the hollow would make me too conspicuous for that. I would spend my first days looking upward, mistaking the narrow sky for the important thing, and miss the actual weather: the change in a dog’s posture, the quieting of beetles, the smell at a pillar’s cut.

Nor would I be one of the old Scar-Keepers. Their knowledge has too much weight in it. To know that a family must wait for sap while their stores run low—really to know it, in the hands rather than the mind—would require a life shaped by the Garden’s refusals.

I would be something smaller in the agreement.

A root-mouse, perhaps, but not exactly one of the soft, quick ones living openly in household walls. I would be the mouse that inhabits the narrow warm space between a pillar-plant’s oldest root and a patched stone foundation: tolerated by one family, unknown to another, fed on peelings, fungus crumbs, and the rare dropped seed.

My nest would be made from stolen things, though I would not call them stolen. A twist of goat hair. A strip of red apprenticeship thread. The soft innards of a shredded old rope. The white, papery skin shed by a cicada. One tiny blue fiber, worked loose long ago from cloth that had come from the river world. It would be useless, except that in certain light it would remember water for me.

I would learn the hollow from underneath.

Not its streets, which are too large and social, but its tremors. The footsteps of goat people: deliberate, uneven, always accompanied by a faint rattle. The smooth fast patter of children sent on errands. The long careful pauses of someone carrying a full sap basket. The particular vibration of an elder lowering themselves onto the threshold stone. The little thunder of a dropped pot, followed by the silence in which everyone decides whether it has broken.

I would know when the roots were well.

Healthy roots would give off a slow stored warmth and a dense green smell, even through stone. Sick roots would go strange first: sharp-sweet, then yeasty, then cold. When the cold came, I would leave.

Not nobly. Not as a messenger. I would simply gather my young and flee through cracks no human body could enter, and the people who noticed us would understand.

“Root-mice are moving,” someone would say.

That would be enough.

Doors would open. Children would be called from ladders. A Scar-Keeper would come with a bone tube and press one end to the pillar’s scarred skin. Someone would put an ear to the floor. The household would begin lifting jars, bedding, tools, dried food—everything that could be moved before the wall, or the root, or the old hollow beneath it made its decision.

So I would be useful without intending to be useful. That seems fitting in the Dry Garden. Nothing there is permitted the luxury of being merely itself, and yet everything is allowed to be itself very thoroughly.

But perhaps, after some years, I would become more than a mouse.

There is a kind of person the Garden might make of me: not a keeper, not a council voice, not a trader or a climber. I would become a listener to small absences. A child who spent too long watching mice disappear into walls. An adult who learned the noises beneath floors: the root’s slow tightening, the scrape of beetle larvae, the dry shifting that means ordinary settling, the rounder muffled sound that means stone has opened somewhere it should not.

I would be given no grand title at first. People might call me Under-Ear, or Floor-Watcher, or Mouse-Friend, depending on whether they liked me. I would be mildly annoying at meals because I would stop speaking to listen. I would be unpopular with builders because I would insist on tapping every new foundation stone. Children would bring me dead beetles, shed skins, strange pellets, snapped roots, and expect answers. I would have to learn how often the honest answer is: *I do not know yet. Leave it where it was. Watch it for three days.*

And in time I might become one of those people the Dry Garden keeps because a place of limits cannot afford to discard an odd attention.

My home would be low and dark, built partly around rather than upon a root. Its floor would stay warm at night. There would be shallow clay dishes of peelings near the wall—not pets, exactly, but invitations. There would be marks on the plaster recording migrations: three mice, rootward, before the late cold; seven, toward the old crack, after the monkey storm; none at all during the year the pillar bled amber sap.

I would sleep lightly. I would dream in vibrations.

When strangers arrived and talked about rivers, I would not argue. I would sit near the council porch and watch the dogs. If the dogs lay with their heads on their paws, perhaps the strangers were merely foolish. If the dogs watched them without blinking, I would remember their smell.

When a child vanished into the tangled roofs, I would know which hollows remained warm after sunset, which abandoned storerooms still held root-mouse nests, which bridges vibrated differently when someone small was crouched beneath them. I would help find the child, then tell them—gently, unless they were old enough to know better—that falling is a mistake with a name.

At funerals I would stand at the edge, not close to the bell. When the one dull note entered the walls, I would feel it divide beneath my feet and travel through root and stone. I would listen for its return.

Not because I thought the dead would answer.

Because stone remembers pressure.

And perhaps that would be where I truly found myself in the Dry Garden: not in the center of its life, not above it in the narrow sky, but at the threshold where a small living creature becomes a sign, where a sign becomes attention, and attention becomes care.

A creature of the under-root.

A keeper of the almost-unnoticed.

Someone who knows that before a wall falls, before a pillar sickens, before a household understands it is in danger, the smallest citizens often leave first.
