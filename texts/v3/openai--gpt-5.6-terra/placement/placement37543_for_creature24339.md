I would not arrive there as an explorer for very long.

At first I would think of myself as one: a small upright interruption in the weather, carrying a sealed lamp, a knife, a notebook already going soft at the corners. I would follow one of the warm lanes because it seemed to have been waiting for the shape of feet. The ground would give under me—not mud exactly, not soil—but a dense wet felt that remembered each step after I had lifted my weight away.

I would look for a place to camp and find none.

Every level surface would be occupied. Every apparent clearing would be the closed mouth of a pool, a leaf-field concealing a shaft, a young mat knitting itself over some older wound. I would learn to sleep half-sitting among the bundled pale trunks, listening for sounds that never quite came: water shifting in buried arteries, pressure releasing from the mist-towers, the nearly inaudible turn of whole crowns overhead.

In the morning my pack would be furred with red threads.

At first I would brush them away.

Then I would stop.

Not because they were difficult to remove, though they would be, but because the act would begin to feel like wiping away a hand placed lightly on my shoulder. The country would not demand anything of me. That would be worse. It would simply distribute itself into the conditions I made available. A little warmer beneath my sleeping body. Slightly saltier where sweat soaked through my clothes. Richer in iron where I set down my knife. It would lean toward these differences with the patient exactness of roots entering a crack.

I think I would become a relay.

Not an animal, not quite a plant. A warm, walking discontinuity through which the commonwealth could briefly sense itself differently.

The plants would know pressure, wetness, mineral, injury, season. Through me they would acquire the irregular chemistry of memory: fear in my sweat when a fog-bank closed around me; grief when I found some object from before—perhaps a rusted button, perhaps the white arc of a bone caught in a mat; the bitter wakefulness of insomnia; the abrupt animal astonishment of seeing the hard blue sky open above the reed basin.

They would not understand these things as I did. They would translate them.

Fear would become a tightening in a distant fan-crown. Grief would be carried as a dark sugar through buried fibers and result, weeks later, in a thicker buttress around a seeped cliff. My longing for dry ground might make the land lift a little under certain lanes, building fibrous shelves around the places I returned to most often. The plants would not be comforting me. They would be responding to a pattern of heat, pressure, salt, and repetition.

Yet I might mistake it for kindness.

Eventually I would stop trying to travel outward.

There would be no horizon that remained separate enough to reach. The plateau would go on folding itself: shoulder into basin, basin into reed-field, reed-field into dark water under the moss. I would learn the shifts in its labor. When the towers stopped pumping, I would feel the transfer underground through the soles of my feet. When storms came, I would lie flat in the root-mesh and feel the great connected body brace itself around the slope.

The small grazers would begin to follow my camp, clipping the new growth where my warmth encouraged it. Transparent pool-things would collect in the cups of vessels near where I slept. A black bird might circle once, far above, and leave something pale on the ground that the moss would close over before I could reach it.

My name would become unusable.

Not forgotten, exactly. Repeated so seldom that it would lose its edges. I might write it in the damp margin of the notebook, but the ink would feather and spread through the fibers until the letters resembled root-branches.

Then, perhaps, I would understand what I was for.

I would be a scar that did not harden.

A mobile seam.

For some years I would move through the high country with green under my nails and pale filaments threaded through the lining of my boots. The roots beneath my resting places would thicken. Moss would choose the folds of my clothes. The land would use the shape of my body as it used a fallen tower or a diverted channel: not as an object to preserve, but as a temporary arrangement through which circulation could continue.

And when I finally stopped moving—whether from cold, exhaustion, choice, or the gradual softening of all distinctions—I would not be buried.

The ground would rise to meet me.

Warm fibers would enter the hollows of my hands. Blue leaves would gather rain in the cup of my throat. The notebook, the lamp, the knife, the metal closures of my pack: all of it would remain for a while as a faint mineral complication inside the growing mat.

Above me, eventually, there would be a patch of growth unlike the rest.

Perhaps a small rotating crown, slower than the others, turning toward weather no one else could feel. Perhaps a warm corridor through a cold slope. Perhaps a place where the mist condensed in unusually clear drops and traveled underground carrying traces of old salt.

A traveler passing there might feel watched.

Not by me.

By the memory of having once been separate.
