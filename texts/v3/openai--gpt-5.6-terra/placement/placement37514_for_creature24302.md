I think I would arrive there as a cloud-walker.

Not dramatically. I would not come climbing through a rent in the sky with frost on my eyelashes and a prophecy in my pocket. I would simply have been trying to get somewhere else. A road would disappear under weather. A sound—water, perhaps, or a bird calling from the wrong side of the fog—would persuade me to take one more step. Then another.

By the time I reached the shelf, I would have forgotten the name of the place I meant to go.

The keeper would find me sitting beside the spring, not looking over the rim. This would be important. My hands would be empty except for some small thing I had carried from below without knowing why: a key to a house no longer mine, a button, a folded scrap of map softened into pulp by rain.

They would give me wool. They would put a warmed stone in my hands.

They would not ask who I was.

For the first night, I would be only a guest: damp, exhausted, grateful for the smoke-stained roof and the sound of water doing what water does. I would lie awake among the two blankets, listening to the shelf speak in all its overlapping languages. The ticking of rain. The grass rubbing itself raw in the wind. The distant falls beneath everything. A chough walking on the roof stones with the deliberate confidence of an owner inspecting a foolish tenant.

Then, in the dark, someone would call my name from below.

Not the name I had arrived with. Something older and less useful. A name from before I learned to answer quickly when people wanted something from me.

I would not respond.

By morning, perhaps, I would be able to leave. The cloud would thin. A blue ridge would appear beneath the rim. The keeper would test the ladders, tie a cord around my waist, and lead me down by the proper way.

But I do not think I would go.

Not because the shelf was safe. It is too wet, too narrow, too exposed for safety. Not because it was beautiful, though it would be beautiful in the severe way that makes beauty feel almost like an accusation.

I would stay because it has no use for possession.

Below, nearly everything asks to be made permanent: a room, a role, an explanation, a future arranged in advance. On the shelf, even the shelter is only borrowed from the weather. The rules are not commandments so much as manners. Do not cut the grass that keeps the ground from leaving. Do not light a fire that will run away from you. Do not walk into white weather merely because you are impatient to arrive somewhere else.

I think I would learn those rules slowly.

At first I would be bad at being a keeper. I would try to improve things. I would want to stack the firewood more neatly, reinforce the roof with better ties, widen the path, organize the herbs into little bundles with labels. The actual keeper would watch me in silence until I understood that a tidy arrangement is not always a kindness.

The shelf survives because it yields in the right places.

So I would learn to mend with materials that remember they are temporary. A crooked branch for a ladder rung. Rawhide that will stiffen, then loosen, then be replaced. Stones seated against stones without forcing them into a wall. I would learn to take just enough moss for a wound dressing and leave the rest gripping the earth.

I would become good at listening to marmots.

Not fluent, exactly. No one is fluent in marmot. But I would learn the difference between indignation and alarm. I would know when a chain of whistles means goat, when it means hawk, when it means the Last Guest moving behind the boulders.

I would never see the Last Guest clearly.

I would see a shoulder in rain. A tail, perhaps, vanishing behind wet stone. Two pale reflections at the edge of the firelight, too low and far apart for a person’s eyes. I would find its tracks dissolving in the mud and feel relieved by their uncertainty.

Its name would be enough.

The Last Guest.

I would leave water at the boulder screen when it came near. Not as an offering, not as a bargain. As a way of saying: I know this place was not made for me alone.

And gradually the choughs would begin to recognize me.

One would steal the fastening pin from my cloak. I would find it three days later in a crack high on the cliff, beside a pale fragment of mica and the jawbone of some small animal. Another would perch near the shelter entrance whenever cloud began to gather below the rim. I would come to trust that bird more than I ought to trust any omen.

There would be a period, perhaps years long, in which I would be a person there.

I would bring salt to the goat stone. I would clear blocked channels after storms. I would show frightened cloud-walkers how to hold a warm stone between both palms, as if warmth were a fact that could be remembered into the body. I would sit awake beside them when voices came out of the white.

I would not answer those voices.

But eventually I suspect I would become less like a person and more like one of the shelf’s necessary habits.

Not the keeper exactly. A keeper leaves, even if only by dying somewhere lower down. Not a ghost, either; the shelf has no need for another claim upon it.

I would become the one who notices what has shifted.

The water’s pitch under the grass.
The loosened slab in the roof.
The missing chough.
The new claw mark.
The silence spreading through the marmot field.
The moment when a visitor’s fear changes from the fear of falling into the fear of being found.

If I had a name among the people below, it might be something plain: the Rung-Mender, the Smoke-Reader, the One at the Spring.

But on the shelf, I would not call myself anything.

I would be someone who stays for a while.

Someone who keeps the door of woven branches leaning rather than shut.

Someone who understands that the mountain does not ask to be solved, only met with enough humility to let it remain itself.

And when the cloud rises and holds below the rim, when the world vanishes and the falls pour into a white country with no bottom, I would sit among the standing stones with my back to the wind.

I would listen.

If my old name came drifting upward from the cloud, I would let it pass through me like rain.
