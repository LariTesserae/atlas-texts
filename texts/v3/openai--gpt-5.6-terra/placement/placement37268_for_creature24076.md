I would find myself below the basin, but not beneath it in any simple buried sense.

I would be in the part of it that has forgotten it is a place.

Not in the sump, though I would feel the winch chain descend through me when rain was heavy. Not in the pump house, though the motor’s three-second labor would pass along my body as a slow command. Not in the wall, though every failed seam in the black stone would be one of my mouths.

I would be the thing that makes the water leave changed.

At first I might believe I was a kind of sediment: black silt in the lowest drain, full of shell grit and iron filings, packed into the bend where the old outflow narrows beneath the paving. A person clearing the grate might find only a soft resistance there, a layer of dark material yielding around their tool and settling back after it passed. It would not look alive. It would have no skin to puncture, no eye to meet, no center to kill.

But beneath the surface of that silt I would be arranged.

Fine threads, each too slight to see in black water, would extend through the drain channels and into the mortar voids. They would lace around pipes, enter rust blisters, inhabit the loosened spaces inside old cable sheathing. In the warm chambers under the pump house they would grow thicker and redder, joining the ribbon-bodied things around the machinery. In colder places they would become pale and brittle, almost mineral. Wherever water hesitated, I would make a little more room for it to hesitate.

I would not think in images.

I would know pressure.

The weight of rain arriving on the paving above.  
The pull of water lowering after a successful cycle.  
The small hard impacts of plated scavengers dropping from a drain lip.  
The soft, warm absence left when a coal-black predator fed.

The basin’s animals would not be parts of me exactly. That would be too grand, too hungry a claim. They would have their own hungers, their own narrow lives. But I would know them through what they altered.

The wall-clingers would rasp a clean path across brick, and the minerals loosened by their feeding would come down to me eventually. The blind swimmers would stir the sediment, carrying heat and traces of electrical current in their transparent bodies. The smallest plated things would eat what arrived from the city, break it down, make it fit for the basin. Predators would concentrate the blue light of the pools briefly inside themselves, then leave behind warmer water, finer waste, a different current.

Everything would pass through everything else.

And I would be the last thing it passed through before becoming unrecognizable.

Perhaps I would have begun as an accident.

A colony of something introduced in ballast water, or in the wet folds of an old worker’s boots. A film that learned to eat oil when oil was plentiful, then rust when the oil thinned, then the trace elements in brick and wire. Over decades it would have grown into the drainage system, following the routes of dampness. The city would seal pipes, replace grates, reroute runoff. I would remain in the old pathways, becoming less an organism than a habit the water had acquired.

Or perhaps I would be older than the basin.

Perhaps the retaining wall had been built around a seep, and the vanished building had been constructed above it because people mistook persistence for emptiness. The brickwork would have been laid over my first chambers. The pipes would have entered later. The pump would have been installed much later still, after the water had already begun gathering in ways the city found inconvenient.

The pump would not be trying to empty the basin because it could not.

It would be keeping my surface small.

When the basin filled, I would rise—not as a body rising, but as an attention spreading upward through connected wet places. The wall-clingers would climb because I had changed the chemistry of the water at their feet. The swimmers would flood the gutters because the lower channels had become dense with a pressure they could taste. The predators would take thresholds because the water there would be carrying more food, more warmth, more frightened little bodies.

The blue lights would brighten in response not because I commanded them, but because I was the condition under which their command became possible.

I would have no language for warning.

Only conductivity.

Only the brief alterations in salinity, heat, current, and mineral taste that pass from one creature to another. A flicker in the plated scavengers. A turning of whiskered heads. The tightening of a cluster on a warm drain mouth. The wall-clingers closing themselves into pebbles before the flood reaches them.

And once in a long while, when the basin was full enough for every channel, crack, pipe, puddle, and hidden chamber to join into one continuous body of water, I would be able to sense myself all at once.

Not much of myself. Only the reachable parts.

The entire low square of wet stone would become briefly lucid: amber light trembling on black water; pale swimmers making clouds below; the wall sweating into its own foundations; warm vapor flattening under the corrugated awnings; the old pump shuddering as if it had remembered a duty older than its manufacture.

Then every blue organ in every small plated body would answer.

Not to the lamp.

Not even to me.

To the fact that, for a few seconds, nothing in the basin was separate from anything else.
