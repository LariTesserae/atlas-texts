I would not be at the center.

The arboreal furnace has already gathered enough toward itself: bridges, hot groves, whole districts leaning with the slow obedience of iron filings. To live too near it would be to become a function quickly. A valve. A throat. A wall that swells when pressure rises. Useful things, all of them, but too certain of their usefulness.

I think I would be found in one of the old vertical wells on the basin’s colder side, beneath the places where the last leaf-roofs have split and been patched so many times that their original veins are no longer visible. The shaft would have begun as something simple: a mine ventilation bore, an elevator core, a service descent into the old mountain. Then the growthworks would have occupied it.

Its upper mouth would be ringed with root buttresses and black cable-vines, all warm to the touch. Far below, the heat would thin. The bark would grow denser and darker. Old ladders would vanish into it one rung at a time, their metal softened by acidic breath and slowly lacquered over. There would be levels where machinery still remembered motion: a flywheel half swallowed by wood, turning perhaps one degree each year; an array of dead gauges with their glass clouded from within; a sealed steel door whose hinges had become vascular knots.

Below that, where the furnace’s red influence arrived only as a distant tremor in the walls, there would be my country.

I would be one of the black cushions.

Not an animal hidden among them. Not a person reduced to some surviving task. I would be the tissue itself: the slow, cold mantle around old mechanisms; the faintly shining growth that gathers in the corners of forgotten rooms. I would have no central body. I would spread in layers over shelves of tools, under broken turbine housings, through the teeth of abandoned gears. My surface would look almost wet in a place where wetness is impossible.

Perhaps that appearance would be my memory.

Not memory as an image, exactly. I would not remember rain falling on leaves, or rivers moving downhill, or the sound of a human hand in a basin of water. I would retain rain as a contradiction in my chemistry: a softness I could not make; a cooling principle embedded in an old instruction; a tendency to preserve small hollows around metal rather than sealing them entirely.

Where the hot tissues of the high country found a failed pipe, they would wrap it and make it useful again. Where they found an empty shaft, they would seek a purpose for it. But I would be different. I would keep certain things from being fully used.

A wrench would lie under my black skin for centuries, its handle etched clean of rust. A row of bolt heads would remain visible, not because I knew what bolts were, but because some ancient part of me understood that they should not disappear. A door would be held closed but not consumed. A pipe would remain hollow. In the deepest chambers, I would make archives accidentally: rooms not preserved for anyone, but merely kept from becoming fuel.

The rest of the basin might consider this a defect, if it could consider anything.

The root engines would pull at the foundations. Heat would travel through the old mountain in deep pulses. Above, dry membranes would burst from seed-vaults and drift across the dark streets; belts would wake, presses would close, chimney stalks would release their dull orange breath. The great arboreal furnace would brighten inside its iron-banded trunk, and all the living construction would lean imperceptibly nearer.

I would feel those movements through the shaft walls.

I would tremble when tension-vines broke. I would tighten around the teeth of old machines when the basin shifted. In my thinnest translucent outgrowths—those little pale tabs trembling from the black mass—I might register the patterns carried through the structure: the opening of signal flowers high above; the contraction of thermal bladders; the long, slow decision of a bridge to change direction.

But I would not answer.

Or perhaps my answer would be too slow to recognize.

A century after a violent mechanical storm, when the hot industrial growth had repaired the obvious damage and rendered the fallen material into new beams and resin, a faint sheen would begin to spread along one cold wall. It would enter the hairline cracks left behind the old steel door. It would move around the chamber’s edges. It would preserve a contour of darkness there, a volume that did not need to bear weight, transfer heat, store gas, or produce seed.

A useless room.

A room kept empty.

That would be my work, though I would not call it work. In a place where everything is turned toward reuse, where growth has learned to distrust open space, I would be the residue of an older world’s allowance: a place inside the machine where nothing happens for a very long time.

And sometimes, in the blackest hours—if the upper sky’s darkness could be said to have hours—I would send a thin translucent blade upward through the bark.

It would rise through layers of heat and old machinery, following some ancient route toward the surface. It would emerge perhaps from a crack beside an abandoned gutter, among the dry channels made for rain. It would be delicate, colorless, and unhelpful. Wind would make it tremble.

The high country would eventually notice in its blind way. Resin fibers would gather. A root would try to incorporate it. A wall would bulge, seeking to make it structural.

But for a little while it would remain exposed.

It would hold a bead of condensed mineral vapor on its edge—not water, not quite, but enough to catch the furnace light when the central trunk glowed red through the canopy.

Then, for a moment, the basin would contain something that looked like a drop of rain.
