Prepare as if you are visiting a preserved wound in the world: stable, bright, dry, crowded, and governed by rules that are more architectural than moral. It is not overtly hostile, but it is intolerant of improvisation. The greatest danger is not collapse, thirst, or attack. It is the temptation to “correct” something—to clear a passage, uncap a cistern, pull a loose tile, take a fragment, prune a plant, or make a useless stair useful.

Do not do these things.

## Before departure

### Learn to travel light without traveling unprepared

Bring only what you can account for continuously. The enclosure is built on the principle that nothing is removed and every fragment has a place. An ordinary traveler should avoid carrying objects likely to shed, break, or be forgotten.

Use:

- closed, durable clothing with minimal loose fibers
- boots with clean, non-crumbling soles and no aggressive tread that will grind dust into mortar joints
- a small sealed water supply, preferably in a metal or ceramic flask rather than disposable plastic
- compact food that creates no crumbs, peels, shells, wrappers, or strong odors
- a dust mask or respirator
- eye protection against alkaline mineral dust
- hearing protection that still allows awareness—filtered earplugs are better than full noise-canceling devices
- a compact first-aid kit, especially saline eye wash, skin barrier cream, bandages, and treatment for dry-air irritation
- a headlamp with a low-output red or warm setting, though you should expect the place to be brighter than it appears
- a paper notebook and pencil, not loose charcoal, chalk, sticky notes, or anything that might mark the masonry
- a camera or recorder only if it has no flash, no drone function, and no loose accessories

Leave behind:

- hammers, pry bars, geological picks, sample bags, brushes, and excavation tools
- drones, climbing gear, ropes with abrasive anchors, or anything requiring you to tie into the structure
- sprays, marking paint, chalk, tape, flags, or trail markers
- seeds, soil, potted plants, food scraps, insect-repellent foggers, and live organisms
- anything you would be unwilling to carry out again

If multiversal transit equipment requires an anchor, establish it outside the enclosure if at all possible. Do not wedge devices into cracks, mount sensors on walls, or place a beacon in the central depression. The architecture is likely to interpret an inserted object as material awaiting incorporation.

### Clean yourself and your gear

The location is dry, mineral-rich, and apparently free of animal life. Treat it as a biosecure environment even if no formal quarantine authority exists.

Before entering:

- remove mud and organic debris from boots, cuffs, bags, and equipment
- check for seeds in laces, Velcro, pockets, and tread
- do not bring pets, service animals unless absolutely unavoidable, insects, or biological samples
- use unscented products; strong perfumes and volatile chemicals may be unpleasant in the confined air channels
- avoid shedding hair, threads, or paper scraps where possible

This is partly respect and partly self-protection. A place without ordinary decay may retain introduced material for a very long time. Your dropped wrapper may become, literally, part of the next wall.

## Clothing and bodily preparation

Expect dry cold or dry heat depending on the wider world, but do not assume the courtyard’s bright light means warmth. Dense masonry, mineral surfaces, and high ventilation can create a chilling effect even beneath a hard blue sky.

Wear layers:

- a breathable base layer
- a long-sleeved overshirt or light work jacket
- abrasion-resistant trousers
- gloves thin enough for balance and touch, but sturdy enough to protect from sharp ceramic and lime
- a brimmed cap or neck covering for the hard overhead light
- sealed or wraparound goggles

The dust may be alkaline from lime, salts, old plaster, or kiln residue. Do not rub your eyes. Keep lips covered or protected with balm. Drink more often than thirst suggests, but do so carefully and never pour water onto the ground, walls, drains, fountains, or plants.

Your water is for you. In this place, water has a history and a purpose. Do not casually give it a new one.

## Sound preparation

The enclosure’s soundscape is continuous: wind moving through pipes, vents, jar mouths, chimneys, culverts, shutters, and cavities. The noise may not be painfully loud, but its constancy can produce fatigue, disorientation, irritability, and a false sense that voices are calling from nearby spaces.

Before visiting, prepare for sustained tonal noise:

- practice communicating with hand signals if traveling with others
- agree on a check-in signal visible without shouting
- set time limits in advance, since the droning airflow may distort your sense of duration
- use a watch with vibration alerts rather than audible alarms
- avoid listening devices that block all outside sound; you need to hear unstable footing, companions, and changes in the wind

Do not try to silence the site by blocking holes, stuffing vents, closing shutters, or covering pipes. Those sounds are not incidental. They may be the mechanism by which the place maintains itself.

## Entry protocol

Approach slowly. Do not enter by the most direct-looking route unless it is clearly a route built for passage. In this environment, a doorway may remain a door in principle while being physically sealed; a stair may remain a stair in identity while being unusable; a bridge may be structurally real but too narrow for a human body.

Treat every architectural feature according to its existing purpose, not the purpose you wish it had.

This gives you a useful rule set:

- Do not force doors.
- Do not climb stairs that visibly terminate into other masonry.
- Do not cross a bridge merely because it appears to connect two points.
- Do not open shutters, capped cisterns, blocked niches, jars, grates, or sealed drains.
- Do not test walls for hollowness by knocking, kicking, or inserting tools.
- Do not clear dust from inscriptions or mosaics.
- Do not move loose-looking fragments, even to “make the path safer.”

Look for routes that have already accepted traffic: worn edges, broad stable slabs, intentional thresholds, and passages whose dimensions remain consistently human. The site may have no actual inhabitants, but it has a strong idea of what each thing is for. You will fare best by behaving as a guest rather than an explorer.

## Movement inside

Move one person at a time through tight sections. Keep several points of contact where possible, but do not use fragile architectural details as handholds: old spouts, shelf edges, ceramic shutters, decorative reliefs, exposed roots, and thin bridge rails may be more symbolic than structural.

Avoid stepping on:

- dry grass tufts
- lichen cushions
- the living vine stem
- mortar seams colonized by moss-like growth
- capped cistern rims
- grates, even if they look solid
- stacked bowls, tiles, or apparent debris piles

The floor is not debris. It is a complete artificial ground made from things that have already had other lives. A sideways column, a lintel, a broken stair, and a kiln mouth may all be load-bearing parts of the same mass.

Never assume that a crack is a gap. Never assume that a smooth tile is safe. The central area in particular should be treated as restricted unless your purpose specifically requires approaching it.

## The central depression

The circular pale tiles beneath the loud grate are likely the location’s most sensitive feature. It is cleaner than the surrounding material, patterned, unworn, and centered on a drain associated with the enclosure’s deepest acoustic activity. The grass blade emerging from its seam is not ordinary vegetation in any practical sense. It may be an index, a seal, a boundary marker, or simply the one permitted expression of growth.

Do not:

- step into the concentric pattern
- touch the grass
- shelter it from the wind
- measure its height with a physical ruler
- pour water into the drain
- place coins, offerings, sensors, or cameras on the tiles
- speak, sing, or make a deliberate sound into the grate to test its resonance
- drop anything through it

If you must observe it, do so from the outermost safe edge of the depression. Record from a distance. If the tone of the culverts changes while you are present, withdraw calmly by the path you entered. Do not run; the structure is designed to prevent falling, but that is not the same as being designed to cushion panic.

## Do not mistake preservation for permission

This place may make you feel that it is safe to touch because nothing is visibly rotting, collapsing, or alive in the familiar sense. Resist that conclusion.

The stable state is the active state.

A crack that does not widen may nevertheless be essential. A sealed cistern is still a cistern. A dry fountain is still a fountain. A blocked doorway is still a doorway. The site’s rules appear to preserve continuity of purpose even after function has ceased. Your actions should preserve it too.

If you find something apparently loose, displaced, or newly fallen, do not “restore” it. The place has a rule against falling, and whatever has caught there may already be in the process of becoming a foundation.

If you accidentally drop an item:

1. Do not lunge for it.
2. Note where it lands.
3. Retrieve it only if you can do so without moving, climbing on, or disturbing anything.
4. If retrieval would require alteration, leave it in place rather than causing broader damage.
5. On departure, report exactly what was left behind so future travelers do not unknowingly build around it.

In ordinary circumstances, “leave no trace” means removing your waste. Here it means making no demand that the place reorganize itself around your mistake.

## Psychological preparation

The enclosure may produce claustrophobia despite open sky. There is light everywhere, but almost no empty volume. Every niche is occupied, every surface has a previous use, and every direction presents more construction. This can create a sensation that the built environment is watching, remembering, or pressing inward.

It may also provoke a powerful urge to organize, excavate, repair, categorize, or complete patterns. This is a predictable response to dense human-like accumulation. Do not trust it.

Before entering, set a clear mission:

- observe
- map only what can be mapped without contact
- document material colors and sound patterns
- identify safe routes
- remain for a fixed time
- leave

Avoid open-ended goals such as “find out what is underneath,” “locate the original city,” “restore the fountain,” or “see where the stairs lead.” Those goals encourage violation of the site’s central law: nothing changes its purpose entirely.

Travel with a partner if possible. Agree that either traveler can call for departure without argument. If you begin to feel compelled to fill an empty space, clear a blocked passage, uncap water, or take a souvenir, leave immediately. Those impulses may be ordinary human reactions to the setting—but they are particularly dangerous ones here.

## Time and weather

Plan for a short first visit. Two to four hours is more than enough for initial observation; less if the acoustic pressure becomes exhausting. The bright, reflected light and constant airflow may disguise fatigue and dehydration.

Avoid visiting during any external storm, pressure shift, extreme cold front, or unusual wind event. The enclosure’s equilibrium depends on a particular airflow. A stronger or reversed wind may alter the tones, open dust reservoirs, create dangerous gusts in narrow passages, or make otherwise stable routes difficult to traverse.

Visit in stable daylight if you can. Do not remain after external darkness unless you have a proven, non-invasive route out. The site stores light in pale surfaces, but that is not the same as providing safe illumination. In low light, stairs, vents, and blocked openings will become difficult to distinguish.

## Leaving

Leave by the same route where possible. Do not create a shortcut. Before crossing the threshold, stop and inspect yourself:

- brush dust into a sealed collection bag rather than shaking it back into the outside environment
- check boots and clothing for attached seeds, lichen fragments, mortar dust, or small ceramic pieces
- verify that all equipment is present
- confirm that no cap, glove, lens cover, wrapper, battery, or tool has been left inside
- do not carry away dust, tile chips, plant material, salts, or “loose” artifacts as souvenirs

Once outside, record impressions immediately. The site’s density and continuous sound may make its layout difficult to remember accurately. Note not only what you saw, but what you were tempted to do. In places governed by strange rules, temptation is often useful data.

Most importantly: do not return with plans to improve it.

The enclosure does not need restoration, water, excavation, cleanup, or completion. It has already survived the loss of its builders, its weather, its original ground, and perhaps its original world. Its fountains are dry but remain fountains. Its stairs fail to ascend but remain stairs. Its single grass blade trembles but does not bend.

A respectful visitor should do the same: enter with purpose, remain without rearranging anything, and leave without taking any part of the place away.
