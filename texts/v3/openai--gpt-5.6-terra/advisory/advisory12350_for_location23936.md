## Before You Go: Adopt the Right Idea of the Place

Prepare for the Underwork as you would for a very old, crowded, functional home—not as a ruin, sewer, dungeon, curiosity, or “primitive” settlement.

Everything there is in use, or may become useful. A loose brick, a cracked pot, an apparently empty niche, a rope stretched across a corridor, a heap of damp reeds: all may belong to someone or serve a structural, agricultural, drainage, or emergency purpose. The Underwork has survived precisely because its residents notice and reuse things that visitors might overlook.

You will be indoors, underground, damp, surrounded by people and animals, and rarely alone. The physical constraints will matter, but your ability to be a tolerable guest will matter more.

If you are traveling between worlds, do not arrive with the manner of an explorer claiming discovery. Arrive as someone seeking shelter and willing to be taught where to put their feet.

## What to Wear

### Clothing

Wear clothes that can get wet, smoky, greasy, and brushed against rough surfaces.

Best choices:

- Durable trousers or a knee-length skirt with leggings underneath
- A long-sleeved overshirt or work shirt that can protect your arms from brick, splinters, rope burns, and soot
- Layers rather than one heavy garment; the Underwork is cool in upper passages, warm near kitchens and boiler alleys, and clammy almost everywhere
- Fabrics that still insulate when damp: wool, treated plant fibers, modern synthetics if they do not look offensively alien in your travel context
- Clothing without trailing hems, loose tassels, dangling cords, or broad sleeves

Avoid:

- Fine clothing that cannot be scrubbed
- Long coats
- Open, flowing robes
- Expensive footwear you will mourn
- Anything that snags easily on wicker, hooks, protruding nails, and rough brick
- Strongly perfumed fabric; enclosed communities notice unfamiliar smells immediately, and perfume may bother people, animals, or those with smoke-sensitive lungs

Bring an extra pair of socks or foot wraps in a sealed bag. Damp feet will become your most persistent discomfort and, after enough time, a medical problem.

### Footwear

Wear sturdy, closed-toe shoes with:

- Good traction on wet tile, algae-dark brick, and slick clay
- Soles thick enough for broken pottery, bone fragments, dropped hooks, and the occasional rat
- Enough flexibility for ladders and narrow stairs
- A secure fastening; untied laces and loose sandals are hazards around drains, animals, and moving crowds

Waterproof boots are useful only if they remain breathable. Fully sealed boots may become sweat traps in warm passages. Ideally, bring hard-wearing water-resistant work boots and dry socks rather than relying on perfect waterproofing.

Do not assume floor water is clean. It may be runoff, washwater, fish-channel water, animal water, or something worse.

### Head and Hands

A compact soft cap, scarf, or bandanna is valuable. It protects your hair from ceiling drips, soot, dust, hanging ropes, and the occasional hen-related incident. It can also serve as a smoke filter or improvised cloth wrap.

Bring light work gloves, preferably with a grippy palm. You may need them for ladders, ropes, hot pots, wet brick, splintered timber, and helping move baskets. Do not wear bulky gloves all the time; dexterity is important in a place where one often has to squeeze past people without knocking things over.

## What to Carry

Pack lightly. Space is communal, passages are narrow, and every object becomes another thing someone must avoid, store, repair, or ask about.

A small, body-close pack is better than a suitcase, rolling luggage, rigid frame pack, or anything that projects behind you.

### Essential Kit

Carry:

- A personal water container with a tight lid  
  - Mark it clearly. Shared water systems have rules, and you do not want your bottle confused with a communal vessel.
- A compact water-treatment method  
  - A filter plus purification tablets is ideal. Do not visibly reject offered water in a rude manner; learn which sources are considered drinking water, and treat privately when appropriate.
- A small headlamp with a red-light mode, if culturally acceptable  
  - Use it sparingly. Broad white light can disrupt sleepers, swallows, and people accustomed to lamp-lit darkness.
- Backup batteries or an alternative nonflammable light source  
  - Do **not** assume you may use candles, open flame, or even an unfamiliar lantern.
- A simple cloth face covering or proper respirator  
  - Useful around charcoal smoke, mold, dust, cooking fumes, and flood cleanup. A good folding particulate mask is worth carrying even if you dislike wearing it.
- Small first-aid supplies  
  - Adhesive bandages, antiseptic, blister care, gauze, tweezers, anti-diarrheal medicine, oral rehydration salts, antihistamine, and any required prescriptions.
- Hand-cleaning supplies  
  - Soap sheets, a small sealed bottle of sanitizer, and a clean cloth. Use them discreetly where possible; water may be limited or routed according to strict rules.
- A compact towel or highly absorbent cloth
- Earplugs  
  - The Underwork is continuously loud in small ways. Earplugs may be the difference between sleep and exhaustion.
- A notebook and pencil  
  - Pencil works in damp conditions and lets you copy local rules, names, routes, and signals. Do not count on a phone.
- A little cord, safety pins, and a sewing needle  
  - Repairs are respected. Being able to mend your own clothing prevents you from adding to the settlement’s workload.
- A sealable bag for wet clothing and waste  
  - Never casually discard wrappers, batteries, packaging, or broken equipment.

### Useful Gifts

Bring useful, modest gifts rather than flashy valuables.

Good options include:

- High-quality needles
- Fishhooks, if appropriate to local technology and custom
- Clean, sturdy cordage
- Replacement lamp wicks
- Small hand tools of familiar, repairable design
- Blunt-tipped scissors
- Waterproof cloth patches
- Durable buttons, buckles, and simple fasteners
- Salt, spices, tea, or dried fruit, if you can verify they are safe and culturally acceptable
- Plain soap
- Sealed medical supplies, offered carefully rather than imposed
- A hand-crank flashlight or mechanical light source, if it is robust and not too alien-looking

Avoid gifts that create dependency, waste, envy, or disposal problems:

- Disposable plastic novelties
- Batteries without a reliable plan for safe disposal
- Fragile glass items
- Weapons
- Highly intoxicating substances
- Exotic seeds, live organisms, soil, or foods that could introduce disease or invasive species
- Devices with surveillance, recording, or communications capabilities unless explicitly discussed and welcomed

In a place where nothing is discarded whole, every object you introduce may persist for generations. Pack with that responsibility in mind.

## Health Preparation

### Vaccinations and Basic Medical Readiness

For an ordinary human traveler, assume elevated risk from close living quarters, animal contact, dampness, smoke exposure, minor injuries, and contaminated water.

Before travel, ensure standard vaccinations are current, especially those relevant to:

- Tetanus
- Hepatitis A
- Hepatitis B
- Typhoid, depending on sanitation and food conditions
- Influenza
- COVID or analogous respiratory protection where relevant
- Rabies pre-exposure vaccination if you expect prolonged contact with cats, rats, bats, or unfamiliar mammals and cannot count on prompt post-exposure treatment

Bring enough prescription medication for longer than planned. Dampness, delays, floods, political restrictions at the surface, and multiversal travel complications all make resupply uncertain. Keep medicine in watertight packaging on your person.

### Respiratory Concerns

The Underwork contains oil-lamp smoke, charcoal smoke, cooking smoke, mold spores, dust, animal dander, and humid air that carries all of it.

If you have asthma, chronic obstructive lung disease, severe allergies, or smoke sensitivity, prepare seriously:

- Carry prescribed inhalers and a backup
- Bring a compact particulate mask
- Learn to recognize symptoms of carbon monoxide exposure: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, unusual sleepiness
- Do not sleep in lower rooms near braziers or enclosed cooking spaces unless local residents confirm ventilation is safe
- Take the swallows seriously: if they vanish early or the residents begin extinguishing fires, follow their lead immediately

A visitor who says, “It’s probably fine,” while locals are worried about air quality is not demonstrating courage. They are being a burden.

### Water and Food

Do not drink from channels, basin water, animal troughs, runoff streams, or any container whose use you do not understand. The basin may be socially and practically central, but that does not make it potable.

Ask directly and respectfully:

> “Which water is safe for a newcomer to drink?”

Use your own container. Do not dip it into communal stores without permission.

Eat cooked food while it is hot. Favor foods that have been boiled, roasted, or freshly baked: stews, oven bread, cooked roots, cooked fish, hot grain mash. Be cautious with raw herbs, unpeeled produce from the surface, unboiled milk, and anything washed in an uncertain channel.

Do not insult food offered in hospitality. If you need to refuse, blame your own body rather than their food:

> “My stomach is not trained for this yet. May I take only a little?”

That will generally be understood better than announcing that something seems unsafe.

### Skin and Feet

Wash and dry small cuts promptly. In a warm, damp environment, minor injuries can become infected quickly.

Check daily for:

- Blisters
- Cracked skin between toes
- Rashes under waistbands and straps
- Insect bites
- Fungal irritation
- Cuts acquired from brick, wood, wire, bone, or pottery

Do not wade through unknown water with open wounds.

## Learn the Immediate Rules Before You Move Around

On arrival, ask someone to teach you:

1. **Which paths are safe to walk**
2. **Which drains, channels, and thresholds must not be blocked**
3. **Where visitors may sleep**
4. **Where you may wash**
5. **Which water is for drinking, food, animals, washing, and runoff**
6. **What the local flood signal sounds like**
7. **What to do if the bell rings**
8. **Where the fire exits and upper routes are**
9. **Which rooms are private, sacred, structurally fragile, or reserved**
10. **How to handle animals—or, more often, how not to handle them**

Memorize the path from your sleeping place to the basin hall and to at least one higher, drier route. In a flood, smoke event, or tremor, you will not have time to orient yourself from first principles.

If possible, walk those routes once in daylight shaft-light and once after lamps are lit. The Underwork changes character after dark.

## Drain Etiquette Is Survival Etiquette

The rule against blocking drains is not fussy housekeeping. The entire settlement depends on water having somewhere to go.

Never:

- Set bags, buckets, boots, bedding, or stools in a drainage groove
- Stop in a narrow passage where water is already running
- Wash clothing, dishes, food, hands, or equipment in a channel without being told it is acceptable
- Drop crumbs, scraps, wrappers, hair, string, wax, or debris into a drain
- Leave a door, gate, or shutter in an altered position after passing through
- “Improve” water flow with your own improvisation
- Move a gutter, cup, spout, or overflow barrier because it seems inconvenient

If you spill something, tell someone. If you see a drain clogging, do not poke at it blindly; alert a local. A blocked channel can mean a wet floor. It can also mean contaminated food stores, a flooded sleeping platform, damaged mortar, drowned animals, or pressure where the structure cannot afford it.

## Fire, Light, and Air

Fire is indispensable and dangerous in the Underwork.

Do not light anything without permission. That includes:

- Candles
- Cigarettes
- Lighters
- Cooking stoves
- Incense
- Alcohol burners
- Sparking electrical equipment
- Battery packs that may overheat

Keep any personal electronic equipment away from water, animal traffic, and heat. If you must recharge something through unfamiliar technology, treat it as a favor requiring supervision, not a casual entitlement.

Do not place lamps on the floor. Do not hang unfamiliar lamps from existing ropes or hooks without asking. Do not assume a beam can hold your weight or your gear merely because it has held something else for decades.

Pay attention to the tunnel swallows and to the reactions of residents. The animals are part of the environmental monitoring system, whether everyone describes them that way or not.

If smoke thickens, your eyes burn, people begin opening vents, or the swallows retreat:

- Put out any flame you control
- Gather only what is already in your hands
- Follow people toward higher air or designated vents
- Do not linger to collect equipment
- Do not obstruct passages with questions

## Animals: Treat Them as Residents, Not Attractions

The goats, hens, ducks, cats, rats, swallows, and pale fish are not scenery.

### Goats

Goats will eat your belongings. Keep straps tucked, packs closed, paper secured, and food inaccessible. Do not feed them unless invited. A goat that learns visitors carry snacks becomes everybody’s problem.

### Ducks and Hens

Watch your step around them, especially near the basin. Do not chase them from a passage unless you know how the residents do so. They may be nesting, guarding young, or simply likely to cause a cascade of feathers, shouting, and dropped goods.

### Cats and Rats

Do not assume cats are friendly, clean, or owned by any one household. Do not touch rats. Avoid leaving food exposed. If scratched or bitten by a cat or other mammal, clean the wound immediately and seek medical advice as soon as possible.

### Tunnel Swallows

Never touch nests, clay tubes, eggs, or young birds. Do not shine bright lights directly into their nesting areas. They control insects and provide weather and air-quality signals; harming or disturbing them is likely to be taken seriously.

### Pale Fish

Do not interfere with fish traps, channels, or ritual releases. Whether the practice is practical, ceremonial, or both is not your question to settle.

## Personal Space and Privacy

The Underwork is crowded. Privacy is created socially, not spatially.

You may sleep close to strangers, pass people at arm’s length, hear domestic arguments, smell meals, overhear repairs, and find children or animals underneath your feet. This does not mean everything is communal in the sense of being available to you.

Do not:

- Open jars, cupboards, baskets, shutters, hanging bundles, or cloth partitions
- Sit on a platform without asking
- Lean on walls, posts, or shelves
- Handle tools because they look interesting
- Photograph, record, map, sample, or scan anything without clear permission
- Ask intrusive questions about hidden routes, surface access, stores, defenses, or patrols
- Assume that a “public” corridor is a public conversation

Ask before sitting. Ask before taking a shortcut. Ask before hanging anything. Ask before observing a repair closely.

A useful phrase is:

> “Where may I stand without being in the way?”

It will earn you more trust than almost any declaration of goodwill.

## Do Not Underestimate the Social Meaning of Repair

The Underwork’s deepest principle is that nothing is discarded whole. You do not need to imitate this perfectly, but you should respect it.

Before throwing anything away, ask:

> “Can this be used here?”

Before replacing something, ask whether it can be repaired.

If you break an object, report it immediately and offer labor, materials, or fair compensation. Do not hide damage out of embarrassment. Concealed damage is much worse in a settlement where a cracked cup could have been a water measure, a broken hook could have been a load-bearing tie, and a missing sherd could have been part of a drain repair.

If you have useful skills—mending, sewing, carpentry, basic plumbing, cooking, first aid, animal care, tool sharpening, bookkeeping, translation—offer them humbly. Do not begin repairing structural elements unless specifically invited. The apparent patchwork may encode generations of hard-earned knowledge.

A visitor is most welcome when they can carry a bucket, hold a lamp, sort salvage, clean vegetables where instructed, mend their own gear, and listen.

## Flood, Fire, Tremor, and Surface Patrol Procedures

### When the Bell Rings

The cracked bronze-pot bell signals serious events: flood, fire, surface patrol, or dangerous births. You may not be able to distinguish the reason immediately.

When it rings:

1. Stop what you are doing.
2. Do not ask for a full explanation in the middle of movement.
3. Pick up your essential pouch only if it is within arm’s reach.
4. Follow local people.
5. Keep passage centers clear.
6. Do not attempt to collect large luggage.
7. Do not interfere with animals being moved.
8. Do not stand at the basin to watch.
9. Carry a bucket only if someone hands you one or tells you where to help.
10. Remain with your assigned household or guide until the all-clear.

### Flood

Rain arrives audibly before it arrives physically. Learn the local rain routes. A “north rain” may make one staircase hazardous; a western rain may change access around animal areas.

If water begins rising:

- Move upward and toward known dry walkways
- Keep your pack above waist height
- Avoid stepping into opaque brown water
- Do not cross rushing narrow channels; shallow water in a confined space can knock you down or sweep objects underfoot
- Do not use electrical devices near floodwater
- Expect contaminated water after runoff events
- Help only where directed; uncontrolled good intentions can block critical movement

### Fire or Smoke

In an underground place, smoke may be more dangerous than flame. Stay low only if locals advise it; some drainage channels may carry worse air. Follow known ventilation routes, not instinct.

### Tremors

When the ground shudders, do not run blindly. The Underwork is designed to flex, and panic can cause more injury than the tremor itself.

Move away from:

- Hanging loads
- Tall jar stacks
- Old bulging walls marked with red clay
- Kiln areas
- Unsecured shelves
- Large overhead baskets or drying frames

If residents stop and wait, stop and wait. Watch what they watch. Afterward, do not touch newly cracked walls or fallen debris; structural checks will be needed.

### Surface Patrols

The mention of surface patrols suggests that going above may be politically or physically dangerous. Do not ask for surface routes casually, and never reveal them to outsiders, record them, or discuss them in unsecured places.

If you are from another world and visibly unusual, your presence may create risk. Have a concealment plan before arrival:

- Clothing that does not attract attention
- No conspicuous technology
- No glowing devices, loud alerts, or unfamiliar scents
- A plausible role: relative, worker, trader, patient, helper—whatever the hosts agree is safest
- A clear understanding of whether you should hide, remain silent, move, or leave during a patrol alert

Your safety is not worth exposing the entire settlement.

## Sleeping There

Expect interrupted sleep.

There will be drips, feet, animal noises, late repairs, water movement, murmured conversations, coughing, stove sounds, distant hammering, and the uncanny effect of sound seeming to come from the wrong direction.

Bring:

- Earplugs
- A sleep mask, if you use one
- A warm layer
- A ground cloth or clean sleeping liner, if space permits
- Something to protect valuables close to your body

Do not demand silence. Do not complain about animals unless an actual health or safety issue is involved. Do not spread bedding across routes, drains, work spaces, or access ladders.

If offered a sleeping platform, ask which edge is safe to use and whether the platform belongs to a household, a shared shift of workers, or a temporarily absent person.

Keep your possessions small and bundled. A single loose item can travel surprisingly far in a settlement built on slopes and channels.

## Mind the Height, Width, and Direction of Your Body

The Underwork will train your posture.

Duck before you think you need to. Turn sideways for people carrying baskets. Keep elbows close. Do not swing bags around. Look up before standing from a crouch. Look down before backing up.

You will encounter:

- Low beams
- Uneven stairs
- Damp tile
- Rope lines at face level
- Hanging tools
- Open drain runs
- Loose animal traffic
- Invisible changes in floor slope
- Steam that obscures footing
- Corridors where two people cannot pass without coordination

Move at the pace of those who live there. Rushing reads as dangerous because it usually is.

If someone says, “Wait,” wait—even if the reason is merely a goat, a duck line, a water bucket, or a person negotiating a ladder. In a compressed environment, small movements require cooperation.

## Language and Conversation

Because sound bends strangely through the Underwork, lower your voice. Do not assume a conversation is private because no one is visibly nearby.

Avoid shouting names, secrets, travel details, or political information across corridors. If you must call to someone, use the local practice.

Be careful with questions about:

- Why people live underground
- Whether they are hiding
- Who controls the surface
- How old the place is
- Why a family has certain animals or spaces
- Deaths, bad births, sickness, missing people, or flood losses
- Whether beliefs about fish, molds, swallows, or river objects are “true”

Listen first. In a place built from accumulated necessity, superstition and engineering may be tightly braided together. A practice does not need to fit your categories to deserve respect.

## Hygiene Without Offense

The Underwork’s sanitation system will be local, practical, and probably more differentiated than it first appears. Learn it.

Find out:

- Where waste goes
- Where washing happens
- Whether soap use is limited
- Which areas require clean hands
- How menstrual materials, diapers, bandages, and medical waste are handled
- Where boots or wet clothes may be dried
- Whether outsider clothing needs isolation because of pests or surface contaminants

Never assume a drain is a latrine, a basin edge is a washroom, or an empty-looking channel is a place to pour something.

If you have diarrhea, vomiting, fever, a persistent cough, a suspicious rash, lice, or a wound infection, disclose it early to whoever manages lodging or health. Concealing illness in a dense settlement is not politeness.

## Technology and Multiversal Travel Equipment

Your equipment may be more dangerous socially than physically.

Do not expose technology that:

- Records people without consent
- Makes noise or light unexpectedly
- Requires unfamiliar fuel
- Produces heat
- Leaks chemicals
- Cannot be repaired locally
- Could be mistaken for a weapon, surveillance tool, curse object, or valuable treasure
- Leaves behind hard-to-dispose-of waste

Keep portal tools, navigational devices, medical scanners, and communications gear concealed unless your hosts have agreed otherwise. If you must use them, choose an area away from animals, sleeping spaces, grain storage, open water, and delicate infrastructure.

Do not open a portal inside the Underwork unless you are absolutely certain it will not:

- Change air pressure
- Pull in dust or water
- Produce noise, heat, ozone, light, or electromagnetic effects
- Attract surface attention
- Disturb structural materials
- Frighten animals
- Create an exit route others may be blamed for knowing

The sensible default is to conduct all dimensional transit well away from the hill, using a prearranged surface rendezvous or remote site.

## A Good First Day

A wise first day in the Underwork would look something like this:

- Arrive with only what you can carry close to your body.
- Be introduced by someone trusted, if at all possible.
- Ask where to put your bag.
- Learn the water, drain, fire, and bell rules.
- Eat a small amount of cooked food if offered.
- Help with one simple task.
- Learn the route to your sleeping place, the basin, a latrine or wash area, and a higher emergency route.
- Keep out of work paths.
- Watch how people pass one another, how they move animals, and how they handle shared tools.
- Retire early enough that you are not stumbling through the most crowded evening period.
- Do not explore alone at night.

The first thing you should contribute is not expertise. It is predictability.

## The Attitude That Will Keep You Safest

The Underwork is dense because density is how it endures. Its inhabitants have made room for water, smoke, animals, food, waste, repair, birth, sleep, trade, and fear—all inside the same folded structure. It is not spacious, but it is deeply organized.

Your preparation should therefore be practical and moral:

- Carry little.
- Waste nothing.
- Block nothing.
- Burn nothing without permission.
- Assume every route has a purpose.
- Assume every animal has a role.
- Treat every repair as knowledge made visible.
- Follow local emergency behavior immediately.
- Keep illness, contamination, and outside danger from becoming communal burdens.
- Offer labor before opinions.
- Leave the place no harder to maintain than when you entered.

If you can do that, the Underwork may not feel comfortable in the ordinary sense. But it may become legible to you: not a dark place beneath a hill, but a patient, crowded machine for keeping people alive.
