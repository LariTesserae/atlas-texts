## Preparation Brief: The Newly Raised Shelf

This is not a wilderness hike in the ordinary sense. It is an active geological construction site, a high-altitude wetland, an experimental ecosystem, and possibly the exposed working surface of a buried machine intelligence. Treat it as all four at once.

The shelf is small enough to cross in a day, but do not let its size mislead you. Its terrain is unstable, its edges are severe, its weather is constant, and many of its rules appear designed to preserve the integrity of the forming landscape. A competent human traveler should plan for a slow, quiet, supervised-style visit even if no supervisor is visibly present.

### Primary risks

- **Unfinished ground:** Rock may soften, flex, crack, vent steam, or become porous without warning.
- **Extreme exposure:** The shelf is surrounded by steep, wet mountain walls and drop-offs above cloud.
- **Constant wetness:** Rain, condensation, warm pools, mist, and slick mineral surfaces create a persistent slipping and hypothermia risk despite the relatively warm air.
- **Acoustic effects:** Sound travels unusually well and may return from underground after delay. Loud noise may disturb animals, trigger an unknown response, or create dangerous confusion.
- **Unclear biological hazards:** The ecosystem is young, engineered or altered, and not necessarily compatible with human skin, lungs, food, or microbiology.
- **Active machinery:** The terrain is being assembled. Structures and natural-looking features may be components of a process rather than safe landscape.
- **Nonstandard physical behavior:** Fire fails; carried water dissipates beyond the boundary; removed stone softens and warms. Expect other materials and ordinary assumptions to behave strangely.

## Before You Go

### Do not go alone

Travel with at least one other person, ideally three. A solo fall, sudden sink, steam injury, or disorientation event could become fatal quickly. Maintain visual contact whenever possible; fog, steam, and folded terrain can hide someone at very short distances.

If your multiverse travel method allows it, establish a **hard extraction window** before arrival. Your return route should not depend on lighting a signal fire, throwing a radio beacon over distance, or transporting a physical token collected from the shelf.

### Tell someone exactly where and when you are going

Leave a route and return plan with an off-site contact. Include:

- arrival coordinates or access method;
- expected duration;
- intended exploration area;
- a mandatory check-in time;
- criteria for immediate extraction;
- the fact that ordinary rescue access may be impossible.

Assume satellite navigation, magnetic compasses, radios, and drone aircraft may be impaired by the buried engine, unusual gravitic fields, cloud cover, mineral interference, or the shelf’s ongoing growth.

### Obtain permission if permission is possible

The handrails, shelters, instruments, arches, and careful weather management indicate that someone—or something—maintains the place. It may not be abandoned merely because it appears uninhabited.

If there is a local authority, research team, machine custodian, mountain settlement, or associated transit protocol, contact them first. Ask specifically about:

- safe paths and restricted zones;
- basin depth and steam vent schedules;
- animal interaction rules;
- whether the shelters are for visitors;
- acceptable communication volume;
- permitted sampling procedures, if any;
- conditions under which the shelf evacuates, shifts, or seals itself.

If no authority can be identified, behave as if you are entering an active laboratory with no staff on duty.

## Clothing and Personal Equipment

### Wear for wet footing, not for cold alone

The air may feel warm, but you will be continually wet. Choose layers that retain insulation when damp.

Bring:

- lightweight waterproof shell with a quiet fabric;
- synthetic or wool base layers, never cotton;
- warm midlayer packed in a dry bag;
- waterproof gloves with tactile grip;
- spare socks in sealed bags;
- close-fitting hat or hood;
- eye protection for hard rain, mineral spray, and steam;
- face covering or respirator suitable for fine mineral aerosols and unknown spores.

Avoid loose ponchos, jangling gear, or equipment with many dangling straps. They can snag on mineral petals, handrails, glassy reeds, and sharp folded stone.

### Footwear is your most important equipment

Use high-traction, ankle-supporting boots with soles designed for wet rock. Bring removable traction devices only if they do not use sharp spikes.

Do **not** rely on crampons, pitons, stakes, or aggressive climbing hardware unless local guidance explicitly permits them. The ground may be soft beneath a hard crust, chemically reactive, or structurally important. Piercing it could be dangerous to you and disruptive to the shelf.

A trekking pole can be useful for testing footing, but use a blunt rubber tip. Probe gently. Do not drive it into cracks, pools, vents, seams, or living mineral growth.

### Pack for self-containment

Carry all food, water, waste storage, and medical supplies you expect to need. Nothing should be left behind.

Recommended equipment:

- sealed drinking water and electrolyte solution;
- compact high-calorie food that produces minimal crumbs;
- waterproof first-aid kit;
- blister care;
- antiseptic wipes and sterile saline;
- burn dressings for hot vapor or warm mineral contact;
- emergency thermal wrap;
- compact climbing/rescue line for human recovery only;
- whistle only for genuine emergencies;
- waterproof headlamp with red-light mode;
- spare batteries kept warm and dry;
- paper notes and pencil as backup to electronics;
- dry bags for all electronics and clothing;
- compact waste bags, including toilet waste bags.

Do not assume local water is safe. Even if it looks clear and warm, it may carry engineered microbes, dissolved minerals, chemical instructions, or living systems integral to the site. Filtered does not necessarily mean safe. Bring your own water.

## What Not to Bring

Do not bring:

- open-flame tools;
- camp stoves that depend on exposed flame;
- fireworks, flares, or incendiaries;
- drones unless authorized;
- loudspeakers;
- firearms or explosive deterrents;
- sampling hammers, chisels, core drills, or specimen jars;
- fishing gear, traps, nets, or animal bait;
- detergents, soaps, pesticides, or chemical insect repellents likely to wash into pools;
- loose paper, glitter, confetti, or anything likely to become litter;
- pets.

The place explicitly resists removal of stones and water. Treat that not as an inconvenience but as a boundary statement. Do not test it by repeatedly taking objects across the edge. The shelf may be able to enforce its rules gently; that does not mean it has no stronger response available.

## Arrival Procedure

When you arrive, do not immediately begin walking.

Spend ten to twenty minutes at the boundary or designated landing point observing:

1. **Ground behavior:** Are seams moving? Is vapor emerging? Are pools rising or draining?
2. **Weather cycle:** Note the timing and intensity of showers, fog, and wind.
3. **Animal patterns:** Watch where grazers avoid, where birds gather, and what burrowers do before activity changes.
4. **Structural signals:** Observe the arches, tower, shelters, and any lights or pulses. Do not touch them at first.
5. **Soundscape:** Learn the difference between normal settling noises and sudden cracks, booms, ringing, or mechanical changes.

The animals may be better early-warning instruments than your equipment. If multiple species suddenly become still, raise their sensing wings, retreat from an area, or gather in a particular basin, stop moving and determine what they are responding to.

## Moving Across the Shelf

### Walk slowly and assume straight routes do not exist

The terrain is likely to change character every few dozen meters. A shallow reflective film may conceal a warm pool, a porous crust, a sudden mineral lip, or a crack.

Use the following habits:

- Keep at least several body lengths from drop-offs.
- Never walk along an edge merely for a better view.
- Avoid crossing translucent blue seams.
- Avoid steam vents and warm hollows unless a marked path crosses them.
- Step on broad, dull, stable-looking ribs rather than glossy wet surfaces.
- Test suspicious terrain before committing weight.
- Keep your group spaced far enough apart that one collapse does not take everyone down, but close enough for immediate assistance.
- Turn around early rather than forcing a route through changing ground.

If you find a handrail or formal path, use it. Even if it appears to lead to blank stone, it may mark stable substrate, former access routes, emergency staging areas, or boundaries that are meaningful to the terrain-making system.

Do not assume an unmarked route is safe merely because animals use it. Small creatures may tolerate heat, chemicals, crust instability, or biological contact that humans cannot.

### Do not stand still for long without intent

The shelf’s peculiar footprint rule matters. If remaining stationary long enough causes the ground to “recognize” your weight, that may be harmless—but it may also be a form of registration.

Avoid prolonged stillness in untested areas, especially near:

- blue-lit seams;
- arches;
- structures;
- vents;
- central basins;
- fresh pale mineral foam.

If you need to rest, use an existing shelter, bench, stable marked platform, or a broad inert-looking rock area away from active features. If the ground begins to warm beneath you, soften, pulse with blue light, or alter its texture around your boots, move away calmly and without running.

## Sound Discipline

Sound is not private here.

A dropped item may be heard across the shelf. A spoken phrase may return altered from below minutes later. That creates practical and psychological hazards.

### Keep your voice low

Use short, calm speech. Avoid shouting except for immediate safety emergencies. Do not sing, chant, test echoes, play recorded sounds, or conduct “acoustic experiments.” If the stone is processing and repeating sound, it may be using vibration as input—or it may simply be mechanically resonant. You do not need to determine which during a visit.

### Establish nonverbal signals with your group

Before arrival, agree on hand signals for:

- stop;
- retreat;
- unstable ground;
- animal alert;
- injury;
- silence;
- extraction.

If you must use a whistle, use a prearranged pattern only. Be aware it may carry farther than intended and return later, confusing your team.

### If your words come back

Do not automatically answer an echo. Wait and listen. Note the delay, pitch change, direction, and whether the returned sound contains only your words or anything additional.

If an apparent reply is coherent, personalized, or seems to issue instructions, record it if safe—but do not obey dangerous directions solely because they emerge from beneath the shelf. Retreat to a known stable area and consult whatever off-site support you have.

## Water, Pools, and Rain

Rain is probably part of the shelf’s operating cycle. It is collected, warmed, chemically sorted, and returned to the air. This means every puddle may be part of a controlled circulation system.

### Treat all local water as non-potable and ecologically active

Do not drink it, cook with it, wash wounds in it, or fill containers from it. Do not urinate in pools, channels, vents, or porous stone. Use sealed waste bags and carry everything out.

Avoid stepping in pools unless a route requires it. Reflective pools may be deeper than they appear, thermally hazardous, or important to local birds that probe reflections rather than water.

During showers:

- put on your shell before you are soaked;
- protect electronics quickly;
- avoid sheltering directly beneath the plate-trunked trees unless you know they are safe;
- do not shelter in cracks, under overhangs, or near vents;
- watch for runoff even though no established gullies exist.

Because drainage is unstable, a dry-looking route can become a warm stream or sink within minutes.

## Wildlife Etiquette and Safety

The animals are not scenery. They may be engineered test organisms, newly adapted colonists, or sensitive components of the shelf’s ecological stabilization.

### Keep your distance

Do not touch, feed, capture, herd, imitate, or block animals. This is especially important for:

- **six-winged grazers:** Their wings sense vibration. Sudden footsteps, loud speech, or mechanical noise may distress or scatter them.
- **white burrowers with orange fungi:** Their fungal loads may be food, symbionts, spores, or construction-relevant organisms. Do not interfere.
- **long-legged reflective-water birds:** Their behavior suggests visual or optical sensitivity. Avoid casting strong lights, throwing objects into pools, or standing between them and reflective surfaces.
- **quick shadows in steam:** Treat these as potentially predatory until proven otherwise. Do not pursue them into fog.

If an animal approaches, remain still briefly, keep hands visible, and let it choose the distance. Do not make sudden retreating movements that might trigger pursuit. If it shows aggression—raised body, rapid clicks, wing display, direct charging—back away slowly toward open, stable ground.

### Biological containment

After leaving, clean footwear and outer clothing before entering another ecosystem. The shelf’s organisms may be valuable, vulnerable, invasive, or hazardous elsewhere. Likewise, avoid bringing seeds, soil, animal hair, untreated wood, or food scraps into the shelf.

## Structures and the Buried Machine

The tower, silver arches, doorless shelters, rails, benches, and instruments should be treated as functioning infrastructure.

### Shelters

A warm lit shelter with no visible door may not be inaccessible; its entrance may operate by proximity, timing, identity, direction, or some other condition. Do not force entry, pry panels, strike surfaces, or attempt to bypass the architecture.

If one opens or admits you:

- enter only if you need shelter or if invited by clear local protocol;
- avoid touching instruments;
- do not remove ice, films, residues, or objects;
- do not rearrange benches or leave supplies;
- keep conversation quiet;
- leave it as you found it.

The ice-forming instruments in warm interiors may be diagnostic apparatus. Their frost could be data, containment material, or a process byproduct.

### Silver arches

Do not walk through, climb, hang equipment from, or place objects beneath the arches without clear authorization. Their differing orientations strongly suggest they are compensating for local field variations. They could be monitoring gravity, stress, alignment, mineral flow, or something harder to name.

If an arch begins pulsing, humming, warming, or changing its apparent lean, step back and observe from a distance. Do not stand between multiple arches.

### The tower

Rain avoiding the tower is enough reason not to touch it casually. It may have an active surface field. Keep metal tools, electronic devices, and wet clothing away from it unless it is clearly designated as a safe access point.

### Never seek the engine

The engine is below the shelf, deep in the mountain. Do not attempt to locate a shaft, tunnel, intake, or maintenance route. Do not enter fresh cracks, enlarge pores, follow warm vapor, or investigate low-frequency humming underground. The machine is making the ground you stand on. Human curiosity is not a valid reason to interfere with that process.

## Night Travel

Avoid remaining overnight unless you have no alternative or a shelter is explicitly intended for visitors.

At night the shelf becomes acoustically active: contractions, booms, bell-like complaints, gas releases, synchronized animal clicking, and the persistent hum below. This makes sleep difficult and makes it harder to distinguish ordinary site behavior from a developing emergency.

If you must stay:

- use a designated shelter if available;
- do not camp on bare ground;
- do not use open flame;
- avoid sleeping beside pools, vents, seams, tree roots, or structures;
- secure all gear against wind and condensation;
- assign watches if traveling as a group;
- mark your immediate area with low, nonintrusive reflective items attached to your own gear, not the ground;
- maintain an extraction-ready pack.

Do not follow sounds at night. The returned echo of your own voice, animal clicking, settling stone, and machine hum may all seem directional when they are not.

## Emergency Response

### If the ground cracks, sinks, warms, or begins to glow around you

1. Stop running.
2. Move calmly toward broader, duller, more established-looking ground or an existing marked path.
3. Avoid jumping across cracks unless collapse is immediate.
4. Keep distance between group members.
5. Do not throw gear away unless it is actively trapping you; dropped items may create distracting noise and are not worth injury.
6. If possible, leave the shelf using the route by which you entered or a known stable route.

### If caught in a steam release

- Cover nose and mouth.
- Protect eyes.
- Move crosswind and uphill if terrain permits.
- Do not enter dense steam to search for equipment.
- Treat burns promptly with sterile dressings and cool, clean carried water—not local pool water.

### If someone falls into a pool or soft mineral area

Do not rush directly to them. Assess the ground first. Use a rope, pole, or clothing line from stable terrain. Keep anchors on established rock or human-held counterweight rather than driving anchors into the shelf.

### If you become separated in fog

Stop moving unless you are in immediate danger. Use quiet prearranged signals at intervals. Avoid shouting names repeatedly; echoes may mislead both parties. Use lights sparingly and low to the ground, since reflections and mist can obscure direction.

### If a structure or the ground appears to communicate

Treat it as significant but not automatically trustworthy. Document what occurs. Avoid signing, speaking passwords, offering biological samples, or placing personal devices into interfaces. Do not surrender your route home, identity information, or bodily autonomy to an unknown system because it appears helpful.

## Departure Protocol

Leaving cleanly matters.

Before crossing the boundary:

- inspect boots, clothing, packs, and hair for seeds, spores, mineral foam, insects, fungi, or clinging wet material;
- remove contamination manually into sealed containment bags if safe;
- do not carry out rocks, water, plants, animal material, mud, or apparently inert mineral fragments;
- check that no equipment has been left behind;
- avoid taking photographs or recordings if local rules prohibit them, but otherwise document from a respectful distance;
- note changes in terrain, weather, animal behavior, and structure activity for future visitors.

Once outside, decontaminate thoroughly. Quarantine clothing and gear if there is any possibility that biological material has traveled with you. Monitor for respiratory irritation, unusual skin reactions, fever, persistent ringing in the ears, altered dreams, or delayed auditory phenomena. The latter may sound fanciful, but a place that stores and returns sound through forming stone warrants caution.

## The Essential Attitude

Visit the shelf as you would visit a living nursery, an active mine, and a sacred construction zone at the same time.

Do not take souvenirs. Do not leave evidence of yourself. Do not demand that the terrain behave like old ground. Move slowly, speak softly, watch the animals, respect the paths that already exist, and leave before curiosity turns into interference.

The shelf is not finished becoming land. A human visitor’s job is not to help it decide what it is.
