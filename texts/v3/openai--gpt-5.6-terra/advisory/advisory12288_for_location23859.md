## Preparing to Visit the Weatherworks Garden

Treat this as an industrial high-altitude expedition, not a botanical outing.

The garden is beautiful, but it is not gentle. It survives because it has adapted to an enormous machine whose purposes are partly forgotten, and you will be entering on the terms of both. The vegetation, animals, clouds, noise, heat, and water cycle are all connected to the weatherworks. Do not assume that anything natural-looking is independent of the machinery beneath it.

Your priorities are:

1. Do not fall.
2. Do not lose your hearing, balance, or orientation.
3. Do not interfere with the garden’s established rhythms.
4. Leave immediately if the stacks stop singing.

---

## Before You Go

### Travel with at least one other person

Do not visit alone unless there is absolutely no alternative.

The noise makes normal speech nearly useless, the mist can obscure a companion within a few strides, and the deck’s motion may make an injured person unable to walk safely. A partner should be close enough to touch when crossing exposed areas, but not tethered so tightly that one person’s fall can pull down the other.

Establish simple visual signals before entering:

- **Closed fist:** stop.
- **Two fingers pointed at eyes, then toward an exit:** leave now.
- **Palm down, pressing motion:** get low / brace.
- **Arms crossed over chest:** machinery change; wait and observe.
- **Hand slicing across throat:** stacks have gone quiet; emergency evacuation.
- **Point at ears, then a direction:** move to sheltered alcove / cannot hear.

Use waterproof slates, grease pencils, or a few large prewritten cards for anything more complicated. Electronics may suffer from condensation, vibration, electromagnetic interference, or whatever passes for weather at this altitude.

### Tell someone where you are going

If your multiversal travel method permits a return anchor, place it **outside the garden proper**, ideally in a stable maintenance corridor or entrance platform. Do not anchor your return route among vines, near hatches, on the central deck, or beside a cooling stack.

The weatherworks may rearrange pressure, temperature, access gates, and airflow on cycles longer than a human visit. A portal or route that is safe on arrival may not remain safe during a mist release or machinery transition.

Leave an itinerary with someone who understands one essential fact:

> If contact is lost after the stacks become silent, do not send an unprepared rescue party directly into the garden.

A rescue team should arrive with respiratory protection, hearing protection, fall-arrest equipment, and a preplanned extraction route.

## Physical Readiness

You do not need to be an elite climber, but you should be able to:

- Walk carefully over wet metal for several hours.
- Climb ladders and step over low rails without panicking.
- Function in extreme noise and partial visual obstruction.
- Keep your balance while the ground vibrates.
- Remain calm around large machinery and unseen animals.
- Recognize early symptoms of altitude stress, dehydration, heat exhaustion, and sensory overload.

The garden is at extreme elevation. Even if the local physics gives you breathable air, assume thin air until proven otherwise. You may become tired, confused, clumsy, or unusually emotional faster than expected.

If you have severe vertigo, untreated panic disorder, major hearing sensitivity, or a condition aggravated by heat, vibration, or low oxygen, this is not a casual destination.

## Essential Equipment

### Fall protection

Wear:

- A climbing or industrial safety harness.
- Twin shock-absorbing lanyards or self-retracting lifelines.
- Locking carabiners.
- Nonconductive attachment hardware if available.
- A helmet with a chin strap.

Do not clip onto:

- Vines.
- Handrails hidden by vegetation.
- Red cables.
- Pipework.
- Crane rails.
- Any fixture whose maintenance marking cannot be read.
- Insulation bundles or animal nests.

Clip only to obvious structural anchor points: marked steel rings, load-rated beams, intact catwalk rail bases, or purpose-built lifeline cable where visible. If no safe attachment point is available, do not cross exposed ground simply because it looks level.

The railings are not a promise. They may be old, wet, overgrown, or intended to guide workers rather than stop falls.

### Footwear and clothing

Wear sturdy, slip-resistant boots with:

- Soft but aggressive rubber soles for wet metal.
- Ankle support.
- No loose laces, straps, or dangling decorative pieces.

Bring layered clothing. You may experience hot exhaust, cold mist, high sun, and damp shade in one short walk.

Recommended:

- Flame-resistant outer layer.
- Long sleeves and trousers.
- Gloves with good grip but enough dexterity for clips and tools.
- A waterproof shell that does not flap loudly in strong airflow.
- A brimmed cap or sun hood under the helmet for the intense white light.
- UV-blocking eyewear, preferably clear-to-dark adaptive lenses.

Avoid bright red clothing if possible. This is not because it is known to provoke anything, but because the garden has red emergency cabling, crimson plants, and warning systems. In an emergency, you want your signals and equipment to be visually unambiguous.

### Hearing protection

Bring **two forms** of hearing protection:

1. High-quality industrial earplugs.
2. Over-ear defenders or a helmet-mounted communications headset.

The stacks’ sound is not merely loud. It is layered, low-frequency, and disorienting. You may feel it through your teeth and chest even with protection. Earplugs alone will reduce pain but may not prevent fatigue. Over-ear protection helps preserve enough attention to notice changes in the machinery.

Do not seal yourself off so completely that you cannot detect one critical event: **the stacks stopping.** Learn the baseline vibration through your boots and body. The silence may first be felt, rather than heard.

### Respiratory and eye protection

Carry a respirator with replaceable particulate and chemical vapor filters. You may not need to wear it constantly, but have it ready for:

- Dense rust-scented mist.
- Sudden exhaust discharge.
- Fungal foam around leaking seals.
- Maintenance corridors.
- Any moment when you taste metal in the air.

Use sealed goggles during mist surges if your eyes begin to sting or if visibility becomes strange. Ordinary sunglasses will not protect you from aerosols.

### Light, navigation, and communication

Bring:

- A headlamp with a physical switch and a red-light setting.
- Spare batteries stored dry.
- A small high-visibility lantern or beacon.
- A compass only as a sentimental backup; magnetic and mechanical systems may behave poorly around the works.
- A paper map or hand-drawn route sketch.
- Chalk or removable wax markers to mark your path on **metal surfaces only**.

Never mark living plants, nests, animal trails, red cables, or control panels.

Use physical landmarks: entrance hatch, crane track, control booth, stack bases, large valve clusters, and numbered handrails. Do not rely on cloud position, light direction, or the apparent movement of the sun.

### Water, food, and medical supplies

Bring all drinking water you expect to need, plus more.

Even though the garden visibly contains moisture, its water is not automatically yours. The rule about pipe water should be treated as medical, not folkloric. Water from leaves is apparently safe, but that means it is safer than pipe water—not necessarily safe for every human body in every universe.

Bring:

- Sealed water.
- Electrolytes.
- Compact high-energy food.
- Antiemetic medicine if you are prone to motion sickness.
- Pain relief.
- Antihistamine.
- Burn gel and sterile dressings.
- Eye wash.
- A basic trauma kit.
- A thermal wrap; people can chill quickly when wet and exhausted, even in warm industrial spaces.

If you drink gathered mist from leaves, do so only as a backup. Choose clean upper leaf surfaces away from pipe mouths, valve leaks, fungal foam, animal droppings, and crimson hollow stalks. Collect droplets in a clean cup rather than licking leaves directly.

Never drink from a pipe. Never “just taste” a pipe drip. Never fill a bottle from a valve, condensation pan, gauge line, or steam trap.

The reported copper taste and gear-dreams may be the least serious effects.

## What Not to Bring

Leave behind:

- Drones that buzz, hover near nests, or may fall into machinery.
- Open flames.
- Loose paper, scarves, capes, plastic bags, or anything that can be drawn into intakes.
- Strong perfumes, scented oils, and aerosol sprays.
- Aggressive herbicides, insecticides, or disinfectants.
- Weapons unless your travel circumstances require them; if carried, keep them inaccessible and do not fire them in the garden.
- Tools meant for pruning, cutting, harvesting, or “clearing a path.”
- Amplified music, speakers, alarms, or anything likely to add unfamiliar sound to the machinery’s rhythm.

Especially do not bring a machete.

The garden is overgrown because overgrowth is part of its operating condition.

## Entering the Garden

When you first emerge onto the deck, do not immediately walk into the vegetation.

Stop in the entry area and spend several minutes acclimating. Let your body learn:

- The deck’s normal vibration.
- The pitch and pressure of the stacks.
- The direction of airflow.
- The location of the nearest visible exit.
- The timing of drips, vents, crane movement, and warning lights.
- Whether the vegetation is moving because of air, machinery, or something beneath it.

The first few minutes will feel overwhelming. That is normal. Wait until you can distinguish at least three separate mechanical rhythms and identify the direction from which the strongest windless pressure is coming. This gives you a baseline. If something changes later, you will recognize it.

Do not stand beneath a crane path, under hanging cable runs, near a hatch, or directly in front of a valve mouth while acclimating.

## Moving Through the Garden

### Stay on obvious routes

Use catwalks, open plates, marked paths, and established clearances wherever they remain visible.

Do not assume lush growth means stable footing. Ferns may conceal:

- Missing deck plates.
- Open inspection hatches.
- Drainage grates.
- Hot casings.
- Cable trenches.
- Sharp rivets.
- Animal burrows.
- Leaking seals.

Probe uncertain ground with a nonconductive walking staff before putting full weight on it. Keep your center of gravity low. Move slowly. Three points of contact are wise on ladders and steep plates.

Avoid stepping on moss-covered engine casings. The moss may conceal heat sufficient to burn through clothing, and the casing may be a vibrating machine component rather than a support surface.

### Give the red cables a wide berth

Do not cut, pull, untangle, climb over, or attempt to expose the red emergency cables. Do not hang equipment from them.

If the vines have covered them for generations, then the weatherworks has accepted—or requires—that arrangement. Treat the red glow during a storm as an alarm for the entire garden, not an invitation to investigate.

If you see the hidden cables pulsing:

1. Stop moving.
2. Move away from railings, crane tracks, hatches, and pipe outlets.
3. Do not touch plants or animals.
4. Find the nearest robust sheltered alcove—not a sealed chamber, not beneath a large pipe.
5. Secure harnesses to structural metal.
6. Wait for the immediate environmental change to reveal itself, then leave when the route is safe.

### Respect the dead control booth

The control booth may look like shelter, and in some situations it may be. But do not casually operate gauges, switches, wheels, levers, or chairs.

A dead gauge is not necessarily disconnected. A sleeping panel may be waiting for a condition you do not understand. A system old enough to run unattended may also be old enough to respond very badly to unauthorized inputs.

Use the booth only as an emergency refuge if:

- The structure looks intact.
- There is no active electrical arcing, smoke, or unusual heat.
- You can enter without forcing anything.
- You leave all controls untouched.

Read plaques, scratched messages, and inspection markings. Photograph them if possible. Do not erase, clean, “restore,” or overwrite them.

## Water and Mist Protocol

The mist is a scheduled event, even if the schedule is not readable.

When it approaches, you may notice a pressure change, whitening of the gap between stacks, a rust-like scent, altered behavior in the winged animals, and plants turning or opening toward it.

Prepare before it reaches you:

- Secure loose gear.
- Put on goggles and, if the air is harsh, respirator protection.
- Move away from exposed electrical equipment and open maintenance panels.
- Avoid standing under heavy leaf clusters that may dump accumulated water.
- Keep one hand free for railings or harness management.
- Watch your companion.

The mist may be biologically important and relatively safe, but it is still industrial condensate passing through unknown systems. Let it settle; do not deliberately inhale deeply, bathe in it, or collect it from metal surfaces.

If mist exposure causes a persistent metallic taste, dizziness, unusual dreams, confusion, tremors, or fixation on the machinery, leave the garden and seek medical observation. “Dreaming of turning gears” should not be dismissed as quaint local color. It suggests neurological, chemical, memetic, or other systemic exposure.

## Wildlife Etiquette

The animals are not pets, pests, or specimens.

They have lived here longer than any visible worker, and they understand the machinery better than you do.

### The plated winged creatures

Do not approach nests in insulation bundles. Do not shine bright lights into them. Do not touch fallen feathers or plates unless necessary for immediate safety.

Their wings are part drinking vessel, part thermal system, and perhaps part sensor. A bird-like creature that lives between cooling stacks may be exquisitely attuned to pressure and temperature changes you cannot perceive.

If they abruptly leave the upper scaffolds, cluster tightly, become silent, or begin nesting low to the deck, take notice. Do not panic, but prepare to leave. Their behavior may predict a mist surge, a heat release, a storm, or a machinery transition.

### The flower-faced grazers

You may feed them fruit, but do so sparingly.

Use only fruit already growing in the garden, preferably fallen fruit. Place it on an open patch of deck and step back. Do not hand-feed. Do not feed packaged human food, salt, sweets, meat, or anything with artificial additives.

Most importantly:

> Do not touch them.

Not gently. Not through gloves. Not to help one caught in a vine. Not for a photograph.

Their alarm note apparently activates every warning light on the deck. This could summon a system response, trigger machinery changes, expose your location to something else, or simply create panic in an already hazardous environment.

If one approaches you, stand still, keep your hands visible, and let it decide your relevance. If it unfolds its face, backs away slowly without sudden gestures.

### The thing beneath the deck

Do not investigate the breathing beneath drainage grates.

Do not put a hand, light, camera, food, rope, or listening device through the grate. Do not call to it. Do not imitate its breathing. Do not toss objects down “to see how far it is.”

The fact that the ferns bow when it exhales means it is either large, close, connected to ductwork, or all three. None of those possibilities improve with human curiosity.

If the breathing changes pace, becomes wet or irregular, moves closer, or stops while the stacks continue: leave the immediate area calmly and without using the drainage routes.

## Food and Plant Safety

Assume all garden vegetation is adapted to metals, heat, exhaust, and condensation.

Do not eat:

- Yellow fruit.
- Moss.
- Fungal foam.
- Crimson hollow stalks.
- Seeds.
- Flowers.
- Anything growing around a leaking seal, valve mouth, or warm casing.

Even if local animals eat them, they may be concentrating trace metals or using biochemistry incompatible with humans. The yellow fruit’s boiler-paint skin is a warning of its habitat, not evidence of edibility.

Do not collect seeds or cuttings unless you have a containment plan appropriate to multiversal ecological travel. A plant adapted to industrial weather may become invasive, toxic, or mechanically disruptive somewhere else. Conversely, removing it may damage an ecosystem whose balance depends on every vine and bloom.

Take photographs, sketches, sound recordings, and environmental readings—not specimens.

## Recognizing Machinery Changes

The weatherworks is never truly quiet, but it has a pace. Your safety depends on noticing deviations.

Potential warning signs include:

- A new vibration underfoot.
- A rhythm slowing or accelerating.
- Belts or fan sounds dropping out one by one.
- The crane stopping somewhere unusual.
- Warning lamps activating without an obvious cause.
- Condensation dripping faster, slower, or in a new pattern.
- Plants closing, flattening, or turning in one direction.
- Grazers freezing.
- Winged creatures abandoning their nests.
- A sudden absence of the usual pressure in your chest.
- The sense that your own breathing is too loud.

Any major change should make you stop, assess your exit route, and reduce exploration.

Do not remain merely because you want to document the event.

## The Absolute Rule: If the Stacks Stop Singing, Leave

Do not wait for confirmation.

Do not wait for a second person to agree.

Do not wait to photograph the silence.

Do not wait for the machinery to restart.

The instant the stacks stop singing—or the instant you realize the continuous industrial pressure has vanished—begin evacuation.

This includes a silence that is partial, muffled, or felt more than heard. If the garden’s normal machine-noise is suddenly absent enough that you can hear your own footsteps, drips, leaf movement, or breathing clearly, treat it as the warning.

### Immediate actions

1. **Signal your group:** emergency departure.
2. **Keep harnesses attached while moving**, but do not become tangled in an overcomplicated extraction.
3. **Take the route you entered by** if it remains clear.
4. **Avoid the central vegetation, control booth, drainage grates, and red cables.**
5. **Do not touch animals, even to move them from your path.**
6. **Do not collect equipment you set down.**
7. **Do not enter newly opened gates, shafts, or hatches.**
8. **Do not shelter and wait for the sound to return.**
9. **Exit the garden boundary before reassessing anything.**

If the route behind you becomes blocked, choose the nearest known structural corridor or maintenance access that leads away from the deck—not downward through grates, not upward into stack interiors, and not outward over exposed rails.

Once outside, continue moving until the garden is no longer visible or audible. Do not remain at the entrance threshold. The instruction says not to see what the garden does in silence; that almost certainly includes watching from just beyond the door.

## If Someone Becomes Injured

For slips, burns, breathing trouble, or disorientation:

- Get them clipped to a structural anchor first.
- Move them only far enough to avoid immediate heat, moving machinery, or exposed edges.
- Protect their hearing and airway.
- Use hand signals; do not waste energy shouting.
- Do not lay an injured person directly on moss, fungal growth, or bare warm metal.
- Evacuate rather than attempting prolonged treatment inside.

If someone becomes mentally altered after drinking pipe water or heavy mist exposure—speaking mechanically, staring at gears, insisting they “understand” the controls, or wanting to remain—do not let them operate anything. Use calm physical guidance, secure them to a safe anchor if necessary, and remove them from the weatherworks.

Do not argue about whether the experience is real. The location may make unusual perceptions consequential.

## A Good Visitor’s Mindset

The garden is not abandoned in the ordinary sense. It is inhabited by processes.

It may be maintained by no visible human hand, but it is not neglected. The vines know where the cables are. The animals know when mist comes. The plants use exhaust as wind, condensation as rain, warm casings as ground, and the stacks’ song as a horizon.

Your job is not to solve the mystery of the weatherworks, repair it, rescue it, tame it, or prove that it should not exist.

Your job is to arrive quietly, observe with respect, leave no trace, and depart before the place has reason to notice you.

Pack for noise, heights, wet steel, strange water, and sudden evacuation.

And remember the only rule that matters more than all the others:

> When the stacks stop singing, leave.
