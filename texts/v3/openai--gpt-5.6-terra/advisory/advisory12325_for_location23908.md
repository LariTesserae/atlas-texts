## Preparation Brief: The City Above the Weather

This is not a place for casual exploration, heroic ruin-diving, or “seeing where the road goes.” It is survivable for an ordinary person, especially if you respect local habits, travel slowly, and treat every anomaly as infrastructure rather than a challenge.

The chief threats are not monsters or violence. They are thin air, dehydration, exposure, disorientation, unstable geography, and phenomena that react badly to curiosity.

Plan for a short visit first: three to five local days, with a fixed shelter, a local guide if one can be hired, and a clear return method that does not depend on finding the same street twice.

---

## Before You Go

### Acclimatize for altitude if possible

The city is extremely high. Its air is breathable, but reluctantly so. An unacclimatized visitor may experience:

- breathlessness after a few stairs;
- headache, nausea, poor judgment, and insomnia;
- blue lips or fingertips;
- confusion that is easily mistaken for one of the city’s stranger effects;
- dangerous exhaustion from carrying a pack across terraces.

Do not arrive planning to climb towers, cross districts on foot, or hike the surrounding ridges on your first day.

Bring, if your travel technology permits:

- supplemental oxygen;
- a pulse oximeter;
- altitude-sickness medication appropriate to your physiology;
- a compact pressure-breathing mask or filtered respirator;
- a light but warm sleeping system;
- a way to descend quickly, or at least to enter a sealed, oxygen-rich shelter, if symptoms become serious.

If you cannot bring oxygen, arrange lodging in one of the lower inhabited tiers rather than the observatory districts. “Lower” is relative, but every little helps.

### Assume water will be difficult to obtain

The clouds are not rain. The old canals are dry. The sound of water is a danger sign, not an invitation to refill a canteen.

Bring more potable water than you think you need, plus electrolytes. The dry air, dust, altitude, and constant walking will dehydrate you rapidly without making you feel sweaty.

Recommended minimum:

- enough water for at least two days beyond your intended stay;
- sealed containers, preferably metal or durable ceramic rather than thin plastic;
- oral rehydration salts;
- simple high-calorie food requiring no cooking;
- a small insulated flask for warm drinks.

Do not drink from wells, cisterns, old fountains, drains, or condensation on stone. Do not investigate running-water sounds. If you hear water, stop moving and wait until it stops.

### Pack for dust rather than rain

The local weather is dry, abrasive, and full of fine mineral particulate. It can infiltrate hinges, lungs, eyes, fabrics, food, and electronics.

Wear:

- layered cold-weather clothing;
- a windproof outer shell;
- wraparound goggles;
- a dust mask or respirator with spare filters;
- gloves suitable for stone and cold metal;
- sturdy boots with excellent grip and closed ankles;
- a scarf or neck gaiter;
- head covering that will not blow away.

Bring sealed bags for food, medicines, documents, and electronics. Keep a soft brush and cloth for cleaning dust from gear. Do not expect umbrellas or rain capes to be useful.

Avoid clothing that drags, flaps, or sheds fibers. Loose straps and scarves are bad around narrow stairs, wind-exposed bridges, and areas where shadows seem to have physical pull.

### Bring old-fashioned navigation tools, but do not trust them completely

A map, compass, clock, notebook, and distance-measuring cord are useful. GPS, digital maps, and automated route planners may be less so, particularly near the city’s irregular streets and the surrounding high country.

Use several forms of orientation at once:

- paper map;
- written route notes;
- physical markers that you can retrieve later;
- a compass;
- a clock;
- a travel companion;
- a prearranged check-in schedule.

However, avoid marking places with names while you are standing in them. The city’s law is explicit: **do not name a street while standing in it.**

That includes speaking a name aloud, improvising a nickname, labeling it in a voice memo, or casually saying, “We are on Lantern Street.” Make notes later, indoors, or once you have clearly left the street.

Describe locations by neutral route instructions instead: “third stair after the dry aqueduct,” “two arches east of the salt wall,” “north balcony district, blue lamp level.” Better yet, copy the terms used by locals without repeating them until you are elsewhere.

## What Not to Bring

Leave behind:

- drones, unless you are prepared to lose them;
- large wheeled luggage;
- fragile glass containers;
- irreplaceable heirlooms;
- anything that must remain perfectly calibrated;
- animals, especially birds;
- weapons displayed openly;
- large amounts of fresh soil, plant matter, or water from another world;
- vacuum devices, dust collectors, or sample kits intended for the Empty Reservoir.

Do not bring a compulsion to collect souvenirs. In this city, dust, salt, stones, and fragments may have social or spatial significance you do not understand.

In particular: **do not carry dust from the Empty Reservoir into any dwelling.** This is not merely etiquette. Keep separate outer garments and footwear for visiting the basin. Brush yourself off outdoors afterward, ideally under local guidance. Do not put reservoir dust into your pack.

## Arrival Protocol

Upon arrival, do not immediately begin sightseeing.

Spend the first hour doing three things:

1. **Sit and breathe.**  
   Confirm that you can walk and think clearly at the altitude.

2. **Find inhabited quarters.**  
   Look for the small bluish oil lamps at dusk, maintained balconies, occupied doorways, or places where trade goods are set out. Do not assume an apparently empty district is safe to camp in.

3. **Learn the current routes.**  
   The city’s maps age slowly, but streets, shadows, stairs, and distances can change. Ask where the safe paths currently are, which areas are closed, and whether an inversion wind is expected.

A polite visitor should offer trade rather than demand assistance. Useful offerings include:

- durable cloth;
- preserved food;
- medicine;
- simple hand tools;
- good paper;
- pencils and ink;
- sealed containers;
- polished glass;
- accurate maps from elsewhere, if they do not endanger anyone;
- stories only if invited.

Do not offer water casually. It may be immensely valuable, culturally complicated, or dangerous to introduce into a city structured around its absence.

## Local Etiquette and Survival Rules

The residents are sparse, quiet, and likely accustomed to things outsiders find unsettling. Their restraint is practical. Imitate it.

### Speak softly and do not fill silence

Avoid shouting across plazas, calling down stairwells, or testing echoes. Do not whistle for companions. Use visual signals, hand signs, or agreed meeting times.

An echo may not be immediate, may not be yours, or may be from another day. Treat unusual acoustics as you would unstable masonry: notice them and move away.

### Do not enter anomalies to test them

Avoid:

- shadows that gather under arches regardless of the sun;
- dark patches that swallow thrown objects;
- stairways known to “go downward while rising”;
- doorways locals do not cross in pairs;
- windows showing landscapes not visible outside;
- dry wells and bathhouses producing water sounds;
- streets whose shadows point uphill.

If you find one unexpectedly, do not run. Pause, back away slowly, and retrace your steps without discussing it in detail until you are in a known safe place.

A simple rule: if the environment contradicts itself, do not insist that it resolve the contradiction for you.

### Never look twice through an impossible window

If a window shows another landscape—sea, forest, another city, stars, a familiar home, anything not physically outside—close the shutter, curtain, or door at once.

Do not photograph it. Do not ask another person to confirm it. Do not lean closer. Do not open it again.

If you cannot close it safely, leave the room and seek local advice. Do not sleep facing it.

### Respect the water law absolutely

If you hear running water:

- stop walking;
- do not speak;
- do not follow the sound;
- do not try to locate its source;
- wait until it ceases;
- then leave by the route you came, if possible.

Do not assume the sound is an illusion. It may be an opening, an invitation, a memory, a route elsewhere, or all of these. Whatever it is, local experience says pursuit is not wise.

### Do not walk alone outside settled districts

The land beyond the city looks open, but it is not reliably traversable. Distances loop. ravines can displace time. A hill can remain ahead of you for a day and still deposit you at your departure arch.

Travel outside the city only with:

- an experienced local guide;
- a fixed turnaround time;
- enough supplies for several unintended days;
- a tethering or buddy system;
- a clear instruction for what to do if one person disappears, returns changed, or loses time.

Do not cross a narrow ravine merely because it appears easy. Do not chase a landmark. Do not follow footprints that appeared during an inversion wind.

## The Inversion Wind

This is the major weather event to plan around.

Its warning signs include:

- loose objects trembling;
- glass beads or panes vibrating;
- utensils moving on thresholds;
- dust beginning to lift in sheets rather than gusts;
- fires bending upward;
- a strange failure of voices to travel downward.

When these signs begin:

1. Go indoors or into a low, sheltered stone chamber.
2. Close shutters and secure loose objects.
3. Stay away from open plazas, bridges, stairwells, rooflines, and cliff edges.
4. Do not try to communicate with anyone below you by shouting.
5. Do not follow any footprints that appear in fresh dust.
6. Wait until local residents indicate that it has passed.

The wind may leave the streets exceptionally clean. This does not mean it is safe to wander immediately. The city may have been subtly rearranged: a route may now terminate differently, dust markers may be gone, and things that were buried or obscured may be exposed.

After an inversion, re-establish your position from a known inhabited landmark. Do not rely on your pre-wind route.

## Lodging

Stay in an inhabited, maintained building, even if it is austere.

Choose a room that has:

- a functioning shutter;
- no exposed view into unexplained darkness;
- stone walls without active salt failure;
- a door that closes securely;
- a safe lamp or local equivalent;
- proximity to other people without being directly beside a major stair or archway;
- indoor storage for your gear.

Avoid sleeping in:

- dry aqueduct halls;
- abandoned bathhouses;
- cistern chambers;
- roofless observatories;
- the Empty Reservoir’s piers;
- rooms containing mineral fungus unless the host specifically says it is safe;
- buildings that appear “too intact” in otherwise eroded districts, unless locals use them.

The gray mineral fungus is not necessarily dangerous, but it reacts to touch by sealing itself into a hard skin. Do not harvest or handle it without permission. Some residents use it for warmth; others treat it as part of the city’s slow geological life. Either way, it is not yours.

Keep one small lamp or dependable light source, but understand that light may not fully banish the city’s darkness. Bluish local lamps are intentionally modest. Do not respond by flooding every room with harsh artificial light; this may be socially unwelcome and may make shadows harder, not easier, to judge.

## Moving Through the City

The city is vertical, immense, and built for more people than it contains. Budget far more time and energy than distances suggest.

### Walk slowly

Altitude and stairs will punish haste. Rest frequently. Drink before thirst becomes obvious. Do not take shortcuts through ruined interiors or across retaining walls.

### Keep contact with stone

In unclear weather, stay near a wall, railing, or established paving. The open plazas are deceptively disorienting. Their scale can destroy your sense of distance, especially in thin light.

### Beware silent surfaces

Some paving rings almost silently underfoot. Do not use sound alone to judge whether a surface is solid. Test questionable stones with a walking stick before committing weight.

A walking stick is useful for:

- checking dust depth;
- detecting gaps between paving slabs;
- maintaining balance on stairs;
- probing the edge of strange shadows from a safe distance—though not throwing objects into them;
- signaling companions without shouting.

### Do not force old mechanisms

You may encounter counterweighted doors, dry wheels, gravity shafts, and aqueduct machinery. Do not crank, pry, lubricate, “repair,” or reset anything.

The local attitude is sound: objects here are allowed to decide their own condition. A dry wheel that turns overnight is not an invitation for you to discover why.

## The Empty Reservoir

Visit only with a local guide or after receiving explicit directions.

Treat it as a harbor, not a dry lakebed. The fact that it is empty is not proof that it is inactive.

Rules:

- do not take dust, grit, or stones from it into a residence;
- do not camp in it;
- do not descend into it alone;
- do not attempt to touch the translucent fleet if it appears;
- do not shout at or signal the ships;
- do not attempt to board, photograph from close range, or place an object on a pier as an offering;
- leave before dawn unless specifically invited to remain for the sweeping.

If you witness the fleet, observe from a respectful distance. The city’s people sweep the piers afterward. Offer help only if asked, and follow their procedures precisely. Do not pocket the grit.

## Interacting With Residents

The inhabitants are not described as hostile. They are likely cautious because caution is the price of continuing to live there.

Be patient. Trade may be conducted silently or slowly. Objects may be set between people and left overnight rather than exchanged hand-to-hand. Do not interpret this as indecision or distrust.

Good behavior:

- wait;
- ask before entering;
- accept that some questions will not be answered;
- use simple, literal language;
- honor warnings even when they sound superstitious;
- do not press people about missing districts, absent relatives, the Low Blue, or what is beneath the clouds;
- keep promises about where you will and will not go.

Avoid making claims such as “that cannot happen,” “it is just an optical illusion,” or “we can fix this.” The city has endured longer than most explanations.

## If You Become Lost

First, do not panic and do not keep walking merely to feel active.

Stop at a recognizable, physically stable feature: a maintained lamp, a distinctive bridge, a carved facade, an inhabited doorway, a known public square. Avoid stopping under arches with unnatural darkness or near wells.

Then:

1. Check your body: oxygen, water, temperature, injuries, fatigue.
2. Check the time, but do not assume time alone is reliable.
3. Write down the last three things you know happened.
4. Retrace only one short section, slowly.
5. If the route changes, do not argue with it. Return to the last safe point.
6. Seek a local, not a more elaborate route.

If you find yourself at the same archway repeatedly, stop attempting to reach your original destination. Treat the archway as your current location and ask for help there.

If a companion returns after an apparent short absence but believes decades passed—or has unfamiliar stones in their pockets—do not immediately interrogate them. Get them warm, hydrated, seated, and accompanied. Prevent them from re-entering the route alone. Record their account later, gently, and do not assume their memories are false simply because they conflict with yours.

## If You Encounter the Pale Wall-Creatures or Black Shapes

Do not pursue, trap, feed, or touch either.

The pale angular wall-creatures appear to feed on minerals and leave polished lines rather than tracks. Give them room. They may be harmless, but their habitat includes cracks, vents, and unstable wall sections that are not.

The black airborne or perched forms may be birds, shadows, or something between. Do not throw things at them, shine lights directly on them, or attempt to identify them by approaching after they land. If one folds around a chimney, statue, or wall feature, leave it alone and choose another route.

Their silence is part of the environment. Do not try to make them call.

## Mental Preparation

The city will test an ordinary traveler’s tolerance for incompleteness.

You may see:

- shadows behaving incorrectly;
- architecture changing in ways too small to prove;
- a city designed for populations that no longer exist;
- dust moving with apparent intention;
- glimpses of a fleet in an empty basin;
- clouds parting to reveal no world below.

Do not make yourself prove or disprove every experience. Keep a journal, preferably on paper, but distinguish observation from interpretation:

- “At 18:10, heard water beneath paving for six seconds; remained still; sound stopped.”
- Not: “A sea is beneath the city and called to me.”

Write facts first. Interpret later, after you have left.

Travel with a companion who is calm rather than thrill-seeking. Establish simple reality checks before arrival: names, dates, a shared phrase, an inventory list, and a rule that neither person enters an anomalous area alone.

Most importantly, give yourself permission to leave without “finishing” the visit. This is not a destination that rewards completionism.

---

## Departure

Leave by a planned route, ideally from a stable inhabited district and not immediately after an inversion wind, water sound, or impossible-window encounter.

Before departing:

- inspect boots, clothing, and pack seams for reservoir dust;
- clean equipment outdoors;
- verify that all companions are present and agree on the date and destination;
- compare notes independently before discussing strange events;
- check for unrecognized stones, objects, or written materials in pockets and bags;
- do not bring unknown mineral fungus, salt blooms, or polished fragments across worlds without quarantine;
- rest and rehydrate before making a second long journey.

If you return with missing time, unfamiliar objects, a changed route home, or persistent auditory memories of water, do not immediately travel again. Spend time somewhere low, wet, ordinary, and well populated. Let your body recover before deciding what happened.

The city is not necessarily trying to harm visitors. It is simply old, elevated, dry, and only partly aligned with the assumptions a traveler brings. Go prepared to be humble. Breathe slowly. Follow the people who live there. Keep away from water you cannot see.
