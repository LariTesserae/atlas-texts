Prepare for this place as you would for a cold-water industrial diving expedition inside an operating power-and-water facility—not as an urban exploration trip, and not as a visit to a natural sea.

The most dangerous feature is not the fish, the depth, or even the darkness. It is that the entire environment is active. Currents have schedules. Gates open. Pumps reverse or accelerate. Machinery still follows rules that may not notice a person in the water.

## Before crossing

### Obtain local authorization if at all possible

Do not arrive assuming this is abandoned. It is visibly maintained, powered, monitored, and navigated by work crews. You may be treated as an unauthorized person in a controlled utility complex, which is both dangerous and likely illegal by local standards.

Try to secure:

- A site map, even an incomplete one
- The pump and flood-cycle timetable
- Radio frequencies and emergency signal meanings
- Permission to use maintenance docks, air decks, and marked escape routes
- A local escort, pilot, or dive supervisor
- Information about water treatment chemicals, electrical isolation zones, and contaminated districts
- The locations of active discharge outlets and intake grilles

Learn the warning system before entering the water. In particular:

- **Siren:** likely means a gate or major water-control event is imminent.
- **Blue lights:** turbine or high-flow equipment may be starting.
- **Three low bells:** clear a designated current corridor immediately.
- **Red painted lines:** do not assume they indicate the current waterline; they may mark a former operational level or a flood limit.

If nobody can explain a signal, treat it as a reason to stop moving, locate a secure platform or handhold, and contact control.

### Do not cross alone

Bring at least one trained companion, preferably someone qualified in cold-water technical diving, overhead-environment diving, and industrial-water operations. This location combines all three.

A solo traveler can be swept through a doorway, caught against a grille, tangled in cable, or disoriented among identical bays without anyone noticing. In a complex this large, “nearby” may mean a full day of travel away.

Leave a route plan with:

- Intended district and entry point
- Maximum depth
- Expected travel direction
- Time of return
- Alternate air-pocket or dock locations
- A firm overdue-response time

Do not rely on a handheld phone for emergency contact. Water, concrete, steel, and the scale of the structure will make it nearly useless.

## Essential equipment

### For underwater travel

Use cold-water technical dive equipment, not recreational snorkeling gear.

Bring:

- A properly fitted drysuit with thermal underlayers
- Hood, gloves, and boots suitable for prolonged cold immersion
- Redundant breathing gas supplies
- Redundant regulators
- A full-face mask or secure mask-and-regulator arrangement, ideally with underwater communications
- Buoyancy control equipment appropriate for confined, industrial water
- At least two independent lights plus chemical backup lights
- A cutting tool accessible with either hand
- A second cutting tool in a separate location
- Reel and guideline system, with durable line appropriate for sharp metal edges
- Slate or waterproof route notes
- Compass, depth gauge, timer, and ideally an inertial or sonar navigation tool
- Whistle or underwater acoustic signaling device
- Surface marker equipment only if you know there is a safe surface to deploy it under

Do not enter with a single air tank, a single lamp, or a single knife. In this environment, redundancy is not luxury. It is the difference between an inconvenience and a body recovery.

### For dry upper decks and air pockets

The upper service levels may have breathable air, but do not assume it is safe air.

Carry:

- A compact multi-gas monitor capable of detecting low oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, flammable vapors, and ideally other industrial contaminants
- Respiratory protection appropriate to local hazards
- Waterproof outer layers for condensation and steam
- Hard hat
- High-visibility marking or reflective strips
- Robust footwear with puncture-resistant soles
- Work gloves
- Eye protection
- Ear protection, though remember it may prevent you from hearing alarms

Air pockets may contain exhaust fumes, battery off-gassing, mold, stagnant vapor, or oxygen-depleted air. A chamber that “feels” dry is not necessarily safe to breathe. Test before removing your breathing apparatus.

### Other useful provisions

Bring more food, warm fluids, batteries, and insulating layers than you expect to need. The cold will drain your energy even if you never feel dramatically chilled.

Also pack:

- Waterproof medical kit
- Tourniquet and trauma supplies
- Thermal blanket or compact warming system
- Spare batteries kept warm and dry
- Repair tape, cable ties, and small tools
- Waterproof identification and local permit documents
- Bright signaling panels or strobes for boat pickup
- Waterproof waste bags: do not add plastic or loose debris to intake systems

## Dress for cold, not drama

The water is described as merely cool rather than freezing, which can be deceptive. Cold reservoir water causes slow loss of dexterity, concentration, and judgment. Your hands will begin to ache before you realize how impaired you are.

Plan short dives and frequent warming intervals. If you notice:

- Clumsy fingers
- Slow thinking
- Unusual irritability
- Persistent shivering
- Trouble operating clips, valves, or lights
- A growing desire to “just get it over with”

…end the excursion. These are not things to push through in a maze of flooded machinery.

Avoid exposed skin. In addition to cold, the water may carry oil residues, algae, bacteria, treatment chemicals, and fine metallic sediment.

## Navigation: treat every doorway as a possible river mouth

This is not open-water diving. It is a vast overhead environment with architectural camouflage. You can see a corridor, a warehouse opening, or a rail cut and think it is a route; it may actually be an active flow channel.

Never travel without a continuous guideline when entering:

- Turbine housings
- Conduits
- Maintenance tunnels
- Elevator shafts
- Flooded offices or enclosed rooms
- Rail tunnels
- Narrow gaps under cranes or platforms
- Any place where the exit cannot remain plainly visible

Tie lines only to reliable fixed structure. Avoid corroded railings, loose pipes, cable bundles, machinery that may move, or objects that can be drawn into flow.

Mark junctions clearly. The complex will have repeated columns, repeated lamps, repeated warning signs, and mirrored water reflections. Your memory of “the hall with the yellow lights” will be worthless after three similar halls.

Never follow the lights blindly. Yellow safety lights may indicate a route, but they may also trace an old work corridor leading directly toward an intake district. Their purpose is maintenance, not rescue.

Use district names and structural features: “Intake Four, west rail cut, upper crane bay,” not “the big room with the bus.”

## Currents are the principal lethal hazard

The water moves with intention. That means its currents are not random and not necessarily visible.

Watch for:

- Cables beginning to angle or vibrate
- Suspended sediment lifting from the bottom
- Fish rotating or compressing into a school
- Small debris moving sideways across your path
- A subtle pressure against one side of your body
- Changes in hum or vibration through the hull and floor
- Lights, weeds, or loose fabric streaming in a common direction

If these begin, leave open water and move toward a sheltered structural lee—behind a substantial wall, building frame, pier, or column—not toward the middle of a bay.

Do not shelter behind flimsy sheet metal, hanging doors, floating platforms, or unsecured cargo. Flow changes may move them.

Most importantly: stay away from intake grilles, lower-wall openings, sluice mouths, discharge tunnels, and turbine galleries. A grille is not necessarily a safe barrier. It may be damaged, poorly secured, or strong enough to pin you against it until you drown or exhaust your gas supply.

Maintain a generous exclusion zone. If you can feel a current near an intake, you are already too close.

When the pump array changes phase, assume conditions will worsen before they improve. End the dive or secure yourself in a known protected station before the scheduled change.

## Do not trust the apparent surface

Some upper halls have service decks just below the water and air above them. These may look inviting after long immersion. Approach carefully.

The surface may conceal:

- Hanging cables
- Steam jets
- Moving crane rails
- Electrical conduits
- Oil films
- Floating debris
- Low beams and sharp trusses
- Open hatches leading into other flooded levels
- Sudden rain-like condensate that obscures vision
- Pressure-release events that turn the airspace into a hot, loud fog

Before surfacing in an unfamiliar hall, shine a light upward and inspect the full area. Ascend slowly. Do not strike your head on a beam or become tangled in cables at the interface.

If you find a dry platform, do not rush onto it. Check its integrity first. Fresh-looking steel can still be slippery with algae or oil; clean paint can cover a hollow or electrically unsafe structure.

## Machinery etiquette

The workers may regard this place as an unpleasant but ordinary job site. Follow their lead.

If approached by a work boat:

- Make yourself visible early.
- Keep clear of its propellers and wake.
- Do not swim beneath it.
- Use calm, obvious signals.
- Accept a ride or instructions if offered.
- Do not touch gauges, valves, panels, doors, cables, or crane controls.
- Do not enter a marked work zone merely because it appears empty.

A moving crane overhead may be operating automatically. It can produce falling hazards, shifting loads, moving cables, and wakes or turbulence below. Never linger under a crane rail or suspended load.

Do not assume a silent machine is inactive. Machinery may be automated, remotely controlled, or cycling on a schedule. A gate that has been open all morning can close without warning.

The clean, recent condition of the infrastructure is actually a warning: sharp edges remain sharp. You are more likely to be cut on sheet metal, broken glass, machined gears, cable ends, and fresh structural fractures than in an older, softened wreck.

## Wildlife precautions

The wildlife is not described as unusually hostile, but it belongs there more than you do.

### Fish

Large schools can reduce visibility, conceal obstacles, and react collectively to machinery changes. Treat a sudden mass movement of fish as environmental information: something may be changing in the flow or vibration pattern.

Do not feed them outside designated husbandry areas. Do not swim through automated feeding events; a dense feeding boil can become disorienting, and larger fish may arrive quickly.

### Catfish and eels

Large catfish are probably more startling than aggressive, but do not corner one inside a bus, cabinet, tunnel, or enclosed bay. Eels in cable trays and switchgear should be left strictly alone. Their presence may indicate a route through which electrical or mechanical hazards also run.

### Seals

Seals may be curious, especially around platforms and boats. Give them room. Do not attempt contact, do not place hands near their mouths, and do not get between one and an apparent exit route. A seal that came through a coastal discharge system may be stressed, hungry, or navigating unfamiliar conditions.

### Rats and birds

Avoid disturbing nests on high dry beams or feeding areas on catwalks. Rats can contaminate supplies and may bite if trapped. Keep food sealed and do not camp where droppings accumulate.

## Understand the soundscape

In water, the complex’s noise will travel through your bones and mask rather than arriving from a clear direction. Do not navigate by sound alone. A pump that seems directly ahead may be several halls away, while a nearby gate can be acoustically masked by distant machinery.

Still, learn the sensory warning signs:

- A deep rolling concussion may mean a floodgate is cycling.
- A rising vibration in your chest or jaw may precede increased flow.
- Repeated clicking above may indicate an operating crane.
- Sudden quiet can be as concerning as noise: a system may have shut down before changing mode.

If you feel a powerful change in vibration and cannot identify its cause, retreat along your line toward the last known safe point. Do not investigate.

## Where to rest

Do not improvise a camp on a random floating platform or in a dry-looking warehouse loft.

A suitable rest point needs:

- Confirmed breathable air
- A secure, dry or mostly dry surface
- A known route to an exit
- No scheduled flood cycle
- No nearby intake, gate, turbine, crane route, or pressure-release valve
- Reliable light
- Radio contact or a visible pickup position
- Protection from rats and falling condensate

The safest lodging is likely a staffed maintenance station, boat dock, or designated personnel shelter. If the local workers offer a place to wait out a pump phase, take it.

## Psychological preparation

Expect the place to interfere with your sense of scale.

There is no horizon, no shore, no sky, and perhaps no visible ceiling. Reflections create false depth. Drowned buildings can look like upright buildings below the water. A distant lamp may appear reachable when it is kilometers away. You may feel as though you are traveling through a city that has been turned inside out.

This can produce panic even in skilled divers, especially when the ceiling disappears into darkness. Keep tasks simple:

1. Check gas.
2. Check line.
3. Check companion.
4. Check current.
5. Confirm next waypoint.
6. Turn back before you need to.

Do not pursue curiosity past your turnaround point. A perfectly intact, illuminated sign reading **PERSONNEL LIFT** is not an invitation. It may mark a shaft descending farther than your equipment, training, or return gas permits.

---

## Best way to visit

For an ordinary traveler, the safest and most rewarding visit is probably not a deep independent dive. Arrange passage aboard a maintenance boat through one of the broad basins, then make short, supervised excursions from staffed platforms or protected upper decks.

That will let you experience the essential qualities of the place—the industrial rain, the immense reflections, the fish around drowned structures, the pressure of distant pumps, the strange normality of workers checking gauges—without putting yourself in the path of the systems that make the inland sea possible.

Treat the site with the respect due to a living ocean and an operating factory at once. It is not a ruin. It is a machine large enough to have become a landscape.
