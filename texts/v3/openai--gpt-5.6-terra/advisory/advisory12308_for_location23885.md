## Before You Go

Treat the Condensate Recovery Basin as an active industrial disaster zone that happens to be large enough to resemble a landscape. Do not approach it as ruins, wilderness, or a sightseeing location. It is all three in appearance, but it is primarily infrastructure—and infrastructure can injure you without malice, warning, or visible movement.

An ordinary person should not visit alone. Ideally, arrange one of the following:

- A local maintenance escort familiar with Sector Nine’s current drainage routes and alarm meanings.
- Written access clearance, including authorization to cross active conveyor and pump territories.
- A route plan with named shelters, lift shafts, and high-ground locations.
- A reliable extraction plan that does not depend on “following the way back.” Vapor, changed drainage, and closed gates can make a familiar route unrecognizable within hours.

If you cannot obtain a local guide, stay on marked upper causeways only. Do not descend to the marsh shelves, drainage edges, abandoned pump houses, or construction district.

The place is new enough that maps may be wrong, repairs may be unfinished, and warning signs may describe systems no longer functioning as intended. It is old enough for unofficial rules to matter.

## Physical Conditioning and Health

You do not need exceptional athletic ability, but you should be capable of:

- Walking several kilometers over wet grating, uneven rubble, and steep industrial stairs.
- Keeping balance while wearing a respirator and carrying a pack.
- Climbing ladders or emergency stairwells in cold, damp conditions.
- Remaining calm during loud alarms, darkness, sudden steam release, and vibration.
- Handling periods of low visibility without separating from your group.

Do not visit if you have severe asthma, unstable balance, panic triggered by enclosed spaces, sensitivity to industrial noise, or medical vulnerability to cold and damp conditions. The basin constantly alternates between near-freezing metal surfaces and pockets of humid heat. This is exhausting even when it is not immediately dangerous.

Bring any necessary medications in waterproof, impact-resistant containers. Carry more than one day’s supply. A dropped inhaler, soaked insulin kit, or crushed medication packet may be unrecoverable in the lower levels.

Ensure your tetanus protection is current. The valley contains rust, corroded metal, sharp scrap, unknown runoff, and manufactured mud full of abrasive and potentially contaminated material.

## Clothing

Dress in layers that can survive wetness, oil, cold metal, and intermittent heat.

Recommended:

- Waterproof industrial boots with deep non-slip soles, toe protection, puncture-resistant midsoles, and ankle support.
- Two pairs of socks at minimum, preferably wool or synthetic rather than cotton.
- Durable work trousers rather than loose fabric. Avoid trailing hems, scarves, dangling straps, or anything that can catch in rollers, fans, chain runs, or grating.
- Moisture-wicking base layer.
- Insulating mid-layer.
- Waterproof outer shell that is not overly loose.
- Chemical-resistant work gloves plus a spare pair.
- Hard hat or climbing/industrial helmet with a mounted lamp.
- Eye protection: sealed safety goggles are better than ordinary glasses in vapor, spray, and grit.
- Hearing protection. Use filtered earplugs or industrial earmuffs that still permit you to hear alarms if possible.
- High-visibility outer layer, unless local workers explicitly say reflective material is unsafe around particular sensors or machinery.

Do not assume that “warm clothing” alone is sufficient. The problem is wet cold: condensation enters gloves, boots, cuffs, and collar gaps, then the metal structure draws heat away from you. Change out of wet layers as soon as you reach a safe shelter.

## Breathing Protection

The signs saying **NO UNMASKED ENTRY** should be taken literally.

At a minimum, carry a properly fitted respirator rated for particulates and industrial organic vapors. The air contains corrosion dust, insulation fibers, soot, mineral aerosols, exhaust vapor, and possibly chemical residues from filtration or cooling systems.

Better still, use a full-face respirator with replaceable filters, assuming you have trained with one beforehand.

However, understand its limits:

- A standard respirator does **not** make oxygen-poor air safe.
- It does **not** protect you against every unknown gas.
- It may fog, clog, or become difficult to seal in wet conditions.
- You may need to remove it in designated clean shelters; do so only when local conditions are known.

Carry spare cartridges in sealed packaging. If you smell strong solvent, burning plastic, bitter metal, sulfur, ammonia, or an odor suddenly disappears where it ought to be present, leave the area. Odor changes can indicate a ventilation shift, filter failure, or displacement of breathable air.

Never enter a pit, dry duct, tank, low sump, enclosed pump room, or stagnant vapor pocket merely because it “looks clear.” Treat every such area as potentially oxygen-deficient or toxic.

## Essential Equipment

A conservative visitor’s pack should include:

- Waterproof map case and physical map, even if incomplete.
- Compass or local positioning device that does not rely solely on external signals.
- Headlamp mounted to helmet, plus a handheld backup light.
- Spare batteries kept warm and dry.
- Chemical light sticks or low-power marker lights for emergency route marking.
- Whistle; electronic communications can fail under heavy structure and machinery.
- Radio compatible with local maintenance frequencies, if available.
- Personal locator beacon suitable for subterranean or industrial use, if such a system exists in this universe.
- Small first-aid kit with blister care, sterile dressings, bandages, antiseptic, eye wash, and burn treatment.
- Emergency thermal blanket or compact insulated bivy.
- Water in a sealed bottle or bladder. Do not drink basin water, condensate, runoff, or apparently clean water from pipes.
- High-energy food that can be eaten while gloved.
- Spare dry socks, gloves, and a compact towel.
- Multi-tool only if allowed. Do not use it on basin equipment except in a genuine survival emergency.
- Duct tape, cable ties, and a few meters of strong cord—not for climbing or crossing gaps, but for temporary repairs to clothing or gear.
- Notebook and pencil. Electronics do poorly in water and cold; notes about routes, alarms, and shelter locations may matter.

Avoid bringing valuables, delicate fabrics, exposed cameras, unsecured jewelry, or anything difficult to decontaminate. The smell and residue of the basin may remain in belongings long after you leave.

## Navigation: Do Not Trust Your Eyes Alone

The basin has low ceilings, vapor curtains, reflective water, repeated structural motifs, and long distances that make scale difficult to judge. It can create a strong but false feeling that a nearby light, bridge, or shelter is reachable by simply walking toward it.

Do not navigate by reflections.

Specifically, follow the local rule: **do not follow a light reflected in water.** A reflection may belong to moving machinery on another level, a lift shaft, a passing cart, or some lower service space whose geometry does not match your own. A reflected light can lead you toward an edge, channel, or hidden drop.

Instead:

- Use numbered markers, structural labels, and physical route identifiers.
- Confirm your location at every major junction.
- Mark your route only where permitted; some markings may interfere with maintenance procedures.
- Keep visual contact with at least one companion.
- Establish a regrouping rule before entering vapor: for example, stop immediately rather than pushing forward if sightlines vanish.
- Never assume a path is stable because it was stable an hour ago. Water, vibrations, lift operations, and thermal stress can alter access routes.

If the fans reverse and vapor begins moving differently, pause and reassess. This may be normal ventilation cycling, but it can also redistribute fumes, obscure paths, and change the safety of lower terrain.

## Water, Mud, and Marshes

The water is not recreational, potable, or predictable.

It may be cold condensate, oily runoff, mineral-rich drainage, warm discharge, corrosive residue, or a mixture of all of them. Even shallow water can conceal holes, tools, sharp grating, energized equipment, and unstable sediment.

Never:

- Wade into a dark channel.
- Step onto a fog-covered marsh surface.
- Reach into pooled water for dropped gear.
- Touch water near electrical cabinets, cable conduits, damaged insulation, or active machinery.
- Use a drainage channel as a shortcut.
- Sit on wet steps, rails, or pipe housings.

The manufactured mud is especially deceptive. It may look like ordinary silt but can contain fibrous insulation, abrasive powders, corrosion flakes, and chemical residues. It may crust over while remaining liquid underneath. If you step in it, do not struggle wildly; distribute weight, use a stable object or companion for support, and retreat by the route you came.

After contact with basin water or mud, wash exposed skin promptly in a verified clean facility. Do not eat, drink, smoke, or touch your face until you have cleaned your hands.

## Pipes, Ceilings, and Structures

The most important local rule is excellent:

**Do not stand beneath a dry pipe.**

A pipe that is normally wet but has gone dry may be depressurized, blocked, heating, freezing, emptying, or preparing to rupture. It may release steam, hot water, cold condensate, chemical waste, debris, or simply fail structurally. “Dry” is not reassuring here; it is a change in the expected condition.

Also:

- Do not lean on railings unless they are visibly maintained and rated for use.
- Do not use pipes as handholds, ladders, bridges, or seats.
- Do not pass beneath suspended loads, freight lifts, conveyor bridges, crane arms, or hanging chains.
- Avoid the undersides of stairways and shaded pump-house walls where frost accumulates.
- Watch for ice even where the air feels warm.
- Step deliberately on grating: water films and oily residue can make it unexpectedly slick.
- Do not enter visibly slumped embankments, fresh erosion fans, or areas beneath damaged spillways.
- Stay clear of exposed rebar and newly cut channels. They are evidence of active structural change, not scenery.

The valley is unfinished. Fresh construction is often more dangerous than old decay because the hazards have not yet settled into familiar patterns.

## Machinery Etiquette

Assume every machine is active, remotely controlled, or capable of starting without warning.

Conveyor bridges may run with no visible cargo. Pumps may engage after long silence. Freight lifts can move through shafts with little notice. Automated welders can activate in an apparently vacant work zone. Maintenance systems may cycle according to schedules no visitor can see.

Keep away from:

- Conveyor rollers and belts.
- Lift shafts and platform edges.
- Pump intake zones.
- Valve clusters.
- Electrical cabinets.
- Heat-exchanger mesh.
- Turbine conduits.
- Areas marked for robotic welding or automated handling.
- Any machine making a new sound.

If you hear a bearing whine, metal grinding, rhythmic impact, or a pressure whistle that changes pitch, do not investigate. Move away from the machinery, preferably toward known high ground or a designated shelter.

Never “help” by clearing a jammed valve, moving debris from a drain, opening a cabinet, or cutting away growth from machinery. The basin’s systems may depend on states that look wrong to outsiders.

## Alarm Protocol

Memorize the workers’ alarm rules before entering:

- **One siren:** Continue working—but for a visitor, this means stay alert, stop wandering, and identify the nearest shelter or higher route.
- **Two sirens:** Find height immediately. Leave drainage channels, low shelves, marsh edges, and underpasses. Expect water movement, flooding, discharge, or a shift in atmospheric conditions.
- **Three sirens:** Hold something bolted down and do not look up.

The third instruction should be followed exactly. Do not look up to identify the danger. It may be overhead movement, falling debris, a pressure event, swinging loads, ceiling discharge, or something else that causes people to freeze, stumble, or abandon stable support. Grab a rated railing, fixed ladder, structural column, or bolted fixture. Do not grab a pipe, cable, moving chain, or anything that might be part of a system under load.

Before setting foot in the basin, ask a local worker:

1. What does the current alarm system sound like?
2. Are one, two, and three sirens still reliable distinctions?
3. Where is the nearest designated high ground?
4. Which shelters are currently pressurized, stocked, and unlocked?
5. What alarm indicates evacuation rather than flooding?
6. Is there a shift-change or test-siren schedule?

In an industrial place, old folk rules are valuable—but only if you know which systems have changed since the last time someone relied on them.

## The Pale Growth and Other Life

Do not touch, collect, burn, eat, or remove the cable moss, translucent fronds, black brush colonies, or pale growth on drainage markers.

The instruction **DO NOT FEED THE FILTER GROWTH** should also be taken seriously. Do not pour drinks, food scraps, chemicals, batteries, biological samples, or “offerings” onto any growth. Even if it seems biological, it may be functioning as part of filtration, corrosion control, sensing, electrical grounding, or some poorly understood hybrid ecology.

Most importantly:

**Never remove pale growth from drainage markers.**

It may indicate flow direction, chemical condition, pressure boundaries, safe footing, active filtration, or a maintenance state no one has successfully formalized. The fact that local workers leave it in place is enough.

Regarding animals:

- Do not handle the white cabinet insects.
- Do not attempt to photograph them closely with open electrical cabinets.
- Do not corner rat-shaped things on pipe runs.
- Do not catch the dark “fish” in the lower channels.
- Assume anything living near electrical or filtration systems may be conductive, contaminated, monitored, territorial, or integral to the facility.

Whether the fish are fish or failed sensors is not a question you need to settle before visiting.

## Interacting With Workers

Workers are rare because the environment is large and hostile, not because it is abandoned. If you encounter a crew:

- Make yourself visible from a distance.
- Do not approach moving equipment or a work zone until acknowledged.
- Keep your respirator and eye protection on unless instructed otherwise in a safe area.
- Ask before taking photographs, recording sound, or mapping equipment.
- Follow short instructions immediately; crews may not have time to explain.
- Do not assume they are employees of one organization. The basin may contain contractors, municipal crews, industrial operators, automated-system tenders, and people whose relationship to the place is less formal.

Useful questions to ask:

- “Which way is high ground from here?”
- “Are the drains expected to move?”
- “Is this shelter safe and unlocked?”
- “Is that vapor normal?”
- “Which lights are route lights, and which are machine lights?”
- “Are there any current exclusion zones?”
- “What does the pale growth indicate in this section?”

Do not ask workers to explain every strange feature while they are managing equipment. Save curiosity for a safe shelter.

## The New Construction District

The far district of open frames, crane arms, dangling cables, weld flashes, standing water, and frost should be considered off-limits unless you have explicit authorization and an escort.

It combines all basin hazards at once:

- Incomplete floors and unprotected edges.
- Active automated welding.
- Suspended materials.
- Energized but unfinished cabling.
- Flooded lower spaces.
- Unmarked structural changes.
- New growth occupying seams before the construction crews understand it.
- Routes that may not exist in any available plan.

The absence of windows does not mean openness; it means exposure. Buildings there may trap vapor, concentrate fumes, or channel sound so badly that alarms become confusing.

Do not shelter in an unfinished frame. Do not take photographs under crane activity. Do not step through an opening simply because it resembles a doorway.

## Emergency Priorities

If something goes wrong, your priorities are:

1. Get out of immediate machinery, water, and overhead-load zones.
2. Reach known height if flooding or drainage movement is possible.
3. Find a designated shelter or staffed maintenance station.
4. Account for companions.
5. Communicate your location using structural markers, not vague descriptions.
6. Preserve warmth and breathing protection.
7. Do not split up unless remaining together is immediately more dangerous.

If lost in vapor:

- Stop moving before you walk into water, machinery, or a drop.
- Call or radio for your group.
- Use a whistle in deliberate intervals.
- Listen for known fixed sounds: a pump station, lift bell, marked ventilation route.
- Do not chase lights.
- Do not descend in search of an exit.
- Move only after you have a confirmed direction.

If you fall into contaminated water, get out by the nearest safe stable route, remove soaked outer layers when possible, rinse in a designated decontamination station, and seek medical evaluation. Hypothermia and chemical exposure may develop after you feel initially fine.

If someone is trapped near machinery, energized water, a shaft, or a steam release, do not rush in unprotected. Raise an alarm. The basin is designed around hazards that can turn a rescuer into a second casualty.

## Mental Preparation

The basin will feel alive, but not in a simple biological sense. It has weather, seasons of frost and vapor, drainage patterns, growth, migration-like movements of machinery, and rules learned through experience rather than written engineering. It may provoke a strong desire to interpret everything: the fish, the pale growth, the breathing fans, the distant lights, the empty shelters.

Resist that desire while you are inside.

You do not need to decide whether it is a machine, an ecosystem, a ruin, a construction site, or a living industrial organ. For a visitor, it is enough to recognize that it is actively becoming something, and that becoming process does not make room for carelessness.

Go equipped, guided, masked, warm, and humble. Stay on maintained routes. Respect every alarm. Keep to high ground when told. And if the basin seems to offer you an intriguing shortcut through fog, water, or a newly opened service way, assume it is not offering anything at all.
