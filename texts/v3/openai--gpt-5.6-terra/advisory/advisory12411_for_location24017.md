## Preparation for the Plateau

Prepare as though you are visiting a cold, high-altitude, low-resource country whose geography is reliable only in the short term. The people are likely practical, observant, and generous within the limits of what survival permits—but a visitor who treats the plateau as a wilderness attraction, archaeological site, or dimensional curiosity will become a problem very quickly.

Your first priority is not adventure. It is becoming a person who can be sheltered, fed, accounted for, and moved when the land decides to revise itself.

### Before arrival: establish a safe point of entry

Do not arrive alone in open country if you can avoid it. Arrange arrival at, or within easy sight of, an inhabited settlement. If your transit device permits a destination lock, choose:

- A communal hall, gatehouse, market walk, or known traveler’s shelter.
- A low, inhabited city rather than a ruin.
- A location well away from the Still Basin, old exposed streets, deep melt channels, and cliff edges.
- Daylight, ideally after local morning but before the usual period of weather change.

Do not plan to materialize inside a building. Structures may be partly subterranean, low-roofed, or subject to subtle terrain shifts. An apparently empty patch of ground may cover water channels, storage rooms, buried streets, or a roof designed to bear turf but not a sudden human arrival.

If you have any means of communicating ahead across worlds, tell the settlement:
- Your intended arrival point and time.
- That you are human and need acclimatization.
- Whether you carry medicine, unusual tools, or anything with lights, motors, radios, or unfamiliar materials.
- That you understand you will not travel beyond their bell range without local guidance.

A local escort is more valuable than almost any piece of wilderness equipment.

## Physical conditioning and medical preparation

This plateau begins above ordinary weather and likely well above comfortable oxygen levels. Even if its atmosphere is breathable, expect altitude sickness, cold stress, dehydration, and fatigue from walking over uneven ground.

Before visiting, prepare for:

- Several days of sustained uphill walking.
- Cold wind combined with wet conditions.
- Thin air and slow recovery after exertion.
- Long distances between settlements.
- A landscape where direct routes may become indirect overnight.

Bring, if your physiology requires it:

- Acclimatization medication appropriate to altitude, after consulting a medical professional familiar with your body and any cross-world exposures.
- A pulse oximeter, if it will function reliably, but do not trust its readings over your own symptoms or local experience.
- Headache, nausea, diarrhea, pain, allergy, and anti-inflammatory medications.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics only for emergencies and only with knowledge of their risks; you do not want to introduce an organism or treatment regime casually into a small, isolated population.
- Blister treatment, foot powder, antiseptic, bandages, wound closure strips, and a splinting kit.
- Water purification equipment that does not leave toxic residues.
- A compact emergency oxygen supply if possible—but do not use it as an excuse to overexert yourself.

Know the warning signs of altitude illness: persistent severe headache, confusion, stumbling, breathlessness while resting, chest tightness, blue lips, wet cough, or strange exhaustion. On this plateau, confusion may also be interpreted as a response to local spatial anomalies. Either way, treat it seriously. Descend, shelter, and seek local help.

## Clothing: dress for cold rain, wind, stone, and long silence

You need layers more than bulk. The plateau’s rain is fine, cold, and nearly horizontal. Its ground can be wet, abrasive, and unexpectedly unstable. Bring subdued, repairable clothing; loud colors and glossy synthetic fabrics may make you conspicuous or culturally unsettling, though high-visibility gear is still wise for emergencies.

A good kit includes:

- Moisture-wicking base layers.
- Warm insulating layers that retain warmth when damp: wool or modern equivalents.
- A windproof, waterproof shell with a serious hood and closures that can be operated with numb hands.
- Waterproof over-trousers or a tough outer layer.
- Two or three pairs of insulated gloves: one work pair, one dry reserve pair, and one fine-dexterity pair.
- Warm hat, neck gaiter, and face covering for wind-driven rain.
- Durable boots with excellent ankle support, non-slip soles, and enough room for thick socks.
- Multiple pairs of wool socks kept dry in sealed bags.
- Gaiters: wet black powder, moss, loose mineral soil, and shallow frost basins may all find their way into your boots.
- Simple knee and elbow protection if you expect to cross ruins or work during a relocation.

Avoid relying on umbrellas. The wind will make them useless, and a tall, rigid object can be dangerous near shifting ground or in a culture accustomed to reading silhouettes across long distances.

Bring a compact sleeping system rated substantially below the expected temperatures, even if you expect hospitality. A settlement may need to move, a road may change, or a communal hall may be full during a weather event.

## Water and food: carry redundancy, but respect local scarcity

Water is present but seldom obvious. It can lie under translucent vegetation, behind stone, within thin wall-runs, or as nightly ice over shallow basins. Do not assume a green surface means safe water, and do not break into plant mats or condensation works without permission. These may be a city’s carefully managed reservoir.

Carry:

- A durable insulated water bottle.
- A collapsible reserve bladder.
- A reliable filter suited to sediment and microorganisms.
- Chemical treatment as backup, chosen to be compatible with very cold water.
- A small metal cup or pot for melting ice and boiling water.
- Electrolyte salts.

Assume that local water systems are communal infrastructure. Ask before filling your containers. Offer to help maintain catch-ditches or condensation fields rather than merely drawing from them.

Bring compact, high-calorie food for at least a week beyond your intended travel plan:
- Nuts, dried fats, dense grain bars, jerky or vegetarian equivalents, dried fruit, hard cheese if your health allows it.
- Foods that can be shared without requiring unfamiliar preparation.
- A small supply of comfort food is worthwhile; life on a cold plateau gives morale practical value.

Do not introduce seeds, soil, live cultures, insects, or fresh plant material. This is an ecosystem with no animal life and a very particular botanical balance. Even a harmless-seeming apple core, yeast packet, houseplant seed, or soil-stained boot could be an ecological event.

Clean all gear before arrival. Pay special attention to boot treads, tent stakes, clothing seams, bags, and food packaging.

## Navigation: maps are historical documents, not guarantees

Bring navigation tools, but assume none of them is sufficient alone.

Useful equipment:
- Compass.
- Paper maps in waterproof cases.
- Notebook and pencils; pencils work better than pens in wet cold.
- Mechanical watch.
- Altimeter/barometer if calibrated.
- Handheld GPS or other positional equipment, if it functions in this world.
- Rangefinder or binoculars.
- Bright emergency signal cloth or flare substitute, used only with local agreement.
- Plenty of cord, marker tabs, and reflective strips for temporary route marking.

Do not count on stars, sun, horizon, or compass bearings remaining meaningful during local sky events. The sky may lose depth; the sun may behave incorrectly; sound may not move normally; distant landmarks may shift without drama.

The inhabitants’ horizon-watchers have methods refined over generations. Learn them. A local may care more about the angle of a particular escarpment, the shape of a dark seam in the stone, or the sound beneath a path than about your GPS coordinates. Treat that knowledge as technical expertise, not folklore.

When traveling:
- Never leave sight or hearing range of a settlement bell without permission and a guide.
- Travel in a group, ideally with one person assigned solely to observe terrain changes.
- Note departure time, weather, intended destination, and fallback shelters.
- Recheck bearings frequently, especially after rain, dusk, or periods of silence.
- Do not camp in dry channels, shallow basins, beneath overhangs, on black powder deposits, or near old foundations.
- If a ridge, path, or distant building seems not to occupy the position it should, stop. Do not rush to “beat” the change.

The land’s movement is described as quiet and gradual, which makes it more dangerous than a dramatic quake. You may not feel it happening. A road can become a slope. A wall can become a buried line. A familiar route can acquire a drainage cut, fissure, or impossible detour while you are looking elsewhere.

## Shelter and campcraft

If possible, stay indoors in a settlement. Plateau architecture exists because local people have spent generations learning where wind, water, and stone are least hostile.

If you must shelter outside:
- Choose a site with local approval.
- Favor shallow protected hollows only after checking drainage, subsurface water, and ground movement history.
- Use a low-profile shelter capable of being securely anchored to stone or weighted with slabs.
- Avoid depending on long stakes; the soil may be shallow, powdery, wet, or concealing old structures.
- Keep gear packed so it can be moved quickly.
- Establish a night watch when camped beyond a city, not because of animals, but because terrain and sky conditions can change quietly.
- Do not build large fires. Fuel is likely limited and may be culturally regulated.

A small, efficient stove may be useful, but introduce it cautiously. An engine-like roar, bright flame, or unfamiliar fuel smell can be alarming in a society accustomed to low communal fires. Bring quiet equipment, and ask before using it near buildings, moss gardens, or resin-cloth roofs.

## Conduct in settlements

The people of the plateau are likely accustomed to change, loss, careful maintenance, and communal coordination. Their material simplicity should not be mistaken for lack of sophistication. Their cities survive because nearly every object, path, water source, and structure has a reason behind it.

Offer value in forms that do not destabilize their society.

Good offerings include:
- Labor: carrying stone, repairing roof layers, cleaning drainage channels, helping move stored goods.
- Medical assistance, only when requested and with humility.
- Durable needles, plain thread, repair patches, simple hand tools, high-quality cord, lenses, water-safe containers, notebooks, and pencils.
- Knowledge that is useful without creating dependence: sanitation techniques, first aid, insulation principles, recordkeeping methods, crop preservation, non-invasive surveying.
- Careful maps and observations—provided you do not falsely imply permanence.

Avoid casually giving:
- Weapons.
- Industrial machinery.
- Radio equipment, surveillance devices, or powerful lights.
- Seeds, animals, microbes, live cultures, or biological samples.
- Precious metals in quantities that disrupt local economies.
- “Miracle” technology requiring replacement parts, fuels, expertise, or a supply chain they do not possess.
- Promises of resettlement, rescue, or contact with other worlds unless you can honor them transparently and ethically.

Ask before recording people, homes, rituals, bells, maps, sky phenomena, or city movement. In a place where the ground itself may preserve traces of former inhabitants, records can carry unusual emotional and political weight.

If invited into a communal hall, observe how people store boots, tools, wet garments, and water vessels. Follow those systems exactly. In a low-resource environment, disorder is not merely rude; it can damage someone’s winter reserves or water access.

## Bells, silence, and emergency signals

Learn the local bell code before leaving the first settlement.

The bells are not ornamental. They may communicate:
- Terrain movement.
- Weather shifts.
- A missing traveler.
- Need for labor during a move.
- Water emergencies.
- News transferred across apparently impossible distances.
- Warnings relating to the Still Basin or sky events.

Do not ring a bell out of curiosity. Do not strike unfamiliar metal or stone instruments to see what they sound like. In a landscape where sound can vanish, travel strangely, or return later, a casual sound may become an alarm somewhere else.

Carry a whistle, but use it only if locals agree it is appropriate. Your own voice may carry unpredictably. Establish visual signals for your group: cloth flags, hand signs, lamp shutters, or reflective panels.

If you hear an answer to a sound you did not make, especially one that seems delayed or comes from the sky, do not immediately follow it. Report it. The safest assumption is that it is meaningful to someone local, even if nobody can fully explain it.

## Ruins: look, do not claim

The abandoned cities are not empty in the ordinary sense. They may contain unstable structures, concealed streets, altered gravity or rain behavior, temporal echoes, and cultural remains that are probably regarded as ancestral, mournful, hazardous, or all three.

Do not enter a ruin alone.

Do not:
- Remove artifacts.
- Dig into exposed streets.
- Camp in abandoned houses.
- Follow sounds of domestic activity.
- Attempt to record or reproduce echoes without permission.
- Touch “impossible” architectural features merely to test whether they are real.
- Step through rain that appears to be falling upward or through shadows that arrive before their sources.

If you hear tools, bowls, footsteps, or other traces of former habitation, stop at the threshold. Tell your guide. The correct response may be ritualized, practical, or simply to leave.

Even if the effect is not dangerous, it is not yours. A former home remains someone’s former home.

## Sky anomalies

Treat unusual celestial events as weather hazards first and metaphysical invitations second.

If clouds flatten against the sky, the sun appears in the wrong place, or stars seem to descend into the land:

1. Do not navigate by the sky.
2. Do not separate from your group.
3. Avoid cliffs, basins, ruins, and unfamiliar paths.
4. Find known shelter if possible.
5. Record only basic observations: time, location, weather, direction, duration, physical symptoms.
6. Follow local procedures without argument.

Do not assume an altered sky means you can return to your own world, communicate with it, or safely cross into another reflected or apparent landscape. Dimensional travelers are particularly vulnerable to this temptation. The plateau may present a view, reflection, echo, or overlap—not a usable passage.

If your travel equipment reacts to an event, resist acting impulsively. Power it down or shield it if that is safe, mark your location, and consult locals. Their municipal records may contain centuries of relevant observation.

## The Still Basin: prepare to refuse the obvious urge

The Still Basin is the place most likely to attract a multiverse traveler and the place you should approach with the greatest restraint.

Its nighttime reflection may show other worlds. It may show your world. It may show a world that resembles yours closely enough to defeat caution. Figures in the reflected country may appear to see you. Objects placed on its surface vanish and are sometimes exchanged for objects from elsewhere.

None of this establishes that it is a portal, that it is safe to cross, or that the figures are people you should contact.

If you visit at all:

- Go only with explicit permission from nearby communities.
- Go with local guides and follow their route, stopping points, and rules.
- Do not visit alone, at night, or during cold rain.
- Do not step onto the black stone unless invited to do so by people who understand the local custom.
- Do not throw, drop, or offer objects casually.
- Do not offer biological material: blood, hair, saliva, skin cells, food scraps, seeds, living organisms, or tissue samples.
- Do not offer devices that transmit, record autonomously, emit signals, or could be interpreted as surveillance or weapons.
- Do not call out to reflected figures.
- Do not attempt to touch the reflection, test its depth, shine lasers into it, fly drones over it, or send probes across it.
- Do not retrieve returned objects without protective handling and local consultation.

The annual offerings—seeds, broken tools, maps, names of the dead—are not a tourist ritual. If invited to witness, remain quiet and let residents determine whether a visitor’s participation is appropriate. Do not offer an item merely because it seems symbolic. A thing given to the basin may be permanently gone; an exchanged item may carry obligations or dangers across worlds.

Quarantine any object returned by the basin. Place it in a sealed inert container. Photograph it only if that is culturally acceptable. Do not bring it into a settlement, expose it to local soil or water, take it apart, consume it, plant it, wear it, or activate it. If you later carry it home, maintain strict biological and informational containment.

Most importantly: do not let your knowledge of multiversal travel make you overconfident. The basin may not operate according to any transit system you understand.

## What to bring as a traveler, beyond survival gear

A plateau-appropriate pack should emphasize repair, patience, and accountability:

- Waterproof notebook, pencils, spare erasers.
- Paper maps and blank graph paper.
- Sewing kit, heavy thread, waxed cord, repair tape, patches.
- Compact hand tools: folding saw, small pry bar, awl, knife, sharpening stone.
- Spare boot laces and durable straps.
- Headlamp with red-light option, spare batteries, and a mechanical backup lantern.
- Simple field microscope or hand lens, used responsibly and not on living local specimens without permission.
- Water containers and purification equipment.
- Compact medical kit.
- High-calorie food reserve.
- Insulated sleeping gear.
- A quiet, sturdy pack that can be carried over rough ground.
- Small trade goods selected in consultation with your host settlement.
- Sealed bags for waste and contamination control.

Pack out all non-biodegradable waste. More importantly, do not assume that something biodegradable belongs there. Food scraps, soap residues, human waste, and fibers can all matter in an ecosystem this sparse.

Use sanitation practices that locals approve. Human waste may need to be packed out or handled through designated systems; do not bury it casually in shallow soil near water channels, moss beds, or settlements.

## Psychological preparation

The plateau will probably feel lonely even among people.

There are no birds, insects, grazing animals, barking dogs, buzzing flies, or ordinary signs that an unattended place is alive. Silence may be vast enough to make you notice your own breathing. A distant city may seem close for days and remain unreachable. The ground may alter without warning. Familiar celestial cues may become unreliable. Ruins may produce sounds that resemble human life without containing it.

Prepare for:
- Isolation and sensory deprivation.
- Anxiety caused by slow environmental change.
- The temptation to assign intention to terrain, sound, or reflected worlds.
- Grief or unease around old settlements.
- A deep sense of being observed when nothing is visibly present.

Keep a routine: eat, drink, check feet, check companions, log weather, log direction, sleep. In strange places, routine is not denial; it is a survival instrument.

Travel with people you trust, and agree in advance on reality-check procedures. If one of you sees an impossible landmark, hears a familiar voice from a ruin, or becomes convinced the basin is showing home, the group should stop, record the report, confirm physical safety, and seek local guidance before acting.

## The central rule

The plateau’s people have adapted to a world in which permanence is provisional. Respect that.

Do not arrive expecting to conquer distance, solve mysteries, extract artifacts, or impose stable coordinates on a land that has outlasted such confidence. Learn where the water is hidden. Help carry a roof frame when a district must move. Listen when the bells sound. Mark your path lightly. Leave no living thing behind.

If you can do that, the plateau may permit you to see one of the rarest kinds of civilization: not one built against an unstable world, but one built with enough humility to move when the world moves.
