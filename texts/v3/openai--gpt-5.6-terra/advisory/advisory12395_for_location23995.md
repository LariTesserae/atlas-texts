## Preparation Brief: Visiting the Kiln

Treat the Kiln as an active geothermal ecosystem, not a ruin, wilderness overlook, or survivable “hot cave.” Its greatest dangers are not any single predator. They are dehydration, poisonous and oxygen-poor air, darkness, unstable ground, periodic pressure reversals, and the fact that the entire basin is already occupied by life that knows exactly where it is safe to stand.

An ordinary human should not enter without a reliable way back out.

### The minimum requirement: independent life support

Do not rely on the Kiln for air, water, light, footing, shelter, food, or navigational reference.

Bring:

- **A sealed breathing system**, ideally a positive-pressure respirator or compact supplied-air rig, not merely a filter mask. Sulfur compounds, carbon dioxide, mineral vapor, ash, and low oxygen are all likely. The air may be breathable in patches and deadly a few steps away.
- **At least two independent air supplies.** Assume one canister will be damaged, fouled by ash, or lost during a pressure event.
- **More water than seems reasonable**, carried in protected containers. The air will desiccate lips, eyes, lungs, and skin rapidly. Electrolytes are essential.
- **A sealed hydration interface** that lets you drink without unmasking.
- **Full eye protection**, preferably a face shield or goggles rated against grit, hot ash, and irritating vapor. Contact lenses are a poor choice.
- **Heat-resistant but breathable clothing.** The Kiln is warm, but its thermal danger is uneven: one step may be merely hot, the next may be above the point at which boots soften or skin burns. Wear layered flame-resistant fabric under abrasion-resistant outer clothing.
- **Hard-soled geothermal boots** with high ankle support, heat insulation, puncture resistance, and soles that grip smooth obsidian. Carry spare laces and repair tape; hot stone destroys adhesives and rubber.
- **Gloves** with both heat protection and tactile sensitivity. You may need to distinguish ordinary stone from vent crust by touch, but never touch a surface bare-handed.
- **A hood or neck seal.** Fine ash gets everywhere and can turn every joint of your clothing into sandpaper.

Do not bring exposed electronics if alternatives exist. Ash, sulfur, heat, and static-charged grit are unfriendly to screens, fans, ports, and delicate mechanisms. Use ruggedized, sealed devices, paper maps in waterproof sleeves, mechanical compasses if they work in the local reality, and physical signal markers.

## Light and navigation

The Kiln is not simply dark. It is visually deceptive.

The rock glows in fractures. Animals resemble stone. Stone may be moving. Warm vapor can redden the ground without making it safe. Your eyes will keep trying to convert dim shapes into familiar terrain and will often be wrong.

Carry several forms of light:

- A **low-output red or amber lamp** for routine movement.
- A **strong directional lamp** for examining a route ahead.
- A **downward-facing foot light** or illuminated walking pole.
- Chemical light markers or reflective tabs for marking your return route.
- A **thermal viewer**, if available, but do not trust it alone. In the Kiln, heat is everywhere; the most dangerous openings may show up only as subtle thermal gradients.

Avoid broad floodlighting. It may distress or provoke the basin’s inhabitants, ruin your own dark adaptation, and make you conspicuous to vibration-hunting animals that associate sudden activity with prey or intrusion.

Mark your route frequently, but use removable, inert markers. Do not hammer pitons into the walls, spray paint the stone, or leave plastic tape to become part of the ecosystem. Small magnetic tags, weighted ceramic markers, or low-profile reflective stones are better—provided the local geology does not make them misleading.

Most importantly: **do not navigate by “open ground.”** Open ground may be a vent field, a thin crust over a fissure, or the only space where an animal expects another animal to run.

## Movement: walk as though the ground is listening

It probably is.

The blind predators hunt by vibration, and every other animal has adapted to minimize unnecessary noise. A human stride, dropped tool, shouted conversation, or rhythmic walking pattern will be highly conspicuous.

Move with these rules:

1. **No running.**
2. **No jumping between rocks unless escape is already impossible.**
3. **Set each foot down slowly and completely.**
4. **Use a probe pole before committing weight to ash-covered ground.**
5. **Keep your load close to your body; do not let equipment clatter.**
6. **Avoid repetitive, heavy rhythms.** Pause irregularly rather than marching.
7. **Do not drag gear.**
8. **Never sit or kneel beside a warm seam without verifying it is not an active vent.**

A three-point stance—two feet and a pole, or two hands and one foot on steep surfaces—is safer than a normal hiking posture. But remember that placing a hand on “rock” may mean placing it on the plated back of a grazing creature.

If you must cross among dense animals, do not try to part them by force. Wait for a natural current in their movement. Follow the direction of their bodies at a distance. The Kiln’s creatures have local knowledge encoded in habit: where not to stand, when to climb, and which channels lead away from danger.

You are not smarter than thousands of animals that have survived there for generations.

## Learn the warning signs of a deep pulse

The deep pulse is the central hazard of the Kiln. It is not merely a heat wave. It is a cycle of pressure, heat, movement, and then reversal. The reversal can pull loose objects—and perhaps people—down through vents.

You must identify an approaching pulse before it becomes obvious.

Possible signs include:

- A sudden communal stillness or tightening among nearby animals.
- Ash runners disappearing into cracks.
- Grazers raising or orienting their horn-crowns toward a common direction.
- Membrane-winged scavengers climbing higher and flattening themselves against walls.
- A pressure sensation in ears, sinuses, or chest.
- Changes in the pitch of fumarole sounds.
- Fine ash lifting rather than settling.
- A smell of hot metal intensifying.
- The ground becoming subtly warmer through your boots.

When these begin:

- **Move away from all vents immediately.**
- Do not stand in channels, depressions, or ash drifts.
- Do not shelter behind a low boulder if it sits in a vent field.
- Seek a stable wall-adjacent position only if it is clearly outside the zone used by climbing scavengers and has no cracks or breath-holes at its base.
- Secure every loose item.
- Lower your center of gravity and protect your face and breathing apparatus from grit.
- Do not anchor yourself over or near a vent. A rope can become a line that pulls you into the opening if its anchor fails or the ground shifts.
- Remain in place until the pressure cycle has clearly ended and the local animals resume ordinary motion.

If you see animals abandoning a location, do not stop to study why. Leave in the same general direction.

## The vents are not campfires

Do not warm yourself beside them. Do not cook over them. Do not dispose of waste in them. Do not drop samples, coins, stones, or ritual offerings into them.

A vent is a mouth connected to an unknown depth. The description of small creatures being “taken by the breath” suggests active pressure cycling, possibly with narrow passages that can widen suddenly. The crust around a vent may be load-bearing only until it is not.

Maintain a generous exclusion radius around every visible vent and every suspiciously warm crack. In darkness, use your probe, thermal sensor, and the behavior of grazing animals—but do not assume that an animal feeding near a vent means it is safe for a human. Their feet, hides, senses, and reaction speed are adapted to conditions yours are not.

If you must sample vent material, do so remotely with a long tool, from a stable position, and only between pulses.

## Wildlife etiquette: do not become the loud, pale, soft animal

The basin is crowded. You will never be alone in it, even when you cannot see anything moving.

### Ash runners

They are likely the least directly dangerous, but their density can be hazardous. Do not step into braided streams if you can avoid it. Crushing them may trigger defensive behavior from larger creatures, attract scavengers, alter local traffic patterns, or simply leave you standing on unstable moving bodies.

If a stream crosses your route, wait for a thinner interval or step across only where the ground beneath is visible and firm.

### Plated grazers

These creatures may be heavy, armored, and nearly indistinguishable from stone. They are probably not aggressive, but a herd compressing around a vent is a moving wall of mass and sharp horn structures.

Never wedge yourself between a grazer and a heat source. Never approach its head, horn-crown, young, or feeding site. If a herd begins to compress, retreat before it completes the formation. Once packed together, they may be unable or unwilling to make room for you.

Do not interpret stillness as permission. In the Kiln, stillness is often alertness.

### Kiln gulls and other scavengers

Do not feed them. Do not wave fabric, shine lights upward, or expose food smells. Their attention to heat-radiance may make your warm suit and exhaled air interesting. A single scavenger is unlikely to be a severe threat; a gathering of them on a narrow ledge could become one.

### Blind vibration-hunting predators

These require the most caution.

Do not try to frighten them with shouting. Sound may attract them. Do not throw stones. Do not use an ultrasonic deterrent unless you know their sensory range; it may be an invitation rather than a defense.

If one is nearby:

- Stop moving heavily.
- Avoid sudden stomping or scrambling.
- Put stable rock, an arch pillar, or a steep obstacle between yourself and it if possible.
- Retreat slowly along ground you have already tested.
- Do not climb into a crack or low crevice; that is exactly the kind of terrain in which a many-jointed hunter may be more mobile than you.
- If forced to use a deterrent, prefer a brief burst of light, heat, odor, or compressed air over impact and noise—but test nothing for the first time in the basin.

The predators appear patient. Do not mistake a motionless shape for an inactive one.

## The arches: treat them as neutral ground

The three black-stone arches are the closest thing described to a social institution. Animals use them constantly; predators do not attack within them; no animal fights there.

That does not mean they are safe for humans. It means they are important.

Do not:

- Camp inside them.
- Block the passage.
- Leave equipment in them.
- Take samples from them.
- Mark them.
- Make loud noise in them.
- Use them as a defensive kill zone.
- Enter them during a pulse without observing how the animals respond first.

If you need to pass through, do so quietly, steadily, and without interrupting traffic. Allow animals to use the arches ahead of you. If a predator waits outside one, do not attempt to exploit the apparent rule by lingering within the passage; you may disrupt a behavior you do not understand, and the rule may apply only to native life.

The arches may be structurally ancient, culturally meaningful, ecologically important, or all three. In a place with no spare space, any place where violence is suspended is not yours to test.

## Never camp in the basin if you can avoid it

The Kiln has no truly empty ground. A human campsite would occupy a route, a feeding site, a shelter crack, a heat gradient, or a pressure-safe zone used by animals.

The best plan is a short, purposeful transit or survey with an exit window. Establish your main camp outside the basin—above the ash lid if possible, or in a protected dimensional staging area.

If an overnight stay is unavoidable, choose a position only after observing at least one full pulse cycle from that area. Your shelter should be:

- Elevated off the ground on broad, heat-insulated feet.
- Anchored to verified stable rock, not to loose blocks or arch structures.
- Sealed against ash and gas.
- Equipped with independent air and water.
- Low-profile and dark, without constantly running lights.
- Positioned away from animal routes and at least two escape paths from vents.
- Equipped with a quiet alarm system that detects heat increase, CO₂, sulfur gases, ground vibration, and pressure change.

Do not use an open flame. In a sulfurous, oxygen-variable environment, fire is both unreliable and foolish.

## Medical preparation

Bring a medical kit designed for burns, dehydration, eye contamination, respiratory distress, cuts, and panic in confined darkness.

Include:

- Sterile saline for eye flushing.
- Burn dressings and hydrogel.
- Electrolyte solution.
- Skin barrier ointment for lips and nose.
- Antihistamines and bronchodilator medication if medically appropriate.
- Antiemetics; sulfur gases and heat can cause nausea.
- Pain control that does not significantly impair coordination.
- Bandages suitable for abrasive mineral grit.
- A compact emergency oxygen supply even if you already have breathing gas.
- A means of immobilizing an ankle or wrist; obsidian terrain is unforgiving.
- A buddy extraction harness.

Do not expose an open wound to the black vent films, animal secretions, ash slurry, or mineral deposits. The ecology may be alien in the literal sense. “Not plant, not exactly fungus” is enough reason to assume unknown biochemical and infectious risk.

Decontaminate before returning to other worlds. Seal boots, clothing, samples, and waste. Do not casually carry a heat-feeding microbial film into a station, forest, apartment, or spacecraft.

## Team discipline

Do not visit alone.

A minimum team of three is preferable:

- One person moving and observing forward terrain.
- One person monitoring the group, rear approach, and wildlife behavior.
- One person handling navigation, atmosphere readings, and emergency retreat decisions.

Use hand signals, touch signals, or short low-volume radio bursts. Continuous conversation is both noisy and mentally exhausting. Establish signals for:

- Stop.
- Pulse warning.
- Vent.
- Predator.
- Herd compression.
- Bad air.
- Retreat.
- Injury.
- Lost route.
- No-light movement.

Before entering, agree on an absolute retreat threshold: a gas reading, temperature level, pulse frequency, injury, navigation failure, or wildlife change that ends the visit immediately. The Kiln’s atmosphere encourages fascination and poor judgment. Make the decision before you are tired, thirsty, overheated, and surrounded.

## Psychological preparation

The Kiln may create a particular kind of stress: not emptiness, but **crowded isolation**.

There are creatures everywhere, but none are familiar. There is little horizon, no sky, no water, no vegetation, and almost no sound large enough to orient you. Darkness will conceal motion until it is close. Silence will make every scrape feel significant. Heat and dry air will increase irritability, fatigue, and the sense that you cannot breathe even when your equipment is working.

Prepare for this deliberately.

- Practice working in darkness with a respirator.
- Practice moving slowly over uneven terrain without verbal coordination.
- Practice remaining calm while surrounded by unseen animal sounds.
- Do not send anyone with untreated claustrophobia, severe panic responses, or a tendency to remove respiratory gear under stress.
- Rotate decision-making; heat stress impairs judgment.
- Keep a written log of time, pulse events, route markers, and air consumption. Subjective time may become unreliable.

The basin’s packed life may also trigger an urge either to flee or to force a path through. Resist both. The correct pace is deliberate and observant.

## A simple operating rule

If the animals all agree about something, believe them.

If they avoid a patch of ground, avoid it.  
If they climb, climb or retreat.  
If they go still, go still.  
If they clear an arch, do not enter it.  
If they compress around a vent, do not approach.  
If the small runners disappear, assume the ground is about to change.

Their behavior is the closest thing you will have to a local warning system.

---

## What not to take

Do not take:

- Living animals.
- Eggs, larvae, skins, bones, or black vent films.
- Pieces of the arches.
- Rock from marked walls or hand-like scratches.
- A predator’s remains.
- Material from a vent during or immediately after a pulse.
- “Souvenirs” from an apparently abandoned burrow.
- Anything that native animals are rubbing against, feeding from, sheltering beneath, or repeatedly circling.

The Kiln is a closed system. Even a small removal may matter more there than it would in a broad, productive ecosystem. And in multiversal travel, biological quarantine is not politeness; it is survival.

---

## Final advice

Visit the Kiln as you would visit the interior of a living furnace that happens to contain an ancient city of animals.

Do not expect hospitality. Do not expect hostility either. The creatures are not waiting for you; they are fitting themselves into a world with no spare water, no daylight, and almost no room. The safest visitor is one who takes up as little space, makes as little noise, leaves as little trace, and departs before the mountain has reason to notice them.
