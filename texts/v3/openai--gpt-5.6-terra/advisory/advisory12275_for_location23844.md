Prepare for this city as you would for a long stay inside a living, dimly lit machine that happens to be underwater. It is not a place where an ordinary surface human can arrive with a diving mask, a tank, and confidence. Its hazards are structural: pressure, breathing, buoyancy, darkness, disorientation, and dependence on communal infrastructure.

Your first concern is whether your body can physically survive the local pressure.

The city lies far enough below the sea’s moving upper boundary that the water overhead is not merely an inconvenience. It is an atmospheric weight measured in crushing multiples of your home-world experience. Do not assume its inhabitants are baseline humans just because they have houses, families, tools, and hands. They may have evolved, adapted, or been altered for this pressure; they may have different lungs, blood chemistry, bones, ears, and tolerance for dissolved gases. Their casual use of kelp air-bladders does not mean that a visitor can use one safely.

Arrange pressure-compatible arrival through whatever multiversal transit authority, guide, or local host is available. Ideally, arrive in a pressure-balanced chamber already at local ambient pressure, wearing an articulated pressure suit or a body-adaptation package designed specifically for prolonged underwater habitation. You need a way to equalize gradually, breathe safely, and avoid decompression injury if you ever return to a lower-pressure environment.

Do not attempt to swim upward toward the distant “surface.” Even if you could navigate there, ascending too fast can kill you through decompression sickness, embolism, lung injury, or whatever local equivalents apply. Descending or ascending only with local guidance, scheduled stops, and equipment rated for the actual depth is essential.

## Breathing: treat air as borrowed life

The city’s air chambers are communal survival infrastructure, not inns.

The inhabitants maintain pockets of trapped gas in cliffside domes, and they use kelp-bulb bladders for travel between them. Those systems may work because the residents know precisely how long a bladder lasts, how local gases behave under pressure, how to patch a seam, and where currents will carry them. You do not.

Bring an independent, redundant breathing system:

- A primary closed-circuit rebreather or locally compatible respiratory apparatus.
- At least two independent emergency gas sources.
- Carbon-dioxide monitoring and scrubber replacements.
- A pressure-safe mask or full-face breathing interface that does not rely on you holding a mouthpiece while exhausted or startled.
- A buoyancy-control system designed not to snag on reeds, ropes, terraces, or growing structures.
- A compact emergency habitat or breathing hood in case you become separated from the route network.
- Repair patches, clamps, sealing resin, and cutting tools suitable for fiber, shell-lime, and kelp cordage.

Do not casually use local breathing bladders without a host’s explicit instruction. Their gas mixture may be unsuitable for you, contaminated, too humid, too rich in carbon dioxide, or hazardous at ambient pressure. Likewise, do not enter an old trapped-air building simply because you are desperate for air. The city regards these chambers as dangerous for good reason. A stale air pocket may contain too little oxygen, too much carbon dioxide, poisonous gases released from old materials, fungal spores, dust, or pressure instability. In an underwater city, a single bad breath can turn a manageable problem into a drowning emergency.

If invited into a breathing chamber, understand the etiquette: enter slowly, avoid contaminating the space, do not waste air with loud speech or unnecessary exertion, and do not monopolize an access point. You are entering something closer to a shared well, hospital, shrine, and storm shelter than a room.

## Learn to move vertically

This is not a city you walk through. It is a city you descend, climb, float, pull yourself along, and orient yourself within.

The routes include cliff ladders, root bridges, garden tunnels, ropes, suspended paths, and open water. “Down” can become psychologically unstable in dim water, especially near the basin where the darkness continues beyond the limits of vision. A surface-trained traveler may panic because every route feels like a fall.

Before arrival, train in:

- Slow, controlled swimming in enclosed or low-visibility water.
- Rope and ladder traversal while wearing bulky equipment.
- Moving in three dimensions without stirring silt.
- Handhold discipline: always know your next secure point before releasing the last.
- Navigation by touch, tether lines, and sound rather than sight.
- Emergency self-rescue from entanglement.
- Calm response to equipment snagging or loss of orientation.

Bring gloves that preserve dexterity. You will need to feel knots, shell markers, fiber textures, stone seams, and signaling lines. Thick hard gloves may protect your hands but make you clumsy, and clumsiness around woven infrastructure can be dangerous and insulting.

Wear close-fitting clothing with no trailing straps, loose scarves, dangling tools, or ornamental chains. Anything that catches in kelp, reed-coral, root bridges, or tether nets can trap you. Secure every object to your body. If you drop an item, do not blindly dive after it into a garden or below a terrace. Ask for help, mark the location, and assess whether the recovery is worth the risk.

A compact line cutter is wise, but use it only in an immediate emergency. In this city, cords are architecture, roads, messages, fences, supports, family labor, and sometimes someone’s only safe path to air. Cutting a line casually is comparable to smashing a staircase while people are using it.

## Pack for wetness, warmth, and cold fear

The water is warm, but warmth is not comfort. You may still become exhausted, dehydrated, abraded, infected, or hypothermic during long periods of inactivity. The mineral-rich water may irritate skin, corrode unfamiliar materials, and damage seals.

Bring:

- A pressure-rated exposure suit compatible with local water temperature and chemistry.
- Anti-chafing layers; continuous immersion makes small abrasions serious.
- Waterproof medical supplies, including antiseptic appropriate for aquatic wounds.
- Eye protection or optics designed for dim green-blue visibility.
- Redundant illumination with low-impact modes.
- Seal-safe food and drinking-water stores.
- Electrolytes and a hydration method you can use underwater or in a chamber.
- Corrosion-resistant repair tools.
- Compact personal sanitation supplies.

Do not rely entirely on visible light. The city is built in muted blue-grey dimness, with green bioluminescent lamps at “night.” Bright white searchlights may be disruptive, frightening, or simply rude. They can also make it harder for your eyes to read subtler movement beyond the beam. Use shielded, adjustable lamps, preferably with amber or red modes if locals indicate those are less harmful to cultivated luminous organisms. Keep your lights pointed downward or at your immediate work area rather than sweeping them through homes and gardens.

More importantly, prepare mentally for darkness that is not empty. The water may look open, but it is full of stems, silt, ropes, terraces, cliff faces, drifting leaves, and distances you cannot judge. The basin below will invite imagination. Resist the urge to prove your courage by exploring beyond marked routes.

## Establish a local guide and a tether protocol

Do not visit independently.

You need a resident guide, ideally someone responsible for a specific terrace and familiar with the route between air chambers. Make your first request simple: ask who is authorized to host visitors and what you may safely touch, enter, eat, signal, photograph, illuminate, or swim near.

Agree on a tether protocol before leaving your arrival chamber:

- Which line you will follow.
- What shell markers mean.
- Which knots indicate a turn, hazard, private residence, garden boundary, or air chamber.
- What tug patterns mean “stop,” “danger,” “lost,” “need air,” and “return.”
- Where to wait if separated.
- How long your breathing reserve must last before turning back.
- Who is responsible for counting arrivals and departures.

Assume that a taut cord may carry messages. Do not lean on it, pluck it idly, cut it, tie your own gear to it, or allow your equipment to rub against it. What feels like an incidental rootline may be the only communication link between several terraces.

Speech is inefficient underwater. Learn basic local hand signs and touch signs before arrival, especially:

- Greeting.
- Thank you.
- I need air.
- I am injured.
- I am lost.
- Stop.
- Help.
- I do not understand.
- May I pass?
- I will not touch this.
- Danger below / danger behind / broken tether.

If their communication includes bell taps or line-plucking patterns, learn enough to recognize alarms. A drumbeat through the mountain or a sudden patterned vibration in the route lines may mean a broken tether, a community summons, an evacuation, a death, or a warning about movement in the basin. When locals respond, follow them immediately. Do not demand an explanation first.

## Treat gardens as farms, not scenery

The kelps, reed-corals, waterwoods, algae baskets, and root rafts are food systems, building material, medicine, light, rope, buoyancy, habitat, and likely social inheritance. A garden may look wild because it is designed to grow rather than be cleared.

Do not:

- Grab plants for balance unless told they are safe to hold.
- Cut, pick, or sample vegetation.
- Swim through cultivated beds to save time.
- Touch feathery crowns, vent-growths, or luminous moss.
- Anchor equipment to a living structure.
- Release foreign food, soil, microbes, bait, bodily waste, or synthetic debris.
- Assume a drifting house or raft is abandoned.

Your contamination risk is enormous. You could introduce microorganisms to an unusually isolated ecosystem, and the ecosystem could introduce pathogens or parasites to you. Use sealed waste containment. Sterilize equipment between environments. Avoid open wounds. Do not eat local food raw, however hospitable it appears, until it has been evaluated for your biology.

Bring gifts that do not become trash. The city values binding and durable useful materials. Suitable gifts might include sterile fine cord, repair needles, noncorroding small fasteners only if culturally appropriate, high-quality cutting edges, durable transparent containers, medical supplies, or carefully translated technical diagrams. But do not assume that “metal” is automatically a good gift. A small blade may carry immense economic and ceremonial weight. Giving one casually can embarrass a host, create obligation, or destabilize local relationships.

Ask what is useful. Better still, bring a broad selection of small, practical, non-disposable goods and let a local representative choose what can be accepted.

## Respect a society organized around shared maintenance

This city survives because everyone maintains something: lines, gardens, air chambers, walls, ladders, bladders, lamps, anchors, and growing foundations. The work is not background labor. It is civic life.

You will make a better visitor if you arrive prepared to contribute without assuming your methods are superior.

Offer labor only under instruction. You may be useful at tasks such as cleaning tools, sorting materials, holding a line, carrying sealed loads, patching equipment, preparing algae lamps, or documenting a repair with consent. Do not “improve” knots, tension a bridge to your own standards, rearrange a breathing chamber, or replace fiber structures with imported materials without understanding why things are built as they are. A flexible wall that seems flimsy may be intentionally designed to yield under current changes or tectonic shifts. A drifting house may be safer than a rigid one.

Remember that the mountains are still changing. Cracks, vents, mineral growths, tilted shelves, and new ledges are not necessarily emergencies. They are part of the city’s ongoing relationship with its landscape. Conversely, something that appears stable to you may be newly dangerous. Trust local assessments of stone and water.

## Be extremely cautious around old air-filled ruins

The upper, formerly dry buildings are likely to be one of the most tempting destinations. They are also among the worst places for a visitor to wander.

They may contain:

- Unbreathable or toxic gas.
- Unstable ceilings and tilework.
- Sharp dry debris that behaves unpredictably underwater.
- Silt clouds that erase visibility.
- Valuable relics under community protection.
- Sacred associations connected to the former sky.
- Fragile materials that can be destroyed by touch, bubbles, light, or careless retrieval.

If you are invited on a salvage expedition, follow the workers’ lead exactly. Their stone knives, clay lamps, and slow procedures are not evidence of primitiveness; they are evidence of techniques adapted to scarcity, pressure, and preservation. Do not bring in power tools, pry bars, or high-output lights unless specifically requested. Do not remove objects as souvenirs. In a place where copper and old wood are rare enough to justify dangerous expeditions, even a seemingly trivial fragment may belong to the whole terrace.

## Do not pursue the large presence in the basin

The city has a recurring phenomenon: pressure changes, sudden quiet in the leaves, and an immense shadow in water too dark to interpret. Whether it is an animal, several animals, geological disturbance, a current pattern, or a cultural understanding of the drowned valley is not yours to settle during a visit.

Do not investigate alone. Do not deploy sonar, bait, drones, bright lights, explosive markers, or recording equipment without consent. Active sonar in particular may disrupt communication, frighten residents, damage sensitive organisms, or provoke whatever the city wisely avoids.

If vegetation falls still, locals stop moving, or your guide signals retreat, do so quietly. Stay close to stone or to the designated line; do not flee upward blindly. Panic ascent is one of the most dangerous things you can do.

## Prepare your mind for communal dependence

This environment strips away several surface-world assumptions:

- Privacy is different when people share air.
- Property is different when homes drift and routes are tied together.
- Time is different when travel is slow and vertical.
- Silence is meaningful.
- Maintenance may be ceremonial.
- Survival may be inherently collective.

You may find the city intimate in ways that feel uncomfortable. You will be noticed: your bubbles, light, swimming style, strange equipment, inability to hear or signal properly, and dependence on imported technology may all make you conspicuous. Be modest. Ask before recording people or homes. Do not photograph breathing chambers, shrines, salvage sites, children, or communication patterns without explicit permission.

Expect people to communicate by touch more than you are accustomed to. A hand on your wrist, shoulder, tether, or suit may be instruction rather than intimacy. Still, establish boundaries through your guide and learn the appropriate sign for refusal. If you have trauma related to confinement, drowning, darkness, crowding, or touch, take that seriously before committing to the trip.

## A sensible visitor’s itinerary

For a first visit, do not plan an expedition. Plan a supervised cultural stay on one terrace or a small linked group of terraces.

1. **Arrive in a pressure-stabilized chamber.**  
   Spend time acclimating physically and learning local signals.

2. **Meet terrace elders or air-chamber keepers.**  
   They are likely among the people whose trust matters most, because they oversee the conditions that keep everyone alive.

3. **Learn the nearest route by repetition.**  
   Chamber to home, home to garden edge, garden edge to emergency shelter. Do it until you can follow it by touch and marker pattern.

4. **Participate in a low-risk maintenance task.**  
   This is the best way to understand the city’s ethics.

5. **Visit a garden under escort.**  
   Learn which plants are structural, edible, luminous, fragile, or dangerous.

6. **Observe communal signaling.**  
   Listen—or feel—for drums through the rock, cord messages, shell clicks, and the choreography of people responding.

7. **Only then consider deeper or older areas.**  
   The basin edge, vent fields, old rooftops, and wilderness rafts should be later privileges, not arrival-day attractions.

## Your essential packing list

At minimum, bring:

- Pressure-rated environmental suit or verified biological adaptation.
- Redundant closed-circuit breathing system.
- Emergency buoyancy and tether equipment.
- Line cutter for life-threatening entanglement only.
- Dimmable, shielded lights and spare power.
- Nonverbal communication cards or translation system that functions underwater.
- Waterproof notebook and stylus, though writing may be impractical in open water.
- Medical kit for immersion injuries and pressure emergencies.
- Contained food, drinking water, electrolytes, and waste system.
- Sterilization and biosecurity supplies.
- Flexible gloves and abrasion-resistant footwear.
- Small, non-disposable, locally approved gifts.
- A local guide, a return plan, and enough reserve time that you never need to rush.

Most of all, bring patience.

This is a city made from adaptation rather than conquest. Its people do not force the water, gardens, stone, and rising mountains into fixed shapes; they tie themselves into the changes and keep tying. A good visitor should do the same: move carefully, ask often, waste nothing, respect every line that bears weight or carries a message, and remember that every breath you take there has been maintained by someone.
