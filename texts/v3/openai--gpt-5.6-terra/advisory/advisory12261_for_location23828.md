## Preparation Brief: The Basin Town

This is not a place to visit casually, loudly, or with the assumption that your presence can be absorbed without consequence. The town is small, old, resource-limited, and deliberately bounded. Its people have built a stable life by refusing growth—not because they are primitive, fearful, or incapable, but because every additional body, structure, animal, demand, and noise alters a balance they have maintained for generations.

An ordinary human traveler can probably visit safely, provided they arrive gently, carry their own needs, and understand that they are entering somebody’s home rather than discovering an empty curiosity.

The most important preparation is mental:

**Do not arrive expecting the town to make room for you.**  
Make yourself small instead.

---

# Before Departure

## Learn the boundary

The town is visually concealed within a shallow, cold basin, apparently somewhere in or folded into a larger country. The surrounding white plain and inward-bent trees are not merely scenery: they are the town’s protective shell.

Do not approach by landing a vehicle, opening a portal, or setting up a camp directly over the basin. From above, you may mistake the place for empty ground. It is not empty. The willow-and-conifer curtain marks a practical and likely social boundary.

If possible, arrange an introduction through:

- a resident who has traveled outside;
- a neighboring settlement;
- a recognized trader or messenger;
- a multiversal cultural liaison;
- or, at minimum, a local guide who understands the approach paths.

If you must make first contact alone, stop outside the tree boundary and wait visibly but quietly. Do not force your way through the willow tunnels. A person will probably notice you long before you see them.

Avoid arrival after dark, in heavy frost, or during the rare fine rain. Those are bad conditions for finding stable paths, and an unexpected stranger moving through root passages can easily be mistaken for a threat, a poacher, or someone searching for resources.

## Make your stay short by default

Unless explicitly invited, plan for a day visit or a single overnight stay. Do not assume you may remain several days simply because there is unused floor space, an empty-looking shed, or a spare corner under a roof.

In this town, “empty” often means:

- drying space;
- winter fuel storage;
- a family’s future sleeping area;
- a goat’s shelter;
- a water route;
- a protected nesting spot;
- a drainage gap;
- or simply the room required for a path to function.

The town’s central rule—nothing should be made merely larger—will shape how people think about guests. Your presence may be welcome; your footprint must not become permanent.

# What to Bring

## Bring your own provisions

You should be as close to self-sufficient as possible without appearing ostentatious or creating waste.

Carry:

- sufficient food for the entire planned visit, plus an extra day;
- your own drinking water in a durable, non-leaking container;
- a compact personal cup, bowl, and spoon;
- any necessary medicines;
- a small first-aid kit;
- a washable cloth towel;
- a sealable bag for all personal waste;
- repair supplies for your own clothing and footwear;
- a low-smoke, low-odor emergency heat source only if local rules permit it.

Do not assume the unfrozen pool is drinkable. It is central to the settlement’s water system and may be culturally protected, practically contaminated by nearby use, or simply not intended for outsiders. Do not draw water from it, wash in it, throw coins into it, test it, photograph yourself beside it theatrically, or touch it without explicit permission.

The town uses every drop of water intentionally. Even a seemingly harmless act—rinsing a cup in a channel, emptying tea dregs outside, taking a long wash, leaving soap residue—may disrupt water allocation downstream.

## Food etiquette

Bring food that is:

- shelf-stable;
- compact;
- low-odor;
- free of excessive packaging;
- unlikely to shed seeds;
- easy to share in very small portions;
- compatible with a cold-climate diet.

Good examples include sealed hard breads, dried fruit without viable seeds, smoked or dried protein, plain salt, tea, medicinal herbs from verified disease-free sources, and high-quality cooking fats in small containers.

Avoid bringing:

- fresh fruits or vegetables with seeds;
- live cultures, sourdough starters, kombucha, or unverified fermented foods;
- invasive plant material;
- honey or other substances that could introduce insects or spores;
- strongly perfumed foods;
- large quantities of alcohol;
- foods with disposal-heavy wrappers;
- anything likely to attract or harm animals.

Do not feed the goats, dogs, chickens, fish, birds, salamanders, moths, or the little deer if you see it. The residents know their animals’ diets and relationships. A “treat” can be a hazard.

## Clothing

Dress for persistent cold, damp surfaces, constrained movement, and bright reflected light.

Wear:

- layered wool or modern equivalent base layers;
- a quiet insulating middle layer;
- a matte, non-rustling outer shell;
- warm socks, preferably with spares;
- flexible boots with clean, soft soles and good grip;
- gloves that allow fine work and do not shed fibers;
- a brimmed cap, hood, or eye protection against snow glare;
- subdued colors: gray, brown, moss green, dull blue, off-white.

Avoid:

- hard-soled boots that strike loudly on stone;
- crampons or aggressive cleats unless conditions genuinely require them;
- brightly colored expedition gear;
- reflective emergency blankets worn openly;
- jangling zippers, loose metal clips, bells, key rings, or dangling tools;
- heavily scented waterproofing agents;
- clothing that sheds microplastic fibers excessively.

The pathways are narrow, often damp, and may pass under low roofs, roots, branches, and eaves. You will need to duck often, turn sideways, and carry your pack close to your body. A large framed hiking pack is a poor choice. Use a narrow, soft-sided pack that can be worn on the front when passing through tight spaces.

Bring a small pair of clean indoor shoes, soft slippers, or thick wool socks if appropriate. Many paths and rooms may be used barefoot or in hide soles. Ask before entering any dwelling.

## Light and power

Do not rely on the town to provide lighting or electricity. Bring a small, warm-toned, dimmable lamp with a red-light setting. Keep it covered or pointed down.

Do not flood lanes with bright white LEDs. The settlement has adapted to dim interiors, reflected daylight, lamps, and a restrained night environment. Harsh artificial light can disturb people and animals, particularly owls, moths, birds, and anyone sleeping close to passageways.

If you carry a phone, camera, radio, translator, or other device:

- silence all alerts;
- disable vibration if it is strong enough to be felt or heard through wood;
- use airplane mode unless communication is necessary;
- bring enough battery capacity not to seek charging;
- never deploy a generator;
- do not fly a drone.

A drone would be especially offensive and damaging here: noisy, invasive, likely to frighten birds and animals, and symbolically the opposite of the town’s inward, sheltered character.

# Biosecurity and Environmental Hygiene

This is one of the most important practical concerns.

The basin appears ecologically unusual: luxuriant vegetation in cold conditions, distinctive amphibians, pale fish, quiet birds, dense mosses and fungi, and water systems that pass through the whole settlement. Such places can be extremely vulnerable to imported organisms.

Before entering the tree boundary:

1. **Clean all footwear thoroughly.**
   Remove soil, seeds, mud, insect eggs, and plant fragments from soles, laces, treads, and cuffs.

2. **Clean your clothing and gear.**
   Check pockets, Velcro, hems, backpack seams, tent fabric, and walking poles.

3. **Do not bring pets.**
   No dogs, cats, service animals without prior arrangement, insects, livestock, or animal-derived untreated materials.

4. **Do not introduce plant matter.**
   Do not scatter crumbs, compostable waste, flower petals, tea leaves, tobacco, ashes, or seed-bearing food.

5. **Use no soap, sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, or chemical treatment near open water or drainage channels.**
   If these are medically necessary, disclose that before visiting.

6. **Do not collect specimens.**
   No moss, fungi, stones, water, salamander skin shed, feathers, fish scales, bark, soil, or “souvenirs.”

Likewise, do not leave behind anything—especially not hair, food remnants, batteries, plastic tabs, tissues, or sanitary waste. Pack it all out.

If you have recently visited wetlands, farms, animal markets, caves, amphibian habitats, hospitals, or places with contagious disease outbreaks, disclose that. Postpone the visit if there is any meaningful chance you are carrying an infection. In a tightly connected settlement, illness could move quickly through shared passages and close households.

# How to Enter

## Stop at the edge and announce yourself softly

Do not shout. Do not call repeatedly. Do not use a whistle, horn, loudspeaker, or locator beacon.

At the boundary, stand where you can be seen. Make your hands visible. If there is a path, remain on it. A calm spoken greeting at ordinary conversational volume is enough. If you know local language or custom, use it; otherwise, a simple statement of intent is best:

> “I am a traveler. I have come only to ask permission to enter. I carry my own food and water, and I will leave no trace.”

Then wait.

Silence may be part of the response. Someone may watch you without speaking. That is not necessarily hostility. In a place where sound carries intimacy rather than distance, people may take time before deciding how to receive an outsider.

Do not interpret quietness as an invitation to proceed.

## Accept refusal without argument

There may be periods when visitors cannot be accommodated:

- illness in the settlement;
- water scarcity;
- repairs to a critical roof, path, or channel;
- births, deaths, mourning, or local rites;
- winter stores running low;
- wildlife nesting or migration;
- a temporary imbalance no outsider can see.

If refused, thank them, leave gently, and do not linger near the boundary. Do not demand explanation. A refusal may protect the town, and perhaps you as well.

# Behavior Inside the Town

## Be physically small

The town’s spaces are intimate and functional. You should move as though every path is also someone’s hallway, workshop, water route, pantry edge, animal corridor, and roof support—because it may be.

Practical rules:

- Walk single file.
- Keep your bag close.
- Do not stop in narrow passages unless directed.
- Yield to residents, children, animals, and anyone carrying water, fuel, food, or building materials.
- Never lean your weight on a wall, rail, roof edge, woven fence, or tree-trained structure.
- Do not step on moss-covered surfaces, garden beds, roof turf, or platforms unless invited.
- Do not move stones, wedges, bowls, gutters, reed mats, or pieces of wood.
- Do not assume a low wall is a seat.
- Do not touch hanging hides, drying herbs, nets, tools, food stores, or pale hides in the sunlight.

The town is maintained by countless small arrangements. What looks improvised may be structural. A stick bracing a gutter, a stone holding a roof layer, or a branch bent across a lane may be doing essential work.

## Keep your voice lower than you think necessary

In this town, silence is not emptiness. It is shared space.

Speak close to the person you are addressing. Use short sentences. Let pauses happen. Do not fill quietness with nervous chatter.

Avoid:

- calling to companions across lanes;
- laughing loudly;
- playing music aloud;
- taking calls;
- speaking in a booming “outdoor voice”;
- arguing in public;
- using vocal translation devices at high volume;
- snapping fingers, clapping, or whistling to get attention.

If traveling with children, prepare them in advance. They should understand that this is not a place for running, shrieking, chasing animals, or playing hide-and-seek through passages. The local children may play subtle, quiet games; follow their lead only if invited.

## Do not mistake quiet for weakness

The inhabitants’ restrained speech and old tools should not cause you to patronize them. Do not describe their town as quaint, untouched, primitive, frozen in time, or “like a museum.” They are not preserving an antique. They are actively maintaining a sophisticated living system.

Ask before offering solutions. A water filter, metal tool, solar panel, saw, portable heater, seed packet, or construction technique may seem helpful from outside but can create dependency, waste, hierarchy, noise, pollution, or pressure to expand.

If they ask about other worlds, answer honestly but avoid presenting outside abundance as a moral judgment against their way of life.

# The Pool and the Central Structure

Treat the unfrozen pool and the six-trunk shelter beside it as the town’s most sensitive area.

You may be invited there. You may not.

If invited:

- remove your hat or outer gloves if locals do so;
- wait to see where people stand;
- do not step onto the central stone;
- do not touch the wet surface;
- do not kneel to drink;
- do not photograph or record without clear permission;
- do not toss in gifts, coins, flowers, food, or “offerings”;
- do not ask whether the place is magical in a mocking or demanding way.

The rule kept there—nothing is to be made merely larger—should be understood as an ethical boundary, not merely a zoning rule. It likely governs population, building, resource use, ambition, inheritance, trade, and perhaps the acceptance of visitors.

A respectful question might be:

> “What does the rule ask of a guest?”

That question shows you understand that you, too, may need to limit yourself.

# Gifts

A good gift is small, durable, repairable, useful, and does not impose a future burden.

Potentially appropriate gifts, if accepted:

- a fine hand tool of simple, serviceable design;
- high-quality needles;
- plain salt;
- a small quantity of durable thread, linen, waxed cord, or fishing line;
- whetstones;
- a compact repair kit;
- clear glass lenses or reading lenses, if culturally appropriate;
- carefully documented medicinal knowledge rather than unfamiliar medicine itself;
- a book or hand-copied materials in a requested language, if literacy and storage make this useful;
- a sealed packet of locally non-invasive, requested seed stock only after expert approval.

Possibly better than an object: offer a skill.

You might offer to:

- mend clothing;
- sharpen tools under supervision;
- patch a roof where directed;
- carry water;
- clean and sort wool;
- help weave;
- prepare food using local instructions;
- assist with careful recordkeeping;
- teach a requested skill in a way that does not require imported equipment.

Do not arrive with a large “aid” package. Large gifts create storage problems, social obligations, unequal access, and eventual disposal problems. A town built on limits may find excess more burdensome than scarcity.

Never leave a gift behind without someone explicitly accepting responsibility for it.

# Photography, Recording, and Mapping

Assume recording is not permitted until someone clearly says otherwise.

The town is hidden for a reason, whether practical, historical, cultural, or protective. A detailed map, GPS coordinate, aerial image, architectural survey, social-media post, or travelogue could expose it to visitors, extraction, state attention, tourism, land claims, or multiversal exploitation.

If photography is permitted, observe strict limits:

- ask separately before photographing people, homes, children, animals, work areas, and the pool;
- do not photograph through doorways;
- do not geotag;
- do not record private conversations;
- do not publish images of routes into the town;
- do not capture identifiable structural details if those details could enable outsiders to locate it;
- show people what you took if asked;
- accept deletion requests immediately.

The safest default is to leave with memories, notes generalized after departure, and no precise location data.

# Accommodation and Sleeping

If you are offered a place to sleep, understand that it may be somebody’s ordinary work area rearranged at cost to them.

Keep your bedding compact. Do not unpack widely. Do not hang wet garments wherever there is open space; drying space may be rationed. Ask where boots, coats, bags, and water containers belong.

Do not use an open flame, stove, battery heater, candle, incense, or chemical warmer indoors unless specifically directed. Fire and smoke are communal risks in dense timber-and-thatch construction. Even “clean” modern devices may produce fumes, glare, or fire hazards unfamiliar to residents.

At night:

- move slowly;
- use minimal red light;
- avoid stepping outside repeatedly;
- keep coughing, snoring, and other unavoidable disturbances as controlled as possible;
- do not wander;
- do not explore passages because they look interesting.

A lane that appears public may lead through sleeping spaces, storage rooms, animal shelters, or private family routes.

# Health and Safety

The cold is described as stable rather than brutal, but do not underestimate it. Cold, dampness, low activity, and close quarters can produce hypothermia before you notice. Eat regularly, change damp socks, and do not rely on the mild steam from the pool for warmth.

Main physical hazards include:

- slippery frost-polished flagstones;
- low beams and branches;
- uneven steps;
- hidden drainage channels;
- peat edges near the basin;
- weak roof surfaces;
- open cooking pits;
- smoke exposure;
- limited emergency medical resources;
- contamination from unfamiliar food or water;
- getting lost in visually repetitive, enclosed passages.

Carry a personal medical information card in clear pictorial form if language is uncertain. Include allergies, medications, blood type if known, emergency treatment preferences, and any implanted devices or conditions that might require outside intervention.

Do not disclose all advanced technology immediately, especially if it could alter local power relationships or provoke pressure for access. But do disclose any device that could pose danger—medical implants, radio transmitters, batteries, tracking systems, hazardous materials, or portal anchors.

Most importantly: **do not establish a portal aperture, beacon, or return marker inside the town.** Put your return point well outside the willow boundary, in a stable and agreed location. A gateway can bring noise, strangers, contamination, light, wind, and unwanted attention into a settlement designed to remain enclosed.

# How to Leave Well

Leaving well matters as much as arriving well.

Before departure:

- ask whether you have accidentally caused inconvenience or left something misplaced;
- retrieve every item you brought;
- pack out all waste;
- clean your footwear again before moving onward, so you do not carry the basin’s organisms elsewhere;
- offer thanks without demanding a formal farewell;
- do not promise to return unless invited;
- do not leave a marker, sign, cairn, ribbon, cache, or hidden supply.

If people gave you food, knowledge, shelter, or time, do not assume a conventional monetary payment is appropriate. Ask what exchange is acceptable. It may be labor, a small useful gift, a story, a message carried elsewhere, or simply respectful discretion.

The greatest gift you can offer may be this:

**Do not turn the town into a destination.**

Do not publicize it. Do not bring curious friends. Do not send researchers, philanthropists, documentarians, traders, or officials without explicit consent. Do not treat its concealment as a puzzle you solved and therefore a secret you own.

This community has survived by fitting itself carefully inside a boundary. A good visitor recognizes that the boundary is part of the town—not an obstacle to overcome, but one of the things that keeps it alive.
