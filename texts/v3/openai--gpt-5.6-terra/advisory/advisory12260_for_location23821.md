Prepare as if you are visiting an active industrial wetland suspended above the weather: not a ruin to explore casually, but a functioning hydraulic organism whose safety systems are subtle, persistent, and not designed around human convenience.

## Before arrival

### Do not go alone
Bring at least one companion, preferably someone comfortable with:
- confined industrial spaces
- cold-to-warm transitions
- swimming in clothing or using immersion gear
- rope work and careful footing
- reading mechanical hazards rather than treating them as scenery

Establish simple signals before entering. Sound may be damped, distorted by water walls, or swallowed by machinery. Hand signals, line tugs, and waterproof lights are more reliable than shouting.

Tell someone outside the works where you entered, your intended route, and when you expect to return. The site’s elevators, water levels, and routes may change slowly enough to seem stable until they are not.

### Be physically prepared
You do not need to be an athlete, but you should be able to:
- climb ladders and grated stairs while wet
- balance on narrow catwalks
- swim calmly for several minutes
- move through waist- to chest-deep water without panicking
- tolerate humid heat followed by exposure to high-altitude cold

Do not visit if you have severe claustrophobia, uncontrolled panic around deep water, or a tendency to touch switches, valves, or “obviously inactive” machinery.

## Clothing and personal gear

The challenge is not simply getting wet. It is being wet in a warm, conductive, mechanically active environment, then potentially emerging into cold cloud air.

### Wear
- **Non-slip waterproof boots** with excellent grip on wet steel, algae, ceramic, and ladder rungs. Avoid heavy rigid boots that become dangerous in deep water.
- **Quick-drying layered clothing:** synthetic or wool base layer, light insulating layer, durable shell. Cotton is a poor choice.
- **A close-fitting waterproof outer layer** that will not snag on valves, railings, cable trays, or submerged hardware.
- **Work gloves:** thin enough to feel handholds, grippy when wet, and insulated enough for hot pipes and cold railings.
- **Eye protection:** clear safety glasses or swim goggles. Steam, mineral water, falling condensation, and submerged debris can all interfere with vision.
- **A helmet** with a secure chin strap. Low beams, pipework, moving elevator cages, and concealed overhead growth make it worthwhile.
- **A personal flotation device** that is compact or manually inflatable. Avoid bulky automatic inflation unless you are certain you will not need to pass through narrow flooded passages; an inflated vest can trap you under low structures.
- **A harness**, if intending to use high gantries, exterior catwalks, or any route where water begins beyond an unrailed edge.

Tie back long hair. Secure every loose strap. The works has innumerable grilles, fan housings, cable runs, and slow machinery that may not look dangerous because it moves quietly.

### Carry in waterproof pouches
- Two independent light sources, preferably headlamp plus handheld lamp
- Spare batteries
- Whistle, though do not rely on it as your only signal
- Waterproof map material, notebook, pencil, and grease marker
- Compass and offline navigation device; do not assume either will behave normally near active electrical infrastructure or unusual water pressure systems
- Compact first-aid kit, including blister care and waterproof dressings
- Water purification method and drinking water of your own
- High-calorie food in sealed packages
- Dry emergency layer in a fully sealed bag
- Compact throw line or rescue cord
- Small folding knife or line cutter, accessible with either hand
- Waterproof watch or timer
- Rebreather, snorkel, or at minimum a mask only if you are trained to use it; do not treat submerged route exploration as casual swimming

A thermal camera, moisture-resistant camera, and water-depth marker can be useful, but do not let documentation slow your movement through changing areas.

## Treat the water as architecture

This is the central rule.

The water is not merely pooled in the works. It is part of the works’ structure. It may be held in open spaces without visible walls, pass vertically through frames, fill passages that appear dry on a map, or remain contained after the masonry meant to hold it has failed.

Do not assume:
- that a water surface marks the bottom of a basin
- that an apparent opening allows water to spill through
- that a dry corridor will remain dry
- that a flooded corridor connects to the next visible chamber
- that a wall of water is shallow, harmless, or passable
- that a high pool has an edge you can safely lean over

Mark your route frequently. Use chalk or tape only on surfaces where doing so will not block gauges, vents, drainage points, or moving components. Better still, record junctions, stair numbers, distinctive plants, lamp colors, and gauge clusters in a waterproof notebook.

If a door begins to seep, do not wait for the leak to “settle.” A bead becoming a thread is a warning that the room’s status is changing. Leave by the highest available route. Do not attempt to brace, seal, or hold a door closed unless you understand the pressure on both sides.

## Crossing water thresholds

Some passages may continue directly through stable vertical sheets of water. Approach these as you would a pressure boundary.

Before entering:
1. Watch for several minutes.
2. Note whether leaves, bubbles, steam, or suspended debris move through it.
3. Check whether the handrail continues beyond it.
4. Determine whether your exit is visible and reachable.
5. Secure loose equipment.
6. Have one person remain outside if possible.
7. Use a tether only if it cannot snag; fixed rails and grilles make entanglement a genuine risk.

Never dive blindly through an opaque or refracting water wall. The chamber beyond may be deeper, shallower, sideways, occupied by vegetation, or crossed by machinery. If the water produces strange buoyancy, resistance, or disorientation, retreat immediately rather than forcing passage.

If you must enter a fully flooded section:
- Use trained cave-diving discipline, even if the water looks clear.
- Maintain a continuous guideline to known air.
- Do not depend on finding a surface; the “ceiling” may be water retained in a frame, and the nearest air may not be above you.
- Avoid silting up sediment and plant mulch. Visibility may disappear instantly.
- Do not follow elevator shafts, intake pipes, turbine housings, or large conduits. These are the most likely routes to active flow, changing pressure, or inaccessible depths.

## Respect active machinery

The works appears quiet because its systems operate gently, not because they are dead.

Assume every pump, elevator, lamp bank, valve, cable conduit, and black-glass reservoir is active unless proven otherwise. The slowest machinery is often the most dangerous because it invites impatience and close inspection.

### Do not
- turn wheels, pull levers, or press switches
- open control cabinets
- climb on pipework
- enter turbine halls merely because their water is still
- board an elevator cage unless you have confirmed where it travels and how to stop it
- use maintenance ladders that disappear into water
- stand beneath relief valves or steam vents
- touch exposed metal with both hands while standing in water
- attempt to drain, divert, or sample water from a system connection

Keep clear of:
- intake grilles
- pump chamber thresholds
- submerged cable bundles
- black glass columns
- pressure doors
- moving cables
- warm-water outlets
- any gauge whose needle is shaking rather than resting

The site may be self-regulating. Interfering with a minor component could change water distribution across an entire sector.

## Heat, steam, and electrical safety

The warmth is a comfort only until it becomes an exposure hazard.

Humidity can prevent sweat from cooling you. You may become overheated without realizing it, particularly while wearing waterproof gear. Drink regularly, rest in cooler exposed areas when possible, and loosen outer layers before you become dizzy.

Avoid breathing directly from steam plumes. Even a thin valve release can scald airways or conceal superheated vapor. Check surfaces near steam lines before gripping them.

Electrical danger may be difficult to read. The works contains active lighting, insulation, cabinets, and cables in a constantly wet environment. Trust the architecture’s insulation; do not test it.

If you see:
- unusual arcing
- blue-white glow
- buzzing where there should be silence
- a metallic taste
- hair rising
- water trembling around a cable or cabinet
- sudden warmth in the water

back away without splashing, touching adjacent metalwork, or taking large steps. Move carefully to dry, nonconductive footing if available.

## Vegetation is not necessarily safe footing

The plants are beautiful, but they grow where the water and heat permit them, not where humans can stand.

Do not use vines as ropes, root mats as bridges, or lily-covered water as evidence of shallowness. Their roots may lead directly into submerged machinery, and their apparent support may be only a few millimeters of plant matter over a deep shaft.

Be especially wary of:
- mulch accumulated on grates
- moss over bolt holes
- ferns masking broken stair treads
- vines covering live pipe runs
- root systems wrapped around railings
- fruits floating in flooded halls

The absence of insects, birds, and fish suggests that the ecology is incomplete, controlled, or chemically unusual. Do not eat fruit, drink untreated water, or assume the plants are harmless to touch. Avoid breaking or collecting specimens. The vegetation may be part of the works’ filtration, thermal regulation, or structural water-management system.

## Follow the place’s own routes

Use catwalks, marked stairs, broad terraces, and maintained railings even if they seem indirect. The most efficient route is often the route the works expects something—human, machine, or water—to take.

A useful practical rule: favor routes with multiple redundant features:
- handrails
- lamps
- gauges
- drain channels
- visible service markings
- maintained grating
- regular condensation patterns

These are signs that the space remains within the working system.

Avoid routes that are too quiet, too clean, or too inviting. A pristine submerged gallery may be a controlled pressure space. A dry platform surrounded by water may be isolated when the water shifts. A bright stair descending into clear green depths is not an invitation unless you know where it emerges.

When choosing between an upper and lower route, choose the upper route unless there is a compelling reason not to. Water can enter a space gradually but become impassable long before it feels dangerous.

## Navigation and timing

The site changes on slow cycles. This makes it deceptively easy to overstay.

Set a strict turnaround time before entering. Do not judge by daylight alone: lamps may simulate a daily rhythm, cloud cover may conceal actual time, and the complex may brighten or dim independent of the sky.

At every major junction, record:
- current water height against a fixed mark
- lamp state
- pump vibration or audible throb
- steam activity
- whether elevator cages have moved
- direction of leaf drift

If two or more of these change unexpectedly, return toward your entry point or the nearest known high, dry refuge.

Do not rely on retracing wet footprints. Condensation erases them, water redistributes them, and the site’s own dampness makes all paths look recently traveled.

## If you become trapped by rising water

Stay calm and move upward early. Do not wait for obvious flooding.

1. Identify the highest maintained structure nearby: tower, upper platform, pump roof, service bridge, or exterior parapet.
2. Avoid elevator shafts and ventilation ducts unless you know they lead to air.
3. Do not force sealed doors. Water may be retained behind them under pressure.
4. If a room fills slowly, look for intended overflow features: high grilles, marked stairs, relief channels, or rails leading to upper maintenance access.
5. Preserve light and battery power.
6. Keep your flotation device uninflated until you are in open enough water to use it safely.
7. If water enters as a silent wall or seam, assume it has a destination; move perpendicular to its apparent path rather than trying to outrun it along the same corridor.

If stranded on an elevated platform, avoid tampering with controls to summon help. Signal visually with lights, reflective material, or deliberate movement near exterior-facing windows. The works may notice changes in its normal patterns more readily than it responds to noise.

## If you encounter an elevator in motion

Do not step into a cage just because it is open.

Watch at least one full movement cycle if possible. Determine:
- whether it travels through air, water, or both
- whether its doors seal
- whether it stops at recognizable levels
- whether it creates suction, wake, or pressure changes
- whether it carries anything besides itself

If you use one, secure yourself, keep hands and gear inside the cage, and be prepared for complete submersion. Do not exit into water without confirming there is a safe route beyond the doors.

## Conduct and attitude

This is not a dead industrial site and not a wilderness. It is closer to visiting an enormous working conservatory, reservoir, and machine body at once.

Move slowly. Touch little. Leave gates, valves, and plant growth as you find them. Do not remove gauges, fruit, metal fragments, seeds, or “souvenirs” from active surfaces. Do not introduce animals, insects, soil, food scraps, or untreated biological waste. In a place with no ordinary fauna, even a small introduction may be consequential.

Expect the works to be indifferent rather than hostile. Its rules are not punishments. Water enters where it is meant to enter; water stays where it is meant to stay; plants follow heat and submerged channels; machinery continues its cycles. A visitor is safest when they stop expecting the environment to behave like ordinary architecture.

## Minimum sensible kit checklist

- Companion and external check-in plan  
- Waterproof boots, gloves, helmet, layers  
- Manual-inflation buoyancy aid  
- Two lights and spare batteries  
- Waterproof map/note kit  
- Food, water, first aid, dry insulation layer  
- Rope or throw line, line cutter  
- Eye protection or mask  
- Harness for exposed routes  
- Training or refusal: do not enter submerged passages without it  

Most importantly: give yourself permission to leave early. The drowned works will still be there, quietly holding its water among the clouds.
