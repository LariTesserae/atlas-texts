## Before You Go: Treat This as a Hostile Subterranean Climate

This is not a place where you can “rough it” with a bottle of water and a flashlight. The city is survivable for its inhabitants because every part of their lives is adapted to heat, mineral dust, pressure shifts, unusual plants, and complete waterlessness. As an ordinary human, you are likely to be physically conspicuous, culturally confusing, and medically vulnerable.

Your principal risks are:

- **Dehydration.** There is no local water supply, and the inhabitants may not have practical infrastructure for giving you any.
- **Heat stress.** The chamber is hot even at rest, with periodic surges of hotter air from vents.
- **Respiratory irritation or poisoning.** The “metallic” atmosphere, vent gases, salt dust, mineral pollen, and low-lying heat layers may be unsafe to breathe untreated.
- **Pressure and wind events.** Vent-season can turn streets into wind tunnels filled with abrasive grit and airborne plant matter.
- **Navigation and isolation.** There is no sky, no familiar day-night cycle, few people in large spaces, and acoustics that can make direction difficult.
- **Biological contamination.** The city has no animals or insects. Your skin flora, clothing fibers, food crumbs, hair, and any living hitchhiker could be more ecologically consequential than you expect.
- **Social hazards caused by sound.** Ordinary conversation may travel far beyond where you think it does.

Do not arrive casually or unannounced if you can avoid it.

## Essential Personal Equipment

### 1. Carry a closed-loop water system

Bring substantially more water than you would for an equivalent trip on the surface. Assume you cannot replenish it locally.

At minimum, have:

- A hard, insulated water reservoir protected from heat.
- A personal water recycler, condenser, or closed-loop hydration suit if your multiversal travel technology permits it.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Cooling-compatible drinking hardware: bite valves and tubes may clog with dust, so use sealed threaded containers as backup.
- A separate small reserve that you do **not** reveal casually.

Water will not merely be valuable there. It may be culturally strange, sacred, alarming, or treated as a dangerous imported substance. Do not spill it. Do not wash with it in public. Do not pour it onto plants, stone, or streets out of kindness or curiosity.

A dropped cup of water may be interpreted as anything from a chemical spill to an incomprehensible ritual.

### 2. Protect your lungs

Bring a respirator that can handle both particulates and unknown gases. Ideally:

- Full-face mask or tightly sealed goggles plus a half-mask respirator.
- Replaceable particulate filters for salt dust, plant powder, and dry molds.
- Gas filtration rated for sulfurous, metallic, and acidic volcanic/mineral gases.
- A compact emergency oxygen supply or independent breathing apparatus for sudden vent releases.
- Spare seals, filter cartridges, and cleaning tools.

Do not assume the locals’ apparent comfort means the air is healthy for you. They may be adapted genetically, culturally, technologically, or simply accustomed to a baseline level of irritation and illness you cannot tolerate.

The luminous brown pollen from the red cup-flowers deserves particular caution. It is likely not “pollen” in the harmless garden sense. It may be abrasive, allergenic, chemically active, reproductive material, or a fine nutrient-rich powder that local plants and molds exploit. Keep it out of your eyes, food, lungs, electronics, and sleeping space.

### 3. Dress for heat, dust, and abrasive wind

Your clothing should be:

- Loose enough to vent heat, but tightly closable at cuffs, collar, and ankles.
- Pale or reflective on the exterior, but not mirror-bright; the locals may use mica reflectors and mineral light deliberately, and glaring gear could be distracting.
- Durable against salt abrasion.
- Non-shedding, or as close as possible. Avoid fuzzy synthetics, loose feathers, wool, glitter, or fabrics that leave fibers everywhere.
- Layered, because dry heat and drafts can change abruptly.

Bring:

- Wraparound goggles.
- A hood or dust scarf that can be secured under a respirator.
- Gloves with strong grip; salt brick, mica, volcanic glass, and dry plant fiber may all cut you.
- High-ankle boots with sealed tongues and heat-resistant soles.
- Knee and elbow protection if you expect to use the narrow stairs or work terraces.

Avoid exposed metal jewelry. Metal is scarce in the city and may attract attention, envy, suspicion, or requests you are not prepared to answer. It is also likely to become uncomfortably hot and can react badly with mineral dust or vent gases. If you need metal tools, keep them discreet, explain them honestly if asked, and do not casually use a steel knife where locals are relying on glass or mineral blades.

### 4. Bring reliable light—but use it carefully

The cavern’s weak violet-white mineral glow will not be enough for a visitor to navigate safely. Carry:

- A headlamp with a red-light setting.
- A low-output warm lamp for close work.
- At least two independent backup lights.
- Spare power not dependent on local charging.
- A physical map board, notebook, and compass/inertial navigation device.

However, do not flood public spaces with bright white light. The city’s people live in dimness, their eyes may be adapted to it, and its plants may respond to light and heat in ways you do not understand. A bright lamp pointed at someone’s home or garden may feel intrusive. Use a hooded, downward-facing light unless you have permission otherwise.

Do not rely entirely on magnetic compasses. Mineral formations, salt, volcanic deposits, and unknown local geology may make them unreliable. Mark routes physically on your own map and learn the city’s landmarks: the central stone bulge, the bell tower, major vents, kiln districts, planted roofs, and the black-reed square.

### 5. Bring a heat-management plan

You will need more than breathable clothing.

Pack:

- A cooling vest or phase-change cooling packs.
- Electrolytes.
- A skin-safe barrier cream against salt and dust.
- Lip balm, eye drops, and saline-free dry-eye treatment if available.
- Burn dressings.
- A thermometer and pulse-oximeter.
- A compact medical kit designed for heat illness, respiratory distress, cuts, and chemical irritation.

Be cautious with ordinary evaporative cooling. Sweating may be your body’s normal response, but here it represents a direct loss of scarce water from your body. Do not assume you can simply “drink more later.”

## Biological and Ecological Precautions

This city’s lack of animals is not a decorative detail. It is one of the most important things about the place.

The inhabitants live in a world where every moving creature is meaningful. There are no flies, mice, spiders, birds, pets, livestock, worms, or beetles. Your arrival introduces an animal body into that world.

Prepare accordingly.

### Quarantine yourself before entry

Before visiting:

- Inspect clothing, luggage, footwear, and hair for seeds, insects, fungal spores, soil, food debris, and animal hairs.
- Use clean, sealed garments.
- Avoid bringing fresh fruit, bread with live yeast, houseplants, soil, untreated wood, animal products, or open food.
- Do not bring pets, service animals, insects, biological samples, or “harmless” ecosystem companions.
- If you require a medical device with biological components, disclose this to hosts in advance if possible.

Your own microbiome cannot be made sterile, but you can avoid adding unnecessary contamination. The local dry crystalline molds and mineral-feeding plants may be exquisitely balanced. A single ordinary mold spore or a smear of nutrient-rich food may establish something new.

### Do not touch plants without permission

The gardens are not ornamental in the casual sense. They may be food, fuel, building material, air-management infrastructure, heat indicators, ancestral property, or all of these at once.

Especially avoid:

- Turning leaves by hand.
- Blocking vents with your body or baggage.
- Brushing pollen from flowers.
- Taking cuttings, seeds, fibers, or dried leaves as souvenirs.
- Watering anything.
- Standing on planted roofs.
- Leaning heavily on coiling ribbons around rails or chimneys; they may be structural, alive, or both.

The plants appear to track heat and gas flows. If a whole grove turns, bends, clicks, or darkens, assume it is responding to a real atmospheric change. Follow local people indoors rather than stopping to observe.

## Arrival Protocol

If you can choose your arrival point, do **not** materialize in a street, garden, roof, market court, near a vent, or under the central arch.

Best options are:

1. A designated traveler’s chamber, if such a thing exists.
2. A broad, unplanted edge plaza away from vents and kilns.
3. The threshold of a known host household, with advance consent.
4. A quiet storage or receiving area where you can be inspected and oriented.

On first contact:

- Keep your hands visible.
- Keep your face protection on if the air requires it, but indicate clearly that it is breathing equipment, not armor or concealment.
- Do not rush toward people; sparse streets may mean that unexpected motion is alarming.
- Avoid making sudden gestures when a dust devil, vent roar, or bell sound is underway.
- Speak slowly and softly—but not in whispers under resonant arches.
- Ask where you may stand, where you may breathe safely, and where you may dispose of waste.

If you carry water, say so early to your host or guide. Do not make it a surprise. They need to know whether your presence brings a spill risk, a trade item, a ritual object, or a possible contaminant.

## Learn the City’s Weather Before Walking Alone

The city has weather without sky. Treat vent activity as you would a severe storm warning.

Ask a local guide to teach you:

- What the bell’s different tones mean.
- Which vents are active, dormant, or dangerous.
- Which streets become wind channels.
- Which terraces trap hot air.
- Where to shelter during a pressure reversal.
- What leaf movements predict a strong draft.
- Which pollen clouds are ordinary and which indicate flowering or vent events.
- How long people expect shutters to stay closed during a vent-season.

When the stone bell sings, do not stand in the square to admire it. The bell exists as a warning system. Follow the response of the people around you.

During a major vent event:

- Get indoors or into a designated sheltered space.
- Close all clothing seals.
- Put on eye and respiratory protection.
- Secure loose gear.
- Stay away from upper ledges, pulleys, lifts, chimneys, and planted roofs.
- Do not use open flame near unknown gas flows.
- Do not assume a quiet interval means the event is over.

The chamber’s acoustics can make a vent seem farther away than it is. Trust local visual signs and your guide over sound alone.

## Social Etiquette: Sound Is Public Here

In this city, privacy is architectural and acoustic, not merely social. A phrase spoken beneath the wrong arch may travel to strangers. A whispered comment may arrive in a plaza as a fragment. The inhabitants know this and will have customs built around it.

Until taught otherwise:

- Do not discuss private matters outdoors.
- Do not say names, insults, secrets, diagnoses, or travel coordinates beneath large arches or concave ceilings.
- Do not test echoes for entertainment.
- Do not shout to locate friends.
- Avoid playing recorded music, alarms, or loud devices.
- Mute or silence communications equipment.
- Ask before recording audio; a recording may capture conversations far beyond your intended subject.

You may notice locals communicating with gestures, close speech behind fiber drapes, tapping systems, or carefully selected public phrases. Follow their lead.

Be especially cautious in the old civic square and near the bell tower. Places that amplify sound often accumulate taboo, ceremony, law, memory, and fear. The warning against speaking certain names beneath the central arch suggests that acoustics are culturally significant, perhaps spiritually significant, perhaps politically significant.

A name repeated by stone can be heard as an omen, an accusation, a summons, or an invasion of privacy.

## Food, Rest, and Hygiene

### Food

Bring sealed food suitable for hot, dry storage. Choose things that produce minimal crumbs and waste:

- Dense ration bars in durable wrapping.
- Dried or shelf-stable meals.
- Electrolyte powder.
- Foods that do not strongly smell of animal fat, fermentation, or moisture.

Do not eat openly in gardens or near vents unless invited. Crumbs are conspicuous in a world without scavengers. In a city where fallen leaves remain until people deal with them, food waste will be noticed—and may carry a moral weight you do not expect.

Ask before offering food. Meat, dairy, insects, honey, eggs, fruit, or wet foods may be unknown, taboo, repulsive, biologically alarming, or extraordinarily valuable.

### Hygiene

Your normal hygiene routines will need adaptation.

- Use wipes, dry brushing, or a closed-loop cleaning system rather than water.
- Keep sweat, skin flakes, hair, and nail clippings contained.
- Pack out all personal waste unless hosts provide a specific local method.
- Do not urinate outdoors, into cracks, near plants, or down vents. Your waste contains water, salts, organic material, and microbes; it is not environmentally neutral.
- Seal used filters, tissues, and medical waste.

You may find the place makes you feel constantly dirty: dust in your hair, salt on your lips, pollen on your clothes, dry grit in every seam. Resist the urge to “solve” that with water unless you are in a controlled private environment.

### Sleep

Sleep in a sealed or screened room away from:

- Major vents.
- Kilns.
- Public arches.
- Plant-heavy courtyards during pollen release.
- Rope lifts and mechanical works.
- The bell tower.

Bring ear protection, but do not make yourself unable to hear emergency warnings. A good compromise is filtered earplugs that reduce clatter while allowing low warning tones and nearby speech through.

A portable air-quality monitor is worth its weight. The chamber may develop invisible layers of hot or chemically concentrated air, especially near the floor, ceiling, vents, and poorly ventilated rooms.

## Trade and Gifts

The locals have ceramic, stone, fiber, mica, salt brick, volcanic glass, mineral deposits, and heat-adapted plant products. They have little metal. That does not mean you should arrive with a bag of knives and coins.

Useful, respectful gifts may include:

- Durable ceramic repair techniques or neutral repair materials.
- High-quality cordage that does not shed.
- Fine hand tools only if you understand the consequences of introducing metal.
- Sealed medical supplies.
- Safe low-energy lighting.
- Nonbiological measuring instruments: thermometers, pressure gauges, air-quality indicators.
- Books, diagrams, stories, or maps—provided you have translated or contextualized them.
- Small mirrors or mica-safe optical tools, if not disruptive to local light customs.

Potentially dangerous gifts include:

- Water, unless requested and carefully contained.
- Living organisms.
- Seeds or soil.
- Metal in large quantities.
- Batteries that may leak.
- Strong magnets.
- Plastics that shed or degrade.
- Chemicals, soaps, perfumes, alcohol, fuel, or fertilizers.
- Loud machinery.

If you do trade metal, understand that a small steel needle may be more transformative than a bag of exotic trinkets. It could change craft traditions, labor patterns, status relations, weaponry, repair practices, and dependence on outsiders. Trade slowly and through informed local intermediaries.

## What Not to Do

Do not:

- Spill water.
- Release anything living.
- Bring an animal.
- Assume empty streets mean abandoned neighborhoods.
- Enter a dark alley merely because it seems quiet.
- Climb on salt structures; salt brick and mineral shelves can fail unpredictably.
- Touch a bell, vent channel, kiln, or counterweight system without invitation.
- Stand beneath suspended loads.
- Walk under planted balconies during high draft.
- Collect mineral veins, glowing sap, pollen, glassy ribbons, or black reeds.
- Treat the absence of wildlife as proof that there is no ecology.
- Joke about “rain,” drowning, oceans, or flooding until you understand local associations.
- Whisper secrets beneath arches.
- Interpret the city’s sparse population as loneliness or decline without asking.

The city may be spacious because its people need distance from noise paths, vents, gardens, kin obligations, or dangerous atmospheric corridors. Empty market courts may be used seasonally, ceremonially, or during particular pressure conditions.

## If Something Goes Wrong

### If you lose your guide

Do not wander randomly. Move toward a known stable landmark, preferably a settled building rather than a vent, garden, or central acoustic feature. Stay visible. Use a low, controlled light signal rather than shouting.

If you must call for help, do it from a location your guide has identified as appropriate. In this place, shouting may spread panic or disclose more than your location.

### If you run low on water

Tell your host early. Do not conceal it until you are collapsing. Your condition may require a major decision: emergency resupply from your own travel system, immediate departure, or controlled use of any local moisture-equivalent resource. It is unlikely that local people can simply spare drinking water.

Symptoms of danger include headache, confusion, irritability, rapid pulse, reduced urination, dizziness, muscle cramps, and poor judgment. In the dry heat and strange light, you may not notice how impaired you are becoming.

### If a vent release catches you outdoors

Cover mouth and nose, protect eyes, get behind a solid structure, and seek enclosed shelter. Avoid low depressions if gases may be heavier than air, but do not climb blindly toward hotter upper layers. Local knowledge is essential; follow residents if present.

### If you see unexplained motion

Stop. Do not chase it, photograph it immediately, or announce it loudly. In a place with no animals, an unexpected moving shadow is significant. It may be a person, machinery, a wind effect, a plant response, structural failure, or something genuinely anomalous. Establish the cause before escalating.

## The Most Important Preparation: Arrive Humble

This is a city built around scarcity that is hard for you to feel in your bones. You know water as background reality; they know it as an abstraction. You know weather as sky, cloud, and rain; they know weather as pressure, heat, plant movement, stone sound, and the breathing of vents. You know silence as the absence of noise; they may know it as a warning, because in their hollow every ordinary sound has a source.

Do not frame the place as a curiosity: “a city with no water,” “a cavern civilization,” “a strange underground garden.” For the people who live there, it is a complete world with practical systems, inherited knowledge, and dangers you cannot read.

Your first goal is not exploration. It is to become non-disruptive.

Carry your own water. Contain your own biology. Cover your face when the air changes. Listen to the bell. Learn which leaves are telling you to go inside. Speak carefully under stone.
