Prepare for this town as you would for a cool, wet working settlement rather than a wilderness ruin. It is inhabited, maintained, and productive. Its apparent quietness is not permission to wander carelessly: much of the town’s infrastructure is alive, water-fed, and interdependent.

## Before entering

Bring durable identification and, if multiversal travel permits, a concise explanation of where you are from and what you intend to do. The residents may be used to unusual visitors, but this is likely a place where strangers are noticed through small disruptions: an unfamiliar light in a window, footprints in moss, a door held open too long, a pump’s rhythm interrupted.

Assume that access to certain areas—pumping rooms, cisterns, greenhouse halls, tram sheds, root-filter galleries, seed archives, and conveyor buildings—requires permission. These are not abandoned industrial sites. They are the town’s waterworks, food system, structural maintenance network, and perhaps much of its livelihood.

Learn a few basic concepts before arrival:

- Water channels may run beneath apparently ordinary paving.
- Plants may be structural material, insulation, filtration, weatherproofing, food production, and mechanical components.
- A clear path is likely clear because someone or something has carefully maintained it.
- A sealed door, trimmed vine, metal plaque, marked rail, or planted gutter may carry practical meaning even if you cannot read the local language.
- Machinery may move slowly and quietly, but it is still machinery.

Do not arrive expecting dramatic danger, grand ruins, or wilderness solitude. The main risk is not violent weather or predation; it is underestimating dampness, hidden infrastructure, and the social importance of maintenance.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for constant cool moisture, slippery surfaces, and enclosed spaces with humid air.

### Wear

- Waterproof ankle or mid-calf boots with excellent grip. Smooth-soled shoes will be dangerous on wet cobbles, iron tram rails, mossy brick, timber ramps, and glazed tiles.
- Wool or synthetic insulating layers rather than cotton. Even in mild weather, prolonged damp and shade can chill you.
- A breathable waterproof outer shell with a hood.
- Tough trousers that can withstand wet masonry, thorns, oily machinery, and kneeling beside drains.
- Thin work gloves, ideally with good tactile grip. You may need to steady yourself on railings, open old latches, or handle damp tools—but do not grab vines, tendrils, or cable-grown plants without invitation.
- A warm cap for mornings, nights, and underground pump rooms.

Avoid loose fabric, dangling straps, trailing scarves, and exposed long hair around conveyors, belts, turning wheels, shutters, chain lifts, and climbing plants trained into mechanical systems.

### Carry

- A compact umbrella only if it does not obstruct narrow paths. A hood is usually better.
- A small headlamp with a low, warm setting. Do not assume all corridors are illuminated, but avoid shining bright light into homes, greenhouse beds, or active control rooms.
- Spare dry socks in a waterproof bag.
- A refillable bottle, preferably with a personal filter as backup. Public cistern water may be safe, but ask before drinking; different channels may serve irrigation, structural root systems, industry, or domestic use.
- A compact first-aid kit, especially blister care, antiseptic, antihistamines, bandages, and treatment for minor cuts.
- A respirator or good mask if you are sensitive to mold, spores, compost dust, steam, or old industrial particulates. The town is damp, and lower rooms may contain fungal growth even when they are well maintained.
- Insect repellent, though fauna appears sparse. It is better to have it than to discover that the local moths, flies, or wet-weather insects have unusual habits.
- A paper notebook in a waterproof cover or, better, a durable reusable writing slate. Ordinary paper may rapidly curl, mold, or be impractical in wet conditions. Metal-tag style notes may be culturally appropriate if you have the means.
- A small cloth for drying lenses, glasses, or tools. Condensation will be persistent.

Do not rely solely on electronics. Moisture, fogged glass, wet hands, and old electrical systems make waterproof, low-power, or analog backups valuable.

## Health precautions

The town’s ecology is stable, but it is dense with plant life, damp sediment, root systems, stored water, compost, and cultivated fungi. An ordinary human should prepare for ordinary biological risks amplified by moisture.

If possible, update tetanus vaccination before visiting. Carry any required personal allergy medication. If you have severe mold allergies, asthma, a compromised immune system, or strong sensitivity to plant pollen and fungal spores, take the visit seriously as an environmental exposure rather than a casual urban excursion.

Do not eat plants, fungi, seeds, fermented products, or cistern water without local approval. The market hall may cultivate food, medicine, industrial fiber, filter species, and structural vegetation side by side. A leaf that looks edible may be bitter, medicinal, toxic, or integral to a machine.

Wash cuts promptly. Avoid wading in channels, especially those near old warehouses, power facilities, filtered runoff, or sediment basins. The water may be clean by local standards but still contain unfamiliar microbes, oils, mineral residues, or root-associated compounds.

If you begin to feel unusually cold, fatigued, dizzy, or short of breath, do not dismiss it as atmosphere. The basin’s cool damp can creep into you gradually, particularly after standing still in shade or visiting underground rooms.

## Moving through the town

Walk slowly. This is not because the town is fragile in a theatrical sense, but because it is subtly uneven.

Expect:

- Rounded brick edges slick with moss.
- Cobblestones with shallow water in their joints.
- Tram rails that are wet, polished, and surprisingly slippery.
- Hidden drops where a former channel has subsided.
- Root-raised paving.
- Low bridges and narrow drainage cuts.
- Old stairways with rusted but functional rails.
- Doorways partly occupied by vegetation.
- Floors that look dry but are fed by slow seepage beneath.

Stay on the visibly maintained routes: open streets, trimmed lanes, cleared platforms, marked bridges, and regularly used stairways. A path overtaken by sedge, leaf litter, or shallow standing water may still be passable, but it may also cover a culvert, intake, old cellar opening, or a planted system meant not to be disturbed.

Never assume that a shallow groove is just a groove. During or after rain, the former river channels may fill quietly and redirect water through the town. They may not flood dramatically, but they can become slippery, conceal debris, or lead to deeper underground drainage sections.

Cross rails at an angle rather than walking along them. Watch for movement in sheds and former loading areas. Conveyors, chains, shutters, belts, and plant-driven mechanisms may begin operating with little warning and little sound.

If a route is marked by metal plaques, painted symbols, trimmed vegetation, colored ties, or a distinct style of paving, treat it as signage. The town may communicate through durable, weather-resistant cues rather than posters or paper notices.

## Rain protocol

Rain is normal here. It is also operational.

When rain begins:

1. Put on waterproof layers before you are soaked.
2. Move out of drainage lanes and away from the mouths of culverts.
3. Avoid standing beneath roof edges, especially heavily vegetated roofs. Water may release suddenly from leaves, gutters, and accumulated mats of growth.
4. Do not shelter in a pump house, greenhouse, or service corridor unless invited. These may become active during wet conditions.
5. Watch local residents. Their route choices will reveal which courtyards, ramps, and passages remain safe as water starts moving.

Do not be fooled by the gentle character of the rainfall. The danger is not a violent downpour; it is the town’s patient response to water. Channels wake, valves change state, pumps engage, and surfaces become slick in ways that are hard for a newcomer to anticipate.

## Respecting the living infrastructure

The defining etiquette of this place is simple: do not interfere with growth unless you understand its role.

Do not:

- Pull vines from walls, grilles, rails, or machinery.
- Cut, pick, or taste plants without permission.
- Lean heavily on root-bound fences or vegetated retaining walls.
- Block drains with bags, litter, boots, or temporary equipment.
- Move planters, trays, seed markers, cuttings bins, or wooden cultivation frames.
- Rest gear on conveyor belts, control boxes, turning wheels, or greenhouse beds.
- Step on moss roofs, planted gutters, or sedge mats.
- Throw anything into channels, cisterns, the river, or root filters.
- Feed the sparse wildlife.

A vine threaded through a ventilation grille may be part of insulation or air control. A patch of moss may be holding mortar in place. A trained climber around a wheel may regulate shade or venting. A wet strip along a foundation may be deliberately maintained to preserve a root-reinforced structure. In this town, “weed” is probably a category used carefully, if at all.

If you need to pass through a curtain of plants, ask. Someone may be able to tie it back, open a different route, or explain whether the growth is harmless.

## Interacting with residents

Approach people calmly and openly. Keep your hands visible, avoid startling workers in low-light industrial spaces, and do not assume that infrequent visible residents means the town lacks social structure. People may be indoors, on maintenance rounds, in upper rooms, underground, or working within greenhouse systems.

A useful opening would be practical rather than grandiose:

> “I am a visitor. I would like to walk through the public parts of town and learn the rules for staying out of the way.”

Offer help only after being shown how things work. In a place built on slow coordination, enthusiastic untrained assistance can be destructive. Clearing “overgrowth,” opening “blocked” vents, lubricating a mechanism, or moving a tray out of the rain might undo a system that has been tuned over decades.

If accepted as a guest, suitable contributions may include:

- Helping carry dry supplies.
- Cleaning tools under supervision.
- Recording readings or observations.
- Sorting harvested material.
- Sweeping maintained paths.
- Mending labels, seals, or simple fittings.
- Sharing durable goods that resist damp: stainless fasteners, quality waterproof fabric, sealed batteries, repair tools, medical supplies, or archival media.

Avoid presenting plastic trinkets, chemically aggressive cleaning products, herbicides, or high-noise machinery as gifts. They may be useless or actively unwelcome.

## Where to sleep and rest

Choose a maintained upper-floor room, an inhabited former office, a guest space near the market hall, or another location explicitly offered by residents. Avoid basements, empty warehouses, greenhouse aisles, rail sheds, and low rooms near channels.

For an overnight stay, use:

- A raised sleeping surface.
- A waterproof groundsheet if sleeping near any floor-level dampness.
- Dry layers reserved only for sleep.
- A sealed bag for clothing and electronics.
- Earplugs only if you can still hear alarms or evacuation calls; the town’s low machinery sounds may be comforting, but a new repeated noise could be important.

Expect smells of wet brick, compost, old timber, mineral water, machine oil, fermentation, and vegetation. They are likely normal. What should concern you is a sharp solvent smell, unusual smoke, strong electrical burning, sudden sewer gas, or a dramatic change in the familiar pattern of hums and pumps.

## Night travel

The town is lit, but not brightly. At night, wet surfaces reflect lamps and can conceal changes in level. Green-covered buildings may become nearly featureless black masses beyond the light.

Do not walk alone into unfamiliar industrial areas after dark. Stay near tramway lamps, inhabited buildings, public squares, and known routes. Carry your light, but use it modestly. Bright beams may disrupt workers, attract insects, or interfere with people monitoring water and plant conditions by sight.

The town may be most beautiful at night, but it is also when sound carries: your boots, your bag buckles, your conversation, and any careless disturbance of machinery will be conspicuous.

## Psychological preparation

Prepare for a place that may feel both abandoned and attentively inhabited. This can be disorienting. You may repeatedly see signs of age—rust, softened brick, old signs, obsolete switchboards, empty platforms—while also hearing pumps, noticing fresh pruning, smelling recent fermentation, or seeing a light in an upper window.

Do not interpret quietness as neglect or invitation to explore private spaces. The town’s inhabitants seem to value restraint, maintenance, and continuity. Match that mood. Speak softly. Leave no trace. Let pauses remain pauses.

You may also find the town oddly calming: its systems are visible, slow, and mutually supporting. Be alert not to become too relaxed. The most likely accidents will arise from inattention—placing a foot where water is meant to go, entering a room where machinery is about to engage, or treating living structural material as scenery.

## Best attitude to bring

Bring patience, waterproof boots, respect for maintenance labor, and a willingness to ask before touching anything.

This is not a dead industrial town reclaimed by nature. It is a town where industry, hydrology, cultivation, and habitation have been made into one continuous practice. Visit as a guest inside a functioning organism: observe closely, move gently, and assume that every clear street, trimmed doorway, turning pump, and green wall is being kept that way by someone’s ongoing work.
