## Before You Go: Treat This as a Wet, Loud, Vertical Living System

This is not a city where you can rely on a street map, a weather forecast, or the assumption that a building is a single building. It is a densely inhabited hydraulic machine spread across a basin below an old sea level. Flooding is routine, infrastructure is adaptive, and neighborhoods may change their physical arrangement with the season or the hour.

Prepare as though visiting a major coastal megacity during monsoon season, inside an industrial complex, on a moving vessel, with constant crowds and uneven authority.

Do not arrive expecting wilderness, quiet, dry ground, private space, conventional landmarks, or an uninterrupted route from one place to another.

## Essential Gear

### Wear and carry waterproof equipment

You should expect water at ankle level even on a clear day, and deeper water after a localized storm. Your belongings need to survive sudden immersion.

Bring:

- Waterproof boots with excellent grip on wet ceramic and metal. They should be high enough for shallow flooding, quick-draining, and easy to remove if necessary.
- A compact pair of amphibious sandals or water shoes. In some lower wards, walking footwear is less useful than footwear suitable for skiffs, wet decking, and public wash stations.
- Lightweight waterproof outer layers rather than one heavy coat. The city is humid and warm even when rain is cold.
- A sealed dry bag for documents, electronics, medicine, and valuables.
- Several small waterproof pouches rather than one large bag; a dropped bag can disappear into a drainage channel or be swept into a flood corridor.
- A water-resistant, impact-resistant communicator that can function offline. Local networks may be compartmentalized, old, proprietary, or responsive to conditions you do not understand.
- A physical light source, ideally head-mounted and waterproof. The city is illuminated at night, but lower accessways, power recesses, and flood shelters may not be.
- A simple analog wristwatch. Do not assume local public clocks, signal systems, or augmented-reality directions translate across realities.

Avoid long trailing clothing, loose scarves, open bags, and anything that can snag in bridge joints, transit machinery, water gates, or dense crowds.

### Protect your hearing and lungs

The noise is not merely unpleasant. It may cause fatigue, disorientation, panic, and physical discomfort, especially near turbines, pumps, rail systems, atmospheric collectors, and enclosed service corridors.

Carry:

- High-fidelity earplugs, which reduce harmful volume while still allowing you to understand speech and alarms.
- Full noise-blocking protection for emergency use, but do not wear it while navigating traffic, flood zones, or animal corridors.
- A breathable particulate mask or respirator rated for damp urban industrial conditions. The air contains steam, mineral aerosols, exhaust residue, salt bloom, insulation fibers, and whatever the city’s nonliving “lichen” sheds.
- Spare mask filters, stored dry.

Do not assume the visible haze is harmless. Even if it is locally normal, your biology may not be accustomed to its salts, metallic particles, spores, nanomachines, or atmospheric electrical effects.

### Keep your body temperature stable

The basin is wet, humid, crowded, and full of waste heat. You can overheat in rain.

Bring:

- Moisture-wicking clothing.
- A cooling scarf or evaporative neck wrap, if it still works in high humidity.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- A personal water filter only as a backup, not as permission to drink canal water.
- A refillable bottle marked clearly as your own.

Use public drinking cisterns only after confirming they are intended for biological human consumption. The city sorts water into many categories; “clean,” “clear,” or “public” may not mean safe for your physiology.

## Health and Multiversal Biosecurity

The absence of plants does not mean the city is biologically simple. It has aquatic films, engineered animals, unusual mineral deposits, dense human habitation, airborne moisture, and autonomous microscopic systems in the water.

Before entering:

- Obtain broad-spectrum travel vaccinations and a medical screening appropriate to the destination reality.
- Carry a concise medical profile in durable physical form: blood type, allergies, chronic conditions, medications, emergency contacts, and translation notes.
- Bring at least several days of essential medication in separately sealed containers.
- Pack basic wound care supplies. Wet environments turn small cuts into serious problems quickly.
- Include antiseptic, blister treatment, anti-diarrheal medication, antihistamines, electrolyte packets, and any needed anti-inflammatory medicine.
- If your multiverse travel system permits it, use a low-profile environmental monitoring patch to warn of unusual atmospheric chemistry, radiation, electrical charge, or biologically active aerosols.

Do not enter open water with cuts, rashes, fresh tattoos, or compromised skin. Do not touch mineral foam, metallic crusts, or pale branching wall deposits without local guidance. They may be inert, industrially active, caustic, contaminated, or part of a monitoring system.

If you become feverish, confused, unusually thirsty, numb, short of breath, or sensitive to sound/light after a storm, seek help promptly. In a place where water, waste heat, and machines all interact, environmental illness can resemble ordinary fatigue until it is not.

## Learn the City’s Flood Language Immediately

Your first task after arrival is not sightseeing. It is learning how the district announces water movement.

Find out:

- What flood sirens, warning chords, light signals, and public notices mean.
- Which colors indicate drinking-water routing, industrial-water routing, gray runoff, electrical danger, animal right-of-way, and evacuation.
- Where the nearest high-ground refuge, floating platform, sealed transit station, and public cistern are.
- Whether your lodging rises, seals, floats, retracts its walkways, or expects occupants to relocate during severe weather.
- Which routes remain walkable in rain and which turn into boat corridors.
- Whether local maps update automatically, and how to access the current version without relying on a single device.
- What a “rise” means locally: a minor canal swell, a neighborhood-scale float event, or a dangerous undercity drainage shift.

Never assume water is still because it looks shallow. Some channels are load-bearing, electrically active, pressurized, or connected to gates large enough to produce sudden currents. Avoid drainage throats, storm slots, maintenance trenches, and any marked seam in the pavement during rain.

If people begin lifting goods, tying doors, moving animals, or stepping onto elevated braces, do the same general thing they do. Do not stop to record the event from a convenient low bridge.

## Navigation: Use District Logic, Not Straight Lines

The city’s layout is packed, layered, and partly mobile. An address may specify a vertical level, a bridge lineage, a utility spine, a flood status, and a seasonal location. A route that existed yesterday may now be a canal, a market fleet, or a sealed maintenance zone.

When arranging accommodation or meeting someone, ask for:

- District name.
- Nearest stable transit node.
- Elevation or deck level.
- Flood-safe route.
- Alternate water route.
- Local landmark that is unlikely to migrate.
- The door’s pressure-safe entry protocol, if applicable.
- A neighborhood contact rather than only a formal address.

Good landmarks may include major cisterns, long-established bridges, publicly named collector towers, shrine complexes, large transit interchanges, or permanent market basins. Bad landmarks include small storefronts, temporary scaffolds, street positions, and “the corner,” which may not remain a corner.

Do not force doors, gates, bridges, lifts, or access panels. The city’s infrastructure may be making a safety decision based on pressure, weight, current, crowd density, or an internal pattern no individual person can override. A locked door in this city may be preventing a flood, a pressure injury, or an entire structure from destabilizing.

## Stay Off Critical Infrastructure

Much of what looks like ordinary architecture is doing another job.

A wall may be a water channel. A plaza may be a buoyancy lid. A roof may be a heat exchanger. A bridge may be a flexible crowd sensor. A decorative cable bundle may be carrying power, communications, or structural load between districts.

Follow these rules:

- Walk only where residents walk.
- Do not lean on rails unless you see others doing so.
- Do not sit on pipes, grates, canal edges, utility housings, or apparently unused platforms.
- Do not attach locks, signs, ropes, cameras, or charging equipment to public structures.
- Do not fly drones without explicit local authorization. Atmospheric collectors, wirewing flocks, suspended water-sorting systems, and transit routes make casual aerial devices especially dangerous.
- Do not attempt to “help” with a stuck door, flooding channel, damaged cable, or wandering service machine unless directed by trained local personnel.

The city has endured by allowing its systems to move. Your instinct to stabilize, brace, plug, or clear something may conflict with a larger drainage or load-balancing process.

## Animals: Give Them Their Routes

The fauna is adapted to the city and, in some cases, protected by strong custom and law.

### Flood hounds

Treat flood hounds as you would a combination of working dogs, urban predators, and emergency infrastructure.

- Never block a drainage throat, ledge, retaining-wall path, or marked water corridor during a rise.
- Do not approach a pack, especially at night or near floodwater.
- Do not feed them.
- If one notices you, remain calm, move slowly away from its route, and let it pass.
- Do not run through shallow water; vibration is part of how they navigate and hunt.

The public’s habit of yielding to them is not quaint local color. It is probably a survival practice.

### Canal grazers

They may be enormous, placid, and useful, but they are not public furniture.

- Do not climb on them, touch their armor, feed them, or position a boat too close to their mouths.
- Give them room around pilings and submerged structures.
- Watch for wake and body displacement in narrow canals.

### Wirewings

Flocks around vents can become dense enough to obscure movement and create hazardous debris or sudden noise.

- Avoid standing directly below nests.
- Do not shine bright lights into roosting groups.
- Protect your eyes and face if a flock is disturbed.
- Keep food sealed; scavenging behavior may be common.

### Slow scavengers

Do not remove objects from their backs. Their carried debris may be camouflage, nest material, cultural matter, or part of a symbiotic relationship. More importantly, reaching into shallow, opaque water is a poor idea in a city full of machinery and animals.

## Social Preparation: Expect Dense Interdependence

People here likely survive through neighborhood knowledge, maintenance customs, flood etiquette, and mutual dependence. A visitor who is polite but helpless may be tolerated; a visitor who treats the city as an exotic spectacle, ignores local warnings, or interferes with shared systems will not be.

Useful habits:

- Ask before photographing people, homes, shrines, repair work, animals, and any water-management process.
- Learn basic local phrases for: “Is this route safe?”, “Where is the high refuge?”, “Which water is potable?”, “I am lost,” “I need medical help,” and “I will move.”
- Carry small practical trade goods only if culturally appropriate: sealed medical supplies, durable fasteners, batteries, water-resistant writing materials, or repair tools may be valued more than luxury items. But do not assume; imported technology can cause suspicion or unintended economic harm.
- Tip guides, boat operators, porters, and maintenance workers fairly. In a vertical wet city, their local knowledge may be worth more than any map.
- Respect informal occupancy. A ruin, service alcove, or tank platform may be someone’s home, workshop, shrine, or inherited claim.
- Be cautious about discussing “how things worked in your world.” Some systems will be politically contested, sacred, legally restricted, or dangerous to replicate.

If offered a local guide for a first visit, accept. A good guide is not a luxury here; they are flood insurance, translator, etiquette instructor, and route planner.

## Lodging: Choose High, Connected, and Legible

Do not choose accommodation merely because it is cheap, scenic, or close to the water.

Favor a place that has:

- A known flood procedure.
- At least two egress routes, ideally one dry/elevated and one water-based.
- Staff present through storm periods.
- Reliable public-cistern access.
- A pressure-safe entrance you understand how to use.
- Proximity to a stable transit node.
- A room above likely flood level, but not so high that you are dependent on unreliable lifts during an evacuation.
- Good ventilation and sound insulation.
- Clearly marked emergency communications.

Ask whether the room is adjacent to pumps, collectors, exhaust organs, rail lines, animal ledges, or major flexible bridges. “Central” can mean “unbearably loud.” Bring sleep earplugs, but make sure you have an alarm method that can wake you through them, such as a vibration device.

## Weather Protocol

Storms form locally and fast. A bright sky over your block does not mean the next district is safe.

When you see a storm wall approaching, or local warnings begin:

1. Stop trying to complete errands.
2. Check your district’s current flood status.
3. Move toward your planned refuge or lodging before walkways retract.
4. Keep your documents, medicine, water, and communicator on your body.
5. Avoid elevators unless local authorities explicitly direct their use.
6. Do not shelter under collector structures, exposed bridges, transmission galleries, or large exhaust systems.
7. Stay clear of canal edges, retracting decks, and visible electrical arcing.
8. Follow local crowd flow. In a dense city, the crowd may be responding to infrastructure information you do not possess.
9. Once sheltered, wait for local clearance rather than assuming the worst is over when rain lightens.

After a storm, do not rush out. Drainage can be more dangerous than rainfall. The city’s ancient underworks may pull water through streets, grates, and lower corridors with little visible warning.

## Electrical and Atmospheric Hazards

The atmospheric collectors turn storm clouds blue-white. Treat every thunderstorm as an electrical event, not just a rain event.

During collector activity:

- Avoid exposed rooftops, upper galleries, open water, long metal railings, and tall isolated structures.
- Do not use improvised antennas, conductive umbrellas, or unapproved charging connections.
- Keep devices sealed and disconnected from unfamiliar public ports.
- If your hair rises, you feel tingling, nearby metal hums, or light becomes unusually blue-white, get indoors or into an approved shelter immediately.

The city may have grounding customs unfamiliar to you: particular floor markings, handrails, entry mats, or public alcoves. Observe and imitate local behavior.

## Money, Documentation, and Communication

Because the city contains overlapping governments, markets, informal districts, ancient systems, and possibly machine-administered services, bring redundancy.

Carry:

- Local exchange medium if available.
- Small denominations.
- A non-networked emergency reserve.
- Durable copies of your identity and travel authorization.
- A way to prove you are not carrying dangerous autonomous technology, if that is relevant across realities.
- A written lodging address and district contact.
- A translation tool that works offline, plus a printed phrase card.

Do not assume a payment system is universal. One neighborhood may use formal credit, another water allotments, labor notes, energy tokens, or reputation networks. Ask before ordering, boarding, or accepting services.

## Things Not to Do

- Do not drink from canals, floodwater, drainage seams, or attractive “clear” water slots.
- Do not enter the undercity without an authorized guide.
- Do not follow strange lights, open service doors, or apparently convenient shortcuts after rain.
- Do not interfere with water-sorting columns, grit swarms, pressure doors, collector webs, or mobile foundations.
- Do not block drainage throats, animal ledges, bridge joints, or marked utility lanes.
- Do not assume a silent machine is inactive.
- Do not assume a busy machine is malfunctioning.
- Do not treat abandoned-looking spaces as unclaimed.
- Do not try to rescue objects from moving water.
- Do not travel alone at night in the lower wards.
- Do not run from flood hounds or through water where you cannot see the bottom.
- Do not stay in a district once residents begin telling outsiders to leave.

## The Right Mindset

The city is not failing because it floods, groans, migrates, leaks, and changes shape. Those are normal functions. Its people have built their lives around managed instability, and the safest visitor is one who understands that stability here means flexibility.

Travel light. Stay observant. Keep dry essentials close. Let residents set the pace. Learn where the water is going before you decide where you are going.

Most importantly: make room.

Make room on walkways for workers and animals. Make room in your schedule for storms. Make room in your assumptions for systems older and stranger than you. This city survives because nothing in it is entirely separate from anything else. A visitor who respects that interdependence can see an extraordinary place. A visitor who ignores it may become one more small obstruction the water has to carry away.
