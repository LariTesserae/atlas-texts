## First principle: do not visit this town as an ordinary swimmer

For an unmodified human, the drowned town is a serious deep-water environment. “Several hundred feet” of cold freshwater above the roofs means crushing ambient pressure, limited natural light, a long ascent, and lethal decompression risk if you arrive or depart improperly. Even if the water is clear and the streets look calm, this is not a place for improvised diving.

Before entering, arrange one of the following:

- **A pressure-neutral multiversal arrival method** that places you at the town’s ambient pressure without a violent transition.
- **A certified deep mixed-gas / saturation-diving system**, with a competent tender team and decompression plan.
- **Local physiological accommodation**, if offered safely and consensually: a temporary adaptation for cold tolerance, pressure, and extended underwater respiration may be more appropriate than importing bulky machinery.

Do not assume that because the town’s people live underwater, they can rescue an unprepared visitor. Their methods, bodies, air needs, and relationship to pressure may not match yours. They may be extraordinarily practiced breath-holders, may use biological adaptations unavailable to you, or may simply have a network of tiny air spaces whose use is carefully rationed. In any case, your emergency becomes a burden on a community built around not wasting air.

## Understand the pressure problem

The surface is not a nearby ceiling. It is a distant, hazardous border.

At a few hundred feet down, an ordinary human cannot simply decide to “swim up for air.” A rapid ascent can cause severe decompression injury, gas embolism, lung damage, disorientation, and death. A rapid descent without pressure equalization can injure ears, sinuses, lungs, and mask spaces. Cold water makes poor judgment more likely.

Your travel plan should include:

- Exact **depth and ambient pressure** at your arrival point.
- A **breathing-gas plan** appropriate to that pressure; ordinary compressed air may be unsuitable at depth.
- A **redundant breathing system**: primary supply, reserve supply, independent bailout, and a method of sharing gas with another person.
- **Dive computer or depth timer** compatible with local conditions.
- A rehearsed **decompression schedule**.
- A contingency for a blocked route to the surface, because rockslides, weed growth, storms, or surface predators may make ordinary ascent impossible.
- A designated **safe return point** that does not require you to enter the civic hall or another community air chamber.

If you can avoid carrying large quantities of compressed gas into the settlement, do so. Bulky tanks catch on roots, damage living walls, disturb fish, and advertise that you are dependent on a resource the residents regard as unreliable and dangerous.

A compact closed-circuit apparatus, if technically safe at the local pressure, is likely less disruptive than loud, bubbling open-circuit equipment. However, do not use a rebreather unless you are thoroughly trained. A silent system is not inherently a safe system.

## Prepare for cold, not just water

The town’s cold “is persistent but not cruel” only to people adapted to it. In an ordinary human, persistent cold steadily erodes dexterity, speech, calculation, patience, and the ability to recognize danger. You can become confused before you feel dramatically ill.

Bring or arrange:

- A properly rated **thermal suit**: likely a drysuit or pressure-suitable heated suit rather than a thin wetsuit.
- Gloves that preserve enough finger control for knots, valves, and tools.
- A hood or insulated head covering; heat loss around the head and neck will matter.
- Reliable heating with independent battery reserve, kept in a sealed housing.
- A way to eat or take warm fluids after the visit, outside the settlement.
- A strict time limit. Do not make your first visit an open-ended cultural immersion.

Be especially cautious around the warm seepage districts. They will feel wonderful after cold avenues, but thermal gradients can hide changes in current, sediment instability, unusual biological growth, and concentrated animal life. Warm water may also encourage you to linger beyond your safe exposure time.

## Do not rely on local air chambers

The largest air chamber in the civic hall is culturally important and physically precious. It is not a visitor lounge, emergency decompression station, or place to test whether your gear works.

The town’s fundamental rule—**“nothing that needs air may be trusted”**—is both environmental knowledge and moral instruction. A visitor who arrives asking for air, making bubbles, carrying fragile pressurized equipment, or treating every trapped pocket as a convenience may be understood as careless at best.

Unless explicitly invited:

- Do not enter leaf-house air pockets.
- Do not open, enlarge, vent, or “inspect” trapped air spaces.
- Do not use a community chamber to equalize, warm up, recharge equipment, dry clothing, or rest.
- Do not bring flame, ignition sources, combustion devices, or volatile fuels.
- Do not test the air in a chamber by putting your face into it. It may be low in oxygen, high in carbon dioxide, humid, contaminated by decay gases, or simply reserved.

If you are invited into the civic hall’s preserved-air chamber, regard it as entering a sanctuary. Arrive clean, quiet, and prepared to follow direction immediately. The sound there will be startling after hours underwater. Speak softly. Do not shout because you are relieved to hear your own voice. Do not strike drums, blow shells, sing, or record ceremonies unless specifically invited.

## Travel with a local guide and accept local routing

The streets are channels, but they are not public corridors in the way dry-world travelers imagine streets. A broad avenue may be a spawning route. A doorway may open into a family leaf-house. A dark root tunnel may carry voices between districts. A stairway that looks abandoned may be a turtle-grazing boundary, a nursery, an eel pond, or a place deliberately left undisturbed.

Arrange an escort from the community, ideally one who can communicate with both visitors and local households. Your guide should decide:

- Which channels may be used.
- Where you may anchor, rest, or hold position.
- Which plants may be touched.
- When surface access is safe.
- How close you may approach homes, shrines, fisheries, gardens, and thermal vents.
- What local signals mean.
- Whether photography, mapping, sampling, or recording are acceptable.

Do not improvise a shortcut through reeds. The roots are infrastructure: they stabilize soil, filter water, shelter animals, distribute current, and support homes. A boot, fin, tank, tripod, or careless handhold can do more harm than it appears.

Move slowly and with excellent buoyancy control. You should be able to hover without kicking silt or brushing foliage. If you cannot maintain neutral buoyancy, you are not ready for the town.

## Learn to communicate underwater before arrival

Speech is mostly absent in the streets. That does not mean communication is absent. It means communication is embedded in current, vibration, route, timing, and sound carried through hollow growth.

Before visiting, learn a modest protocol rather than inventing one.

Useful preparations include:

- A set of **simple visual signs** for “I am well,” “I need help,” “I am low on breathing supply,” “I cannot follow,” “may I pass?”, and “thank you.”
- A quiet **tactile signaling method** for communicating with a buddy in low visibility.
- A way to write briefly underwater—slate, waterproof tablet, or sealed display—but do not assume written language is shared.
- A nonintrusive means of hearing or detecting low-frequency vibration, if your equipment permits it without broadcasting noise.
- A commitment not to tap arbitrarily on roots, stalks, stone pipes, or walls.

In this town, tapping is potentially speech. A “curious knock” on a hollow reed could be equivalent to shouting into someone’s home, imitating an emergency call, or sending a message you do not understand.

Mechanical noise should be minimized. Avoid propellers, loud pumps, sonar, bright strobes, and repeated metal clatter. The settlement is acoustically alive. Your machinery could drown out messages, frighten fish, disturb birds, or make local residents unable to tell whether a root tunnel is carrying a warning.

## Treat the living architecture as both home and ecosystem

Everything growing around you is doing work.

The reed walls hold the settlement together. Their roots bind the basin floor. Algae and moss filter the water. Leaf roofs shelter families and air pockets. Gardens feed people and animals. A collapsed building may have become a nursery, a food patch, a reef, or a sacred record of adaptation.

Therefore:

- Do not cut, prune, collect, sample, or “tidy” vegetation.
- Do not take shells, roots, algae, fungus, fish, stone fragments, metal artifacts, or pottery.
- Do not feed wildlife.
- Do not touch eggs, nests, turtle grazing beds, fishery structures, or lily cultivation areas.
- Do not rest against old stone walls; they may be softened, colonized, or structurally integral.
- Do not leave anything behind: no cable ties, food wrappers, batteries, discarded weights, marker lines, or trace chemicals.
- Avoid sunscreen, insect repellent, lubricants, and suit treatments that can leach into freshwater. If you must use any, choose substances confirmed safe by local custom and your own ecological assessment.

Especially avoid sediment disturbance. The town survives because the water remains clear. A few careless fin kicks can turn an avenue into a brown cloud, clog feeding structures, obscure routes, damage plant surfaces, and ruin visibility for everyone. Use frog kicks or careful hand-assisted movement only where appropriate; never fin against the basin floor.

## Respect the surface taboo without romanticizing it

The people are correct that the surface is dangerous: weather, lightning, bright light, diving birds, waves, and the sheer vulnerability of being exposed after living beneath a protective volume of water. But do not mock or exoticize their caution as superstition.

If your route requires surfacing:

- Do it only with local agreement and a surface-watch plan.
- Use a tether if local practice requires it.
- Check weather above before ascent, not merely conditions below.
- Be alert for birds diving at reflective gear, exposed hoses, or flotation devices.
- Avoid surfacing at noon, when glare and ultraviolet exposure may be intense.
- Do not assume floating mats are load-bearing. They are cultivated places, not docks.
- Never drag a tether across planted mats or reed roofs.

If a storm begins, follow local instructions. Do not flee upward by reflex. In this place, “up” is not automatically safer.

## Gifts should be useful, quiet, and non-disruptive

Do not bring fire technologies as a novelty. Do not bring disposable plastics, weapons, luxury metal objects, or complicated devices that require imported consumables. Metal may be culturally significant as inherited material; casually giving steel tools could disrupt local value systems and ecological practices.

Better gifts, offered only after asking a cultural liaison, might include:

- High-quality noncorroding **bone- or wood-compatible hand tools**, if their use would not displace local craft.
- Fine **natural-fiber cordage** or repair material, only if it will not introduce invasive fibers or dyes.
- Durable **optical lenses**, magnifiers, or sealed light-diffusing materials, if welcomed.
- Knowledge: illustrations, songs, stories, maps of harmless distant phenomena, or methods that can be shared without imposing dependency.
- Medical supplies in minimal, fully retrievable packaging—though offering medicine requires much more caution than it seems.

Often the best gift is labor performed under supervision: helping remove invasive vine-moss, carrying harvested material, repairing a permitted nonliving structure, or contributing to a communal task without damaging the living fabric.

Ask before giving anything. A community that has endured for centuries by balancing growth, decay, and scarcity does not necessarily need dry-world solutions.

## Prepare psychologically for slow, unfamiliar space

The town will not feel like a submerged ruin. It will feel inhabited, but not crowded. You may travel through long avenues where no one appears, while surrounded by evidence of other lives: fish, roots, distant calls, flickering algae, drifting pollen, old stairs that are now gardens.

This can produce several reactions in visitors:

- **Claustrophobia**, despite the physical openness.
- **Agoraphobia**, because the water above is vast and the surface is visible but unreachable.
- **Time distortion**, caused by cold, muted sound, slow-moving clouds, and the absence of ordinary walking.
- **Loneliness**, because residents may communicate without visible speech and may not acknowledge you as quickly as surface-city people would.
- **Panic at silence**, even though the place is not silent.
- **Unwise enthusiasm**: the urge to touch everything because every wall seems miraculous.

Practice controlled breathing, slow decision-making, and attention to your buddy or guide. If you begin to feel panic, stop moving, stabilize your buoyancy, signal, and let someone lead you. Do not bolt upward.

## A sensible first-visit itinerary

For an ordinary human traveler, a first visit should be short, guided, and limited to one district. A good first-day plan might be:

1. **Arrive at a designated external threshold**, not inside a home or air chamber.
2. Spend several minutes acclimating to pressure, light, equipment, and local signals.
3. Travel one open channel with a guide, maintaining distance from reed walls.
4. Observe an old stone structure that has been repurposed—a tower fishery or lily schoolhouse—without entering restricted areas.
5. Visit, from the edge, a warm seepage garden or public gathering route.
6. Exchange greetings or a prepared gift through your guide.
7. Leave before cold, gas supply, fatigue, or fascination compromises judgment.
8. Complete decompression and medical monitoring away from the settlement.

Do not attempt a festival, civic-hall ceremony, night visit, surface-mat excursion, or thermal-vent descent on your first trip.

## Emergency rules

Memorize these before you enter:

- **Lost?** Stop. Do not force your way through vegetation. Signal and wait if safe.
- **Low on breathing gas?** Tell your buddy and guide immediately. Do not conceal it to avoid embarrassment.
- **Equipment snagged?** Do not yank. Stabilize, identify the snag, and cut only your own disposable line if necessary—not local roots or fiberwork.
- **Silt cloud?** Freeze your movement, orient by buddy line or guide signal, and retreat slowly along the known route.
- **Cold confusion?** End the visit early.
- **Air-space temptation?** Do not enter unless invited and medically safe.
- **Rockslide or current shift?** Move away from walls and root foundations, not blindly upward.
- **Wildlife interaction?** Withdraw quietly. Otters, birds, turtles, fish, and frogs belong there more than you do.
- **Cultural uncertainty?** Ask through your guide. Waiting is almost always better than assuming.

## What to bring

A restrained, practical kit:

- Pressure-appropriate life support with redundant reserve.
- Thermal protection and backup heat.
- Buoyancy system you can control precisely.
- Mask and vision correction backups.
- Compact cutting tool for emergency entanglement only.
- Nonpolluting underwater slate or communication board.
- Personal locator / buddy line compatible with local routing.
- Small repair kit contained against loss.
- Medical and decompression support outside the settlement.
- A locally approved gift, if any.
- Nothing disposable, brightly invasive, noisy, or difficult to retrieve.

## What to leave behind

Leave behind the urge to conquer, survey, improve, extract, or prove courage.

The drowned town has survived by accepting change without treating every old thing as broken and every living thing as material. Its stone ruins are not merely ruins; its plants are not merely plants; its water is not merely an obstacle. A respectful visitor should arrive prepared to be guided by a place whose inhabitants have made a stable life inside conditions that would kill an unprepared stranger.

Your goal is not to demonstrate that you can endure their world.

Your goal is to pass through it without making it less able to endure itself.
