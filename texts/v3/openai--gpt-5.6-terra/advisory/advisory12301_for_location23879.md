## Preparing to Visit the White Basin

Do not approach the White Basin as a tourist destination, a settlement to be mapped, or a problem to be solved. It is an inhabited place, and its people are experts in surviving there. You will not improve their arrangements by offering to straighten a watercourse, reinforce a house permanently, survey the pond, capture an ice bear, or determine why the houses change. Such gestures will read not as help, but as proof that you have not understood where you are.

Prepare to be useful, light, quiet, and easy to move.

The basic rule is this:

**Nothing in the Basin should be allowed to depend on staying where it is. Including you.**

### Before Arrival

Do not enter alone if you can avoid it. Find a trader, herder, messenger, or family returning through the pass and ask—not demand—permission to accompany them. If nobody is traveling in, wait. The high valley is too narrow, bright, and structurally strange to navigate safely by ordinary maps.

A traveler should arrive with a purpose that can be explained simply:

- You are carrying trade goods.
- You are looking for temporary shelter during a pass closure.
- You have been sent with a message.
- You wish to learn a craft and will work in exchange for food and space.
- You are passing through.

Avoid saying you came because the Basin is famous, anomalous, picturesque, or “important.” Its people likely know it is unusual. They do not need outsiders to make an event of their daily life.

If you have multiversal equipment, conceal anything that looks too much like a weapon, a surveying instrument, a divination device, or a machine capable of making repeated tones. The Basin’s physical rules may be sensitive to resonance, rhythm, heat gradients, and observation. Even if the local warning about bells is only partly literal, treating it literally is wiser.

Do not activate beacons, drones, automated mapping systems, repeating alarms, or signal pings within the valley.

If you must maintain a return anchor or interdimensional locator, establish it **outside the basin**, ideally beyond the upper pass and out of sight of the valley mouth. Use a passive system. Do not rely on a fixed coordinate within the town; a doorway, ledge, or even building that is present when you arrive may not be present when you leave.

### What to Wear

Dress for wet cold rather than dry cold. The greatest danger is not simply freezing; it is alternating between meltwater, steam, wind, and ice until your clothing becomes heavy, rigid, or dangerously chilled.

Wear layers that remain insulating when damp:

- Fine wool, treated hide, waxed cloth, or other moisture-tolerant underlayers.
- A close-fitting insulating middle layer with no loose hems.
- A waterproof outer shell that does not crack in deep cold.
- Waterproof boots that rise well above the knee, with flexible soles and excellent grip.
- Spare dry foot wrappings carried in a watertight pouch.
- Thin work gloves beneath warmer outer mitts.
- A hood or goggles for snow glare and wind bursts.
- A light climbing harness worn beneath your outer layer.

Avoid long coats, scarves, dangling straps, rigid backpacks, and anything that catches on ladders, pulleys, goat rails, roof edges, or flood debris. In the White Basin, clothing should have no opinion about which direction is down.

Bring at least one short length of strong rope on your person at all times. Learn to tie and untie it with cold hands. You are more likely to need rope for securing a pot, a child’s basket, a door, or yourself during a sudden surge than for climbing.

A small flotation bladder is useful, but do not rely on it as your main flood protection. The channels are narrow, obstructed, fast-changing, and often roofed by ice. In some currents, floating may be worse than bracing yourself against a wall or climbing upward.

### Protect Your Eyes

The brightness is not decorative. It can incapacitate you.

The Basin reflects light from snow, wet stone, ice, steam, roofs, water, and even animal hides. Visitors unused to this may experience snow blindness, headaches, nausea, tears that freeze on the lashes, and visual afterimages severe enough to make moving through the town dangerous.

Bring:

- Dark, wraparound snow goggles.
- A second pair in case the first is lost or fogged.
- A soft cloth for drying lenses.
- A hood or brim that can reduce overhead glare.
- Eye drops if your physiology tolerates them.

Do not wear mirrored lenses unless you have a reason to believe the inhabitants will not find them unsettling. In a community accustomed to reading one another’s faces closely, hiding your eyes completely may impede trust. Smoked or darkened lenses are usually better than reflective ones.

When indoors, allow your vision time to recover before going back into the light. Do not step from a dim room directly onto a roof path while half-blind.

### Carry Less Than You Think You Need

The White Basin rewards compactness. Every object becomes a burden when water rises, a roof path shifts, or a host needs to clear a passage.

Your ideal pack should contain:

- Waterproof sleeping layer or compact bedding.
- High-calorie food that can be shared: dried fruit, fat, hard cheese, grain cakes, tea, salt, sugar, or preserved fish if culturally appropriate.
- Spare cordage.
- Needles, thread, wax, leather patches, and repair materials.
- A simple medical kit focused on cuts, sprains, cold injury, and waterborne illness.
- Small trade items: sewing needles, good cord, durable cloth, fishhooks, useful wax, plain salt, or carefully chosen metal goods.
- A watertight notebook, if you must record anything.
- A cup, spoon, and bowl that can be tied to your belt.
- A compact light source that does not hum, beep, strobe, or require frequent activation.

Do not bring bulky furniture, rigid cases, wheeled luggage, or heavy crates. Do not assume you will be able to set your pack down and find it again. Tie it to yourself, to an assigned beam, or to a location your host explicitly approves.

Keep nothing irreplaceable on the lowest level of any building.

### Trade and Gifts

Metal is scarce. A good knife, a steel needle, a bronze fitting, or a small durable tool may be worth more than you realize. Do not casually give a local child a metal trinket and create a family dispute, obligation, or theft risk.

Useful gifts are humble and divisible:

- Quality rope.
- Waterproof thread.
- Plain cloth.
- Wax.
- Salt.
- Durable leather.
- Food that stores well.
- Fine needles.
- Fishhooks.
- A small sharpening stone.

If you bring metal, offer it to an elder, household head, craft worker, or whoever receives trade—not to the first person who notices it. Do not offer a bell, chime, music box, clock with an alarm, repeating toy, or anything that makes a regular sound.

Avoid gifting printed maps of the Basin. Maps imply fixed edges, fixed houses, and a stable arrangement of routes. That may be inaccurate in ways that are not merely embarrassing.

### Learn the Local Flood Response Immediately

Your first question after being welcomed should not be “Where can I sleep?” It should be:

**“When the water rises, where should I go, what should I carry, and what should I not touch?”**

Have someone show you:

- The nearest high route.
- The handholds and roof paths.
- The safe places to stand during a pond convulsion.
- Which doors are meant to be removed.
- Which rooms are flood rooms and which are sleeping rooms.
- Which ropes are communal and must never be cut, moved, or used as personal lines.
- Where children, animals, and food stores are moved.
- The signal for an incoming surge, if there is one.
- The location of the nearest sheltered dry beam.

Practice the route once in daylight, slowly, with your pack on. You may not have the luxury of learning it during a real rise.

When the water begins to press upward, do not freeze in fascination. Do not stand on a bridge to watch. Do not attempt to photograph or measure the pond. Do not ask whether this surge is “normal.” Find something to lift, tie, carry, or brace.

If you are physically capable, make yourself useful in simple ways:

- Carry baskets upward.
- Hold a rope.
- Move tools and food stores.
- Guide goats along a route chosen by locals.
- Keep a child close without blocking their assigned task.
- Lash objects to beams.
- Bail, drain, or clear a watercourse only when instructed.

Never improvise a dam. Never block a channel because it seems to threaten a house. In the Basin, water is not merely an intruder; it is part of the settlement’s negotiated structure. Redirecting it may endanger homes you cannot see.

### Etiquette: Keep Close, Keep Quiet

The Basin is quiet because silence is useful, respectful, and possibly protective.

Speak softly. Speak close. Do not shout across water. Do not call to people on roofs. Do not test echoes.

Most importantly, do not create repeated sounds in the same place:

- No bells.
- No rhythmic hammering unless directed.
- No clapping games.
- No repeated whistles.
- No tapping a walking stick against stone.
- No humming the same bar over and over.
- No mechanical metronomes, ticking devices, warning alarms, or looping recordings.

If you need to get attention, follow local practice. It may involve touch, hand signs, a single sharp call, or sending someone physically to fetch another person.

Do not assume quiet means fear. The inhabitants may be warm, funny, affectionate, and sociable. Their restraint is a practiced form of awareness. Match it.

### Do Not Count the Houses

Take this literally.

Do not count houses, doors, bridges, chimneys, windows, ladders, families, or visible rooflines. Do not make a game of estimating population. Do not announce that you have found a discrepancy between morning and evening. Do not mark buildings with chalk, paint, tags, GPS pins, or numbered notes.

The warning may be social, cosmological, practical, or all three. In a place where structures are dismantled, rebuilt, shifted by flood, absorbed into adjacent homes, and occasionally altered by forces nobody has fully described, counting is at best rude and at worst a way of inviting fixation.

You may keep a personal route record, but make it relational rather than numerical:

- “From the drying racks to the blue-slate stair.”
- “Past the household with three goat skins over the lintel.”
- “Across the narrow bridge with the repaired rawhide rail.”
- “Upward toward the willow ledge.”

Even then, expect your route to fail after a storm.

If you wake to find a doorway where there was a wall, or a room beyond a familiar threshold that you do not recognize, do not enter it alone. Inform your host. Do not insist that the room “wasn’t there yesterday.” They may already know, and their response will tell you whether it is ordinary rebuilding, flood damage, a private matter, or something better left undisturbed.

### The Pond and the Pillar

Treat the central thaw as you would a sacred furnace, a dangerous animal, and an unstable shoreline at once.

Do not:

- Swim in it.
- Throw objects into it.
- Take water from it without permission.
- Test its temperature.
- Drop dye, tracer compounds, sensors, coins, offerings, or instruments into it.
- Chip its ice.
- Step onto its surface, even if it appears frozen.
- Stand beneath bridges that cross it during a rise.
- Attempt to reach the stone pillar.

Do not ask who ties the cloth to the pillar unless a local wishes to tell you. If you see cloth that was not there before, especially black cloth, do not make it the subject of public curiosity.

If you are awake after dark and notice movement near the pond, remain where you are unless summoned. Do not follow anyone onto the bridges. Do not assume that every silhouette in steam is a person.

The bronze awl is not a curiosity, museum object, or artifact for technological analysis. It is a communal survival tool. Do not touch it. Do not offer to replace it with steel. A replacement may be materially superior and socially useless.

### Animals

The goats are residents, not obstacles. They may have priority over paths you consider narrow or inconvenient. Do not startle them, herd them for amusement, ring anything near them, or attempt to feed them unfamiliar foods.

The birds are woven into the town’s life. Do not disturb nests under eaves or in chimneys. Their sudden collective departure may mean more than weather, and locals may read their behavior closely.

Blind trout and red-backed salamanders should not be collected, dissected, sampled, or removed from the Basin. A salamander in a household basin is not a novelty pet. If offered the chance to help release one, do so gently, with clean hands, and do not ask whether it “really cures fever.”

If you see an ice bear:

1. Stop moving.
2. Do not approach.
3. Do not run.
4. Do not make noise to drive it away.
5. Do not activate bright lights, sensors, or defensive devices unless it attacks.
6. Follow the behavior of the nearest local person exactly.

The ice bear may tolerate people and goats with unsettling indifference. That does not mean it is tame. It may be one animal, several similar animals, or something whose identity does not remain fixed in the way yours does. None of those possibilities makes it safer.

Never stand beneath its head, between it and water, or between it and a retaining wall marked with salt.

### Sleeping

You may not be offered a conventional bed. You may sleep on a loft, a shelf, a suspended platform, a roof room, or a shared raised space among supplies and children. Accept this without complaint. The location of a sleeping place is determined by flood behavior, household obligations, warmth, and structural confidence—not hospitality alone.

Before sleeping:

- Tie your boots where you can reach them immediately.
- Keep your outer layer arranged for rapid dressing.
- Secure your pack overhead.
- Keep one handhold or rope within reach.
- Ask what sound, touch, or movement will mean “get up now.”
- Do not sleep in a lower chamber merely because it is warmer.
- Do not obstruct ladders or access to pulley shelves.
- Do not leave gear near drains.

If you are prone to sleepwalking, dissociation, trance states, or involuntary dimensional displacement, tell your host in plain terms. The Basin is not a good place to wake in the wrong room, on the wrong roof, or beside the pond.

If you dream of water rising through stone, a bare pillar wrapped in cloth, or a house opening into a room full of snow, do not assume the dream is a message. Do not announce it at breakfast as a revelation. Write it down privately. If it repeats, or if you wake wet when your bedding is dry, seek the quiet advice of an elder or healer.

### Health Concerns

Expect:

- Cold injuries from dampness and wind.
- Sun blindness and severe glare headaches.
- Cuts and punctures from bone, stone, old wood, and ice.
- Slips on wet slate.
- Respiratory irritation from smoke, steam, mineral vapors, and dense cold air.
- Digestive trouble from unfamiliar water and preserved foods.
- Fatigue from constant low-level balancing and alertness.

Treat all cuts promptly. Water that looks clear may pass through animal pens, old drainage channels, heated mineral seams, or places that are not easily classified. Boil drinking water or follow local purification methods. Do not insult a household by refusing their water dramatically; a simple explanation that you have a sensitive constitution is enough.

Do not assume the hot springs are safe. Some may be pleasantly warm; others may carry mineral concentrations, gases, or microbial life your body is not prepared for. Never bathe without being invited and shown where.

If the peculiar density of the air causes dizziness, confusion, unusual hearing effects, or difficulty judging distance, stop moving. Sit or brace yourself somewhere high and secure. Tell someone. The town may have remedies, but they cannot help if you conceal symptoms until you fall into a channel.

### Multiversal Precautions

The White Basin appears to possess local instability centered on water, structure, repetition, and perhaps observation. Whether this is a genuine reality anomaly or merely a culture built around extreme environmental conditions is not a distinction you need to settle during your visit.

Assume the following:

- Fixed spatial coordinates may drift.
- Building interiors may not remain consistent.
- Measurements taken at different times may disagree.
- Instrument readings may be distorted by mineral water, ice, atmospheric density, or local metaphysical conditions.
- Repeated attempts to verify a discrepancy may worsen it.
- Routes may change after sleep, storms, floods, or darkness.
- The most dangerous thing you can do may be to become determined to prove that something impossible is happening.

Keep your anchoring technology outside the town whenever possible. If your travel method requires a physical doorway, gate, or marked location, do not place it in a house, near the pond, on a bridge, or beside a watercourse. Choose stable rock beyond the inhabited cluster, above known flood lines, and well away from avalanche paths.

Use non-repeating, manually checked timekeeping. Do not trust local sun position as a measure of day length; the sun’s strange fixed brilliance may be geographic, atmospheric, or something else. Eat, rest, and take medicines by your own internal schedule or a silent personal timer.

If your companions become fixated on counting, mapping, measuring the pillar, testing the pond, or proving whether the bear is singular, remove them from the Basin. Curiosity is normal. Compulsion is dangerous.

### How to Leave

Do not depart abruptly after a flood, a storm, the appearance of black cloth on the pillar, or a structural change in your host’s home. Even if you are not directly involved, leaving at such a moment may be interpreted as abandonment—or may simply put you onto a pass that has become impassable.

Tell your hosts your intended departure time well in advance. Ask whether the route is safe. Pay what you owe before you shoulder your pack. Do not leave unwanted “helpful” equipment behind, especially electronics, noisemaking devices, metal tools, or sealed containers.

Before crossing the pass, check:

- Boots and foot wrappings are dry enough.
- Goggles are accessible.
- Your rope is not frayed.
- You have water, food, and a wind layer.
- You are not carrying anything you were merely allowed to borrow.
- You have not accidentally packed a household salamander, fish basket, child’s toy, communal tool, or strip of ritual cloth.

Do not look back from the valley rim expecting to see a stable town arranged below you. If you do look back, accept whatever you see without trying to reconcile it to memory.

The White Basin survives by yielding intelligently to forces that would destroy a more rigid place. A visitor should do the same. Arrive with strong boots, good rope, dimmed eyes, empty hands ready to work, and enough humility to let the people who live beside the unfinished thaw teach you how to stand.
