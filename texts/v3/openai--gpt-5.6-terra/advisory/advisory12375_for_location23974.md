## Treat This as an Underwater Expedition, Not a Ruin Visit

Do not prepare for this place as though you are visiting a flooded city for an afternoon. There is no evident shore, no dependable surface, no wildlife, no local population, and no guarantee that “up” leads to air. The world is water-filled on every accessible scale. Its distances are continental. Its darkness is functional rather than dramatic: it will prevent you from seeing hazards long before you feel endangered by them.

An ordinary human should not enter without a reliable way back to their own world, preferably through a stable transit aperture anchored outside the water. Your arrival point should be treated as a base camp, not merely an entrance.

If your multiversal travel method cannot maintain a return route through water, pressure, darkness, and possibly shifting location, do not visit.

---

## Establish a Dry, Pressurized Base Before Exploring

Your first priority is an air-filled refuge.

Ideally, bring or generate a small pressure-rated habitat with:

- A reliable breathable-air supply
- Carbon-dioxide scrubbing
- Heat
- Lighting
- A dry sleeping area
- Emergency medical supplies
- Communications and navigation equipment
- A way to recharge or replace power cells
- A toilet/waste containment system
- A physical connection to your return portal

Do not assume there are air pockets in buildings. There may be chambers with trapped gas, but they are likely stale, hypoxic, contaminated, or structurally dangerous. A seemingly intact upper room may be full of water, poisonous gas, decomposing organic matter, or a pressure difference capable of causing injury when opened.

Your refuge should be positioned in open water above a broad paved area or other stable terrain, not beside a tower, roofline, old retaining wall, or timber platform. The ruins have endured for centuries, but they are soft with age. A structure can look permanent right until your anchor line, body weight, or equipment vibration gives it the final reason to settle.

Mark the base aggressively. Use lights, reflective panels, sonar beacons, and a physical guide line extending outward along your primary route. In water this dark, losing sight of home can happen in seconds.

## Bring Proper Diving Equipment—With Redundancy

This is not a destination for casual scuba gear.

The water is cold, the depth may vary wildly, and many areas may be beyond safe recreational-diving limits. Use equipment appropriate to technical, cold-water, penetration diving—or, better, an atmospheric diving suit or small submersible if multiversal transport allows it.

At minimum, a human explorer needs:

- A cold-water drysuit or heated diving suit
- Thermal underlayers suitable for prolonged immersion
- Redundant breathing systems
- Redundant regulators and masks
- A full-face mask or helmet, preferably with communications
- Buoyancy control equipment you can operate by touch
- Dive computer(s) capable of logging depth, time, decompression, and temperature
- Multiple independent lights
- Chemical light markers or low-power path lights
- Reel lines, guide lines, and cutting tools
- A sonar unit or compact imaging sonar
- Waterproof slate or voice recorder
- Emergency signaling beacon
- Gloves strong enough for stone, wet timber, rope, and roots
- A compact first-aid kit rated for diving emergencies
- An emergency ascent/return plan that does **not** depend on finding open water above you

Carry at least three light sources. Your primary lamp will fail eventually. Your backup lamp may fail when you are tangled in weed beneath a roof. Your third light is not excess; it is the difference between inconvenience and panic.

Avoid exposed hoses, dangling straps, loose tools, trailing cables, and broad fins. The country is full of kelp ribbons, ancient ropes, trellis frames, drowned branches, roof beams, gate mechanisms, and post forests. Anything that hangs loose can become an anchor.

A streamlined propulsion unit can be useful in broad avenues and reservoir basins, but do not use one inside buildings, post forests, gardens, gates, or narrow streets. A scooter can pull you into a snare before you have time to release it.

## Assume Pressure Is Dangerous Until Measured

The description of the place suggests immense vertical extent. You may arrive at one depth and travel gradually into another without recognizing the change. A road can slope for miles. A reservoir wall can guide you into cold deeper water. An old tower may tempt you upward into a zone where the water thins—but “thinner” is not the same as breathable.

Never travel beyond your decompression plan.

This is especially important because the landscape makes gradual depth change hard to perceive. You will not have a normal shoreline, seabed, or sunlit surface to orient you. A street may gently descend for hours. A garden terrace may become a basin. A broad, seemingly level communal floor may lie several atmospheres deeper at one end than the other.

Set strict depth limits before every excursion. Program alarms. Check instruments at regular intervals, not only when you feel concerned.

If your equipment reports a sudden temperature drop, density change, visibility shift, or pressure anomaly near an old gate, spillway, channel, or reservoir divide, stop and assess. The surviving water-control structures may still create unexpected layers and currents. Passing through a narrow log gate could mean crossing into much colder water, murkier water, or water moving in a direction your body cannot comfortably fight.

## Navigation Will Be Your Main Survival Skill

The ruins are deceptively legible. Roads, plazas, walls, stairways, towers, and gardens can make you feel oriented. But nearly everything has been softened, overgrown, tilted, or turned into terrain. A roof can resemble a hill. A market basin can resemble a valley. A collapsed wall can look like a natural ridge. You may think you are following a road only to discover you have been circling the same reservoir embankment for hours.

Use several forms of navigation at once:

- Physical guideline from base
- Compass or inertial navigator
- Sonar mapping
- Depth tracking
- Timed route logs
- Distinctive marker tags at major turns
- Visual landmarks only as a secondary aid

Do not trust the appearance of “up.” The suspended towers, bridge remnants, and long weed curtains may create a false ceiling. The dark overhead may be structure, open water, or simply water extending beyond your lights.

When entering any enclosed or semi-enclosed space—rooms, granaries, post forests, courtyards, cisterns, gates, covered passages—follow cave-diving rules:

1. Maintain a continuous line to a known exit.
2. Do not enter spaces too narrow to turn around in unless you have a specific retrieval plan.
3. Reserve more than half your breathing supply for return; in unfamiliar conditions, reserve more.
4. Never stir sediment unnecessarily.
5. Never enter alone.

The brown districts described in the location are especially hazardous. Silt can transform a clear corridor into total blindness with one fin stroke. In a silt-out, stop moving, find your line, control breathing, and retreat slowly. Blind flight through old architecture is how divers become permanently lost.

## Travel With a Partner, But Stay Within Reach

The silence and scale of this place will make companionship essential. Take at least one other person, trained in technical diving and underwater rescue. Keep visual, tactile, or line contact in low visibility.

Agree on signals before entering:

- Turn back
- Low air
- Lost line
- Entanglement
- Cold stress
- Equipment failure
- Structural instability
- Unidentified current
- Immediate portal return

Voice communication through full-face masks is valuable, but do not depend on it. Water, stone, pressure, and distance may interfere. Hand signals and touch signals remain necessary.

Do not separate to “look around opposite sides of the room.” Rooms can be entire drowned halls. A few moments apart can become a permanent separation in green water and drifting silt.

## Expect Cold to Be a Constant Threat

This water is described as cold, and deep water steals heat steadily. Even in an excellent suit, fatigue and hypothermia can arrive quietly. The setting’s calmness makes this worse: you may be tempted to linger, move slowly, and spend too long examining a garden wall or a carved stone.

Watch for:

- Clumsiness with clips, valves, and tools
- Delayed thinking
- Unusual calm or apathy
- Shivering
- Difficulty reading instruments
- Slowed responses to your partner
- Fixation on minor tasks

Set a fixed exploration duration and honor it. Return before you feel tired. Warm up fully between dives or excursions.

The local “weather” is pressure and current rather than wind. A cold front can apparently move across the plain for weeks, bringing silt haze, bending vegetation, and altering visibility. If the water begins to darken, weeds begin aligning strongly, ceramic charms or hanging ropes start moving audibly, or a distant haze approaches down an avenue, return to base. Do not wait to see whether the change becomes severe.

## The Plants Are Not Harmless Just Because They Are Quiet

There are no described animals, but the vegetation is extensive and structurally powerful. It fills buildings, streets, courtyards, and foundations. It holds ruins together while also dismantling them.

Treat every plant mass as a possible entanglement hazard.

Avoid swimming through:

- Dense kelp drapes
- Root masses entering doorways
- Trellis frames covered in pondweed
- Reed-roof remnants
- Suspended weed curtains beneath towers or bridges
- Forests of upright timber posts
- Gardens whose bed walls are no longer visible

Carry at least two cutting tools in separate accessible locations: one knife and one line cutter or shears. Make sure you can reach them with either hand and while upside down.

Do not cut vegetation casually. It may be holding a wall, beam, or roof fragment in place. It may also be the only thing distinguishing a safe passage from a chamber packed with silt. If you must cut free, make the smallest cut needed to retreat.

Do not eat the vegetation, drink untreated local water, or assume the plants are biologically compatible with you. The absence of visible animal life could indicate unusual chemistry, ecological isolation, toxins, low nutrient conditions, or something stranger. Even ordinary algae can cause illness; unfamiliar multiversal flora deserves complete caution.

## Do Not Rely on the Silence

The lack of animals will be psychologically unsettling. Humans expect water to contain motion: fish scattering, insects skating, shells clicking, birds calling overhead. Here, there may be only your own bubbles, your own pulse, gear noise, weed movement, and the settling sounds of very old buildings.

This can produce distorted judgment.

A traveler may begin interpreting random sounds as voices, footsteps, knocks, or activity. A distant contraction of timber can sound deliberate. Ceramic charms brushing together may resemble signals. Your own lights may turn pale leaves into moving figures. The long vertical lines of post forests can make depth and distance feel unreliable.

Prepare for this mentally:

- Use routine verbal check-ins with your partner.
- Record observations rather than immediately assigning meaning to them.
- Rest between excursions.
- Do not explore when sleep-deprived.
- If you feel watched, followed, or called by something you cannot independently verify, return to base and reassess.
- Respect fear, but do not let it make you abandon your line or rush through silt.

The place does not sound actively hostile. Its danger is indifference, scale, cold, and the ease with which an ancient environment can make a person small and lost.

## Respect the Ruins as a Whole World, Not a Quarry

Everything here was made by human or human-like labor: fired brick, fitted stone, timber platforms, channels, clay-lined ponds, walls, gates, rope structures, gardens, shrines, granaries, and communal floors. Even when a building is almost indistinguishable from a hill, it may be a home, a storehouse, a water system, or a place of ceremony.

Do not pry loose artifacts. Do not remove masonry. Do not force doors. Do not enter by breaking a wall or cutting through a structural mat. Do not take carvings, charms, pottery, or human-made objects as souvenirs.

Apart from ethical concerns, touching or removing material may destabilize the site. In this world, roots, silt, stone, and timber have reached a fragile equilibrium. A single displaced block can release trapped sediment, collapse a lintel, or alter flow through a chamber.

If you document the place:

- Photograph with low-output, diffuse light where possible.
- Use sonar and non-contact scanning.
- Note location, depth, temperature, current, and surrounding structure.
- Avoid stirring up sediment around inscriptions or mosaics.
- Never scrape growth from a wall merely to make an image clearer.
- Leave markers that are removable and inert; retrieve them afterward.

The vegetation has covered many human traces, but that does not make those traces abandoned in the moral sense. You are a visitor to the remains of a civilization that expected land to stay dry.

## Recommended First Excursions

Do not begin with deep avenues, towers, gates, or interiors.

Your first visits should be short surveys around the base:

### 1. Open pavement survey
Find a broad, low-relief paved region with clear sightlines. Learn how the water affects your movement, buoyancy, lighting, instruments, and communications.

### 2. Road-following reconnaissance
Follow a single visible old road for a limited distance while laying line and recording depth. Turn around well before your planned limit.

### 3. Exterior architecture survey
Examine the outside of a small stone building or wall complex without entering. Learn what “stable” looks like here.

### 4. Garden-edge survey
Approach, but do not penetrate, a submerged garden. Determine how dense the vegetation is and whether it conceals channels, steps, or pits.

### 5. Reservoir-wall observation
From a safe distance, observe an embankment or gate for current patterns, temperature gradients, and visibility changes. Do not pass through an opening until you understand its flow.

Only after repeated successful excursions should you attempt interiors, post forests, elevated structures, or crossings between basins.

## Places to Avoid Until You Have Serious Experience

Avoid these entirely on an early visit:

- Narrow watergates between reservoir basins
- Old spillways and channel mouths
- Cisterns and submerged wells
- Towers and upper platforms
- Long bridges with hanging weed beneath them
- Dense timber colonnades
- Brick districts during poor visibility
- Brown silt-front regions
- Rooms entered through small windows or swollen doorways
- Deep avenues where no return line can be maintained
- Any place where current pulls consistently inward

A gate that has remained partly closed for centuries may be holding back a significant pressure difference. A passage that looks wide enough may narrow beyond sight. An apparently open cistern may descend far below your safe depth. A tower may be structurally intact only because no one has touched it in a thousand years.

## Bring Your Own Food, Water, and Medical Plan

There is no reliable local food web. Even if plants seem abundant, there are no described fish, shellfish, birds, insects, or animals. Do not count on harvesting anything.

Bring sealed food and potable water in your base habitat. Plan for more than the intended trip duration. In multiversal travel, return failures happen; this is not a place where an unprepared person can forage.

Medical supplies should include treatment capability for:

- Hypothermia
- Decompression illness
- Barotrauma
- Lacerations and punctures
- Entrapment injuries
- Panic and acute stress
- Eye/skin irritation from unknown biological material
- Respiratory contamination
- Infection control

If possible, have a medically trained person remain at base or on the far side of the return portal.

## A Simple Rule for Decision-Making

When uncertain, choose the option that leaves you closer to your line, your partner, your air, and your way home.

Do not take risks because the place seems peaceful. It is peaceful in the way a glacier, desert, or abandoned ocean is peaceful: it has no urgency because it does not need you to survive.

The drowned plain will still be there if you turn back. The road will still lead between gardens. The softened towers will still hang above the dark. The roots will still hold the old walls in their slow embrace. Nothing in that vast, water-covered country requires you to hurry deeper.

Visit gently. Move slowly. Leave little behind. And always keep a hand, a light, or a line pointed toward home.
