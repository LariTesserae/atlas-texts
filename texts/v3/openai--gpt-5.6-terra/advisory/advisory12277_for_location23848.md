Prepare for this as you would for a **confined-space industrial waterworks visit**, not as a ruin dive or a pleasant underground swim. It is new, functional infrastructure: bright, beautiful, hot, noisy, and actively dangerous in ways that will not look dramatic until something moves.

## Before you go

Do not enter casually, alone, or without the people responsible for the reservoir knowing you are there. Its layout is simple enough to understand but unforgiving:

- The only reliable exits are narrow vertical shafts.
- Much of the usable floor is underwater.
- The air space is shallow, humid, and potentially poor in oxygen.
- Water flows continuously and machinery cycles on its own schedule.
- Twice daily, the drain opens and the chamber changes from a flooded space to a violent emptying basin.

Find out, specifically:

1. **The drain schedule.**  
   Ask when the bronze drain disk is lifted, how long emptying takes, when refill begins, and whether the cycle can be locked out. Never be inside during an unsupervised drainage or refill cycle. The draining water may create powerful currents toward the floor drain, and a person caught low in the chamber could be pinned, injured against tile or brickwork, or pulled beneath the surface.

2. **The intake and wheel schedule.**  
   Determine whether the incoming flow and waterwheel can be stopped. A rough-built wheel, cup chain, ropes, rails, and sluice gate are not safe to touch while operating. Treat every moving element as capable of catching clothing, fingers, hair, or equipment.

3. **Water depth and temperature.**  
   The floor is visible, but that does not mean it is safely walkable. Confirm the deepest point, the location of submerged ledges and steps, and whether the warm water is clean enough for accidental immersion. “Fresh” water can still carry mineral contaminants, stagnant pockets, pathogens from upstream channels, or residues from pitch, lead, and maintenance materials.

4. **Air quality.**  
   The hot air pocket has little volume and is separated from open air by shafts with lattices. Humidity, carbon dioxide buildup, reduced oxygen, and gases from decay or nearby channels are all plausible. Have the air tested before entry, especially near the ceiling, corners, drains, and intake structures. If no competent test is available, do not enter the chamber itself.

5. **Who can get you out.**  
   There should be a surface attendant who remains outside, knows exactly how many people are inside, has a rope system ready, and can stop water flow or summon rescue. A companion standing waist-deep beside you is not an adequate rescue plan.

## Best time to visit

The safest and most revealing time is likely **shortly after the reservoir has been drained, inspected, and declared safe**, before refill begins.

At that time you can see:

- the pale ceramic floor;
- the slope toward the perforated bronze drain;
- sediment patterns;
- the wheel hanging still;
- filter troughs, reed baskets, braces, and chisel marks;
- the true height of the vault and shafts.

It will still be wet, slippery, loud with dripping, and humid, but you will not be swimming in a confined space around running machinery.

A second-best option is entry while the reservoir is flooded but **isolated from intake, drain, and wheel**, with the water level stabilized and a trained local guide. This is more visually spectacular—the shafts of sunlight and submerged geometry are part of the place—but substantially more hazardous.

Avoid entering during:

- active draining;
- the first part of refilling;
- sluice adjustments;
- maintenance of the wheel or cup chain;
- extreme midday heat if the air pocket is already oppressive;
- any period after a storm, upstream contamination event, structural shift, or unusual water discoloration.

## What to wear

Dress for warm immersion, abrasive masonry, and a wet vertical exit.

### Essential clothing and equipment

- **A properly fitted buoyancy aid or compact life vest.**  
  Choose one that will not easily snag on chains, rails, or the wheel. You want help staying afloat, not a bulky vest that prevents movement under low overhead clearance.

- **Water shoes or thin-soled boots with excellent wet grip.**  
  Ceramic tile, mineral haze, algae near the shafts, submerged steps, and wet brick will all be slick. Avoid loose sandals. The floor may also conceal sharp ceramic edges, grit, wire, or dropped tools.

- **Light, close-fitting clothing.**  
  Synthetic quick-drying layers are better than cotton, which becomes heavy and cold after immersion—even in warm water. Avoid long skirts, dangling belts, scarves, loose sleeves, or anything that can catch in the wheel and cup chain.

- **A helmet.**  
  The barrel vault is low, the shafts are narrow, and iron rings project overhead. A climbing or water-rescue helmet is appropriate.

- **Gloves.**  
  Use grippy work gloves that still allow dexterity. Brick, copper wire, rough rope, pitch, and wet wooden braces can abrade or splinter skin.

- **A waterproof headlamp, even at noon.**  
  The light shafts make the central water bright, but corners, recesses, intake mouths, spaces behind braces, and the lower parts of the vault will be dim. Wear the light on your helmet; do not rely on a handheld lamp while climbing or swimming.

- **Eye protection if you will work rather than merely observe.**  
  Mineral-rich water, lime dust, and splashes from filters can irritate the eyes. Clear sealed goggles are preferable to ordinary glasses.

- **A whistle and waterproof personal locator or radio, if compatible with the setting.**  
  The chamber’s acoustics enlarge and blur sound. A shout may be difficult to distinguish from the wheel, intake, or echo. Establish simple signal calls before entry.

### Avoid bringing

- Valuable loose objects: dropped items will sink toward the drain.
- Paper maps or notebooks unless sealed.
- Open flames: the air pocket may be poor, and fire adds oxygen demand and fumes.
- Long camera straps, necklaces, loose hair, or trailing cables.
- Unprotected electronics.
- Anything you cannot afford to get wet, muddy, or coated in mineral film.

If you bring a camera, tether it securely to your body with a short breakaway lanyard and do not operate it near machinery or during ladder transitions.

## Breathing and heat

The chamber’s warmth is not merely uncomfortable. It can make judgment worse.

Expect:

- heavy humidity;
- sweating that does not evaporate;
- hot wet air;
- condensation dripping from the vault;
- fatigue from treading water, climbing, and noise;
- a sensation of closeness or breathlessness under the low ceiling.

Hydrate before entering, but do not rely on reservoir water. Bring drinking water in a sealed bottle that can be clipped securely to you or kept at the surface. Take short visits rather than trying to endure the environment.

Leave immediately if you develop:

- headache;
- dizziness;
- nausea;
- confusion;
- unusual shortness of breath;
- chest tightness;
- panic;
- sudden tiredness;
- ringing ears severe enough to impair communication.

Those symptoms can come from heat, dehydration, bad air, stress, or noise. In a confined flooded chamber, the cause matters less than getting out promptly.

## Entering safely

The rope ladder descent is a transition point: most accidents will happen there.

Before descending:

- Check that the ladder is secured at both ends and does not hang near moving machinery.
- Use a backup safety line or harness if available.
- Ensure the shaft lattice can be opened from above without trapping the line.
- Confirm where you will land: ledge, submerged step, or open water.
- Keep three points of contact until you deliberately enter the water.
- Have someone at the surface manage the rope and watch you.

Do not jump in. The water may conceal ledges, braces, pipes, or the edge of the channel feeding the wheel.

Once in the water, pause near the entry point. Let your eyes adjust to the unusual illumination. Test your buoyancy. Identify:

- the ladder;
- the nearest maintenance ledge;
- the wheel channel;
- intake flow;
- overflow throat;
- deepest slope toward the drain;
- any ropes or handholds along the walls.

Memorize the route back before moving farther away.

## Moving through the chamber

Treat the reservoir as a place of **currents, hidden edges, and constructed intent**. Its geometry is regular, but it was built for water, not for you.

- Stay close to walls and known handholds.
- Move slowly; vigorous swimming will churn sediment and may make the air pocket feel more unstable or close.
- Never put hands into pipes, grates, cup guides, filter openings, or the drainage disk.
- Keep well clear of the waterwheel and its channel, even if it looks slow. Slow machinery has time to pull and crush.
- Do not lean on wooden braces. They may be temporary, water-softened, or supporting a weak section.
- Assume black pitch at slab joints is sticky and may be chemically unpleasant; do not touch it unnecessarily.
- Do not stand on reed baskets, filter troughs, clay pipes, or bronze grates. They are working components, not platforms.
- Do not deliberately disturb the small patches of algae or reeds. They are part of the filtration system and likely monitored for a reason.

If the reservoir is flooded nearly to the vault, do not attempt to swim beneath obstructions or “explore” the narrow air pocket at the crown. A few inches of breathing space can become disorienting very quickly, especially under the noise of water.

## The noise problem

The reservoir will be louder than it appears. Constant water and repeated echoes can erase distance and direction.

Prepare a communication plan that does not depend on normal conversation:

- one whistle blast: stop and look;
- two: return to the entry point;
- repeated blasts: emergency / evacuate;
- a prearranged tug signal on a safety line if using one.

Agree that **anyone can call an exit without debate**. In this space, delayed communication is dangerous.

Wear hearing protection only with caution. Earplugs may reduce fatigue, but they can also make it harder to hear warnings or changes in machinery. If you use them, use filtered plugs that reduce harsh volume rather than fully blocking sound, and make visual signals part of the plan.

## If you are there for maintenance or close inspection

Do not perform maintenance while the chamber is live unless you are trained for that system.

For any work involving the drain, wheel, sluice, pipes, or filters:

- lock out and physically secure the intake, drain mechanism, and wheel;
- verify that the system cannot restart from another location;
- post an attendant at every control point;
- lower water to a known safe level;
- test air continuously;
- use fall protection where the wet floor slopes into deeper water;
- account for tools before and after work;
- wash exposed skin afterward, especially after handling lead weights, pitch, lime residue, or mineral deposits.

The lead weights deserve particular caution. Do not sand, scrape, heat, or abrade them. Wash hands before eating, drinking, or touching your face.

## Respect the place’s logic

This is not a wild underground lake. It is a disciplined, newly built machine made of brick, clay, bronze, wood, rope, and moving water. Its beauty comes from that exactness: sunlight made into shifting nets, tiles visible under warm clear water, every green thing limited to a designated purpose.

The safest way to visit is to follow its rules rather than impose your own:

- water takes the route it was given;
- light comes only from the shafts;
- every accessible surface has a maintenance reason;
- moving parts are not ornaments;
- the chamber is safest when its keepers say it is safe.

Come with humility, secure footwear, a helmet, buoyancy, reliable supervision, and an exit plan. If you can arrange it, see it once drained and once calmly flooded. The first visit will teach you how it was made. The second will show you what it is for.
