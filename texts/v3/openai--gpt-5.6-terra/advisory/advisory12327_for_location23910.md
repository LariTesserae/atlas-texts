## Before you go

Treat the Stillworks as an active industrial site, a confined cave system, and a temporal anomaly at once. Do not assume that because its machinery appears old, or its ecology appears stable, anything there is harmless or inactive.

Most importantly: arrange a reliable extraction plan that does **not** depend on the steel “Surface Access” door. It is not an exit. It is a sealed boundary whose label may have been true for an immeasurably long time.

Bring a multiversal return method that you can activate manually and that does not require local time to pass, a radio signal to propagate normally, or an automated countdown to complete. Test it before entering. If possible, leave a partner or retrieval team outside the basin with instructions to extract you at a fixed external time, but do not rely solely on their clocks matching your experience inside.

Establish a clear abort rule before descent: for example, leave immediately if you become lost, touch a route, lose respiratory protection, see any change in machine rhythm, or feel compelled to “fix” something. The Stillworks rewards restraint.

## What to wear

Wear clothing appropriate for a hot, dry, abrasive industrial cave.

- A well-fitted particulate respirator or powered air-purifying respirator rated for fine mineral dust. The air contains gypsum, sulfur-bearing dust, copper salts, insulation fungus, and unknown biological particles. A simple cloth mask is inadequate.
- Sealed eye protection: goggles rather than open safety glasses. Dust, static sparks, and low ceilings make eye injury likely.
- Hearing protection. The machinery is loud enough to damage hearing and subtle enough that you still need to notice changes in its rhythm. Use filtered earplugs or electronic hearing protection that reduces the piston and gear noise without making you deaf to alarms, animal movement, or a guide.
- Leather or cut-resistant gloves under insulated electrical overgloves. But understand: gloves are not permission to touch the copper-veined walls, exposed cabling, or machinery.
- Sturdy boots with stiff soles, ankle support, and no loose laces. The floor is sharp, dusty, crossed by animal traffic, and likely electrically charged in places.
- Long sleeves, long trousers, and a close-fitting outer layer. Avoid dangling straps, ponchos, loose scarves, and anything that can enter a belt, snag on mineral spires, or interfere with a wheel-hound route.
- Knee pads and a hard hat. Some passages are low and freshly broken; the sharpness of the rock suggests little natural stabilization.

Avoid conductive jewelry, metal watches, exposed piercings, and any equipment that cannot tolerate dust, static, heat, or corrosive salts.

## What to carry

Carry lightly. You may need to move quickly into a safe gap without stepping into a wheel-hound circuit.

Useful equipment includes:

- Independent headlamp with a warm or red mode, plus spare batteries. Do not depend on the amber work lamps, and do not tamper with their cables.
- Paper map, grease pencil, and a mechanical compass—but do not assume electronic navigation, GPS, or magnetic readings will behave normally around transformers, copper veins, and whatever lies below the floor.
- A simple analog wristwatch and a separate mechanical timer, mainly for personal orientation. Compare them outside afterward; do not use discrepancies as a reason to linger.
- A compact first-aid kit emphasizing eye wash, saline, dressings, burn treatment, electrolyte packets, and supplies for cuts and abrasions.
- Water in sealed bottles, plus more than you expect to need. The dryness will dehydrate you quickly, and the warmth may conceal it until you are already impaired.
- A high-calorie, low-crumb food source in sealed packaging. Do not eat local organisms or let food touch mineral surfaces.
- A nonconductive walking staff or fiberglass probe for checking footing—not for moving animals, testing copper walls, or exploring pipes.
- A camera and notebook if documentation is your purpose. Favor passive recording. Do not place tags, trackers, bait, or instruments on animals, belts, vents, nests, or plants.
- A small nonmetallic ground cloth or kneeling pad, useful if you must rest without sitting directly on salt dust, mineral crust, or a concealed current path.

Do **not** bring explosives, cutting tools, signal flares, open flame, chemical cleaners, magnetic clamps, drones that may collide with moving machinery, or any device that emits strong sound, vibration, radio power, or light without a compelling reason. The Stillworks may be balanced around small, constant inputs. A “minor” disturbance could be the first non-routine event it has encountered in a very long time.

## On entry

Pause at the threshold. Let your eyes, lungs, ears, and sense of footing adjust before walking farther in.

The noise will make conversation difficult and will encourage you to hurry. Do not hurry. Use hand signals with companions. Keep your group small—two to four people is sensible—and remain within sight of one another. A larger group is more likely to obstruct animal paths, stir dust, and make route discipline impossible.

Before proceeding, identify:

1. The route back to the entrance.
2. The nearest safe standing gaps between wheel-hound tracks.
3. The location of Compressor Two.
4. Any warm copper-veined wall sections.
5. The maintenance platforms and their likely fall hazards.
6. Your chosen emergency extraction location, ideally away from moving belts, animal routes, exposed copper, and mineral spires.

Mark your own route only with removable, nonintrusive methods: notes in your own notebook, visual landmarks, or a companion’s observations. Avoid tape, paint, chalk, flags, dropped objects, or anything that might become a new feature in a place where nothing is supposed to accumulate or change.

## Wheel-hound etiquette is survival etiquette

The wheel-hounds are not ordinary wildlife. Their paths are infrastructure.

Watch them long enough to identify the lines before crossing the chamber floor. Their circuits should appear as repeated lanes, often marked by shed plates, dust patterns, and habitual passages through gaps. Do not step onto a line merely because it is temporarily empty. A route is occupied by the pattern, not just by the animal currently visible.

Stand in the gaps between tracks. Cross only when you can do so in one continuous motion without hesitating, stopping, or forcing an animal to alter course. Never test whether they will swerve. The description is explicit: they will not.

If a wheel-hound is approaching:

- Do not kick, grab, shout at, or shine a light directly at it.
- Do not try to jump over a rolling animal at the last second.
- Move calmly into the nearest known safe gap.
- If you cannot move safely, make yourself as compact and stable as possible without entering the route further; protect hands, ankles, and face.

A collision with a fox-sized, plated animal moving at speed may break bones, and a panicked response could put you into the path of the next one. Hundreds may be following.

Do not take a shed plate as a souvenir. It may mark a safety gap, may have an ecological role, and may carry static charge or biological residue.

## Keep away from machinery

Treat every belt, piston, flywheel, compressor, transformer, cable trench, and valve as live and dangerous.

The machinery is not visibly deteriorating, but that does not mean it cannot injure you. In fact, machinery that never slows, wears, or responds to ordinary expectations is more dangerous than a machine that behaves predictably.

Maintain a generous distance from:

- The flywheel and wire guard.
- Moving belts and belt-line extensions.
- Compressor plinths and floor collars.
- Cable runs and transformer housings.
- Vents, ducts, and cracked stone “throats.”
- The sharp white spires near the far door.

Never put an object through a guard, onto a belt, into a vent, or near a piston collar. Do not retrieve a dropped item if retrieving it requires crossing a hazard line, leaning across machinery, or entering an animal route. A lost tool is cheaper than a changed Stillworks.

Under no circumstances should you press buttons, turn valves, pull levers, reset breakers, oil a bearing, tighten a bolt, silence an alarm, or attempt to improve the machines’ condition. Do not assume a stuck-looking control is inert. The safest interpretation is that every visible system is part of a stable whole whose failure modes are unknown.

## Electrical and static hazards

The warm copper walls are specifically dangerous. Assume they are energized even where they look dry, dull, or insulated by mineral deposits.

Do not touch them. Do not lean against them. Do not use them as handholds in narrow passages. Do not set wet equipment, metal gear, or a companion’s body against them.

Bell-moss can discharge static when brushed by wheel-hounds. It is tempting to investigate the blue sparks, but resist that temptation. Static near mineral dust, old electrical equipment, and unknown gases is not a novelty. Keep clear of it, and do not deliberately use it to charge equipment or “ground” yourself.

If someone is electrically locked to a wall or conductor:

- Do not grab them directly.
- Cut local contact only if you can do so with a nonconductive object while standing on dry insulated footing.
- Do not attempt to switch off the Stillworks.
- Use your extraction method or withdraw and seek help from outside if possible.

## Biological precautions

Nothing in the chamber should be handled.

The furnace crickets may look like oversized insects, but they occupy warm cable trenches and feed on substances you do not want on your skin or in your lungs. Their behavior around an immobilized hand is a warning, not an invitation to study them closely.

The blind burrowing doves may be harmless if undisturbed, but their nests are inside machine housings for a reason. Do not look into cavities, put hands into openings, or disturb their felted nesting material. Their coos may mask changes in machine sound, and an animal defending a nest in a confined space can cause a dangerous reflexive movement.

Bristle fans are sharp, rigid, and likely chemically active. Bell-moss can discharge. Avoid both. Do not collect samples unless your visit is specifically equipped and authorized for sterile, contained sampling—and even then, only if removal will not interfere with the Stillworks’ balance.

Before leaving, brush mineral dust from boots and clothing at a designated decontamination point outside the basin. Seal disposable filters, wipes, and sample containers. Do not carry organisms or dust casually into another world.

## The pipe behind Compressor Two

Do not enter it.

Not “do not enter alone.” Not “do not enter without a rope.” Do not enter.

A passage that returns travelers after exactly forty minutes, facing back toward the entrance, with unexplained dust accumulation is not a useful shortcut. It is a closed process. The fact that wheel-hounds use it does not make it safe for people; their bodies, routes, and perhaps their role in the Stillworks may be adapted to it.

Do not throw objects into it, send a drone through it, trail a line behind you, shine patterned lights into it, or wait at its mouth expecting to learn where it goes. The most valuable information has already been established: it does not provide passage.

## Time and memory precautions

The Stillworks has the character of a place where motion occurs without progression. Treat your own sense of duration, fatigue, hunger, distance, and sequence as potentially unreliable.

Use redundant records:

- Write entry time outside the chamber.
- Record observations on paper as they occur.
- Have each companion keep an independent log.
- Take periodic photographs of your own group and equipment, not just the surroundings.
- Agree on a maximum visit duration measured by external time where possible.

Do not become preoccupied with proving that a footprint is unchanged, that a gauge is fixed, or that your old tracks remain fresh. Repeated checking is exactly how an ordinary person loses an afternoon—or much more—to an anomaly that does not recognize ordinary duration.

If you find what appear to be your own earlier footprints, notes, equipment, or images, do not assume you have already completed the visit. Do not follow them deeper. Compare them only long enough to decide whether your exit plan should be activated.

If a companion begins insisting that the machines need help, that the door is “about to open,” that they have been there before, or that they must complete one more circuit, treat this as a possible cognitive hazard. Do not argue at length in the noise. Use your prearranged abort signal and leave together.

## The far steel door

Regard the red-lit door as a boundary marker, not an objective.

Do not attempt to open it from this side. It has no handle for a reason. Do not pry at the seams, drill through the stone, cover the red indicator, scrape the label, or place an ear against it. Do not camp near it; wheel-hound traffic crosses its dust, and the mineral spires nearby make retreat hazardous.

“Temporarily closed” may be a statement from a time system that is not currently available to you. You do not need to discover when it reopens.

## If something changes

The Stillworks’ greatest safety feature may be its consistency. Therefore, any real change is an emergency.

Leave immediately if you observe:

- A machine slowing, speeding up, stopping, surging, smoking, leaking, or changing pitch.
- The eleven-second pressure-valve rhythm changing.
- Lamps flickering, dimming, brightening, or going out.
- Wheel-hounds deviating from circuits, stopping, fleeing, gathering, or noticing you as a group.
- New growth, decay, water, dripping, shifting dust, or fresh cracks.
- A gauge needle moving.
- The red indicator changing state.
- A new sound that cannot be attributed to your group.
- Loss of the normal dryness, heat, or mineral smell.

Do not stay to document the event. A few seconds of footage are not worth being present when a system built around permanent equilibrium begins to transition.

## Leaving

Exit by the same route you entered unless your multiversal extraction plan requires otherwise. Do not use the pipe. Do not use the steel door. Do not improvise a “new” exit through a seam in the rock simply because it appears passable.

At the threshold, account for every person and all critical equipment. Check for dust in clothing folds, organisms in gear, static damage, unusual marks, and unexplained objects. Record your exit time outside the basin, then compare notes and clocks only after you are safely away.

The correct attitude for visiting the Stillworks is not conquest, exploration, repair, or rescue. It is respectful observation of a sealed system.

Walk softly, stand in the gaps, touch nothing warm, interrupt no circuit, and leave before curiosity turns into participation.
