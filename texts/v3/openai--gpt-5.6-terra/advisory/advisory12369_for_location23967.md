Prepare for this city as you would for a cold, high-altitude industrial wetland built inside a living piece of unfinished geology. Do not approach it as a ruin, a wilderness, or a conventional metropolis. It is inhabited, maintained, technologically capable, and profoundly unforgiving of visitors who assume that infrastructure is separate from ecology.

The central rule is simple:

**Stay dry inside your clothing, stay attached to approved routes, stay quiet in the ways locals consider quiet, and never interfere with water, mineral foam, or animals.**

## Before entering the basin

### Acclimatize for altitude

The city is above the snowline and explicitly has thin air. If you arrive from a sea-level or dense-atmosphere world, do not immediately begin climbing causeways or hauling luggage.

Spend at least a day, preferably two, in a controlled arrival facility or lower-elevation staging area if one exists. Watch for:

- headache, nausea, confusion, shortness of breath at rest
- poor coordination on wet walkways
- unusual fatigue or irrational confidence
- inability to sleep despite exhaustion

The city’s darkness, constant low-frequency animal chorus, cold, and unfamiliar pressure sensations will make altitude sickness harder to recognize. Carry a compact oxygen supply, but do not rely on it as permission to overexert yourself. A respirator or oxygen mask may also be necessary if the basin’s cloud-derived precipitate irritates lungs or reacts with your home-universe biology.

If you have heart, lung, circulatory, migraine, seizure, or balance issues, obtain medical clearance before visiting. This is not a good destination for casual “push through it” travel.

### Arrange local sponsorship

Do not enter without a resident contact, licensed guide, host institution, or transit authority liaison.

This city’s warnings are not likely to be visible in ways offworlders expect. Public notices may be transmitted through:

- vibrations underfoot
- tactile rails
- color shifts in route lighting
- changing pulses in structural surfaces
- local collar devices filtering or encoding the bell-throat chorus

A visitor who cannot interpret these signals may walk calmly into a flood closure, predator passage, unstable uplift zone, or evacuation route.

Ask your sponsor to provide:

1. a current basin stability map  
2. permitted routes between your lodging, transit points, and essential destinations  
3. a translation of tactile and light-based warnings  
4. an animal-traffic schedule, if such schedules are public  
5. an emergency shelter location within a few minutes’ walk of every planned route  
6. an explanation of what to do if your route begins to vibrate, warm, darken, or seal itself  

Do not assume an apparent shortcut is safe. In this city, a newly raised pavement, a darkened bridge, or a closed drainage channel may be responding to conditions you cannot see.

### Confirm biosecurity requirements

The place has little or no familiar plant life, a strange mineral precipitation cycle, self-repairing machine-films, managed animals, flooded foundations, and enclosed water chemistry. You should expect strict contamination controls.

Before arrival:

- clean and sterilize boots, outerwear, bags, cameras, and field equipment;
- avoid bringing seeds, soil, insects, untreated natural fibers, live cultures, or food waste;
- declare medications, cosmetics, soaps, lubricants, and batteries;
- use only approved detergents and scent products;
- do not bring pets, service animals, or biological companions unless the city has explicitly cleared them.

Even harmless foreign microbes could be a serious issue in a place where decomposition alters underground chemistry and attracts dangerous channel fauna. Conversely, local organisms, mineral aerosols, and machine-film dust may be unsafe to bring home. Plan for quarantine on both ends of the trip.

## Clothing and personal equipment

You need equipment for **cold, wetness, darkness, altitude, noise, slippery surfaces, and tactile navigation**. A fashionable coat and ordinary hiking boots will not be enough.

### Wear a fully layered waterproof system

Use:

- moisture-wicking base layers;
- insulating mid-layers that retain warmth when damp;
- a breathable waterproof outer shell;
- waterproof over-trousers or a full rain shell;
- waterproof gloves with enough dexterity to use rails, latches, and transit controls;
- a warm cap or helmet-compatible hood;
- neck and face protection that can seal against cold damp air.

Avoid cotton. It will become cold, heavy, and dangerous.

Bring at least one complete dry clothing set in a truly watertight bag. Not water-resistant: watertight. In this city, condensation and seepage are not occasional inconveniences. They are the ambient condition.

### Choose footwear for wet vibrating ground

Your boots should have:

- aggressive non-slip soles;
- ankle support;
- waterproof construction;
- insulating midsoles;
- a sole thin enough to perceive route vibrations;
- no exposed absorbent fabric;
- secure lacing or sealed closures.

The last point matters: the streets may communicate through pressure patterns. You need to feel them, but you do not want the cold current beneath hexagonal plates passing directly into your feet.

Carry removable traction devices if locals recommend them. Rime can form quickly, and “ice” in this environment may be mixed with mineral deposits, machine residue, or unfamiliar precipitate. Do not scrape it from public surfaces unless instructed; what looks like frost may be part of a maintained water-routing system.

### Protect hearing without becoming deaf

The bell-throats’ sustained chorus is not merely loud. It is structural: a basin-wide standing chord likely capable of producing fatigue, nausea, anxiety, sleep disruption, and possibly harmful resonance in poorly protected visitors.

Obtain locally calibrated hearing protection, ideally the collar seals residents use. Do not substitute ordinary earplugs unless a local professional says they are safe. You must still be able to perceive:

- emergency vibrations;
- close-range predator warnings;
- transit signals;
- changes in the bell-throat chorus that residents use as environmental information;
- verbal instructions from guides or emergency workers.

If you wear hearing aids, implanted devices, or have tinnitus, test your setup before entering the main city. Carry backup passive protection.

### Bring light, but use it carefully

Carry a hands-free lamp with adjustable output and a red or low-glare mode. Your lamp is for immediate footing and close work, not for illuminating streets or scanning the darkness.

Bright white beams may:

- disrupt local fauna;
- interfere with visual signaling;
- create dangerous glare on wet black stone;
- make it harder for your eyes to read the city’s dim blue, green, and red infrastructure lights;
- draw unwanted attention in channels or tower gaps.

Keep a second emergency light in a sealed pocket. Use locally approved wavelengths around animal routes and waterworks.

### Carry basic survival items

In a small body-worn pack, not a loose shoulder bag, carry:

- identification and local transit authorization;
- waterproof paper map and a tactile or high-contrast route guide;
- compact power bank rated for cold wet conditions;
- insulated water container;
- approved high-calorie food;
- electrolyte packets approved by local health authorities;
- personal medications in waterproof packaging;
- oxygen or inhaler if prescribed;
- thermal emergency wrap;
- compact first-aid kit;
- chemical hand warmers only if permitted;
- whistle or locator beacon only if permitted—sound signals may be ineffective or culturally inappropriate;
- a tether or hand loop for use near approved open-water routes, if local practice supports it.

Keep both hands as free as possible. You may need rails, balance points, or sudden support when a surface shifts.

## Learn the city’s rules before you walk it

### Treat water as infrastructure, habitat, and hazard

Do not splash, throw objects into, sample, dam, wade in, or casually touch standing water. A shallow black basin may connect to a deep channel, pressure system, water-treatment layer, or animal route. Water beneath transparent walkways is not decoration.

Never assume a surface is solid because it looks paved. Water may be moving immediately below a plate, membrane, or stone skin.

If you drop something into water, leave it unless a local authority says otherwise. Retrieving a phone is not worth introducing contamination, being pulled by a current, or attracting something from below.

Water conservation appears culturally serious. Do not waste potable water, overfill containers, run taps unnecessarily, or pour beverages out in public. Ask before bathing, laundering clothing, or using hotel facilities; even ordinary habits may have consequences in a city whose water chemistry is carefully managed.

### Do not touch mineral foam

The pale “snow” is described as a mineral precipitate from the cloud layer. It may be chemically irritating, abrasive, conductive, structurally meaningful, or biologically entangled.

Avoid stepping in drifts unless the route is marked safe. Do not make snowballs, write messages in it, taste it, collect it as a souvenir, or shake it off onto public surfaces. Keep it out of your boots, eyes, food, and equipment seals.

If it contacts skin, rinse according to local guidance rather than assuming ordinary water is the right response.

### Never disturb machine-films or self-healing surfaces

The gray lattices and bright repair dust are part of the city’s survival system. Do not peel, scrape, photograph at intrusive range with sampling equipment, stick items to them, lean ladders against them, or collect repair dust.

What looks like a wall coating may be monitoring structural stress, collecting condensation, sealing a crack, or redistributing load. In a city built on moving wet stone, vandalism can become a safety incident far beyond the immediate damage.

### Respect closed routes immediately

A pathway that warms, vibrates, rises, changes color, darkens its transparent layer, or retracts a barrier is communicating with you.

Your response should be automatic:

1. Stop moving forward.  
2. Do not gather to watch.  
3. Follow marked retreat directions or your guide.  
4. Do not cross a barrier because it “doesn’t look dangerous.”  
5. Do not stand beneath bridges, beside floodgates, or near drainage mouths during a warning.  

The city’s systems are subtle because they act early. A barrier may rise before a flood is visible. A walkway may close because something large is passing below. Trust the infrastructure more than your eyes.

## Wildlife precautions

The city is not abandoned; its animals are co-residents in a negotiated ecosystem. Do not treat them as pests, attractions, or opportunities for dramatic footage.

### Bell-throats

These ceramic-skinned animals nest in warm recesses and produce the city’s perpetual chord.

Do not:

- touch or feed them;
- block their vents, alcoves, or nesting spaces;
- shine bright lights into their faces;
- imitate their calls;
- use ultrasound, speakers, or tone generators near them;
- attempt recordings that require approaching colonies.

Their chorus may be tied to territory, mating, environmental sensing, or structural conditions. A disturbance to a colony could be more consequential than it appears.

If you must pass closely, move steadily, avoid sudden noise, and follow local routes. Do not run: rapid motion and panic will make you less stable and may trigger attention from other animals.

### Cliff-bats and winged scavengers

Expect dense roosts overhead. Wear eye protection and keep your mouth covered in crowded transit vaults. Avoid standing beneath roosts for long periods. Do not throw objects upward, use flash photography, or open food near them.

If the animals begin clicking loudly, shifting as a group, or abandoning an area, treat it as a possible environmental warning. Leave calmly via a known route.

### Skimmers and channel animals

The broad-backed skimmers may look safe because they move gently and do not splash. They are still animals moving through their own traffic lanes. Never step onto one, touch its legs, place objects in its path, or assume its calmness means domestication.

The larger channel fauna should be regarded like marine megafauna combined with subterranean machinery: fascinating at a distance, potentially lethal at close range, and capable of affecting structures.

If you see vertebrae, a mouth-like structure, unusual blue light, sudden water recession, or a bridge darkening itself:

- move away from water edges;
- keep off transparent panels unless directed otherwise;
- do not knock on glass;
- do not use flash;
- follow the nearest resident or emergency indicator.

## Lodging and sleep

Sleep may be one of the hardest parts of the visit. The chorus is constant, surfaces vibrate, water moves through walls, and the sky provides no natural day-night cue.

Choose lodging that has:

- inward-facing rooms;
- acoustic damping;
- independent ventilation;
- reliable dry storage;
- raised sleeping platforms;
- emergency lighting that can be dimmed;
- clear flood and evacuation procedures;
- local-grade door and window seals.

Bring:

- a sleep mask;
- locally approved noise management equipment;
- a familiar calming routine that does not require open flame, fragrances, or loud audio;
- extra dry socks and base layers near the bed.

Do not sleep in lower-city accommodation unless you have a compelling reason and an experienced host. The lower city is described as drowned, partially inaccessible, and connected to older service systems. Even if a room is legal and maintained, it is not the place for a first-time visitor to test their tolerance for confined wet darkness.

Establish an artificial schedule. Eat, rest, and hydrate by a clock, not by the sky or your sense of fatigue. Permanent darkness can distort judgment rapidly.

## Food, water, and etiquette

Expect food systems unlike those at home: cultivated protein, foam colonies, animal-derived materials, mineral supplements, and carefully managed water use.

Before visiting, clarify dietary restrictions in functional terms rather than familiar labels. “Vegetarian,” for example, may not map cleanly to a culture with no ground-grown food and extensive use of cultured or molted materials.

Ask:

- What is the food’s source?
- Is it safe for offworld human digestion?
- Is it sterile or fermented?
- Does it contain local minerals or proteins likely to provoke allergy?
- Is it compatible with your microbiome and medications?
- How is waste handled?

Use sealed food containers. Never discard food in public, into drains, or near warm mechanical recesses. In this city, organic waste may alter ecosystems and attract dangerous things upward.

Accept that warmth may be shared communally. If someone offers a heated cup, warming pad, dry cover, or sheltered seat, it may be a practical courtesy rather than intimacy. Return such resources promptly and do not monopolize heated public spaces.

## Social conduct

Residents likely have a culture shaped by maintenance, restraint, and long-term reciprocity. You will make a better impression if you behave as though every resource is shared and every action has downstream effects—because here, it probably does.

Good habits:

- keep your gear compact;
- stand clear of drains, access panels, vents, and transit seals;
- follow queues precisely;
- speak at a moderate volume, but do not assume silence is possible or expected;
- ask before recording people, animals, repair systems, or warning signals;
- thank maintenance workers and transit staff;
- do not complain casually about dampness, darkness, food, or noise;
- do not describe the city as “dead,” “barren,” “post-apocalyptic,” or “unnatural.”

Most importantly, do not praise the city for having “conquered” its environment. Its people explicitly understand their survival as keeping terms with the basin. Frame questions around cooperation, maintenance, seasonal—or geological—change, and local knowledge.

## Emergency response

Memorize the local equivalents of these conditions:

- incoming floodwater;
- predator movement;
- route instability;
- air-quality or cloud-chemistry alert;
- evacuation to higher levels;
- loss of structural power;
- shelter-in-place instruction.

Because auditory alarms may be unusable, ensure you have at least two non-auditory notification methods: collar seal, wrist device, tactile map, visual code card, or guide link.

If separated from your group:

1. Do not improvise a new route.  
2. Move to the nearest occupied, lit, officially marked interior space.  
3. Avoid lower routes, open water, bridge undersides, and maintenance corridors.  
4. Show your visitor credentials.  
5. Conserve warmth and battery power.  
6. Follow infrastructure cues even if you do not understand them.  

If you fall into water, prioritize getting clear of currents and structural edges, not recovering belongings. Do not assume you can swim safely in cold, chemically unfamiliar, animal-inhabited water. Shout only if local protocol recommends it; a tactile beacon, light code, or emergency collar signal may be more effective than voice.

If your clothing becomes saturated, seek warmth and dry layers immediately. Hypothermia may develop faster than expected in thin, wet, cold air—even if you are indoors.

## What not to bring home

Do not remove:

- mineral foam;
- rime samples;
- water;
- machine-film fragments;
- repair dust;
- animal molts, feathers, droppings, or nesting material;
- stones from uplift zones;
- anything retrieved from channels or flooded structures.

Even if souvenir collection is legal, it may be biologically irresponsible or structurally disrespectful. Take photographs where permitted, notes, maps, recordings approved by local authorities, and memories. Leave the basin’s materials in the basin.

## The right mindset

A first visit should be short, guided, and conservative. Plan fewer destinations per day than you normally would. The city will slow you: wet surfaces, low oxygen, uncertain visibility, sensory fatigue, transit protocols, and the simple psychological weight of a place where nothing is dry and nothing is fully still.

Do not try to master it in a weekend.

Let the city teach you its pace. Walk where it permits walking. Wait when it tells you to wait. Learn which vibrations mean a lift, which mean water, which mean danger, and which are only the deep continuous chord of the bell-throats passing through your bones.

In a place like this, competence is not bravado. Competence is noticing that the ground is communicating—and listening before it has to speak louder.
