## Before You Go: Treat This as an Extreme-Environment Visit

This is not a place where an ordinary human can arrive casually, even if the inhabitants themselves are human or human-adjacent. The hollow city is a sealed heat-and-vapor ecology built around bodies unlike yours, customs unlike yours, and an infrastructure that may be damaged by ordinary acts of comfort: drinking openly, sweating freely, speaking loudly, lighting a flame, or spilling water.

Your priorities are:

1. **Do not die of heat stress or dehydration.**
2. **Do not introduce liquid water, foreign organisms, or uncontrolled moisture into the city.**
3. **Do not interfere with airflow, gardens, wind organs, or drying galleries.**
4. **Do not assume silence means safety, or noise means alarm.**
5. **Arrive with a local guide and leave before your body becomes a problem for the city.**

A short, carefully managed visit is plausible. An unassisted overnight stay is not.

## Essential Equipment

### Wear a sealed but breathable desert suit

You need clothing that protects you from radiant heat, mineral dust, and moisture loss without becoming a sauna.

Bring:

- A **light-colored outer robe or heat-reflective oversuit**, loose enough to create an insulating air layer.
- A **moisture-conserving underlayer** designed for desert survival: one that captures some perspiration vapor rather than allowing it to evaporate immediately.
- **Full gloves**, preferably with thin, grippy palms. The city is full of hot clay, abrasive chalk, ladders, ropes, copper edges, and plant surfaces that may irritate skin.
- **Closed boots with quiet, non-marking soles**. Avoid heavy tread that might crush root mats or leave imported rubber particles.
- A **broad reflective hood** or sun veil for the outer approach. The exposed ridge is likely more dangerous than the interior in daylight.
- **Eye protection**: dust goggles for the exterior, and tinted but high-contrast lenses for the interior’s severe overhead sunlight. The sunshaft may be painful to look toward.

Avoid synthetic garments that shed fibers. The city’s gardens and ventilation systems are likely extremely sensitive to dust and foreign debris. Natural, tightly woven fibers that have been thoroughly cleaned are best—provided they will not mold or release lint.

### Bring your own water—but make it invisible and impossible to spill

You will need water. The people of the hollow may not.

Carry water in:

- **Double-sealed, impact-resistant bladders or flasks**
- An **insulated pack** beneath your outer clothing
- A **bite-valve or closed drinking tube**, never an open bottle
- A personal **urine containment system**, if staying more than a few hours

Do not drink where local people can see unless your guide has explicitly told you how this is handled. More importantly, do not spill. A dropped flask, leaking hydration bladder, or uncapped bottle could create a dangerous local condensation event, damage a root terrace, attract or kill organisms, or cause profound distress.

Do not assume they will regard water as merely unusual. They may regard free liquid water as dangerous, ritually contaminating, biologically disruptive, or impossible.

A good visitor’s kit includes absorbent emergency pads and sealable waste pouches. If liquid spills, do not try to “wash it away.” Alert your guide immediately, cover the spill only if instructed, and let locals decide how it must be handled.

### Carry a cooling system that does not leak

You will be in a hot enclosed chamber beneath a focused shaft of sun. The city survives this by architecture, airflow, and physiology; you do not.

Prefer:

- A **phase-change cooling vest** with sealed solid-state packs
- A low-power **circulating-air cooling garment**
- Shade cloth and reflective insulation
- A small internal temperature monitor, if available

Avoid evaporative cooling. A wet scarf, misting bottle, soaked clothing, or cooling towel is inappropriate here and may be hazardous. The city’s entire ecosystem is organized around preventing moisture from becoming free water.

### Bring medical supplies for heat and respiratory problems

Your kit should include:

- Oral rehydration salts, to be used privately
- Electrolyte tablets
- Anti-nausea medication
- Basic first aid supplies
- Dust-mask filters, ideally under a local-style mouth veil
- Personal inhaler medication, if relevant
- A compact pulse oximeter and thermometer
- Ear protection with selective attenuation or active noise control
- Communication cards or a text-based device, but do not rely on electronics in heat and dust

The wind-organ noise may be loud enough to cause pain, disorientation, temporary hearing loss, or panic. Ordinary foam earplugs may protect your ears but could prevent you from hearing critical tonal warnings. Better are filtered earplugs or active hearing protection that reduces damaging volume while preserving pitch patterns.

## Learn the First Rule: Your Breath Is Not Private

In this city, breath is material. It is moisture, heat, carbon dioxide, scent, and potentially nourishment. The residents regulate speech and cover their mouths during the brightest part of the day not from shyness but from ecological necessity.

You should bring and wear a **local-style mouth veil**, ideally supplied by your hosts.

It will help you:

- Reduce water loss
- Avoid adding unregulated moisture and microbes to the environment
- Communicate your respect for local practice
- Filter dust and plant particulates
- Conceal the fact that your breathing rate may be high under heat stress

Practice slow breathing before arrival. Hyperventilation wastes water rapidly and may be conspicuous. Do not gasp, shout, sing, laugh explosively, or give long verbal explanations in public spaces.

Expect communication to be largely nonverbal. Learn, at minimum:

- Stop
- Follow
- Danger
- Quiet / conserve breath
- Do not touch
- Heat rising
- Air bad / stale
- Need water
- Need to leave
- Thank you
- Apology
- Bellows-Walker warning, if there is a standard sign

Bring a small card with these signs illustrated, but do not assume a written language is shared.

## Timing Your Visit

### Do not arrive during the annual opening

The yearly opening of the upper fracture is not a festival suitable for sightseeing. It is a controlled ecological discharge: extreme light, violent convection, airborne spores, structural vibration, and likely a surge in heat and dust.

During this event:

- Wind organs become dangerously loud.
- Vines and lightweight objects may lift or stream upward.
- Seed-pod aerosols may fill the air.
- The entrance area may become a furnace-like chimney.
- The city may be inaccessible, closed to outsiders, or simply too occupied with survival to host you.

Unless your purpose is urgent and you have specialized heat, respiratory, and climbing equipment, plan your visit well away from the annual opening.

### Enter near the cooler part of the cycle

The best time is likely just after dawn or during the chamber’s comparatively cool phase, before the sunshaft reaches full intensity. This is also when throat-palm crystals are harvested, so paths may be busy and ladders in use. Ask your guide whether this is an acceptable visiting interval.

Avoid the hottest hours entirely if possible. The city’s own speech restrictions become strict then, and your body will lose moisture quickly even while standing still.

### Keep the visit short

For a first visit, plan for:

- **One to three hours inside**
- A shaded rest point near the entrance or a designated visitor niche
- No strenuous climbing
- No solo wandering
- A clearly defined extraction time

If you stay longer, you will need a host-approved place to sleep, a waste-management protocol, water privacy, a medical contingency plan, and agreement about how your ongoing respiration will be accommodated.

## The Entrance and Exterior Approach

The low ridge outside is likely lethally dry in a mundane way: harsh sun, reflective stone, dust, no shade, no landmarks, and a narrow entrance that can be difficult to relocate.

Prepare by:

- Traveling with a guide or beacon system
- Marking your return route without leaving litter
- Wearing exterior eye and face protection
- Covering exposed skin completely
- Avoiding metal tools or exposed equipment that can heat rapidly
- Carrying enough water for the approach, wait time, and emergency return—not merely the planned visit

At the fracture, you will need to stoop. Remove or secure any tall pack, antenna, loose water tube, or dangling gear before entering. Do not scrape the walls unnecessarily: what appears to be a simple mineral crack may function as a thermal seal, airflow regulator, or living surface.

The heat leaking outward is your first warning. Once you feel it, pause, cover your mouth, and let your eyes adjust before proceeding.

## Inside the Hollow: Move as Though the Whole City Is a Machine

The city is not merely built in a cave. It is a living thermal engine.

Every pipe, fan, terrace, roughened wall, perforated roof, hanging belt of vegetation, ladder, bridge, and empty room may be part of its moisture economy or airflow system.

### Never block airflow

Do not:

- Lean bags against vents or pipes
- Hang clothes to dry
- Stand in narrow alleys longer than necessary
- Camp beneath a wind organ
- Cover a perforated wall
- Close a curtain, screen, or shutter without permission
- Use tape, adhesive, or sealant on local surfaces
- Create a crowd in a passageway

If a local motions you aside from an apparently empty place, move immediately. They may be responding to a change in airflow, temperature, organ tone, or insect behavior that you cannot perceive.

### Do not touch plants without an invitation

The plants are not decorative. They are air processors, food stores, moisture regulators, structural material, insect control, and perhaps the city’s primary sacred inheritance.

In particular, do not touch:

- **Translucent gas pods**: they may release buoyant gases, irritants, spores, or carefully managed vapors when damaged.
- **Throat-palms**: their nightly crystals are likely a vital food and water-equivalent resource.
- **Root mats and powdery soil**: compacting them may harm filtration systems or root-eels.
- **Silver-furred vines**: their surface dust may be biologically active, moisture-collecting, or irritating.
- Any plant marked with cord, mica, shell, or colored fiber.

Do not eat local food without careful medical and diplomatic preparation. The plants may be perfectly edible to residents and poorly compatible with human digestion. They may contain high mineral loads, volatile compounds, or dry crystals that require a local preparation method.

### Watch your footing

The city is vertically organized and much of it is likely fragile by ordinary standards.

Expect:

- Narrow balconies
- Hollow stalk ladders
- Woven bridges
- Uneven clay stairs
- Dust-covered ledges
- Low barriers around deep or hot fissures
- Sudden changes in temperature from shade to sun

Do not step onto a ladder merely because it appears sound. Their ladders sing under weight; residents may judge safety by pitch. Wait for approval. Your mass, footwear, and movement pattern may produce unfamiliar tones and alarm someone.

Move slowly and predictably. Avoid jumping, running, or stomping. Vibrations may be interpreted as structural signals, may disturb dust moths, or may interfere with acoustic monitoring.

## The Noise Will Be More Than Noise

The wind organs are probably the greatest immediate psychological challenge.

You may experience:

- Difficulty speaking or hearing
- Vibration in your ribs and teeth
- Vertigo
- A sense that the sound is physically pushing you
- Panic triggered by low-frequency tones
- Loss of orientation in echoing spaces
- Misreading normal organ sounds as mechanical failure or animal calls

Before entering, establish visual signals with your guide for:

- “I am well.”
- “I need to leave.”
- “I cannot breathe comfortably.”
- “I am dizzy.”
- “I need water.”
- “I have spilled something.”
- “I do not understand.”
- “There is an emergency.”

Never assume a rising howl is an alarm—or that it is not. The residents know which towers indicate danger. You do not. If they lower their eyes, become still, change direction abruptly, cover their faces, or gather up children and tools, imitate them first and ask questions later.

Do not play an instrument, whistle, test an echo, shout into pipes, or tap rhythmically on structures. In a city where sound performs practical work, “making noise for fun” can be indistinguishable from sabotage.

## Social Etiquette

### Do not pity the emptiness

The unused plazas, empty rooms, maintained bridges, and swept neighborhoods are not necessarily evidence of failure. They may be ancestral property, civic memory, a promise to absent kin, contingency space for future population, or an active ecological design.

Do not ask immediately:

- “Where did everyone go?”
- “Why don’t you move into the empty houses?”
- “Was there a disaster?”
- “Are you the last people?”
- “Why maintain all this?”

These may be intimate, painful, political, or simply nonsensical questions from their perspective.

A better approach is to ask your guide what a particular place is *for*, not why it is unused.

### Treat maintenance as ceremony, whether or not it is called that

If you see people sweeping an empty threshold, repairing an unoccupied bridge, rehanging chimes nobody can hear, or tending a vacant terrace, do not interrupt. Do not praise it as quaint. Their city persists through maintenance so continuous that it may not distinguish utility from reverence.

Offer assistance only when invited. Your help could be harmful if you do not understand the moisture and airflow functions of an object.

### Be careful around the dead

The drying galleries are likely among the most restricted and emotionally significant places in the city. The dead are being returned gradually to the gardens rather than discarded or buried.

Do not photograph, enter, point, ask to inspect, or make comparisons to funerary customs from your home world. Do not bring flowers, water, perfume, candles, or incense. All of these could be ecologically inappropriate.

If you are granted access, follow every instruction exactly. Cover your mouth, remain still, and do not touch fiber wrappings or drying racks.

### Respect root-eels absolutely

If you see one, do not pick it up. Do not attempt to identify it with a scanner that emits heat, light, ultrasound, or chemical markers unless authorized. Do not step near exposed soil channels.

A root-eel is not “just a worm.” It is legally protected and likely ecologically essential. Injuring one may be treated as serious harm.

### The Bellows-Walker is not your mystery to solve

You may hear the wet, resonant boom attributed to the Bellows-Walker. Your instinct may be to look, record, investigate, or ask excited questions.

Do none of those things in the moment.

If workers lower their eyes and wait, lower your eyes and wait. Do not shine a light behind the organs. Do not approach deep fissures. Do not put a camera, probe, hand, or drone into dark ventilation folds. Whatever the creature is—animal, natural phenomenon, cultural interpretation, or all three—it occupies a dangerous part of the city’s air system.

Ask about it later, privately, and accept that you may not receive an answer.

## Hygiene and Biosecurity

You are a walking source of water, microbes, skin cells, lint, oils, and foreign spores. In an enclosed city whose agriculture depends on delicate air chemistry, this matters.

Before entry:

- Shower using unscented products, then dry thoroughly.
- Do not wear perfume, deodorant spray, hair products, insect repellent, or scented sunscreen into the hollow.
- Clean footwear and equipment to remove foreign soil, seeds, and insects.
- Quarantine clothing if you have recently visited another unusual ecosystem.
- Trim nails and ensure no loose dirt is trapped beneath them.
- Cover cuts with secure dressings.

Inside:

- Do not spit.
- Do not urinate except in a sealed personal system.
- Do not cough openly; use your veil and turn away from plants or airflow intakes.
- Do not dispose of food wrappers, tissues, or hair.
- Avoid touching walls with bare hands.
- Do not release drones, insects, seeds, or “helpful” microbes.
- Do not offer local people packaged snacks unless they specifically request them. Crumbs and moisture are contaminants.

If you become ill—vomiting, diarrhea, fever, coughing, nosebleed—leave immediately if physically able. Your presence may become a biological burden.

## Food and Water Diplomacy

You should not assume local people consume nothing that resembles water. Their throat-palm crystals may fulfill some of the same physiological roles, but their culture has probably developed around the distinction between stored atmospheric moisture and free liquid water.

Do not make jokes about “finally giving them a drink.” Do not offer water as charity, proof of technology, religious symbolism, or hospitality.

If you wish to offer gifts, choose items that are:

- Dry
- Inert
- Clean
- Non-shedding
- Nonvolatile
- Small
- Useful without disrupting local systems

Potentially acceptable gifts, after local consultation:

- Fine copper needles
- High-quality stone blades
- Polished mica mirrors
- Durable ceramic tools
- Diagrammatic books or memory devices
- Clean cordage made from approved fibers
- Precision hand tools with no lubricant leakage

Potentially disastrous gifts:

- Seeds
- Soil
- Living plants
- Animals
- Water containers
- Candles
- Fuel
- Perfume
- Batteries that can leak
- Electronic devices that produce heat or radio noise
- Anything moist, fermented, oily, or biologically active

## Do Not Expect Conventional Shelter

The shaded sleeping shelves may look appealing, but do not occupy one without permission. They are likely temperature-calibrated spaces, family property, moisture-management structures, or protected sleeping sites.

If you must rest, ask for a designated visitor area. It should ideally be:

- Away from direct sunlight
- Away from major wind-organ outlets
- Near an exit route
- On a hard, stable surface rather than a root terrace
- Supplied with approved shade and airflow
- Close enough to your guide that you can signal distress

Your own body adds heat, vapor, carbon dioxide, scent, and microorganisms. Even sleeping can be a significant ecological event here.

## Emergency Procedures

### If you feel heat illness

Symptoms include headache, confusion, nausea, rapid heartbeat, stumbling, chills despite heat, cessation of sweating, or irrational calm.

Immediately use the prearranged hand sign for departure. Do not try to be polite or “push through.” Sit only where your guide directs. Drink from your sealed system discreetly. Activate your cooling vest. Leave the chamber as soon as it is safe.

Do not pour water on your skin.

### If your water container leaks

1. Alert your guide at once.
2. Stop moving; do not spread droplets.
3. Keep others away from the area if you can signal this.
4. Use sealed absorbent pads only if your hosts instruct you to do so.
5. Do not wipe the spill into dust, soil, roots, cracks, or cloth.
6. Be prepared to surrender contaminated equipment for disposal or quarantine.

### If you become separated

Do not wander toward the sunshaft, deeper fissures, organ fields, gardens, or high terraces.

Stay where you are unless the immediate location is unsafe. Make yourself visible using a reflective signal rather than shouting. Use your emergency light only in a low, shaded direction and only if it will not be confused with local mica signaling. Cover your mouth and conserve breath.

The city is large-feeling, acoustically confusing, and deliberately full of empty spaces. You could become lost within sight of inhabited quarters.

### If the organ tones change suddenly

Follow locals. If no local is present, move away from large organ towers and narrow ventilation lanes toward the nearest known exit or designated shelter. Do not enter black fissures or attempt to inspect the source.

A change in sound may mean a shift in heat, outside air, ventilation, insects, structural stress, or something associated with the Bellows-Walker. You will not be able to diagnose it by intuition.

## Psychological Preparation

The hollow may make you feel several contradictory things at once:

- Claustrophobia from entering through a crack
- Agoraphobia once the chamber opens beyond expectation
- Awe at the severe central light
- Loneliness in the maintained empty districts
- Anxiety from the relentless mechanical voices
- Guilt at consuming water in a place that treats moisture as ancestry
- A strange sense that the city is breathing around you

These reactions are normal. Do not let them make you careless.

Practice beforehand:

- Remaining calm in sustained industrial noise
- Communicating without speech
- Moving slowly under heat stress
- Drinking through a closed tube
- Wearing a face veil for extended periods
- Tolerating confined entries and elevated walkways
- Recognizing your own early dehydration symptoms

If you have severe tinnitus, panic disorder triggered by inescapable noise, serious respiratory conditions, unstable balance, or difficulty with heat regulation, this is not a suitable destination without substantial support.

## What to Remember Most

The people of the hollow have not “failed” to find water. They have built a civilization around another premise: that moisture must remain in motion, in breath, in root, in crystal, in leaf, in the slow return of bodies to gardens.

You arrive as a creature that leaks.

Be sealed, be quiet, be clean, be guided, and be brief. Do not mistake simplicity of tools for simplicity of knowledge. Their clay pipes, woven veils, stalk ladders, rough walls, and empty houses are parts of a system that has kept life green under stone where the world above is too dry to forgive mistakes.
