Prepare for this settlement as you would for a wet, vertical, close-knit farming village where infrastructure is alive, communal, and constantly under repair. Your main risks are not exotic: slipping, becoming chilled, contaminating water, damaging a load-bearing plant or channel, getting in the way during weather, and offending people whose homes and livelihoods occupy every inch of ground.

## Before you arrive

### Learn enough language to be useful and polite
At minimum, be able to say:

- Greetings and your name.
- “I am a traveler.”
- “Where may I step?”
- “May I cross here?”
- “Can I help?”
- “Is this drinking water?”
- “I do not understand.”
- “Thank you.”
- “I am sorry.”
- “Where should waste go?”
- “Is anyone hurt?”

In a place where people shout between levels and act quickly during rain or a broken channel, being unable to understand a warning is a real safety problem.

Learn the local terms for the upper ponds, drinking channels, washing places, animal paths, communal halls, outer fire platforms, and the wet-season labor call. Those categories matter more than street names.

### Arrive with a credible purpose
A tiny crowded settlement may be hospitable, but it cannot casually absorb a stranger. Be ready to explain:

- Where you came from, in terms locals can understand.
- Why you are there.
- How long you expect to remain.
- What work, trade goods, knowledge, or labor you can offer.
- Whether you carry animals, valuables, weapons, or anything that might introduce pests or disease.

Do not lead with multiverse travel unless the local culture has a framework for it and disclosure is safe. “A traveler from a distant land” is usually enough. Fantastic claims can make you seem dangerous, mad, divinely significant, or like a fraud, all of which are inconvenient in a village built on narrow wet ledges.

### Be physically ready
You do not need to be a mountaineer, but you should be capable of:

- Walking on uneven, wet stone.
- Climbing shallow stairs and ladders while carrying your own belongings.
- Balancing on narrow planks and roots.
- Squatting, kneeling, and stepping over channels repeatedly.
- Sleeping amid animal noise, dripping water, smoke, and conversation.
- Working in rain without becoming useless.

If you have poor balance, bad knees, reduced mobility, or a severe cold sensitivity, bring appropriate accommodations—but do not assume the settlement has space to house bulky equipment. A compact walking stick, foldable rain cape, and small personal stool may be much more practical than a large wheeled device. Wheels will be largely useless here.

## What to wear

### Footwear is your most important decision
Bring sturdy, quiet footwear with excellent wet traction. The ideal shoe or boot should have:

- Flexible soles that grip wet slate, wood, mossy stone, and roots.
- Ankle support, but not so much stiffness that you cannot feel your footing.
- A low profile: avoid large heels, rigid projecting hardware, or soles that trap mud.
- Quick drying ability.
- No exposed metal that will rust or tear woven flooring.

Do not rely on smooth modern hiking soles, fashionable leather shoes, or hard plastic sandals. Bare feet may be common locally, but do not imitate them immediately. Residents know the safe stones; you do not.

Carry spare dry socks in a waterproof inner bag. Change them before sleeping. Persistent damp feet become blisters, fungal infections, and misery very quickly.

### Dress in light, breathable layers
The weather is cool, humid, and changeable rather than bitterly cold. Wear:

- A breathable base layer.
- A warm mid-layer such as wool or modern synthetic fleece.
- A durable rain shell or poncho.
- Tough trousers that tolerate mud and kneeling.
- A brimmed cap or hood.
- Thin work gloves with grip.

Avoid cotton as your main insulating layer; it stays wet. Avoid long loose hems, dangling scarves, and flowing garments that can trail in channels, brush animal feed, catch on vines, or dip into waste runoff.

Bring one clean, modest outfit for visiting communal spaces, sharing food, meeting elders, trading, or attending any ritual occasion. In a damp village, clean clothing represents effort and respect.

### Protect your belongings from water
Everything important should be packed in nested waterproof layers. Even if you own an excellent waterproof bag, use internal dry bags, waxed cloth wraps, or sealed containers for:

- Identification and travel records.
- Medicine.
- Fire-starting tools.
- Electronics or dimensional equipment.
- Spare clothing.
- Food.
- Maps and notebooks.

Assume that water will enter your outer bag somehow: rain, a slipping foot, a duck pond, a roof drip, or the sudden need to set your pack down on wet ground.

Keep your load small. A bulky pack is a hazard on narrow stairs and crowded platforms. If you cannot carry it while keeping one hand free, it is too large.

## Health and sanitation

### Treat water sources with great respect
The settlement’s water system is its bloodstream. The upper ponds and clear channels are likely reserved for drinking; lower channels serve washing, animals, gardens, and waste flow. Never assume water is safe simply because it runs clear.

For your own health:

- Drink only from water explicitly identified as potable.
- Use a filter and purification method discreetly if necessary.
- Do not dip your hands, cup, bottle, dirty cloth, or feet into drinking ponds or channels.
- Never wash dishes, clothing, wounds, or yourself upstream of anyone.
- Do not pour leftover tea, chemicals, soap, medicine, or food scraps into any channel.

If your body requires unfamiliar hygiene products, bring biodegradable, unscented versions and ask where they may be used. “Biodegradable” does not mean harmless in a tightly managed watershed.

### Bring a compact medical kit
Useful supplies include:

- Blister care: moleskin, tape, antiseptic wipes, bandages.
- Antifungal foot treatment.
- Water-resistant wound dressings.
- Pain relief medication.
- Antihistamines.
- Anti-diarrheal medicine and oral rehydration salts.
- Insect-bite treatment.
- A small splint or elastic wrap.
- Personal prescriptions, well protected from humidity.
- A high-quality mask if you are sensitive to smoke, animal dander, mold, or crowded indoor air.

Keep any medicine private and secured. Medicines may be valuable, misunderstood, or dangerous if shared casually. Do not offer treatment beyond your competence; if you do possess real medical skills, first ask who locally tends injuries and work with them rather than replacing them.

### Manage disease transmission
This is a densely inhabited settlement with shared water, animals, smoke-filled rooms, and limited separation between households. You could bring illness that is harmless to you but devastating to them, and vice versa.

Before arrival:

- Be vaccinated where possible for ordinary respiratory and waterborne risks relevant to your origin.
- Avoid arriving while ill.
- Carry masks, hand sanitizer, and soap.
- Quarantine yourself through whatever multiversal means are available if you have recently visited biologically unusual worlds.
- Do not bring soil, seeds, insects, unprocessed animal products, or plant cuttings from elsewhere.

Upon arrival, be open about recent illness and accept local caution. If people ask you to sleep apart, boil your water, avoid kitchens, or remain outside the densest quarter initially, regard this as sensible rather than insulting.

## Gifts, trade, and useful offerings

Bring small, durable, genuinely useful items rather than flashy treasures. Good options include:

- Fine sewing needles, especially bronze-compatible or stainless ones.
- Strong thread, waxed cord, fishing line, or repair twine.
- Small sharpening stones.
- Compact hand tools with simple construction.
- Salt, if it is scarce and you can bring it without destabilizing local trade.
- Waterproof writing materials.
- Plain metal fasteners, though only if locals use them.
- Durable fishhooks.
- High-quality cloth patches.
- Simple lenses, if their practical use can be explained.
- Information: weather observation methods, safe food preservation, first aid, drainage techniques, and tool maintenance.

Avoid introducing goods that will create dependency or disrupt local systems:

- Powerful weapons.
- Large quantities of precious metals.
- Highly addictive substances.
- Seeds or livestock from other worlds.
- Plastics or materials that will become permanent waste.
- Devices that require unavailable fuel, batteries, parts, or technical infrastructure.
- “Miracle” water treatments that cause people to neglect their own carefully maintained channels.

A gift should be offered first to your host household or through a recognized local intermediary, not scattered among children or used to buy instant popularity.

The settlement values labor. The most welcome contribution may be several hours of competent work: carrying stones, clearing a channel, gathering firewood under direction, repairing a mat, watching children near a pond, hauling feed, or helping move animals before a storm.

Do not volunteer for retaining-wall repair, tree training, pond maintenance, roof work, or animal birthing without supervision. Those tasks look simple only to people who know the place.

## Arrival etiquette

### Stop at the upper gate or obvious edge
Do not wander directly downhill into the settlement. Dogs will notice first; people soon after. Stay visible, keep your hands clear, and wait to be addressed if possible.

If you have a weapon, leave it packed and disclose it. Better yet, do not bring one into the settlement. A walking staff, knife, or work tool may be normal, but unexpected armed strangers are not.

Ask:

- Who should receive travelers?
- Where may you stand without blocking passage?
- Where should you put your pack?
- Whether your footwear is acceptable indoors.
- Which water is safe to drink.
- Where you may sleep.
- What areas are private or structurally unsafe.

### Do not stand in a channel, doorway, stair, bridge, or animal route
In a settlement this compact, every apparent empty patch has a function. You may be blocking water, a goat’s regular path, a person carrying a load, access to a smokehouse, or the only route around a puddle.

Watch where residents place their feet. If people consistently step on certain stones, planks, roots, or ledges, do the same. Do not “improve” a route by moving stones; the stones may direct runoff, stabilize a bank, or mark a safe footing point.

### Ask before entering living structures
A bent-trunk wall, woven hedge, or root-laced floor is not casual vegetation. It may be part of a household’s roof, drainage system, privacy boundary, or food source.

Do not:

- Lean heavily on trained trees.
- Cut, carve, tie objects to, or hang gear from living walls.
- Pull vines aside without asking.
- Pick leaves, berries, herbs, mushrooms, or flowers.
- Feed animals.
- Toss food scraps outdoors.
- Step across garden borders because they look informal.

The local distinction between “wild” and “built” will be subtle. Assume it is all somebody’s responsibility.

## Daily conduct

### Keep yourself small, adaptable, and observant
You will be more welcome if you learn the rhythm of work before offering opinions. For the first few days:

- Follow your host’s instructions closely.
- Notice the direction water flows.
- Learn which spaces are for animals, food, washing, tools, sleep, and storage.
- Memorize a safe route between your sleeping place, latrine, drinking water, and nearest communal building.
- Learn the evening bells or calls, especially the second bell when goats are tethered.
- Keep your gear packed and off the floor where it will neither get wet nor block traffic.

Never leave belongings on bridges, steps, platforms, or near channel edges. A misplaced bag can become a trip hazard, a flood obstruction, or a soaked burden someone else must move.

### Offer labor, but accept instruction
Useful low-risk jobs for a newcomer include:

- Carrying split wood.
- Sorting dry kindling.
- Shelling beans or cleaning vegetables.
- Mending simple baskets or cordage if you know how.
- Watching a tethered animal while its owner works nearby.
- Sweeping leaves from paths—though ask first where debris should go.
- Carrying messages between levels.
- Helping cover goods before rain.
- Assisting with market setup and breakdown.

During a sudden storm, overflow, or animal escape, do not freeze and do not improvise heroically. Ask, “Where do you need me?” If nobody can answer, get yourself and your equipment out of the way, then follow the nearest competent person’s lead.

### Understand that privacy will be limited
Homes may be warm, crowded, smoky, and shared with animals, stored food, tools, and family members. Sound travels. People may know where you are and what you are doing simply because there is nowhere else for sound to go.

Do not treat this as gossip or surveillance. It is the ordinary consequence of dense communal life. Be discreet in conversation, dress and wash modestly, and do not expect silence.

Earplugs can help you sleep, but use only one or use low-attenuation plugs if you need to hear alarms, calls, crying children, dogs, or flood warnings.

## Weather and terrain safety

### Rain is a normal condition, not an interruption
Keep rain gear accessible at all times. A storm can turn the settlement’s harmless watercourses into hazards within minutes.

When heavy rain begins:

1. Secure your pack and any loose possessions.
2. Move away from low channels, sagging bridges, unstable terrace edges, and the downhill side of retaining walls.
3. Do not shelter beneath visibly overloaded branches or thatch that is already sagging.
4. Keep clear of people working on water control unless asked to assist.
5. Watch for changing water color: clear water becoming brown often means upstream soil, branches, or a small slide.
6. Avoid crossing fast-moving water, even if it was a shallow trickle earlier.
7. Follow locals if they begin moving animals, supplies, or children uphill.

If you hear urgent calls, bells at an unusual time, or a repeated shouted warning, assume it concerns water until proven otherwise.

### Watch for slips, slides, and falling debris
The obvious danger is a long fall over the lower edge, but more common dangers will be mundane: a mossy stone, a flexing plank, loose slate, or a branch knocked down by wind.

Use the “three points of contact” habit on steep routes: two feet and one hand, or two hands and one foot. Do not rush because a local child runs past you barefoot. They have decades of learned terrain sense; you do not.

At night, carry a covered lamp, headlamp, or other hands-free light with a warm, low setting. Do not shine bright light into homes, animal pens, or bird roosts. Save your brightest light for genuine emergencies.

### Treat fog as disorientation, not scenery
Fog can erase the edge of a terrace, hide a familiar turning, and deaden sound. When it thickens:

- Do not leave the settled paths alone.
- Do not take shortcuts.
- Do not follow animal trails.
- Keep within call range of someone familiar with the area.
- Avoid the outer platforms unless you have a reason to be there.

If you are lost, stop. Calling out is better than wandering. The village is loud, but people are accustomed to locating one another by voice.

## Animals

The animals are residents with routines, owners, and opinions.

- Do not pet dogs until invited. They are likely working guards.
- Do not approach goats from above on a narrow terrace; they may bolt into you.
- Give pigs plenty of room, especially sows with young.
- Do not corner chickens, ducks, cats, or livestock in confined spaces.
- Keep food sealed: ravens, dogs, pigs, chickens, and rodents will all investigate.
- Never feed wild animals at the settlement edge.
- Do not leave scraps where they might attract boar, foxes, or larger predators.

At night, respect the outer fires and the rule against wandering into vegetation. The forest is not supernatural, but it is wet, steep, thick, and inhabited by hungry animals. A few steps off-path can make you invisible to anyone searching.

## Food and hospitality

Expect filling food shaped by damp mountain agriculture: roots, greens, cabbage, onions, beans, squash, mushrooms, grain porridges, fermented foods, eggs, duck, goat dairy, pork, occasional fish, and preserved or smoked meat.

For safety and courtesy:

- Eat what is offered when you can, but state allergies or restrictions early and plainly.
- Do not criticize fermentation, smoke flavor, or communal dishes.
- Ask before refusing food; there may be a polite way to do so.
- Bring your own bowl, cup, spoon, and cloth if possible. They are compact gifts to yourself and reduce burden on hosts.
- Be cautious with raw greens, untreated water, and foods held at ambient temperature if your immune system is unaccustomed to local conditions.

If offered alcohol or fermented drink, sip slowly until you understand its strength and social role. Do not become drunk in a vertical wet village.

## Technology and multiverse equipment

Keep advanced tools discreet, robust, and non-disruptive.

Useful equipment:

- Waterproof headlamp with red or low-light mode.
- Water purifier.
- Compact solar charger, only if you can use it without attracting undue attention.
- Durable wristwatch.
- Offline translation reference.
- Medical supplies.
- Small camera, used only with consent.
- Satellite or dimensional beacon, concealed and used away from private homes if it emits sound, light, or radio interference.

Avoid displaying devices as entertainment for children or proof of superiority. A bright screen, drone, loud charger, unfamiliar chemical smell, or humming instrument will quickly become the center of attention—and may alarm animals.

Never fly drones around roofs, ponds, birds, livestock, or the cliff edge. They are intrusive, dangerous to animals, and likely to be interpreted as surveillance or a bad omen.

If you must open a portal or activate travel equipment, do it well outside the residential core, away from water sources, gardens, animal pens, and unstable slopes. Ensure it does not create wind, heat, noise, light, contamination, or a crowd-control problem.

## The rules you must not break

Take these as seriously as local law, because they effectively are:

1. **Do not block water.**
2. **Do not contaminate water.**
3. **Do not damage a living wall, root structure, bridge, or retaining feature.**
4. **Do not interfere with animals or gardens.**
5. **Do not create waste uphill.**
6. **Do not walk into the outer forest alone, especially near dusk or in fog.**
7. **Do not ignore warnings about weather, paths, or household boundaries.**
8. **Do not assume that a “natural” feature is unmanaged or ownerless.**
9. **Do not make yourself someone others must rescue.**
10. **When a communal emergency begins, either help under direction or get out of the way immediately.**

## A sensible first-day plan

- Arrive in daylight, ideally after morning work but well before evening animal rounds.
- Wait at the upper edge or gate.
- Introduce yourself and ask for the appropriate host or elder.
- Offer a modest useful gift.
- Ask where to put your pack, where to drink, and where to relieve yourself.
- Learn the safe route to your sleeping place.
- Spend the rest of the day observing, helping with an uncomplicated task, and learning water and path etiquette.
- Eat with your host if invited.
- Before dark, locate a lamp, rain gear, footwear, and the nearest safe indoor shelter.
- Do not go exploring after sunset.

The right attitude is not rugged independence. It is respectful interdependence. This settlement survives because people notice water, roots, animals, and one another before they become problems. If you arrive prepared to notice the same things—and prepared to carry your share—you will likely find it demanding, damp, noisy, and deeply hospitable.
