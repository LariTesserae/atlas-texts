## Before You Go

Treat this as an extreme-altitude desert expedition and a metaphysical quarantine zone at the same time.

The country is survivable for an ordinary person, apparently, but it is not forgiving. Its greatest immediate dangers are familiar: hypoxia, dehydration, cold, ultraviolet exposure, falls, isolation, and navigation failure. Its greater dangers are subtle: temporal displacement, environmental stasis, things that preserve shapes and traces, and an ecosystem whose rules you do not understand.

Do not go alone. A group of three to five is ideal: enough people to observe one another for confusion or temporal anomalies, but not so many that you create noise, footprints, or social complications among the animals. Establish clear authority before arrival. Decide in advance who makes the call to retreat.

Everyone should agree on one foundational rule:

> Nothing in this country is “just a rock” until proven otherwise, and proof may not be available.

Do not plan to collect samples. Do not expect to hunt, forage, or find water. Do not assume a dead thing is dead, a still thing is inactive, or an old footprint is historical.

## Physical Equipment

### Breathing and altitude

The air is described as extremely thin. Assume an elevation at which an unacclimatized human can become incapacitated quickly.

Bring:

- Supplemental oxygen for every traveler, with substantial reserve.
- A system that does not depend entirely on electronics: compressed oxygen, masks, regulators, and mechanical pressure gauges.
- Pulse oximeters, but do not trust them as your only measure. Cold can degrade their accuracy.
- Medication and training for altitude illness where medically appropriate.
- A portable pressure shelter or hyperbaric bag if your party is staying longer than a brief reconnaissance visit.
- A strict acclimatization plan if the method of arrival permits it.

Know the symptoms of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, poor coordination, confusion, breathlessness at rest, irrational confidence, and altered judgment. In this place, those symptoms may be indistinguishable from the local effects. Treat any ambiguity as a medical emergency first.

No alcohol, sedatives, recreational drugs, or sleep aids unless medically necessary and supervised. You will need accurate judgment.

### Water

There is no water. Do not interpret “there is no water” as “there is little water.” The description suggests a place where water may be physically absent, anomalously unavailable, or replaced by substances that imitate it.

Bring more than your normal desert allocation, carried in redundant sealed containers:

- At least 6–10 liters per person per day for drinking and basic needs, depending on exertion and duration.
- Electrolytes.
- Emergency intravenous fluids only if someone in the party is trained and authorized to use them.
- Water purification gear is still useful for the journey before and after, but should not be relied on inside the country.
- Clearly label all water containers by touch as well as visually. In dimness, fatigue, or altered perception, you must be able to distinguish your own water from anything found there.

Do not drink from mineral tubes, glowing plants, lamps, animal remains, crevasses, polished stone hollows, or apparently fresh condensation. Do not drink a liquid simply because it is clear, cold, or because you are desperate. The local rule is explicit: **do not drink what is not water.** It likely exists because some other substance is convincing.

### Cold, sun, and dry air

Bring clothing for severe dry cold and relentless solar exposure:

- Layered insulating clothing with a windproof outer shell.
- Gloves warm enough for metal and stone handling, though you should avoid handling either unnecessarily.
- UV-rated glacier glasses plus a spare pair.
- Broad sun protection for face and lips.
- A face covering or balaclava to reduce respiratory moisture loss.
- Insulated boots with aggressive traction and removable crampons or microspikes.
- Spare boot laces, straps, and repair materials; cold, dryness, and brittle terrain will damage gear.
- A warm sleep system rated far below the expected temperature, even if the day seems unchanging.
- A reflective emergency bivouac shelter.

Avoid equipment that depends on flexible plastics, exposed batteries, or delicate digital displays. The cold is said to make metal brittle; assume it can be unkind to every engineered material. Bring analog backups: compass, paper maps, mechanical watch, notebooks, pencils, a signal mirror, and a whistle—though sound may behave poorly there.

### Food

Bring all food required for the trip, plus reserve rations. Do not eat local plants, animal tissue, quartz beads, insects, “mineral salts,” or anything offered by an apparently abandoned shelter.

The animals may derive sustenance directly from the ground, may not metabolize food as you do, or may be repetitions rather than organisms in the conventional sense. Their apparent calm is not evidence of edibility.

Use odor-contained food storage. Not necessarily because local animals hunt—nothing seems to—but because you do not know what responds to novelty.

## Navigation and Communication

Do not rely solely on GPS, radios, drones, or satellite communication. The sky may look ordinary while local causality is not. Sound behaves strangely, and shadows can remain after their owners move. Time and position may not agree.

Bring:

- Paper topographic maps, but expect them to become obsolete at a local scale.
- A magnetic compass, a gyrocompass if available, and simple sun-navigation tools.
- Multiple independent timepieces: mechanical watches synchronized before entry, plus battery-powered watches stored warm.
- A written route log maintained by at least two people independently.
- Physical trail markers that you carry in and remove again—bright cord, flagged stakes, or reflective tags—but do **not** tie them to cairns, bone structures, mineral plants, animals, bridges, or stones that appear deliberately placed.
- Long-distance visual signals: flags, high-visibility panels, mirrors, and light strobes. Do not assume shouting carries.
- Radios, but establish hand signals and line-of-sight procedures first.
- A climbing rope or tether system for exposed slopes, crevasses, and moments when a person’s location becomes uncertain.

Every member should write, at fixed intervals:

- Date and agreed external time.
- Apparent local sun position.
- Current location estimate.
- Direction of travel.
- Names and headcount of the party.
- Any unusual observations, especially duplicate tracks, lingering shadows, repeated animals, or encounters with oneself.

Do this on paper. Do not depend on recordings alone. Digital files may retain timestamps that are technically correct but practically meaningless.

## Group Procedures

### The buddy rule

No one goes out of sight of at least one other person. In this region, “out of sight” includes being on the other side of a boulder, crest, animal mass, or shallow fold in the terrain.

Use tethers near crevasses and in heavy animal concentrations. Not to restrain anyone from panic, but to preserve a continuous physical record of who is where.

Conduct frequent headcounts—but do **not count the animals**. Count people only, using names rather than numbers if possible.

If a member of your party appears twice, do not immediately approach either version.

First:

1. Stop moving.
2. Have the rest of the group sit or stand still together.
3. Establish the agreed challenge-response phrase, chosen before arrival.
4. Compare notebooks, timepieces, injuries, dust patterns, and equipment states from a safe distance.
5. Do not touch, restrain, attack, or follow the duplicate.
6. If one person seems to be “ahead” of themselves, follow the local instruction: **wait**.

“Wait” should mean no physical action except preserving warmth, breathing, hydration, and safety from immediate terrain hazards. Do not attempt to force resolution by retracing steps. Do not ask your later self for useful information; information itself may be part of the loop.

Before entering, agree that no one will make promises, bargains, or major decisions based on information obtained from a future or duplicate self.

### The observation rule

The local animals are neither ordinary wildlife nor obviously hostile. Their lack of hunting and flight may reflect peace, paralysis, ecological equilibrium, or an unfamiliar relation to time.

Act as though every animal is protected, sacred, hazardous, and possibly part of the terrain.

- Keep a generous distance.
- Do not feed them.
- Do not touch them.
- Do not separate young-looking animals from larger ones.
- Do not attempt to determine whether they breathe, blink, age, sleep, or repeat.
- Do not count them, estimate their population aloud, or make tally marks.
- Do not move through a herd if an alternate route exists.
- If animals surround you without aggression, remain calm, reduce movement, and retreat slowly along your existing route if possible.
- Never use a local animal bone for a tool, tent peg, fuel, weapon, or souvenir.

The same applies to carcasses. A carcass that has not decayed in centuries is not a resource. Leave it untouched.

## The Rule About Stones

This is the rule to take most seriously:

> Never move a stone that has chosen a shape.

Since you cannot know what “chosen a shape” means, use a broad interpretation. Do not move:

- Cairn stones.
- Stones arranged in lines, arcs, stacks, circles, thresholds, paths, or apparent nests.
- Stones partly embedded in animal gatherings.
- Stones with unusual polish, color, warmth, shadows, inscriptions, or repeated markings.
- Stones resting against bridges, shelters, roots, bones, or mineral growths.
- Stones that resemble tools, faces, animals, body parts, or letters.
- Stones that seem to have shifted between glances.
- Any stone you notice twice.

Do not build your own cairns, either. Do not use stones to anchor tents, weigh down equipment, mark routes, block wind, or make a fire ring. Bring manufactured anchors, stakes, sandbags, and cords if needed.

Even loose-looking gravel should be disturbed minimally. Step on established bare ground or durable rock only when safe. A footprint may remain for longer than you expect, and in a place where traces endure, every action can become part of the environment.

## Structures, Cairns, and the Bridge

The huts are not safe simply because they are human-made. They may be old shelters, ritual sites, preserved moments, traps, or all of these at once.

If you must enter a shelter:

- Announce yourselves quietly at the threshold, even if you believe it empty.
- Do not cross alone.
- Keep one person outside, in sight of the door.
- Photograph or sketch the doorway markings before entering.
- Do not remove tools, lamps, benches, or any object within.
- Do not add fuel to an empty lamp.
- Do not sit on a bench unless necessary for survival.
- Do not sleep inside unless conditions outside are immediately life-threatening.
- Leave exactly as you entered, without rearranging dust, objects, or stones.

For cairns: do not approach closely, sit beside them, lean gear against them, photograph them with flash, or identify whether they are stone, skull, animal, or stationary creature. Observe briefly and move on.

For the bridge: avoid it unless crossing is necessary for survival or your planned exit route. Its lack of approach roads is a warning. If you must cross:

- Cross one at a time.
- Use a safety rope anchored to your own portable anchors, not to the bridge.
- Do not look down into the crevasse for long.
- Do not stop at the midpoint.
- Do not turn around midway.
- Do not photograph footprints at either end.
- Do not attempt to distinguish whether the overlapping tracks are ancient or newly made.
- Once across, do not immediately return “to check” something.

A bottomless crevasse need not be physically bottomless to be dangerous. It may contain distance, time, sound, or memory behaving incorrectly.

## Managing Shadows and Temporal Effects

Your shadow may not obey you. Treat it as an environmental indicator, not as an enemy or a companion.

If a shadow remains behind after you move:

- Do not return to stand inside it.
- Do not touch it or attempt to cover it.
- Do not photograph it repeatedly.
- Mark your position in your log, then continue calmly if the route remains safe.
- Avoid making important directional decisions based on it.

If you see footprints ahead that appear to be yours:

- Stop.
- Compare them to your actual boots without placing your foot into them.
- Do not follow them automatically.
- Do not erase them.
- Do not use them as proof that a route is safe.
- Wait if there is any indication you are encountering a future version of your own journey.

If you see yourself:

- Do not run toward them.
- Do not run away blindly.
- Do not exchange objects.
- Do not accept food, water, maps, tools, or instructions from them.
- Do not attempt to prevent whatever you think you are about to do.
- Preserve the group, stabilize medically, and wait.

Your goal is not to solve the anomaly. Your goal is to leave with your party intact.

## Night Procedures

Night is likely more beautiful than safe. The glowing plants, quartz, eyes, lamp-stones, and unnamed things under gravel can make a traveler feel that the landscape is finally revealing itself. Resist the urge to investigate.

Set camp only on ground that has been examined in full daylight and is clearly separate from:

- Animal concentrations.
- Cairns.
- Shelters.
- Mineral seams and glowing vegetation.
- Footprint fields.
- Crevasse edges.
- Bone deposits.
- Places where shadows seem unusually persistent.
- Stones arranged in meaningful shapes.

Use a low-impact, fully portable camp. Do not dig trenches. Do not gather local fuel. Do not make a fire unless you have no other means of preventing death. Flames may create new shadows, and new shadows may not leave when the fire does.

Establish a two-person watch at all times. Watchers should not stare continuously into the dark or at glowing ground. Their task is practical: observe the camp perimeter, check oxygen and warmth, note changes in party position, and wake everyone if an animal enters camp or if any person appears to be elsewhere than they should be.

At night, maintain a written log of each watch change. A simple line—“All four present; no one outside tent; 02:00 by Watch A”—may become very important.

## Departure and Decontamination

Leave earlier than you think you need to. Do not remain simply because the day seems unchanged, the weather remains stable, or the animals have accepted your presence. Stasis can feel like safety while quietly consuming the time you intended to keep.

When departing:

- Retrace your route only if it remains physically and mentally coherent.
- Do not force yourself to match old footprints.
- Do not retrieve abandoned markers if doing so would require interacting with a changed landscape.
- Take out all equipment you brought in, but do not risk your life to recover an item that has become associated with a cairn, animal, shadow, or altered stone arrangement.
- Do not bring back samples: no stones, plants, bones, quartz beads, dust, tools, water-like fluids, insects, or apparently inert material.
- Check each person for unexplained objects, extra tools, unfamiliar dust, duplicate notebooks, injuries they cannot account for, or items that appear older or newer than they should be.

After exit, quarantine equipment and separate party members for a short, supervised debrief if possible. Compare journals before discussing memories in detail. Memories can converge socially; independent notes may show whether everyone experienced the same sequence.

Medical evaluation should include dehydration, frost injury, altitude illness, retinal damage, respiratory stress, and cognitive assessment. Continue observing for delayed anomalies: persistent shadows, repeated dreams of terrain not visited, identical footprints appearing near home, objects that cannot be traced to the expedition, or discrepancies in elapsed time.

## The Correct Attitude

Do not approach this place as a puzzle to solve, a wilderness to conquer, or a resource to exploit.

It is an old equilibrium. Its inhabitants appear to have found ways of existing without the cycles that make ordinary life legible: predation, decay, weather, growth, thirst, migration, and perhaps a clean distinction between past and future. You are not part of that equilibrium. Your safest role is to be brief, quiet, courteous, fully supplied, and difficult to notice.

Carry your own water. Keep your own count. Trust your companions more than your perceptions, but trust immediate physical danger more than either. Touch little. Move gently. Leave no stone differently shaped than you found it.
