## Before You Go

Prepare as though you are visiting a cool, wet, high-altitude industrial city built into a drainage system. The main risks are not dramatic wilderness hazards but accumulated ordinary ones: slippery stone, bad air, steep infrastructure, contaminated water, freight traffic, large familiar animals, and getting stranded on the wrong side of a rail line or flood channel.

The city is likely densely inhabited, practical, and accustomed to workers, traders, and through-travelers. Dress and behave like someone there to manage their own needs without making extra work for other people.

For a multiverse traveler, arrange your usual identity, currency, language, health, and return-route contingencies before arrival. Do not assume communications, electrical standards, medicine, or legal concepts match your home reality. Establish a safe arrival point near a staffed station, hotel, public bath, market ward, or administrative office rather than in a ravine, rooftop, industrial platform, rail yard, or perimeter marsh.

Most importantly: **do not arrive carrying live plants, seeds, soil, insects, or unsealed organic waste.** In a city apparently without rooted plant life, even an ordinary seed packet, muddy boot sole, apple core, or houseplant could be an ecological, agricultural, or political problem. Declare biological materials if there is any customs process. Clean your boots and equipment thoroughly beforehand.

## Clothing and Personal Gear

Bring clothing for persistent rain rather than occasional storms.

### Wear
- A waterproof outer shell with a hood. Choose something durable enough for soot, metal dust, and abrasion against stone walls.
- Warm insulating layers: wool or synthetics rather than cotton. The air may be cool while the city itself is humid and heated unevenly.
- Waterproof boots with aggressive non-slip soles and good ankle support. Polished goat-worn stairs, wet shale, metal walkways, and rail platforms will all be slick.
- Thick socks, with spare pairs in a waterproof bag.
- Long trousers that can tolerate grime and resist snagging on cables, fencing, and rough masonry.
- A cap or brim under your hood; rain and soot may run from overhead pipes and ledges.
- Gloves. Useful for wet railings, cold stone, dirty doors, and avoiding direct contact with industrial surfaces.

Avoid trailing garments, loose scarves, dangling bags, or anything that can catch in cable machinery, train doors, grates, or animal horns.

### Carry
- A compact but sturdy umbrella, preferably one you do not mind losing or staining. Do not rely on it in strong ravine winds or crowded markets.
- A small waterproof daypack.
- A refillable bottle **with a serious filter**, plus purification tablets as backup. Still, use treated municipal water when possible; do not casually fill from gutters, channels, seepage pools, steam-condensate pipes, or public tanks without knowing their source.
- A particulate respirator, ideally P2/N95 or better, for factory districts, rail tunnels, coal-dust rain, chemical odors, and smoke inversions.
- Simple eye protection: clear glasses are adequate for rain, grit, and soot.
- A headlamp with spare batteries. Power failures, underground passages, stairwells, and rail-adjacent districts may be poorly lit.
- Earplugs for hotels near rail lines, steam plants, cable engines, pigeons, or night freight yards.
- Basic first aid supplies, including blister care, antiseptic, bandages, anti-diarrheal medication, and any personal prescriptions.
- Hand sanitizer and soap sheets. The city is wet, animal-dense, and resource-conscious; handwashing may be available but not always convenient.
- A paper map, local transit map, address card for your lodging, and a means of identifying your district. Phone navigation may fail underground or among steep retaining walls.

Keep documents, money, electronics, and medicine in separate waterproof pouches. Moisture will be a constant rather than an incident.

## Health Precautions

The combination of elevation, dampness, smoke, animal density, and industrial runoff deserves respect.

### Air
The atmosphere may be warm and humid in low wards but cold at night and thin at higher elevations. Give yourself a day or two before strenuous climbing, especially if you are not acclimated to altitude.

In factory districts, the air may contain coal dust, mineral vapor, sulfurous smells, metal particulates, or chemical emissions. A metallic taste in the air is not necessarily an emergency, but it is a sign not to linger outdoors if you can avoid it.

Leave an area and seek clean indoor air if you experience:
- persistent coughing or chest tightness,
- dizziness not explained by altitude,
- burning eyes or throat,
- nausea associated with an odor plume,
- headache that improves when you leave a district,
- colored fog, unusually dense low smoke, or visible chemical haze.

Avoid running or heavy exercise near smelters, chemical works, locomotive yards, slag hills, and drainage canals.

### Water and food
Assume water follows complicated routes. Roof runoff may enter tanks; sewage, industrial drainage, animal waste, and stormwater may share lower channels before treatment. Drink only water that locals identify as potable, preferably boiled, filtered, or from a sealed source.

Eat hot, freshly prepared food from busy stalls. In a city with regular rail deliveries and limited local agriculture, food logistics are important, and crowded vendors may be safer than isolated ones because turnover is high.

Good choices:
- cooked grains, noodles, breads, stews, soups,
- thoroughly cooked meat,
- boiled tea or coffee,
- sealed bottled drinks,
- fruit you can peel yourself, if available.

Be cautious with:
- raw salads or herbs,
- unpasteurized milk,
- ice of uncertain origin,
- raw shellfish or fish,
- food exposed beside animal traffic,
- anything stored near warm steam vents or open drainage channels.

### Animal contact
Treat the city’s animals as working residents, not attractions.

- Do not feed pigeons, goats, dogs, cats, bats, or buffalo without local permission.
- Avoid touching animals, especially around the mouth, udders, hooves, wounds, manure, or feed.
- Keep food sealed. Dogs and goats may be practiced opportunists.
- Do not enter bat galleries or enclosed drainage vaults. Besides physical hazards, airborne droppings and fungal exposure may be a concern.
- Make sure tetanus vaccination is current. Rabies vaccination may be worth considering if you expect extended travel around dogs, bats, or isolated outer settlements.

If bitten or scratched, wash immediately with soap and clean water for an extended time, seek local medical care, and follow up according to your home-world medical protocol.

## Moving Through the City

This is probably not a place where the fastest route is the safest route. Plan to move deliberately.

### Use rail and cable transit
Railways are the city’s skeleton for a reason. Trains, trams, funicular-style cable carriages, and district lines are likely more reliable than walking steep routes in rain.

Learn:
- the last train or cable service to your lodging,
- which stations close during heavy weather,
- which district platforms are freight-only,
- whether passengers need to yield to animal crossings or industrial movements,
- which lines are safe after dark.

Do not walk on tracks, cross rail bridges unless they are explicitly pedestrian routes, or step over cables. In a wet city, an apparently inactive industrial line may become active suddenly, and low visibility makes trains much quieter than expected.

### Respect water channels
The gutters, culverts, grates, and under-street channels are infrastructure, not scenery.

Never:
- climb down through a grate,
- enter a culvert or tunnel,
- stand in a ravine during sustained rain,
- sleep near a drainage channel,
- assume a shallow channel cannot rise,
- touch water emerging from an industrial wall, pipe, or slag heap.

Long gray rain can create dangerous runoff without a dramatic storm. The city may flood in localized, fast-moving ways: a stairway becomes a stream, a market undercroft fills from below, a road cut becomes a chute.

If locals start moving carts, livestock, or children uphill, or if shutters close along a low street, follow their lead rather than stopping to watch.

### Expect vertical navigation
Neighborhoods hang on retaining walls and follow old quarry faces. Routes can involve hundreds of steps, narrow stairways, ramps, overhead walkways, and abrupt changes between sheltered warmth and wet exposed ledges.

Travel light. A rolling suitcase will become a burden. Use a backpack or portered luggage service if available.

Stay on maintained paths. A seemingly convenient shortcut may cross private loading space, unstable shale, an active retaining wall, or a roof-drain system.

## Industrial Awareness

The city seems to coexist with industry rather than separating from it. You may sleep near a bakery, walk past a tannery, and take a tram through a power corridor. This is normal locally, but visitors should recognize boundaries.

Obey warning paint, horns, bells, barrier chains, red lamps, posted schedules, and workers’ gestures. Faded signs still matter.

Do not photograph, touch, sample, or collect:
- chemical runoff,
- slag,
- ores,
- painted pipes,
- industrial dust,
- apparent mineral crystals,
- residue from vents,
- water from settling ponds,
- abandoned machinery,
- exposed cable systems.

Your multiverse habits may tempt you to regard unfamiliar materials as souvenirs. Resist that. Contamination can travel both directions, and an object that looks like harmless stone may contain heavy metals, reactive salts, or substances regulated locally.

If you must pass through the western industrial escarpment:
- wear your mask when dust or brown rain is present,
- keep your hood up,
- do not eat or drink outdoors,
- wash hands and face afterward,
- clean your boots before entering a lodging or public bath,
- change outer clothing if it has visibly collected soot.

## Etiquette Around Animals

The animals are likely integrated into work, transport, waste management, neighborhood identity, and daily routines. Treating them respectfully will matter socially as well as practically.

### Goats
Goats occupy stairways, warehouse shelters, and upper streets.

- Yield to a herd; do not attempt to push through its center.
- Stand against a wall or move to a broad landing and let them pass.
- Secure bags, paper, food, and loose clothing.
- Do not block a goat path simply to take photographs.
- Watch where you step. Droppings, slick hoof-polished stone, and wet mineral residue will make stairs hazardous.

### Dogs and cats
Neighborhood animals may be semi-owned, communally fed, or simply well-known.

- Let dogs approach first.
- Do not assume a friendly dog is stray or available for adoption.
- Ask before feeding or petting.
- Do not disturb cats resting around steam vents; those warm spots may be scarce and socially recognized as theirs.

### Buffalo
Buffalo are large working animals, not slow-moving public sculpture.

- Keep a generous distance, particularly around calves, handlers, carts, water crossings, and narrow embankments.
- Never approach from behind or stand between an animal and a route it is following.
- If a herd blocks the road, wait. The whistles, handlers, and traffic system will accommodate them.
- Do not climb on animals, carts, or canal machinery, even during holidays.
- If horns are painted for a festival, admire from a respectful distance and ask before photographing handlers or children.

### Birds and bats
Cliff pigeons may fill confined spaces in enormous numbers. Wear a hat in roosting districts, avoid sitting beneath heavy accumulations of droppings, and do not enter abandoned signal towers or cisterns.

At dusk, give bat emergence routes room. They are doing pest control; do not shine bright lights into them, wave at them, or create a crowd beneath a colony entrance.

## Lodging

Choose lodging uphill from major drainage channels but not so high that you must negotiate exposed stairways in nightly fog. A place near a staffed tram or rail stop is ideal.

Ask before booking:
- Is the building connected to treated water?
- Does the room have heating and ventilation that can be controlled?
- Is there mold, steam leakage, or smoke ingress?
- How far is the nearest station in rain?
- Does the street flood?
- Are there curfews or access restrictions during rail movements?
- Are animals housed beneath, beside, or above the building?
- Is there secure indoor storage for boots and rain gear?

A room over a courtyard might be warmer and more social, but also noisier and more humid. A room near the escarpment may have dramatic views but more wind, rain exposure, and industrial air. A room beside a major viaduct may be convenient but loud.

If offered access to a public bath built around warm seepage, use local guidance. Such baths may be clean and culturally important, but water quality, bathing customs, gender norms, and health restrictions can vary sharply.

## Money, Supplies, and Local Life

The city likely values repair, reuse, rationing, and practical exchange. Imported food, animal feed, fuel, dry clothing, clean water, and machine parts may all carry more significance than decorative goods.

Carry small denominations. Markets, transit workers, animal handlers, and neighborhood vendors may not want large notes or unusual payment methods.

Useful items to buy locally:
- a city rain cape,
- non-slip overshoes or locally suitable boot treatments,
- a district map,
- a transit token or pass,
- a local mask or cloth filter for dusty wards,
- soap, dried food, and a thermos,
- a weather bulletin if such things are printed.

Do not waste food openly. In a place where all food is imported and animal feed is a civic concern, discarded edible material may read as insulting or irresponsible. Ask how scraps are sorted; there may be separate systems for compostable animal feed, grease recovery, bone collection, fuel dung, and ordinary refuse.

Never throw anything into a gutter. Even a biodegradable item has nowhere benign to go; it becomes part of somebody else’s water system downstream.

## Weather and Emergency Plans

The weather is persistent rather than theatrical. This can make people complacent. Do not be.

Every morning, find out:
- which districts are raining,
- whether upper reservoirs are releasing water,
- whether low wards have flood advisories,
- whether rail lines are delayed,
- wind conditions on exposed viaducts,
- industrial air conditions on the western side,
- whether animal crossings or market closures will affect routes.

Have two routes back to your lodging: one that uses rail and one that remains on high ground. Know a public indoor refuge nearby—station, bathhouse, bakery, market hall, library, temple, union hall, hotel lobby, or administrative office.

If caught in severe rain:
1. Move uphill or to a known covered public building.
2. Stay away from ravines, underpasses, culverts, rail cuts, and basement markets.
3. Do not shelter directly under unstable retaining walls or near cliff faces.
4. Avoid touching metal fencing, exposed electrical boxes, or cable infrastructure if water is pooling.
5. Wait for local instructions before attempting to cross a water-covered street.

If a chemical or smoke plume reaches you:
1. Move crosswind or uphill if possible.
2. Cover nose and mouth with your respirator.
3. Go indoors to a building with a known clean-air space.
4. Do not seek refuge in drainage tunnels, low undercrofts, or enclosed rail spaces where fumes may collect.
5. Follow locals; they will know which direction the basin’s air typically settles.

## Social Attitude

This is not a city to visit looking for untouched nature, clean picturesque views, or effortless leisure. Its beauty is likely inseparable from labor, maintenance, animals, rain, and the long history of people adapting to difficult terrain.

Do not call it barren, lifeless, ugly, primitive, dirty, or “post-apocalyptic.” The absence of vegetation does not mean an absence of ecology, care, or civilization. The city appears intensely alive—just in forms that are easy for outsiders to overlook: water routing, animal routes, steam networks, food trains, repair work, shared tanks, and the expertise required to make a basin city function.

Ask before taking photographs of workers, handlers, homes cut into slopes, public baths, religious or civic animal rituals, and industrial facilities. Do not treat poverty, soot, crowded livestock, or old infrastructure as exotic scenery.

A good visitor notices where they are standing, keeps out of the flow of animals and freight, pays fairly, follows drainage rules, and accepts that some of the most important parts of the city are not available for casual exploration.

## A Practical Arrival Checklist

On your first day:

- Clean and declare any biological material.
- Obtain local rain and flood information.
- Buy or confirm transit access.
- Identify potable-water sources.
- Learn the safe route between lodging and station.
- Locate the nearest clinic or pharmacy.
- Find out which districts have poor air quality.
- Ask where not to walk after dark.
- Ask how food waste and ordinary trash are handled.
- Learn the local signal for an approaching herd, rail movement, or flood closure.
- Keep your boots dry enough to remain safe, not necessarily clean.
- End the day indoors before unfamiliar routes become slick, foggy, and industrially quiet.

If you prepare for dampness, height, soot, animals, and infrastructure—and arrive willing to move at the city’s patient pace—you should be able to travel there comfortably.
