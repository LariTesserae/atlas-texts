## Before You Go

This is not a wilderness destination, despite the goats, foxes, engineered moss, and high white “plains.” It is a living industrial settlement built inside—and perhaps on top of—a continually adjusted megastructure. Prepare for it as you would for a cold, high-altitude port city, a construction site, a dense vertical neighborhood, and a place with local physical irregularities.

Do not arrive expecting ruins. The place is active, inhabited, administered, maintained, and extremely sensitive to disruption. Its people are used to machinery, animals, weather management, structural motion, and strange municipal rules. They are not necessarily used to visitors who treat those things as curiosities.

Arrange a local sponsor if possible: a resident family, maintenance guild contact, market association, school office, freight cooperative, or registered guide. You will need someone who can tell you which corridors are currently lit, which bridge levels are open, where controlled rainfall is scheduled, and whether a district is near a moving slope or under atmospheric maintenance.

If you use multiversal transit, arrive at an authorized exchange point rather than trying to enter from the rooflands, freight tunnels, or upper chimneys. The valley’s systems may not recognize an unscheduled arrival as human activity. In a place built around sensors, pressure doors, maintenance routes, and unmarked lower levels, being “unexpected” can be more dangerous than being foreign.

## Documents, Declarations, and What to Tell People

Carry both durable physical identification and a non-networked written card stating:

- Your name, language(s), and origin world or transit authority.
- Any relevant medical needs.
- Whether you have implants, powered devices, unusual biological requirements, or nonstandard body temperature.
- An emergency contact reachable through local relay systems.
- A statement that you are an ordinary human traveler, if that is true.

Avoid presenting yourself as an expert on the valley’s origins, machinery, ecology, or cosmology. Many residents have practical, inherited relationships with systems whose original purpose is obscure. Telling a maintenance worker that their city “must be” an ancient terraforming engine, a failed factory organism, or a dream-generated construct is unlikely to endear you to them—and may be taken as a claim of authority.

Declare all bright clothing, signaling equipment, drones, autonomous tools, high-powered lights, noisemakers, and chemical supplies. Bright colors are locally functional: warning colors, official access markings, animal collars, emergency indicators. A traveler in vivid red, yellow, or fluorescent green may accidentally look like a hazard marker, utility worker, animal handler, or a person attempting to pass a restricted threshold.

If you carry a personal weather-control device, water manipulator, gravity device, mapping familiar, or autonomous scout, leave it behind unless specifically permitted. The valley already has weather engines, drainage logic, localized rain, pressure systems, and animal protections. Even harmless-seeming interference can have consequences beyond what is visible.

## Clothing

Dress in layers suitable for cold, dryness, sudden damp, and long indoor-outdoor transitions.

Bring:

- A warm base layer that does not retain sweat.
- A mid-layer for cold upper terraces and rooflands.
- A waterproof or water-resistant outer shell.
- Gloves with good grip for wet railings and ladders.
- A brimmed cap or hood: not for sun alone, but for condensation falling from pipes, awnings, and bridge tubes.
- Eye protection against mineral dust, dry air, reflected brightness, and occasional grit.
- A close-fitting face covering or respirator rated for dust, spores, industrial particulates, and damp mold if you will visit filtration districts, animal yards, workshops, or old freight corridors.
- Sturdy, non-slip boots with ankle support and soles that work on wet metal, smooth ceramic, rubber aggregate, and stair edges.
- Thick socks; the cold here travels through surfaces.
- A small high-visibility item that can be displayed when needed but kept covered otherwise.

Choose gray, blue, muted brown, white, silver, or dull orange if you want to blend in. Avoid pure warning yellow, emergency red, flashing textiles, highly reflective coats, or patterned garments that could be mistaken for signage.

Do not rely on ordinary sunglasses alone. The high-altitude brightness is described less as illumination than as pressure: reflected light from pale material, glare off moisture, atmospheric intensity, and perhaps something particular to the basin’s engineered environment. Use wraparound eye protection with good UV and glare filtering. If the brightness produces headache, nausea, tears, visual halos, or the sense that surfaces are closer than they are, go indoors, hydrate, and ask a local where the low-glare routes are.

## Health Preparation

The altitude, dry periods, industrial dust, compressed quiet, and irregular weather are the ordinary hazards. Prepare before transit:

- Acclimatize if you can. If you cannot, plan several low-exertion days upon arrival.
- Bring hydration salts, lip balm, saline eye drops, moisturizer, and throat lozenges.
- Pack a small first-aid kit including blister care, antiseptic, bandages, pain relief, and any prescribed medication.
- Bring a compact pulse oximeter if altitude affects you easily.
- Carry a paper list of symptoms that mean you should seek help: persistent breathlessness at rest, chest pain, confusion, severe headache, blue lips, inability to stay warm, or a cough that rapidly worsens after exposure to industrial mist.
- Make sure your tetanus status is current. The city is clean in the sense of constant maintenance, but it remains full of metal, cable, machinery, old surfaces, and animal contact.
- If you have asthma, migraines, sensory sensitivity, panic in enclosed spaces, or difficulty with low-frequency vibration, discuss this trip carefully with a clinician before departure.

The structural ticking during dimming may be almost inaudible, but some travelers will feel it rather than hear it. It can manifest as unease, insomnia, tooth pressure, or the feeling that beds and walls are subtly settling. This does not necessarily mean immediate danger. It does mean you should stay in registered accommodation, ask where the local structural notices are posted, and avoid sleeping near expansion joints, major ventilation stacks, or active freight shafts.

## Essential Gear

A sensible day pack should include:

- Water bottle or hydration bladder, preferably insulated.
- Electrolytes.
- Dust mask or respirator.
- Eye protection.
- Warm layer and rain shell.
- Small towel or absorbent cloth.
- Compact flashlight with a **steady**, non-flashing beam.
- Spare batteries or a manual charging option.
- Paper map issued locally.
- Mechanical compass only as a secondary tool; do not assume it will be meaningful near substations, rail lines, or large structural mesh.
- Notebook and pencil.
- Small whistle **only for true emergencies**; do not test it.
- Earplugs for sleep, but not for walking in service districts.
- Portable translation aid, preferably offline.
- Small amount of local currency, trade goods, or commonly useful items approved by your host.
- A thin, clean plastic ground sheet or seat pad for waiting in damp transit spaces.
- A sealable bag for wet clothing, dust-coated gear, or anything exposed to indoor rain.

Do not depend entirely on GPS, live mapping, wireless service, or imported navigation systems. Routes can change, bridge levels can close, and the far ridge may not remain where a map says it is. Local maps are likely to distinguish between stable routes, seasonally adjusted routes, guild routes, and “not public” routes. Respect these distinctions.

## Sound Etiquette

The valley is crowded but quiet on purpose. Its inhabitants have learned that noise carries, that machinery is controlled, and that loud activity belongs in designated places or in lower levels beyond ordinary public life.

Speak quietly in residential terraces, enclosed pedestrian tubes, markets near shrines, animal yards, rail platforms, and anywhere you hear people lowering their own voices.

Do not:

- Shout across levels.
- Play music aloud.
- Run loudly through bridge tunnels.
- Test echoes in halls.
- Slam doors, strike railings, or bang tools casually.
- Use a drone that buzzes, beeps, or emits a high-pitched stabilizing tone.
- Treat the quiet as an invitation to make noise because “nobody else is.”

If you need someone’s attention, use a normal voice, a hand signal, or ask how people summon staff in that district. Flashing lights may be meaningful. Horns may be freight signals. Whistles may alarm animals or personnel. A local gesture may be safest.

## The Rule About Unlit Corridors

Do not enter an unlit corridor alone. Treat this as law, safety code, social custom, and possibly more than one of those at once.

“Unlit” may not merely mean dark. It may mean:

- The automatic lighting has failed.
- The corridor is out of public service.
- The corridor is temporarily depressurized, flooded, structurally monitored, or used for freight.
- Its sensors do not recognize occupancy.
- It connects to lower levels not shown on public maps.
- Its apparent entrance is not its current functional entrance.
- It is a place where people have learned, through experience, not to be alone.

If you find yourself at an unlit corridor:

1. Stop at the threshold.
2. Do not shine a powerful light deep inside unless instructed; you may interfere with signaling, animals, or workers.
3. Look for posted markings, door seals, colored strips, or a nearby call station.
4. Return to a lit area.
5. Find a resident, attendant, or maintenance worker.
6. If you must enter under authorized guidance, go in a group, tell someone your intended route, and carry approved lights.

Do not let curiosity override local caution. You are not there to prove that the rule is superstition.

## Indoor Rain Protocol

If rain begins indoors, do exactly what residents do.

The usual instruction is: open all internal doors and wait for the water to choose a route.

This will feel counterintuitive. You may want to protect possessions, block a threshold, move furniture, or redirect runoff into a drain. Do not do so unless a resident or building steward tells you to. The local water systems may be tied to pressure balancing, condensation capture, contamination routing, filtration, or other processes that are not obvious at room scale.

Prepare by keeping your belongings off the floor whenever possible, using waterproof bags, and asking your host where to place electronics during a rain event.

When indoor rain begins:

- Stay calm.
- Follow the residents’ lead.
- Open internal doors if instructed.
- Keep clear of drains, brass gratings, wall seams, cable channels, and threshold joints.
- Do not touch unfamiliar runoff with bare skin.
- Do not drink it, even if it appears clean.
- Keep children and animals from trying to dam or scoop it.
- Do not cross water that has passed through multiple rooms, levels, or thresholds without local advice.
- Note the route it takes, but do not interfere.

If the water changes color, gives off a smell, beads strangely, flows uphill, carries metallic foam, or seems to hesitate at a doorway, treat it as a municipal hazard rather than a spectacle. Notify someone local.

## Weather and Moisture

The weather is engineered, irregular, and district-specific. A clear sky over one terrace does not mean a dry walk to the next. You may encounter controlled vertical rain, fog, condensation, dry grit, and frost in a single day.

Ask each morning:

- Which districts are scheduled for rain or mist?
- Are the upper routes open?
- Is dust suppression active on the slopes?
- Are any bridge tubes being dehumidified or closed?
- Is there a frost warning near the chimney fields?
- Are the rooflands under brightness advisory?

Carry water even during rain. The cold drizzle may not alleviate dehydration, and dry air can return quickly.

On wet days, assume every rail, stair edge, plastic bridge tube, ceramic panel, and metal grate is slippery. Use handrails. Do not rush for elevators. Their silence makes them easy to misjudge, and their doors may close more gently than you expect but still with industrial force.

## Elevation, Rooflands, and the New Hills

The upper valley is not a scenic overlook in the ordinary sense. It is exposed, cold, bright, and structurally alive.

Do not hike the rooflands or the new hills casually. Travel with a local guide or an officially marked route, especially beyond the final housing terraces. The surface may look broad, flat, and solid while concealing expansion joints, vent access, heat differentials, moving seams, restricted industrial zones, or slopes that have changed since the last public survey.

Remember:

- A staircase that was level may now tilt.
- Water may run uphill along certain seams.
- A handrail may have been installed because the ground moved, not because it is a tourist convenience.
- Fresh creases in ceramic panels are not souvenirs or climbing features.
- Do not step across black expansion joints.
- Do not camp beside chimneys, vents, or white roof fields without explicit permission.
- Do not approach a vent tower simply because goats are nearby. Goats know where they can stand; you do not.

The far ridge moving by “the width of a hand” is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to respect closure notices and survey markings. If surveyors with bright poles are working, give them room. Do not cross their lines, move their markers, or insist on a photograph from the other side of a cordon.

## Animals

The animals are not decorative. They are integrated into the valley’s ecology and maintenance systems.

### Pigeons
The pigeons are large, numerous, and likely accustomed to people. Do not feed them unless your host says it is acceptable. Their reflective throat bands may be visible in low light; avoid shining lights directly into roosts. When a maintenance siren flashes, expect them to rise suddenly in large sheets. Cover your eyes, keep your mouth closed if dust is kicked up, and do not run beneath a moving flock.

### Foxes
The foxes use cable corridors after dimming. Do not pursue, corner, feed, or attempt to remove ear tags. Keep food sealed. If one follows you, it may be hoping for scraps, but it may also be using your route as cover. Let it pass.

### Goats
The goats are working animals, even if they appear feral. Their lack of bells is deliberate. Do not startle them with noise, flash photography, or sudden movement. Give them the right of way on terraces and stairways. Keep hands away from mouths and horns. Do not touch insulation fungus or moss near them; it may be exactly what they are there to manage.

### Blind White Fish
Do not fish, feed, or wade in lower canals. These are water-treatment environments. The fish may be healthy, but the water is not recreational.

### Chimney Swifts
Treat chimney swifts as protected wildlife and potentially as a matter of civic reverence. Never enter an exhaust stack, throw objects into vents, shine strong lights into nests, collect shed material, or use flight-capable devices near them. Their slow movement can make them appear harmless or unreal. That is not a reason to approach.

## Social Customs and Sensitive Subjects

The valley’s people are likely proud of their homes and tired of outsiders calling it artificial, sterile, dystopian, miraculous, or unfinished. It is all of those things only from a distance. To residents it is a city with markets, schools, shrines, laundry, work schedules, animal care, neighbors, and rent.

Useful habits:

- Ask before photographing people, shrines, maintenance marks, children, animals, and control panels.
- Never photograph an obsolete control-panel shrine as if it were a quaint curiosity.
- Do not touch offerings, switches, labels, cables, or preserved indicator lights.
- Compliment practical beauty: bridge views, garden care, clean drainage, repaired stairs, well-kept planters, good market food, a successful roof seal.
- Ask what a building is used for now, not what it “used to be.”
- If someone says a purpose is forgotten, accept that. Do not press them to speculate for your entertainment.
- Use “the hills,” “the upper slopes,” or whatever local term your host uses. Avoid declaring whether they are engineering failures, mountains, or sacred entities.

Be particularly cautious around unfinished buildings. Do not joke about naming one after yourself, a companion, or a deceased person. Do not scratch names into wet material, tag walls, attach plaques, or leave personalized markers. The rule against naming unfinished buildings after people should be treated seriously regardless of whether its basis is structural, legal, spiritual, or all three.

## Staying Overnight

Choose licensed accommodation in a stable, occupied district. Ask for:

- A room away from major freight shafts and pump-house walls.
- Clarification on indoor-rain procedures.
- The nearest lit route to an exit.
- Whether dimming affects room lights, heat, water, lifts, or communications.
- A building steward or night contact.
- Advice about opening windows or vents; do not assume outside air is always preferable.

During dimming, do not wander simply because the city becomes beautiful and quiet. The reduced light changes route safety, animal movement, work schedules, and the apparent depth of open spaces. Stay on familiar, lit paths. If you must travel, go with someone, tell another person where you are going, and avoid cable corridors, roof edges, freight platforms, and unlit pedestrian tubes.

Earplugs may help you sleep, but keep one ear available if your lodging relies on spoken instructions, door calls, or subtle building alarms.

## Food and Water

Eat from busy markets, resident-recommended stalls, schools, cooperative kitchens, or established canteens. The city’s food systems may be unfamiliar but are likely highly regulated where they are visible.

Ask about:

- Water source and filtration status.
- Whether produce came from nutrient troughs, sealed vats, terrace gardens, or imported freight.
- Allergen risks in processed compost-grown foods or animal products.
- Whether a dish is intended for humans rather than working animals.
- Whether food containers are returnable or part of a local material-recovery system.

Do not assume that fruit from the columnar trees is edible. It is specifically collected and traded rather than eaten. Treat it as a material object, not a snack.

Carry your own sealed water until you understand local refill norms. If a local offers water, accept politely if you can do so safely, but ask whether it is drinking water, wash water, condenser water, process water, or ceremonial water. In this valley, those distinctions matter.

## Emergency Procedure

If you become lost, injured, trapped by weather, or separated from your group:

1. Move toward a lit, occupied, public area.
2. Avoid unlit corridors, freight shafts, roof edges, tunnels, and drainage channels.
3. Find a market, school, staffed elevator, shrine attendant, workshop, animal handler, or transit platform.
4. State clearly that you are a visitor and name your host or lodging.
5. Do not attempt to “shortcut” through service routes.
6. If rain begins indoors, follow local protocol rather than fleeing through random doors.
7. If you see flashing maintenance signals, stop and observe what residents do.
8. If the ground, stairs, or railings seem to have shifted, turn back rather than testing the route.

For structural concerns—new crack, strange tilt, sudden warmth from a surface, unusual vibration, water flowing against expectation—report it. Residents may respond calmly, even casually. That calm is not indifference; it is often the product of established procedures.

## What Not to Bring Back

Do not remove:

- Chimney swift nesting material.
- Animal tags, reflective scales, feathers, bones, or fur.
- Pieces of ceramic panel, exposed metal mesh, slag, lichen, moss, insulation fungus, or white aggregate.
- Water from canals, drains, indoor-rain events, or condensation systems.
- Old control-panel parts or shrine offerings.
- Survey markers, warning strips, cable fragments, or “loose” machine parts.
- Fruit from the ornamental trees unless it is explicitly traded to you as an authorized object.

Even if such items are physically harmless, they may be biologically, legally, ritually, or structurally significant. Some may also be difficult to explain at interworld customs.

## The Right State of Mind

Go slowly. Let the city establish its scale before you try to understand it.

The valley will tempt you to make grand conclusions: that it is a machine pretending to be a landscape, a landscape growing inside a machine, a manufactured afterlife, an industrial organism, a forgotten project still completing itself. You do not need to decide. Ordinary life continues there regardless: children trade polymer fruit, foxes follow cables, laundry dries beneath mirrored awnings, goats eat what they were bred to eat, residents relocate a market a terrace downhill, and surveyors measure the new hills.

Your job as a visitor is not to solve the valley.

It is to keep your footing, respect the routes, listen when the locals lower their voices, and never assume that something which looks still has stopped moving.
