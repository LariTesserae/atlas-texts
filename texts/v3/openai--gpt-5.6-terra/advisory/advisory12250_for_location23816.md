## Prepare for a city that is maintained, not settled

This is not a place where the ground is inert background. The city is functional, inhabited, and in many ways civilized—but every resident lives in an ongoing agreement with moving stone, migrating water, uncertain weather, and occasional intrusions that local people treat as municipal problems rather than mysteries.

An ordinary human can visit safely if they arrive humble, well-equipped, and willing to obey rules that may not be fully explained.

Do not go expecting a rugged wilderness excursion, but do not treat it as an ordinary industrial city either. Its primary danger is not violence or catastrophe. It is cumulative misjudgment: one bad choice of footwear, one ignored barrier, one attempt to take a shortcut through a tunnel, one decision to “investigate” something locals have learned to leave alone.

The city rewards patience.

## Before departure

### Learn the current municipal conditions

Conditions change enough that maps, routes, and even district names may become outdated. Before entering the basin, obtain the most recent version of:

- The municipal slope and transit bulletin
- A current “safe pedestrian routes” map
- The weather and inversion forecast
- A list of active construction, closure, and subsidence zones
- The daily cable-car and freight-tram schedule
- The present status of the reservoir, if travel near the eastern waterworks is anticipated
- Any notices about unusual animals, second-moon protocols, tunnel closures, or water anomalies

Do not rely on maps from even a week earlier without checking them against posted notices and a local resident’s advice. Bring a paper map anyway. Electronic mapping may struggle with elevation changes, underground passages, fog, wet infrastructure, and streets that are technically still present but no longer meet the ground at the expected angle.

If you have a multiversal arrival mechanism, do not materialize directly inside the city center, in a tunnel, near blue stone, on a bridge, or above an apparent open terrace. Use an established arrival platform if one exists. If not, choose a broad, inhabited transit yard or regulated checkpoint at the lower edge of town, ideally in daylight and under local supervision.

### Leave a detailed itinerary with someone elsewhere

Because the city has many tunnels, shafts, weather pockets, and routes that can change without drama, arrange routine check-ins with an off-site contact.

Your itinerary should include:

- Lodging address and alternate lodging
- Planned districts and travel dates
- A contact at your hotel, hostel, workplace, or local guide service
- A clear rule for when missed communication becomes an emergency
- The identities and locations of any multiversal extraction resources available to you

Do not assume emergency responders from another world can navigate this place quickly. They may need local cooperation, and local responders will be busy dealing with practical terrain issues first.

### Get travel and medical coverage that explicitly includes geological instability

Ordinary travel insurance is unlikely to cover a city that shifts, a reservoir that may relocate, or injuries sustained in “atmospheric inversion conditions.” Ensure coverage includes:

- Landslide and structural movement
- Cable-car and industrial-transit incidents
- Tunnel evacuation
- Cold exposure and damp-related illness
- Respiratory irritation from mineral water, foundry smoke, and mold
- Multiversal extraction or repatriation
- Delays caused by municipal containment orders

If you take medication, carry more than you need. Deliveries can be delayed by weather, cable disruptions, blocked roads, or a newly assertive piece of mountain.

## What to pack

Pack as if visiting a cold, wet, vertical industrial town with unreliable daylight. Favor durable, unremarkable equipment over futuristic or flashy technology. Locals are accustomed to specialized gear, but conspicuous tools may attract attention from curious animals, suspicious officials, or people who would like to know why your equipment does not behave correctly near shifting geology.

### Essential clothing

Bring layers. The temperature may feel moderate in a workshop, sharply cold on an exposed bridge, and clammy in a subterranean corridor all within an hour.

Pack:

- Waterproof, insulated boots with aggressive non-slip soles
- Spare dry socks, preferably wool or synthetic rather than cotton
- Waterproof trousers and a genuinely waterproof outer shell
- Warm midlayers: wool, fleece, or insulated synthetic garments
- Gloves suitable for gripping wet railings and metal stairways
- A warm hat and neck covering
- High-visibility outer layer or reflective vest, especially if you expect to use tram yards, industrial terraces, or foggy pedestrian routes
- Clothing you do not mind smelling faintly of iron, oil, damp stone, and wet wool

Avoid long loose scarves, dangling straps, or coats that can catch in tram doors, cable equipment, escalators, industrial fans, or narrow stairwells.

### Carry every day

Your day pack should include:

- Headlamp plus a small backup light, both with spare batteries
- Paper map, pencil, and waterproof notebook
- Whistle
- Fully charged phone or communicator, protected in a waterproof case
- Power bank
- Compact first-aid kit, including blister treatment and bandages
- Emergency foil blanket or compact bivy sack
- Respirator or good particulate mask for tunnel dust, industrial fumes, mold, and unexpected smoke
- Refillable water bottle with a high-quality filter rated for sediment and chemical contamination
- Electrolyte tablets
- High-calorie shelf-stable food
- Hand sanitizer and soap sheets
- Small length of bright marking tape—not to mark tunnels for exploration, but to make yourself visible in fog or to mark your location if instructed by rescue personnel
- A small amount of local currency in waterproof storage
- Copies of identification and insurance documents

Treat all local tap water as variable unless your host confirms the source is currently safe. Pale green or black water may be normal for certain systems, but “normal” does not necessarily mean suitable for an unfamiliar digestive system. Prefer sealed water, recently tested municipal sources, or filtered water approved by locals.

### Do not bring or casually deploy

Avoid bringing equipment that could create anchoring, drilling, cutting, surveying, or electromagnetic complications without permission. In particular:

- Do not bring climbing anchors intended for rock placement.
- Do not use pitons, bolts, stakes, or ground screws.
- Do not test stone with hammers.
- Do not fly recreational drones in fog, around cranes, cableways, birds, or upper cloud layers.
- Do not set up a tent or temporary shelter without asking about the ground beneath it.
- Do not use rope to secure yourself to exposed rock unless local safety personnel approve the anchor point.

The rule against anchoring anything to blue stone should be interpreted broadly: do not touch, chip, lean on, sample, build against, or tie anything to stone that appears unusually blue, blue-gray, wetly luminous, or blue beneath a surface fracture. If uncertain, assume it is blue stone and ask.

## On arrival

### Find a local orientation point immediately

Before sightseeing, identify:

1. Your nearest staffed transit station  
2. Your nearest public shelter or municipal hall  
3. Your nearest public bath, clinic, or hospital  
4. The safe route back to your lodging  
5. A shop where you can buy current maps, food, and weatherproof supplies  
6. The district office or slope-management department  

Ask the staff at your lodging three questions:

- “Which routes are closed today?”
- “Where should I go if the fog gets heavy?”
- “What should I do if I hear an alarm, see unusual water, or observe a second moon?”

Phrase your questions plainly. Do not ask locals to prove that the rules make sense. They have heard outsiders do that before, and it suggests you intend to become someone else’s preventable emergency.

### Book lodging in a newer or actively maintained building

Choose a hotel, boarding house, or rental with:

- Posted inspection certificates
- Clearly marked emergency exits
- A staffed front desk or resident caretaker
- Reliable indoor heating and drying space
- A room not directly adjacent to a major service tunnel, heavy rail line, or unstable retaining wall
- A room above the ground floor if local flooding is a possibility, but not in a very high upper story if evacuation routes are uncertain
- Visible exterior bracing that appears maintained rather than improvised

Do not be alarmed by braces, rollers, expansion gaps, angled floors, or wall gauges. These are generally signs that a building is being cared for. Be more wary of buildings that insist they are perfectly straight, permanently stable, or exempt from municipal monitoring.

When you enter your room, locate the exits. Note whether the floor slopes, where objects rest naturally, and whether doors swing shut or open by themselves. This is not paranoia; it is useful orientation in a place where familiar geometry may be slightly negotiable.

Keep footwear, coat, documents, light, and water accessible beside your bed. Do not sleep in a room with an unlatched window if vines, fog, birds, or unexplained dampness are entering it.

## Moving through the city

### Do not rush

The city is vertical, wet, and crowded with working infrastructure. Leave far more time than a map suggests. A route that appears to be a ten-minute walk may involve:

- Several hundred stairs
- A lift queue
- A bridge closure
- A slow-moving freight crossing
- Fog reducing visibility
- A goat occupation of a retaining wall
- A temporary diversion around fresh cracks or water flow

If you are late, accept that you are late. Running on slick stairs or trying to cross a tram corridor outside designated points is not worth it.

### Stay on maintained routes

Use posted walkways, municipal stairs, staffed elevators, marked bridges, and passenger transit. Do not explore:

- Unlit service tunnels
- Abandoned aqueducts
- Storm drains
- Ventilation towers
- Railway sidings
- The margins of quarry fields
- Construction platforms
- Reservoir access points
- Unmarked hillside trails above the city

A disused-looking door may lead to a live utility corridor. A harmless-looking stream may be a surface expression of a much larger underground route. A gap in fencing may be evidence that the fencing moved, not an invitation to pass through it.

### Make peace with vertical transport

Freight trams and cable cars are vital infrastructure, not scenic rides by default. Use passenger-approved cabins and platforms. If you are permitted to ride a cargo line, follow crew instructions exactly.

While using lifts, cable cars, bridges, and trams:

- Keep hands away from cables, doors, and moving joints.
- Do not lean over railings in fog.
- Do not disembark until staff say to do so.
- Keep your bag secured and your center of gravity low.
- If a vehicle stops unexpectedly, remain inside unless directed otherwise.
- Do not assume “up” is safer than “down”; in this city, hazards are distributed vertically.

A view may disappear into cloud halfway through a crossing. This is normal. Do not panic, and do not try to photograph outside through an open maintenance panel.

## Follow the posted rules literally

The rules are practical in the sense that they keep people alive. Their mechanisms do not need to be understood before they are obeyed.

### “Do not follow running water uphill.”

If you see water moving uphill, do not approach it, trace it, photograph it closely, or treat it as an unusual natural phenomenon. Turn around and take the nearest known route away from the flow. Inform municipal personnel when possible.

Do not let curiosity override the rule. Water in this city has more routes than the maps admit, and an uphill flow may indicate that you have misunderstood the direction of the terrain, entered an unstable hydrological zone, or encountered something for which locals have learned a very concise warning.

### “If you hear your name from an unlit service tunnel, report it before sleeping.”

Do not answer the voice. Do not shine your light down the tunnel in an attempt to identify the speaker. Do not enter the tunnel, even if you believe the voice belongs to a friend, family member, colleague, or someone who should not possibly be there.

Proceed to a staffed municipal station, your lodging desk, police office, clinic, or designated reporting point. Report the time, place, exact words heard, and whether anyone else heard it. Then follow their instructions before going to sleep.

If you are traveling with companions, agree in advance that no one will respond to a voice from an unlit tunnel without visual confirmation from a safe, staffed location.

### “All livestock must be accounted for during periods of atmospheric inversion.”

This may not seem relevant to a visitor, but it is. During inversion notices:

- Stay indoors or within the district recommended by authorities.
- Do not interfere with goats, dogs, birds, or unfamiliar animals.
- Do not let pets roam.
- Do not agree to watch, transport, feed, or “temporarily hold” an animal unless you understand its owner and the relevant municipal procedure.
- Keep windows shut unless instructed otherwise.

If an apparently ownerless animal enters your lodging, do not assume it is a stray. Notify the caretaker or municipal animal office.

### “In the event of a second moon, remain indoors until municipal clearance.”

Treat this as a shelter-in-place order, not a rare astronomical opportunity.

If you see a second moon—or anything that could plausibly be interpreted as one—go indoors immediately. Close curtains or shutters if available. Turn on ordinary interior lights. Stay away from windows, balconies, roof access, open courtyards, and reflective water. Do not attempt observation through binoculars, cameras, telescopes, mirrors, or recording devices unless municipal authorities specifically announce it is safe.

Account for everyone in your group. Do not go looking for missing people until clearance is issued, unless you are trained local rescue personnel acting under command.

Keep enough food, water, medicine, lighting, and warmth in your lodging for at least two days.

### “Never anchor a structure to blue stone.”

As a traveler, translate this into: never attach anything to blue stone.

Do not lean a bicycle against it. Do not tie a dog to it. Do not set a tripod on a blue outcrop. Do not secure a hammock, tent line, camera mount, or climbing rope to it. Do not take a souvenir chip.

If your lodging, vehicle, or equipment is attached to something that becomes visibly blue, notify building management and move away calmly.

## Weather, water, and air

### Fog is an operational hazard

Fog can make a familiar block become a maze of wet facades, bridge ends, construction barriers, and open drops. When fog thickens:

- Stop trying to navigate by distant landmarks.
- Use numbered buildings, street signage, and known transit stations.
- Avoid isolated shortcuts.
- Walk with a companion if possible.
- Make yourself visible with reflective clothing or a light.
- Stay clear of roads used by trams and industrial vehicles.
- Enter a staffed shop, station, or public building if disoriented.

Do not follow sounds alone. Tram bells, machinery, barking dogs, voices, and water may be distorted by the basin.

### Assume every surface is slick

Railings, steps, paving stones, bridges, platforms, and metal grates may be wet even if it has not rained where you are standing. Use handrails. Test each step. Keep one hand free. Do not walk while looking through a camera or at a phone.

If you are unsteady on stairs, use lifts and trams rather than trying to prove you can manage the terrain. This is a city that respects practical caution.

### Be cautious with water

The local water is mineral-heavy and comes from complex systems. Do not drink directly from:

- Puddles
- Streams
- Roof runoff
- Tunnel seepage
- Decorative fountains
- Condensation off pipes
- Reservoir margins
- Black, green, iridescent, or unusually clear flows

Avoid bathing in unregulated pools, even if they seem warm or locals refer to them casually. Public baths are preferable because their water is monitored and their staff understand local conditions.

If water begins appearing where it should not—through pavement cracks, stairwells, walls, elevator shafts, or uphill streets—leave the immediate area by a known route and report it. Do not try to identify the source yourself.

## Animals and other living complications

The animal life is not necessarily hostile, but it does not always fit your expectations.

### Goats

Municipal goats are infrastructure-adjacent wildlife. They may be tagged, but that does not make them tame.

- Do not feed them.
- Do not touch their tags.
- Do not try to move them from ledges, roofs, or rail lines.
- Keep bags, clothing, food, and laundry secured.
- Give them right of way, particularly on stairs and narrow paths.
- Report a goat inside a hazardous zone rather than attempting a rescue.

They are often calmer around collapses than humans are. Do not take their calmness as evidence that an area is safe.

### Foxes, birds, moths, salamanders, and unfamiliar creatures

Do not handle animals found near industrial runoff, tunnels, ventilation infrastructure, or reservoir pipes. Their appearance may reflect contamination, adaptation, or origins you are not equipped to diagnose.

Avoid touching:

- Silvered foxes
- Pale tunnel moths
- Amphibians in conduits
- Eel-like animals in pipes
- Roof-pool fish
- Antlered animals on tram lines
- Very large condor-like birds

The antlered animals deserve special caution. If they are on the tracks, remain inside a vehicle or behind barriers. Do not approach to take photographs, offer food, or determine where they came from. If transit stops for them, accept the delay.

### Bowls of water outside doors

Do not empty, clean, move, refill, or mock these bowls without asking the resident. They are part of a local custom with reasons you may not be given. Step around them carefully. Do not drink from them.

If you are staying in an old neighborhood, ask your host whether you are expected to maintain one. If so, use the water and placement they specify, and do not improvise additions.

## Social preparation

Residents are likely practical, technically skilled, and accustomed to visitors who mistake their routines for superstition. The respectful posture is simple: assume local knowledge is knowledge.

### Good habits

- Ask before entering tunnels, yards, rooftop gardens, workshops, or construction sites.
- Thank workers for route advice.
- Accept delays without demanding certainty.
- Learn district names, building-angle landmarks, and local transit etiquette.
- Carry small cash for market purchases, maps, repairs, and warm food.
- Support local guides if going beyond ordinary commercial districts.
- Be honest if you are unfamiliar with industrial settings or high-altitude walking.

### Things not to say casually

Avoid dismissive comments such as:

- “That cannot happen.”
- “It is only water.”
- “The map says this is a street.”
- “The animal probably escaped from somewhere.”
- “I just want to see what is in there.”
- “Your city should really fix this.”

The city is already fixing itself constantly. Its residents know better than anyone that “fixed” is a temporary condition.

### Photography and recording

Ask before photographing workers, private braces, municipal notices, tunnel entrances, animals, waterworks, or any place where people leave bowls, charms, measurement marks, or repair materials.

Never use flash in tunnels, around birds, near machinery, or during strange atmospheric events. Do not livestream a second moon, anomalous water, or a name-call from a tunnel. In addition to being rude and dangerous, broadcasting location-specific anomalies can draw unprepared people into danger.

## Food and health

Eat in established market stalls, canteens, and busy restaurants. The local foods—mushrooms, goat dairy, trout, herbs, preserved foods, and industrial-town staples—may be excellent, but start slowly if your body is unfamiliar with the region.

Use extra caution with:

- Mushrooms grown in ventilation shafts
- Luminous lichen products
- Fish from the reservoir
- Unlabeled herbal preparations
- Milk or cheese sold directly from informal goat keepers
- Water-based soups or teas made from unverified sources

Ask whether a dish is intended for visitors and whether its ingredients are from the current season and current water system. This is not an insult; it is sensible.

The altitude, cold damp, and relentless walking can exhaust an otherwise healthy traveler. Plan quiet indoor intervals each day. Dry your boots nightly. Change out of wet socks promptly. Watch for headaches, dizziness, shortness of breath, confusion, and unusual fatigue. These may indicate altitude effects, dehydration, cold exposure, or simply that you have spent too long navigating stairs in fog.

## Emergency behavior

### If the ground begins moving

Most movement is slow, but you may feel a stronger vibration, hear grinding, or notice new cracks.

- Do not run blindly.
- Move away from retaining walls, exposed slopes, crane loads, cable pylons, and old masonry.
- Do not shelter under bridges or overhangs unless directed.
- Follow local evacuation signs and staff instructions.
- If indoors, move to the designated safe area of the building rather than immediately using stairs or elevators.
- Avoid windows and unsecured shelves.
- Once outside, do not return for belongings.

Remember: the safest route may not be downhill. Use official direction, not instinct.

### If trapped in a lift, tram, or cable car

Stay inside. Use the emergency communication equipment. Conserve phone battery. Keep warm and calm. Do not force doors or climb out unless trained crews explicitly direct you.

In a city built around industrial transport, rescue systems exist—but crews may need time because they are navigating weather, terrain, and moving infrastructure.

### If you become lost

Go toward light, staffing, and established transit—not toward water, sounds, or apparently open downhill space. A lit workshop, station, public bath, market, or inhabited building is safer than an empty shortcut.

Tell someone immediately that you are lost. Do not be embarrassed. Visitors get disoriented; locals expect it.

### If something seems impossible

If you see uphill water, an animal with no plausible origin, a new ridge where there should be open ground, a familiar person in an impossible location, or a second moon: do not investigate alone.

Withdraw to a populated, lit place. Report what you saw in practical terms:

- Location
- Time
- Direction of travel
- Weather
- Whether anyone else was present
- Whether it moved toward or away from you
- Whether you touched anything

Do not embellish. This is not because people will disbelieve you. It is because precision is more useful than drama.

## The best mindset

Visit with an engineer’s humility and a guest’s manners.

The city is not asking you to conquer it, decode it, or prove that you are braver than its residents. It is asking you to notice signs, respect barriers, keep your feet dry, let freight move at freight speed, and accept that a route can change while still being part of ordinary life.

Buy the current map. Listen to the tram workers. Eat somewhere warm. Keep your headlamp charged. Learn the angle of the building where you are staying. When fog closes over the upper terraces, go inside rather than seeking a better view.

Most importantly: do not confuse stability with safety. In this city, the safe things are often the things designed to flex.
