## Preparation for the Underfrost Garden

This is not a ruin, cave, greenhouse, or ordinary ecosystem. Treat it as a young living environment that is still establishing its own anatomy. You will be entering something closer to an organ than a landscape: responsive, cold-maintaining, acoustically aware, and inclined to incorporate foreign material into itself.

The chief danger is not overt predation. It is being interpreted as warmth, debris, or usable matter.

Your objective should be a short, low-impact visit. Do not plan to camp there, sleep there, bring open flame, or attempt extensive sample collection.

---

## Before Entry

### Go healthy, clean, and unaccompanied by biological clutter

Do not enter with an untreated wound, fever, infection, or heavy perspiration. The chamber is saturated with moisture and organic activity; anything shed from your body may be taken up rapidly.

Before crossing the threshold:

- Clean mud, seeds, animal fur, food residue, and loose fibers from boots and clothing.
- Remove jewelry that can snag, corrode, or become cold-welded to wet plant matter.
- Avoid perfumes, smoke, insect repellent sprays, and volatile solvents. The vegetation appears capable of sorting airborne particles extremely precisely; strong chemical signatures may provoke an unknown filtering response.
- Do not bring food into the chamber. Pack it in a sealed external container, ideally left outside.
- Tie back hair and cover it. Shed hair is exactly the kind of material the root-felt may readily incorporate.
- Use the toilet beforehand. Do not assume waste can be disposed of safely inside. The chamber may accept it, but that is not consent to contaminate it.

If you are carrying disease organisms, spores, insects, or seeds from another world, your risk is not only to yourself. The chamber is biologically inventive. It may assimilate, reproduce, or weaponize an introduced organism in ways no one can predict.

A quarantine protocol is wise: dedicated outer clothing, sterilizable tools, and a full decontamination period after departure.

## Clothing and Personal Equipment

### Dress for wet cold, but do not overheat

The garden actively maintains temperatures near freezing and treats heat as an intrusion. This creates an awkward balance: you need insulation to avoid hypothermia, but a heavily heated body may attract insects and cold-drawing spores.

Wear:

- A moisture-wicking base layer.
- A thin insulating middle layer, preferably wool or synthetic fleece.
- A waterproof but breathable outer shell.
- Waterproof gloves with good tactile sensitivity.
- Insulated waterproof boots with smooth, nonaggressive soles.
- A hood or close-fitting cap.
- Eye protection: clear sealed goggles are preferable, because spores and cold mist may irritate the eyes.
- A particulate respirator or filter mask rated for biological aerosols. A full-face respirator is better if you have one that does not produce loud mechanical noise.

Avoid:

- Battery-heated clothing.
- Chemical hand warmers.
- Heated boots.
- Open flame.
- Hot drink thermoses carried inside.
- Large active electronics with fans, bright lamps, or noisy cooling systems.
- Crampons, spikes, machetes, pruning tools, or anything that looks useful for cutting your way through roots.

A modestly insulated human will still radiate substantial heat in this place. You cannot eliminate that. Your goal is to make your thermal signature stable rather than intense and fluctuating.

### Carry a “do not get incorporated” kit

Useful equipment includes:

- A rescue line anchored outside the chamber, with a smooth, inert sheath. Do not anchor it to living roots if you can avoid it.
- A small, manually operated cutting tool for emergency extraction only—not for clearing paths.
- A waterproof signal whistle or tone instrument.
- A set of simple tuning forks, a small flute, or a phone/tablet containing pure sustained tones, provided it can be run quietly and in airplane mode.
- Waterproof notebook and pencil. Avoid loose paper.
- A non-heating light source with adjustable dimness and red/green options. The chamber produces its own low illumination; use yours only as needed.
- A compact emergency bivy bag and dry clothing left near the entrance rather than carried deep in.
- Sterile saline eyewash and sealed first-aid supplies.
- A sealed specimen container only if you have explicit reason and permission to take samples. Assume that you do not.
- A thermal camera or simple infrared thermometer if available. It may help identify warm vascular channels and track the larger ceiling animal.
- A passive recording device for sound, if its indicator lights and operational noise can be disabled.

Bring a companion if possible, but do not bring a large group. More bodies mean more heat, more noise, more exhaled moisture, and more chances that the garden will respond dramatically. Two or three quiet, trained people are safer than one; ten people may become an ecological event.

## The Most Important Rule: Manage Heat

The garden does not seem hostile to warm animals in the way a predator is hostile. It simply corrects local warmth.

Its probable sequence is:

1. Heat-sensitive velvet creatures notice you.
2. They gather along your warmest areas: boots, joints, neck, respirator exhaust, backpack straps.
3. Spores are released.
4. Fine moisture films form on you and begin drawing away heat.
5. You become colder, wetter, slower, and more appealing to the chamber’s general recycling processes.

The risk is hypothermia, immobilization, and gradual entanglement—not necessarily attack.

To reduce this risk:

- Move steadily but slowly. Avoid running, panic, and exertion.
- Vent clothing before you sweat.
- Do not sit or lie on the living floor.
- Do not press bare hands into the ground, no matter how tempting the responsive moisture seems.
- Keep hands gloved and off walls.
- Do not huddle against root masses for warmth. They are part of the heat-transfer system.
- If small white creatures cluster on you, do not slap or crush them. Brush them away gently with a soft tool, then retreat toward the entrance while maintaining a calm, regular sound signal.
- If you see a fog emerging from walls or ceiling tissue, put on respiratory protection immediately and leave. Do not wait to see what it does.

If you begin shivering uncontrollably, lose dexterity, become confused, or find frost forming on clothing where no external frost should be, treat that as a medical emergency. Withdraw at once.

## Learn to Speak to It With Sound

The chamber is navigated by resonance. Silence allows passages to close; sound invites vegetation to rearrange.

This does **not** mean “make as much noise as possible.” Sudden loud sound may open light reservoirs, activate bladder-flowers, or provoke broad systemic responses. The goal is predictable, polite, repeatable sound.

### Best sound practices

Use a low-volume repeated tone:

- A sustained hum.
- A simple two- or three-note phrase.
- A tuning fork struck softly at regular intervals.
- A quiet whistle pattern.

Choose one pattern before entering and keep it consistent. Think of it as announcing yourself and asking the chamber to preserve a route.

A practical method:

1. At the entrance, make your chosen tone three times.
2. Wait and observe for movement in roots, changes in glow, or shifts in air flow.
3. Advance only along a route that becomes less dense or more illuminated.
4. Repeat the tone every few meters or whenever the route begins knitting shut.
5. Keep one person responsible for sounding, one for watching footing and ceiling, and one for monitoring the exit path.

Do not shout unless you need an emergency response. Loud cries may indeed reveal light or routes, but they may also open all nearby bladder-flowers and summon a chamber-wide reaction.

Avoid random music, speech, laughter, or competing tones. The garden may treat them as conflicting instructions.

### If the plants begin closing around you

Do not cut immediately.

First:

- Stop moving.
- Make the same tone you used to enter.
- Reduce your body heat by opening outer layers slightly if safe.
- Backtrack slowly toward the last open space while maintaining the tone.
- Have your companion sound from behind you or near the entrance, creating an acoustic “direction” for the chamber to follow.

If roots begin fastening to clothing or equipment, remove the affected outer layer if you can do so without injury. It is better to abandon a glove, boot cover, or pack strap than to turn the event into a struggle. Struggle introduces heat, vibration, damage, and more material for the garden to interpret.

## Do Not Treat the Floor as Ground

The wet plant mat is not stable soil. It contains clear burrowers, roots, water channels, mineral folds, eggs, and living tissues that may shift under pressure.

Walk as though crossing a saturated roof.

- Test each step gently with a smooth probe.
- Prefer visible root braids and darker, denser-looking mats over glossy translucent sheets or bulging wet folds.
- Avoid pale egg clusters, crystal knots, and areas where the floor seems to breathe or rise.
- Never jump.
- Keep your center of gravity low.
- Use trekking poles only if they have broad, blunt ends and only sparingly; puncturing the floor is poor etiquette and may release water, spores, or animals.
- Do not kneel unless absolutely necessary.

If a foot sinks, do not wrench it free. Pause. Make your navigation tone. Shift weight gradually to the other foot. The material may loosen if it recognizes reduced pressure. Pulling hard may cause the mat to close around the boot or rupture something underneath.

## The Ceiling Animal

The large soft-bodied being in the overhead foliage deserves extreme caution. It is not necessarily dangerous, but it is clearly central to the chamber’s cycles.

Signs it is near:

- Leaves folding outward ahead of an unseen movement.
- A static feeling in hair, clothing, or exposed metal.
- Frost appearing on nearby foliage.
- A progressive deepening of the ambient hum.
- The long bell-like call.

If you hear the call:

1. Stop.
2. Lower your profile without touching the floor or walls more than necessary.
3. Do not shine a light upward.
4. Do not make loud sounds.
5. Do not touch metal objects to one another; static discharge may be harmful or interpreted as an alarm.
6. Wait until the insects resume moving and the pale fluid in the walls subsides.
7. Then resume your quiet navigation signal and continue or retreat.

The animal grips through static tension. Conductive gear, wet clothing, and metal frames may become relevant in ways you do not want to discover. Keep electronics insulated and do not carry long exposed antennae, poles, or wires overhead.

If it descends or the ceiling foliage starts to open around you, leave immediately by the route you entered. Do not attempt contact, photography at close range, feeding, or collection of shed frost.

## The Central Mineral Mound

Do not approach the blue-gray mound closely.

It appears to be the chamber’s youngest geological feature and perhaps its organizing center: a place where mineral, root, water, animal behavior, and resonance are converging. To the garden, this may be equivalent to a heart, seed, embryo, reactor, shrine, or all of these at once.

Maintain a generous perimeter—at least several body lengths, more if the surrounding roots appear dense or animals are clustered there.

Do not:

- Touch it.
- Chip it.
- Pour water on it.
- Place instruments directly against it.
- Record or reproduce its deepest tone at high volume.
- Stand over its fissure.
- Leave anything near it.
- Attempt to “help” the young stone grow or prevent it from growing.

The tone enters through bone and teeth. Prolonged exposure may affect balance, hearing, mood, memory, heart rhythm, or body temperature. If you feel the note in your sternum strongly enough to disrupt breathing, retreat. Wear hearing protection only with caution: the sound may be partly conducted through the body, and removing ordinary sound cues may make navigation worse.

Observe from a distance. If the mound is your reason for visiting, use passive methods: visual notes, remote thermal imaging, sound recording from the edge of the chamber, and perhaps a carefully placed instrument outside the immediate root-crowd.

## What Not to Take

Assume that every object within the chamber is part of an active process.

Do not remove:

- Roots, leaves, flowers, moss, spores, eggs, insects, burrowers, or shed skins.
- Mineral samples, especially from the central mound.
- Water from capillaries or pooled folds.
- Frost from the ceiling animal.
- Anything that has begun to turn silver or transparent.
- Objects already threaded by fine white roots.

Even a “dead” branch may only be between stages of translation. Taking it could be biologically disruptive; carrying it out could also spread a living process into another world.

Likewise, do not leave offerings. Organic offerings will be incorporated. Metal, plastic, batteries, fabric, and tools may become permanently rooted in place and potentially introduce toxins or novel materials into the chamber’s developing ecology.

Take only records and any waste you brought in.

## When You See Stars in a Leaf

The silver, briefly transparent leaves are a major unknown. The view of nonlocal stars may be an optical phenomenon, a sensory organ, a portal-like aperture, a memory structure, or something stranger.

Treat these leaves as fragile and consequential.

- Do not touch them.
- Do not place your eye directly against them.
- Do not shine lasers, flashlights, or cameras through them.
- Do not put objects through any apparent depth or opening.
- Do not attempt to map the star patterns in real time while standing beneath the leaf; keep moving and record from a safe distance.
- If the points shift in response to the root-thrum, avoid making strong sounds nearby.

A multiverse traveler should be especially restrained here. If these are genuine cross-world interfaces, they may not be safe doors. A leaf that can show another sky may also be showing the chamber to that sky.

## Decontamination After Exit

Leaving is not the end of the visit.

Before returning to another inhabited space or crossing worlds:

1. Remove outer garments in a controlled quarantine area.
2. Inspect seams, boots, hair, equipment, and respirator filters for white roots, spores, eggs, clear gel, frost residues, or tiny animals.
3. Do not brush potentially living material into open air.
4. Seal contaminated clothing and equipment in separate containers.
5. Warm yourself gradually. Sudden intense heat may activate or damage materials attached to you in unpredictable ways.
6. Shower, including hair, using collected runoff if possible rather than sending water immediately into public drainage.
7. Monitor for at least several days for persistent chills, unusual glowing marks, root-like growth around cuts, respiratory symptoms, altered hearing, or an urge to reproduce the chamber’s tones.
8. Keep any recordings isolated from automated sound systems. Do not casually play the deep mineral tone in a greenhouse, cave, hospital, or near living plant networks.

If you find fine roots growing into clothing or skin, do not simply rip them away. Seek medical support from someone familiar with invasive symbiotic organisms, and keep the material cool and contained until assessed.

## The Right Attitude

The chamber is young. That does not make it harmless; young systems are often more reactive, more adaptive, and less settled than ancient ones. But it also means your presence may matter disproportionately.

Go quietly but not silently. Stay warm enough to leave, cool enough not to become a problem. Let the garden decide where it can safely permit you. Do not force a path, do not demand specimens, and do not mistake responsiveness for invitation.

If you leave with wet boots, a recording of strange music, and the sense that the roots were listening, consider the visit successful.

If you leave with nothing at all except yourself, that may be even better.
