Prepare for this city as you would for a long stay in cold, wet high country—not for an expedition into wilderness exactly, but for a place where weather, stairs, and water govern daily life more thoroughly than clocks or roads.

## Before arrival

Do not assume that the city’s apparent quiet means it is easy to navigate. It is immense, layered, and vertically organized. A route that looks short on a map may include hundreds of steps, covered passages, a detour around a reservoir, and a neighborhood drainage closure after rain. If you can obtain one, carry a locally made district map rather than relying solely on imported navigation. Maps should show:

- stair routes, not just streets;
- public reservoirs and cistern access;
- covered passages;
- public shelters;
- animal paths and load routes;
- seasonal closures, especially icy stairways and flooded lanes;
- the boundary between maintained city fabric and abandoned outer terraces.

Learn the city’s water customs before you arrive. They are not ceremonial niceties: they are infrastructure etiquette. Blocking a channel with luggage, standing in a runoff groove, washing something upstream of a shared cistern, or leaning supplies over a spring opening may be treated as serious selfishness. In a city built around carefully shared drainage, careless water use is equivalent to obstructing traffic, contaminating food, and damaging public works at once.

If the multiversal crossing allows any choice of arrival point, choose a maintained central terrace or a formal gatehouse during daylight. Do not arrive on an outer trail, a reservoir edge, a roofline, or an abandoned stairway. The city’s edges are beautiful but can be confusing, exposed, and poorly serviced in sudden weather.

## Clothing: dress for damp cold rather than dramatic freezing

The dominant discomfort will likely be persistent chill and wetness: mist, rain, condensation, wind, wet stone, and cold interiors. You may experience several kinds of weather in one day without ever seeing a proper storm.

Bring layers:

- a breathable base layer that remains warm while damp;
- wool or equivalent insulating middle layers;
- a waterproof, wind-resistant outer shell with a hood;
- spare dry socks, ideally more than you think necessary;
- gloves that retain grip when wet;
- a warm hat or hood that will not blow away;
- trousers suitable for kneeling, climbing, and brushing wet walls;
- sleepwear warmer than you would normally use indoors.

Avoid clothing that dries slowly, becomes dangerously heavy when wet, or relies on open footwear. Local homes may be sturdy but cool, particularly in lower rooms, cistern-converted spaces, and north-facing dwellings. Even if a room feels dry on arrival, condensation and temperature can change overnight.

A small quick-drying towel, drying line, waterproof pouch for documents, and cloth bags for separating wet garments from dry ones will make daily life far easier.

## Footwear is your most important equipment

Bring durable waterproof boots with excellent grip on wet basalt and worn stone. They should support ankles without being too heavy for sustained stair climbing. The city’s polished steps, mossy edges, shallow gutters, and uneven repair work can all become slippery. Smooth-soled shoes, hard dress shoes, and shoes designed only for level urban pavement will make you miserable and may injure you.

Useful additions:

- traction cleats or removable ice grips for winter;
- spare laces;
- blister treatment;
- waterproofing wax or treatment appropriate to your footwear;
- lightweight indoor shoes, since hosts may not want muddy boots inside;
- a compact walking stick or trekking pole, especially if you have poor balance or expect to visit outer districts.

Be cautious with long coats, loose hems, dangling straps, and rolling luggage. Wheeled cases are not merely inconvenient here; they will be loud, awkward, and potentially obstruct drainage lanes. Carry your belongings in a fitted backpack, soft-sided shoulder bag, or a load frame designed for stairs.

## Pack lightly and compactly

The city moves by human backs, animals, sledges, and hand-carried loads. Even if you can pay someone to transport baggage, your luggage will still need to travel stairs, narrow passages, footbridges, and damp lanes. Bring less than you would for a flat modern city.

Prioritize:

- a compact medical kit;
- any essential medication, with a substantial supply and clear labels;
- water purification capability as a backup, even if local water is generally safe;
- high-energy food for delayed travel or weather confinement;
- a refillable bottle or flask;
- a headlamp with spare power, preferably with a dim red setting;
- a physical notebook and pencil, which work in damp conditions better than many electronics;
- repair supplies: needle, strong thread, patch tape, cord, safety pins;
- a compact universal adapter or independent power source only if you expect your own devices to be useful.

Do not expect abundant electrical infrastructure, vehicle transport, universal mechanical repair, or convenient charging. The absence of wires and engines suggests that imported electronic tools may be difficult to maintain, charge, or explain. Keep technology unobtrusive. A bright, noisy, unfamiliar device may attract attention in a place whose soundscape is built from water and hand labor.

## Acclimatize to altitude and exertion

This is a high basin. Even if the elevation is not extreme by mountaineering standards, the combination of altitude, cold, wet air, and continuous stair climbing can exhaust an unprepared visitor.

For your first two or three days:

- climb slowly;
- avoid carrying heavy loads;
- drink regularly;
- eat enough salt and calories;
- limit alcohol if it is available;
- allow extra time for every journey;
- rest before you feel severely tired.

Seek help if you develop severe headache, confusion, breathlessness at rest, persistent vomiting, chest pain, or unusual weakness. Those may be more than ordinary adjustment.

The city’s pace is slow for a reason. Do not mistake local calm for laziness or lack of urgency. People conserve strength, protect footing, and work with weather windows.

## Learn to read water

Water is the city’s circulatory system. Treat every channel, groove, spillway, and wet seam as functional.

When walking:

- never stand in the lowest part of a lane if rain is beginning;
- do not stop beneath spouts or at the mouths of drainage arches;
- step over channels rather than through them where possible;
- do not place bags, carts, or yourself across a gutter;
- watch for sudden runoff from upper terraces;
- avoid testing the depth of dark puddles near walls, stairs, or reservoir edges;
- assume wet stone can be slick even when it looks merely dark.

After heavy rain, ask locally which routes remain open. Some stairways may become waterfalls in miniature; undercrofts may be closed; certain lanes may be reserved for runoff. Follow the behavior of residents. If they change direction without apparent explanation, they may be avoiding a place that is about to flood, freeze, or shed water from above.

At reservoirs and springs, wait to see how locals draw water, wash goods, or use steps before doing the same. There may be distinct points for drinking, washing, animals, religious observance, and overflow management.

## Respect the built environment

The city is old, repaired continuously, and structurally interdependent. Its walls are not scenery. A retaining wall may support a home; a plaza may roof a cistern; a decorative channel may prevent a whole lane from flooding.

Do not:

- climb walls, roof edges, statues, or reservoir steps;
- remove stones, moss, tiles, or “souvenirs” from ruins;
- wedge doors open;
- hang items from gutters, railings, or spouts;
- use an abandoned-looking stairway without local confirmation;
- enter undercrofts, cistern rooms, or drainage tunnels uninvited;
- assume an apparently empty structure is unowned or safe.

Particularly avoid treating abandoned outer districts as adventure sites. The city has incorporated centuries of partial rebuilding and retreat. An old stair may lead somewhere real, but it may also end at an unstable terrace, a collapsed dwelling, or an exposed route overtaken by weather.

If you damage something—chip a channel edge, crack a step, dislodge a stone—report it promptly and offer practical restitution. In this city, small maintenance failures can become collective hazards after the next hard rain.

## Social conduct: be quiet, attentive, and useful

The inhabitants appear accustomed to close living without constant noise. Loud conversation, amplified music, shouting across courtyards, and noisy wheeled equipment will carry farther than you expect and may feel intrusive.

Adopt a lower-volume manner:

- speak softly in alleys and under covered passages;
- do not ring bells or call for attention unless necessary;
- keep animal-like sudden noises, alarms, and bright flashing lights to a minimum;
- ask before photographing homes, reservoirs, shrines, workers, or water infrastructure;
- do not assume a quiet market lacks activity—transactions may be deliberate and relationship-based.

A visitor will likely be welcomed more readily if they demonstrate practical awareness. Offer to carry a load, sweep a threshold clear of runoff, help move goods before rain, or contribute to a neighborhood stair repair fund—only after asking, not imposing. The culture’s rules are likely grounded in shared survival and maintenance. Respect for local labor will matter more than grand displays of wealth.

Avoid romanticizing hardship aloud. The inhabitants have made a durable life, not a picturesque ruin. Praise skill, workmanship, water management, stonework, food preservation, and neighborhood care rather than calling the place “primitive,” “timeless,” or “untouched.”

## Food, water, and health

Expect a diet shaped by upland constraints: roots, grains if imported or terrace-grown, wool and dairy products, preserved meat, dried herbs, salt, hardy greens, possibly goat or sheep products, and food designed to keep well in cold damp conditions. Fresh fruit and delicate produce may be rare, seasonal, or expensive.

If you have dietary requirements, bring supplies for the first week and explain restrictions plainly. Do not assume substitutes are available. High exertion and cold will increase calorie needs; travelers often under-eat in unfamiliar places and then become exhausted on the stairs.

The city’s water systems may be excellent, but they are complex. Use the source recommended by your host. In unfamiliar districts, carry your own treatment method. Do not casually drink from every picturesque channel: runoff, roof water, spring water, reservoir water, and household cistern water are not necessarily interchangeable.

Damp conditions favor skin irritation, fungal foot problems, and respiratory discomfort. Dry feet nightly, inspect for blisters, air out gear whenever weather permits, and seek a warm dry room promptly if chilled through.

## Weather and emergency habits

Always leave shelter prepared for rain, even under a clear sky. Carry your outer layer, a light source, and something warm whenever going farther than your immediate neighborhood.

Establish, soon after arrival:

1. the safest route back to your lodging in fog;
2. the nearest covered public space;
3. the nearest medical help or trusted household;
4. the local warning signs for heavy rain, freezing conditions, or ridge storms;
5. the name of your district and the landmarks residents use—not merely an address.

North-facing doors painted in mineral colors are likely navigation aids. Learn those colors and other local wayfinding markers. In fog, a city of gray stone can become disorienting with surprising speed, especially when stairways pass through buildings and several terraces look alike.

In winter, assume black ice on shaded stairs, bridge stones, and the margins of pools. Do not hurry downhill. Keep your hands free when possible, and do not rely on railings without testing them; condensation, frost, and age may make them unsafe.

## Bringing goods across worlds

If you carry trade goods, choose modest, durable, repairable items rather than spectacle. Useful offerings might include high-quality needles, waterproof thread, medical supplies, warm textiles, compact hand tools, reliable lamp components, seed stock only with strict ecological caution, or knowledge that can be shared without destabilizing local life.

Do **not** introduce invasive plants, unfamiliar animals, pathogens, powerful chemicals, uncontrolled fuels, or technologies that require infrastructure the city does not possess. The basin is ecologically spare and hydrologically sensitive. A careless introduction could spread through its channels quickly and be difficult to remove.

Likewise, do not offer electrical, industrial, or high-energy devices as casual gifts unless you understand their consequences. A city organized around quiet labor and communal maintenance may have reasons—material, historical, political, or environmental—for not using them.

## The right frame of mind

Come prepared to slow down.

This is not a city to conquer by itinerary. It will reward walking a small area repeatedly, learning which stones remain dry, knowing the sound of rain approaching on roofs, recognizing the route that avoids the wind, and returning to the same market court until people recognize you.

Plan fewer destinations per day than you normally would. Leave room for weather, conversation, detours, and the simple work of moving through steep wet streets. Carry what you can carry yourself. Notice where your water goes. Keep pathways clear. Treat every stair as shared infrastructure.

If you do that, the city is likely to feel less forbidding than it first appears: not a bleak upland maze, but a remarkably competent human settlement—one that has made a home from stone, gravity, and rain.
