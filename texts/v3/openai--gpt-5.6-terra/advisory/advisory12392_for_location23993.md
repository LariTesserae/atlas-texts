Prepare for the Green Engine Vault as you would for a high-altitude industrial ruin, a botanical preserve, and an anomalous containment site simultaneously. It is not a place to enter casually, even if the door is unlocked and the air inside feels safer than the summit.

## Before approaching the mountain

The outside environment may be the most immediately lethal part of the visit.

The vault is at high elevation, amid permanent snow and exposed rock. Plan for severe wind chill, whiteout conditions, falls, and altitude illness. Do not assume that finding the door means you are physically fit to enter it.

Bring:

- Cold-weather mountaineering clothing in layers, including windproof outer shell, insulated gloves, face covering, and goggles.
- Proper alpine boots with crampon compatibility; the summit scree and frozen ground may be unstable.
- Helmet, climbing harness, rope, anchors, and a reliable means of marking a route back to the door.
- Supplemental oxygen, preferably enough for the approach, descent, and a reserve. The outside air is described as thin enough to impair judgment.
- Satellite beacon or multiversal equivalent, though ordinary radio may not function reliably through the mountain or around the machinery.
- A physical map, compass, and analog watch. Do not depend exclusively on GPS, battery-powered navigation, or electronic altimeters.
- A companion if possible. If solo travel is unavoidable, establish a strict check-in and turnaround protocol before leaving the last safe shelter.

Do not rely on the vault as an emergency refuge unless you already know the door can be opened from inside. The Green Engine Vault may be breathable, but it is still a sealed industrial environment in which normal assumptions about exits, time, and mechanical failure do not apply.

## Treat the door as a threshold, not an entrance

Before opening it, inspect it without touching exposed metal.

Look for:

- Electrical arcing, humming, vibration, warmth, or unusual static discharge.
- Root growth passing through seals or hinges.
- Signs that the door closes automatically.
- Mechanical latches that cannot be operated from the inner side.
- A sudden change in pressure or sound when the seal is disturbed.

Secure the door open if doing so does not require forcing it. Use nonconductive wedges or straps rather than metal tools. Leave a highly visible, nonliving marker outside and another just inside: bright survey tape, a painted placard, or a passive reflector. The vault’s darkness and density may make the doorway surprisingly hard to relocate after only a few minutes.

Before fully entering, pause in the threshold and observe for several minutes. Let your eyes, ears, and balance adjust. Note the sound pattern of the machinery. If it changes sharply when you arrive—fans shifting pitch, flowers turning, lights dimming, a new rhythm beginning—take that seriously. The vault may not be hostile, but it clearly responds to electrical conditions, and it may respond to intrusion as a change in its system.

## Air and breathing

The vault’s air is reportedly dense enough to breathe, but it is extremely dry and contaminated with suspended mineral and plant dust.

Wear:

- A fitted respirator with particulate filtration, ideally a P100-class filter or equivalent.
- Spare filters stored in sealed bags.
- Eye protection that seals reasonably well against dust.
- Saline spray, lip balm, and skin barrier cream for the dryness.
- A hydration pack or water bottles. There is no water inside. Do not expect to replenish supplies from plants, pipes, tanks, condensation, or snowmelt.

The atmosphere may be breathable while still being physiologically strange. The plants metabolize mineral dust, carbon-rich air, trace heat, and electromagnetic activity; that is not a normal ecology. Avoid inhaling seed-dust, blue flower fragments, root fibers, rust, ceramic debris, or insulation particles. Do not remove your respirator merely because the air feels calm.

If you experience headache, metallic taste, nosebleed, dizziness, visual persistence, irregular heartbeat, sudden calm, or a sense that time has become repetitive, leave immediately. Any of these could arise from dry air, altitude, industrial contamination, electromagnetic exposure, or the vault’s more unusual rules. It is not necessary to determine which.

## Clothing and equipment inside

Dress as if you will encounter energized equipment, sharp corroded metal, mineral dust, and dense vegetation that may be structurally deceptive.

Recommended equipment:

- Nonconductive, puncture-resistant boots with good ankle support.
- Electrical-hazard-rated gloves under durable outer work gloves.
- Long sleeves and durable trousers; avoid loose fabric, dangling straps, jewelry, and unsecured hair.
- Helmet with a headlamp, but use your light sparingly. Constant ambient illumination may be part of the vault’s equilibrium, and strong beams may conceal rather than reveal depth in the layered gloom.
- Two independent light sources, preferably with warm, low-output settings. Keep one sealed as backup.
- Analog thermometer and hygrometer if available.
- A compact air-quality monitor, but do not consider a normal reading proof of safety.
- Noncontact voltage detector and electromagnetic field meter, used at a distance and interpreted conservatively.
- Nonmetallic trekking pole or probe for testing vegetation-covered footing.
- Small first-aid kit, eye wash, burn dressings, and a compact emergency oxygen source.
- Paper notebook and pencil. Electronic recordings may be unreliable around continuous current or anomalous repetition.

Avoid bringing food with strong smells or loose crumbs. There are no animals or insects inside, but that absence is itself meaningful. Do not test whether the vegetation will consume ordinary organic material.

## Do not touch the machinery

The engine has run long enough to become part of the place’s ecology. Its apparent lack of fuel, maintenance, exhaust, or wear does not mean it is safe.

Assume every pipe, rail, motor housing, cable, bearing, and switchbox may be:

- Energized.
- Hot despite looking old.
- Structurally unsound despite appearing frozen in place.
- Essential to the vault’s stability.
- Connected to machinery far larger than the visible chamber.

Do not:

- Turn switches.
- Open panels.
- Cut, unplug, or reroute cables.
- Take samples from wires, insulation, tank contents, flowers around bearings, or anything rooted into electrical equipment.
- Test a warm pipe with your bare hand.
- Throw objects into grates, vents, shafts, or foliage.
- Attempt to stop the rolling nut, catch a belt, block a fan, or interrupt any repeated action.

The vault’s central rule seems to be continuation without consumption or history. It may tolerate observation because observation changes nothing. Intervention could force change into a system specifically arranged to resist it.

Even harmless-seeming maintenance behavior—tightening a loose bolt, clearing a clogged grille, sweeping dust from a switch, pruning a stem away from a conduit—may be dangerous. The dust layer, rust boundary, plant orientation, and mechanical repetition could all be fixed components of the vault’s established condition.

## Treat the plants as integrated infrastructure

The vegetation is not ordinary plant life. It is adapted to current, heat, mineral material, and vibration. Its roots penetrate cable insulation, mortar, and machinery. Its flowers orient toward load shifts rather than sunlight.

Do not assume it is poisonous, carnivorous, sentient, or harmless. Instead, adopt the safer assumption: it is part of a living electrical system whose rules are unfamiliar.

Keep clear of:

- Flower rings around warm bearings.
- Roots entering cable sheaths.
- Fronds growing from vents.
- Moss-like mats on control boxes and concrete plinths.
- Any vegetation that changes orientation when you move, speak, shine a light, or operate equipment.
- Seed-dust release events, especially during rising motor pitch or changes in the chamber’s hum.

If seed-dust begins to release, cover exposed skin, tighten your respirator seal, close your eyes or use goggles, and retreat toward the entrance without running. Avoid brushing against walls or plants; dry spores may adhere electrostatically to clothing and equipment.

Do not collect specimens unless you have sealed, electrically insulated sample containers and a quarantine plan outside the mountain. A cutting may not “die” in the ordinary sense; it may remain active, orient toward current, or introduce the vault’s closed ecology into another environment.

## Movement and route discipline

There is very little empty space in the vault. Its apparent surfaces may be a blend of catwalk, root mat, cable bundles, corroded metal, and crystalline growth.

Move slowly. Test every foothold.

The lower vegetation floor is described as flexing beneath a boot without yielding. That means it may conceal grating, gaps, warm machinery, ducts, or a vertical drop. Do not trust it because it feels firm. Avoid descending to the lowest level unless you have a specific reason and a secured retrieval line.

Use a “one hand, one foot” principle around ladders and catwalks, but do not grip railings automatically: they tremble with the machinery and may be energized or weakened by corrosion. If you must use a ladder whose lower rungs have been swallowed by roots, consider it unusable unless independently anchored and tested.

Mark your route physically at junctions. The chamber is only about twenty meters across, but it is visually dense and acoustically confusing. The constant hum can erase directional cues, and the repeated geometry of pipes, stems, and platforms may create a strong sense that you have already walked somewhere you have not.

A practical limit for an initial visit would be: remain within direct visual or rope-line connection to the entrance. Do not make a first expedition a descent to the lower trough.

## Timekeeping and the risk of repetition

The Green Engine Vault is characterized by motion without progression. Some objects may repeat the same action indefinitely. It is possible that ordinary time continues normally for visitors; it is also possible that perception, memory, or local causality behaves strangely.

Use redundant time checks:

- Wear an analog watch.
- Carry a mechanical timer.
- Record a written entry time at the doorway.
- At fixed intervals, write a brief note: time, position, machinery sound, physical symptoms, and visible changes.
- Have an external observer track your expected return time if possible.

Set a conservative exit deadline before entering. Obey it even if nothing dramatic has happened.

Leave if you notice:

- The same thought, phrase, or physical action recurring without intention.
- Difficulty remembering whether you have already examined an area.
- A strong urge to “finish” some small mechanical pattern.
- A belief that the vault is protecting you better than the outside world.
- An inability to distinguish the sounds of your own movement from the machinery.
- Your notes becoming repetitive, contradictory, or strangely identical.
- The door seeming farther away than it should be.

The feeling of protection is explicitly part of the vault’s atmosphere: dense air, enclosed darkness, constant noise, and the relief of escaping alpine exposure. Respect that feeling, but do not trust it. A sealed system can feel like shelter precisely because it excludes change, departure, hunger, weather, and other signs of life.

## Noise, vibration, and communication

The sound level may be damaging over time even if it is not immediately painful. The continuous low-frequency vibration may also cause fatigue, nausea, anxiety, or disorientation.

Bring hearing protection, preferably filtered earplugs or earmuffs that reduce machinery noise while still allowing you to hear alarms, your companion, and changing rhythms. Do not completely block your hearing; shifts in the engine’s pattern may be the only warning that conditions are changing.

Use agreed hand signals with companions. Shouting is likely to be ineffective and may be swallowed or reflected confusingly by the iron walls.

If you hear a new sound—especially silence, a missed piston stroke, a belt slowing, a fan winding down, metal tearing, or a sudden collective movement of flowers—withdraw. The usual operation may be self-sustaining; abnormal operation is an unknown event.

## What not to bring back

Before leaving, inspect yourself and your equipment at the threshold.

Brush off visible dust only outside the vault, downwind, and preferably onto a containment sheet. Seal used filters, disposable oversuits, plant fragments, mineral crust, and any debris in labeled bags. Do not carry home a “souvenir” leaf, flower, nut, cable clipping, or sample of dust.

Particularly avoid exporting:

- Seeds or seed-dust.
- Root fragments.
- Dust from switchboxes, gratings, or warm machinery.
- Rust flakes or ceramic insulation.
- Anything that appears mechanically repetitive, such as a rolling object, vibrating wire, or turning fragment.

If you touched vegetation or equipment, isolate your gloves and outer clothing until they can be cleaned or disposed of. If possible, quarantine equipment away from powered electronics for a time. This is not because the vault’s materials are certainly contagious or dangerous, but because they exist in an ecology based on electrical relationships that may not remain harmless elsewhere.

## Recommended visitor attitude

The best way to visit the Green Engine Vault is as a guest in a system that has achieved a precarious perfection.

Do not go there to repair it, solve it, harvest it, or prove that its rules are false. Go to observe: the dry leaves facing current, the dust suspended in vibration, the flowers responding to load, the impossible persistence of a machine that has ceased to be merely a machine.

Enter briefly. Touch little. Change nothing. Keep your route home visible.

Most importantly: do not let the vault’s enclosure persuade you that it is a place to remain. The mountain’s green room is stable because nothing within it needs a future. A human visitor does.
