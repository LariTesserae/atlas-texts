## Visitor’s Briefing: A Country Still Becoming Solid

Treat this destination as an active volcanic, high-altitude construction zone on a planetary scale. It is not simply a desert, mountain range, or lava field. The hazards are continuous, overlapping, and only partly visible: unstable ground, heat from below, toxic or oxygen-poor air, ash and glass storms, darkness, seismic motion, and terrain that can change faster than maps can be made.

Do not plan a casual walk. Plan a short, instrument-supported expedition with a reliable extraction method.

The most important principle is this:

**Never trust an apparent surface merely because it is solid under your feet.**  
In this country, solidity is temporary.

---

## 1. Establish Whether a Human Can Breathe There

The description strongly suggests an atmosphere that may be thin, chemically hostile, or both. “Extreme height” may mean elevation in the ordinary sense, but the strange horizon and suspended-looking terrain mean you should not assume familiar atmospheric behavior.

The air smells or tastes of sulfur, salt, and hot stone; this is enough to treat it as potentially contaminated by:

- sulfur dioxide,
- hydrogen sulfide,
- carbon dioxide collecting in hollows,
- hydrochloric or other acidic gases,
- fine silicate ash,
- mineral dust,
- metallic aerosols,
- oxygen deficiency.

A smell is not a safe warning system. Hydrogen sulfide can deaden the sense of smell; carbon dioxide is odorless; and ash can injure lungs without being obviously toxic.

### Bring
- A **sealed environmental suit** with independent oxygen supply.
- A **full-face respirator** as a backup only; do not rely on filters as primary protection.
- A helmet with:
  - positive-pressure breathing,
  - eye protection,
  - a heads-up atmospheric monitor,
  - thermal imaging or low-light optics,
  - radio and location beacon.
- Portable sensors for:
  - oxygen partial pressure,
  - carbon dioxide,
  - hydrogen sulfide,
  - sulfur dioxide,
  - volatile acids,
  - particulates,
  - temperature,
  - barometric pressure,
  - radiation and electrical-field changes if practical.

Use oxygen conservatively, but assume no local air is safe until tested repeatedly in multiple places and at multiple heights. Low pockets, fissures, shelters, crater-like pans, and lee sides of cliffs may concentrate heavy gases.

**Do not sleep in a depression. Do not seek refuge in a cave, crack, or vent-adjacent hollow without atmospheric monitoring.**

## 2. Dress for Heat Below and Cold or Darkness Above

The ground is warm to hot, vents open unexpectedly, and incandescent grit may fall at a distance. Yet the dim sky, extreme altitude, and lack of atmospheric moisture may permit sudden cold exposure away from the heated terrain. Prepare for both.

### Your suit should provide
- Fire-resistant and heat-reflective outer layers.
- Insulated boots with:
  - rigid puncture-resistant soles,
  - thermal barriers,
  - ankle support,
  - gaiter seals against ash and glass dust.
- Cut-resistant fabric over joints and hands.
- Replaceable outer boot coverings: razor-fine volcanic glass can destroy conventional footwear.
- Active cooling, or at minimum heat-dump capability, for periods near heated cracks.
- Insulation for long pauses on cooler terraces.
- A visor coating resistant to abrasion and anti-fogging systems suitable for dry ash conditions.

Avoid exposed skin entirely. “Warm air” is not a comfort signal here: hot, dry mineral winds can dehydrate and abrade skin rapidly.

## 3. Water Is Your Main Consumable

There is almost no water in the landscape, and the dry heat, breathing apparatus, ash exposure, and exertion will increase water loss. Do not expect to replenish from vents, vapor, crusts, or dust pans. Mineral vapor is not necessarily water vapor, and any apparent condensate may be acidic, saline, or contaminated.

### Carry more water than a normal expedition would require
For a short field excursion, carry:
- at least **6–10 liters per person per day** in protected containers, more if suit cooling consumes water;
- electrolyte supplies;
- a closed-loop hydration system compatible with the suit;
- a reserve cache at the entry/extraction point;
- compact emergency water reclamation if the journey may run long.

Keep water containers insulated and protected from puncture. Do not place them directly on warm ground.

### Food
Bring compact, non-crumbing, high-calorie food that can be eaten inside the suit or in a controlled shelter. Expect exertion from uneven terrain and stress. Avoid anything that requires local water or heating.

## 4. Treat the Ground as a Live Hazard

The country is tectonically and volcanically active. Its major dangers include:

- fissures opening beneath or beside you,
- fault scarps forming suddenly,
- landslides and slow-moving debris flows,
- collapsing crust over hot or empty voids,
- falling fragments from cliffs and suspended platforms,
- ash burial,
- ground-heated zones,
- delayed impacts from falling high rafts of rock.

The danger is not limited to obvious lava. The text specifically describes powdered, incandescent stone and glowing rock that provides little visible warning.

### Movement rules
1. **Travel light and fast, but never rush blindly.**
2. Keep wide spacing between travelers—far enough that one collapse does not take everyone.
3. Use tethers only when they can be anchored to verified stable rock. Never anchor to a fresh ledge, a slab surrounded by cracks, or a seemingly isolated boulder.
4. Probe questionable ground with a nonconductive pole before loading your full weight.
5. Avoid:
   - fault lines,
   - fresh cracks,
   - warm or steaming ground,
   - ash-covered slopes,
   - cliff bases,
   - narrow gullies,
   - basins,
   - vent mouths,
   - overhangs,
   - the underside or projected fall zone of suspended rock platforms.
6. Cross open basins quickly. Their apparent flatness may conceal thin crust, concentrated gases, or incoming ash.
7. Do not follow the most direct route if it passes through newly broken terrain. A longer route over old, thick basalt is better.

### Learn the warning signs
Stop and reassess if you detect:
- vibration through boots or teeth,
- a low subsonic pressure sensation,
- shifting dust without wind,
- fresh sulfurous or metallic odors,
- increased ground temperature,
- widening cracks,
- a sudden stillness before a debris event,
- changes in static activity overhead,
- distant dry cracking that appears to propagate toward you.

When the ground begins to rearrange, do not attempt to “out-run” a whole slope downhill. Move laterally toward higher, older, more coherent ground—if such ground is nearby—and get clear of gullies, cliff faces, and obvious runout channels.

## 5. Prepare for Ash, Salt, and Volcanic Glass Storms

The matter storms may be more dangerous than the seismic events. Fine ash enters seals, blinds optics, abrades surfaces, disables electronics, and damages lungs. Fine volcanic glass can cut exposed material and turn a simple windstorm into a sandblaster.

### Bring
- Replaceable external air filters and pre-filters.
- Abrasion-resistant visor covers, with several spares.
- Sealed equipment cases.
- Dust covers for joints, packs, cameras, sensor ports, and connectors.
- Mechanical rather than exposed delicate equipment where possible.
- Redundant navigation tools, including protected paper or polymer maps and a compass if local magnetic conditions permit.
- A compact storm shelter or bivouac tube with anchoring system.

### Storm protocol
If a matter storm approaches:
1. Do not shelter in a low basin or against the downwind side of a vent.
2. Seek the lee of a **large, old, coherent rock mass**, not a newly fractured wall.
3. Keep clear of any cliff that may shed debris.
4. Seal your suit and protect optical surfaces.
5. Crouch or lie low only if the ground is demonstrably stable and not hot.
6. Mark your position before visibility disappears.
7. Wait after the visible storm ends; ash slopes and nearby walls may continue collapsing.

Do not remove your helmet or open suit seals to clear dust. Carry tools designed to clean equipment externally.

## 6. Navigation Will Be Unreliable

There is poor visibility, no dependable horizon, no vegetation, few persistent landmarks, frequent dust, and terrain that changes. Standard “walk toward that ridge” navigation can become fatal if the ridge collapses, is obscured, or was farther away than it appeared.

The broad static-light flashes may momentarily illuminate the country. Use them for observation, not movement: a flash can reveal a ravine, fault, or unstable slope that darkness had hidden, but it can also ruin night-adapted vision.

### Navigation kit
- Inertial navigation unit.
- Independent satellite/portal/local-positioning receiver if the universe permits one.
- Short-range terrain-mapping lidar or radar.
- Laser rangefinder.
- Route-marking beacons designed to survive heat and ash.
- Physical line or breadcrumb tether for short forays from camp.
- Low-light and thermal imaging devices.
- Redundant hard-copy or etched route records.

### Operational rule
Build your route around **returnability**, not exploration distance.

At every decision point ask:
- Can we still identify the way back?
- Is the ground behind us still intact?
- Is the extraction beacon reachable if visibility falls to near zero?
- Are we entering a place with only one exit?

Do not rely on footprints. Tremors and ash can erase them quickly.

## 7. Do Not Trust the Small Structures as Shelters

The slab circles, spiral shelters, and white-stone cairns may be cultural sites, markers, warnings, or remnants of prior visitors. They may also be the only indications that certain patches were stable long enough for someone to build there.

That does **not** make them safe.

They are empty, partially buried, and situated in a continually changing landscape. A structure that survived may have done so because it is in the lee of a fault—but faults are not dependable protection.

### Respectful approach protocol
- Observe from a distance first.
- Scan for heat, gas, voids, radiation, electrical anomalies, and signs of recent movement.
- Do not dismantle, climb, move stones, take samples, or use cairns as fire rings or anchors.
- Photograph and map before entering.
- If entering a slab shelter:
  - keep a person outside,
  - maintain a line to the entrance,
  - monitor gas continuously,
  - avoid leaning on walls,
  - do not remain during tremors or storms unless no safer alternative exists.

The white stones that occur nowhere nearby are especially significant. Assume they were transported deliberately. They may mark routes, burial sites, boundaries, vents, refuges, or something less obvious. Leave them undisturbed.

## 8. Build Camp Only for Short Duration

A permanent camp is unrealistic unless you have advanced engineering and a reason to remain. The land’s defining feature is that it changes.

Choose a camp site only after a prolonged observation period. Favor:
- broad, thick basalt terraces,
- areas with few active cracks,
- ground that is cool relative to nearby surfaces,
- terrain outside apparent slide paths,
- elevated positions that are not exposed beneath cliffs or suspended masses,
- sites with multiple evacuation routes.

Avoid:
- dust pans,
- basins,
- fissure fields,
- slopes,
- beneath overhangs,
- near vent plumes,
- the lee of weak walls,
- locations dependent on a single narrow exit.

### Camp setup
- Use a low-profile, sealed shelter.
- Anchor it with multiple independent anchors into proven rock.
- Keep all life-support gear elevated from the surface.
- Establish an immediate evacuation pack for each person.
- Set vibration, gas, temperature, and particulate alarms.
- Keep the extraction beacon active and accessible outside the main shelter.
- Establish a maximum wind threshold, ash threshold, and seismic threshold for immediate withdrawal.

If the ground changes noticeably during your rest period—new crack, temperature rise, dust seepage, fresh rockfall—break camp. Do not negotiate with a site that has announced its instability.

## 9. Plan for Psychological Stress and Sensory Distortion

The absence of life, lack of familiar weather, dim sky, low visibility, and deep seismic sound can be mentally exhausting. The terrain may create distance illusions: cliffs seem near but are far, or vice versa; hovering platforms may be optical effects, dust-supported structures, or unfamiliar geology.

The environment’s silence will be broken by sudden, enormous events. This alternation can trigger panic, dissociation, or unsafe overreaction.

### Prepare yourself
- Travel with at least one partner if possible.
- Use scheduled verbal check-ins even when radios work.
- Maintain simple written procedures for alarms and evacuation.
- Keep a fixed work-rest schedule rather than operating solely by the dim sky.
- Use objective measurements—temperature, gas readings, map coordinates—when intuition conflicts with instruments.
- Do not make major route choices immediately after a seismic event. Pause, assess, and check the changed terrain.

A person alone in this place may begin to treat silence as safety. It is not safety. It is merely the interval before the next movement.

## 10. Be Careful Around the Possible Life

The gray branching films and translucent vent crusts may be alive, mineral-organic, or something intermediate. They may be harmless, but they might also react to heat, pressure, oxygen, moisture, touch, or introduced Earth biology.

### Biosecurity
- Do not touch, scrape, taste, burn, or collect them casually.
- Avoid tracking them between sites.
- Keep suit and tools decontaminable.
- Store samples only in sealed, isolated containers.
- Do not introduce water onto them as an experiment.
- Do not assume they are slow merely because they appear mineral-like.
- Quarantine all gear after departure.

If you must study them, begin remotely: imaging, spectroscopy, thermal response, atmospheric sampling, and observation over time. The vent mouths themselves remain dangerous regardless of biology.

## 11. Have a Strict Extraction Plan

This is not a place where rescue can easily find you. It may not retain the route by which you arrived.

Before crossing:
- Establish a stable arrival/extraction platform if possible.
- Put extraction equipment on the most geologically stable accessible ground, not at the most scenic or central location.
- Mark it with multiple beacons: visual, radio, inertial, and physical.
- Keep it clear of cliffs, vents, and ash channels.
- Confirm it can function in abrasive dust, heat, and electromagnetic/static disturbances.
- Create at least one secondary extraction point.
- Set a hard turnaround time based on oxygen, water, and expected return difficulty.
- Reserve enough consumables for a delayed extraction caused by storm or terrain loss.

If portal technology is involved, do not assume the portal’s original location will remain reachable. A fault may open across the route, a basin may fill, or a shelf may collapse. Keep a portable emergency return device on your person if at all possible.

## 12. Suggested Minimum Personal Loadout

For a short, cautious reconnaissance:

- Sealed pressure/environmental suit.
- Independent oxygen supply with emergency reserve.
- Full-face helmet with communications and low-light capability.
- Gas, particulate, thermal, pressure, and seismic monitors.
- Heat-resistant, puncture-resistant boots and gloves.
- Water: 6–10 L/day minimum, plus electrolytes.
- High-calorie food for at least twice the planned duration.
- Navigation suite: inertial system, rangefinder, terrain mapper, physical route notes.
- Multiple location beacons and signal mirrors/lights suitable for dust.
- Compact sealed shelter or storm bivouac.
- First-aid kit adapted for burns, eye injury, inhalation exposure, cuts, fractures, dehydration, and crush injury.
- Climbing/escape equipment: rope, harness, anchors, probe pole, compact shovel—but use only on verified stable terrain.
- Visor covers, air filters, tape, seal repair patches, dust-cleaning tools.
- Sample containment system, if sampling is authorized.
- Emergency extraction device or beacon carried on your body, not only in your pack.

## 13. The Right Attitude

Do not approach this country as a dead wasteland. It is an unfinished landscape with active processes where familiar categories—mountain, valley, storm, shelter, road—are unreliable.

It may be beautiful. The pauses between tremors may be genuinely peaceful. The low violet ridges, static-lit shelves, and the strange gray life around warm fissures may make it feel like you are witnessing a world’s first difficult morning.

But do not let that feeling make you linger.

Stay light. Stay sealed. Stay instrumented. Keep one eye on the ground and one on the route home. In a place that has not finished hardening, survival depends less on conquering the terrain than on recognizing when it is about to become something else.
