## Before You Go

Treat the high country as a wet, living system rather than a landscape with trails. It is not hostile in the dramatic sense, but it is exceptionally good at absorbing the unprepared. A person can be injured, lost, exhausted, infected, or simply incorporated into the terrain without anything ever seeming to attack them.

Do not plan a fast crossing. Plan a slow, low-impact expedition with frequent rests, redundant navigation, and the expectation that every route will change.

If your multiversal travel service permits it, arrange arrival and extraction on a known elevated site: a basalt outcrop, a maintained raised platform, or a ridge crest above the saturated forest. Do not accept a random ground-level insertion in a valley or marsh basin. “Solid ground” may be floating peat, rootfelt over water, the roof of an abandoned structure, or the sleeping surface of a very large animal.

Travel with at least one companion if possible. This is a place where a twisted ankle, a submerged foot, or a moment of disorientation can become serious very quietly.

## Physical Preparation

You do not need alpine conditioning as much as you need endurance, balance, and the ability to move patiently through resistance.

Prepare for:

- Walking over uneven, springy ground that gives underfoot.
- Wading for long periods.
- Stepping over, under, and around roots, vines, pools, and fallen trunks.
- Climbing short wet slopes with few reliable handholds.
- Carrying all essential gear in a waterproof manner.
- Working in warm, humid air where sweat does not evaporate effectively.
- Long periods with poor visibility and no distant landmarks.

Train ankles, knees, hips, and core stability. Practice walking with a loaded pack on wet woodland terrain if you can. Learn basic rope-assisted movement, especially how to cross slick logs, shallow channels, and unstable peat without turning every crossing into a rescue situation.

Acclimatization matters. Although it is high country, the vegetation holds warmth beneath the canopy. You may be moving in air that is both cool enough to chill you when still and warm enough to overheat you when exerting yourself. The constant moisture makes this worse.

## Clothing

Your central clothing problem is not rain alone. It is never becoming dry.

Bring layers that remain useful when wet. Avoid cotton entirely.

### Wear

- Quick-drying synthetic or wool base layers.
- Tough, breathable trousers rather than shorts. The understory will cut, sting, abrade, and cling.
- A long-sleeved shirt with insect protection.
- A light insulating layer sealed in a dry bag, for dawn, high ridges, and stationary periods.
- A waterproof shell with good ventilation. In this country, fully waterproof clothing can become internally soaked from perspiration; pit zips and adjustable cuffs are valuable.
- Broad-brimmed hat or rain cap with a chin strap.
- Thin work gloves for roots, wet stone, and unknown plants.
- Spare socks in multiple sealed bags.

### Footwear

Use sturdy, high-ankle hiking boots or amphibious trekking boots with aggressive grip and excellent drainage. The best choice depends on whether you expect more rootfelt and forest floor or more standing water and channels, but in either case:

- Waterproof boots eventually fill from above and stay wet.
- Fully sealed rubber boots can be useful in marsh, but are clumsy on roots and slopes.
- A drainage-capable boot with wool socks is often the practical compromise.
- Bring camp footwear that lets your feet air out.
- Carry foot powder, blister treatment, antifungal medication, and tape.

Inspect your feet morning and evening. Persistent wetness is a medical issue here, not a comfort issue.

## Water and Food

Water is everywhere, but it should not be assumed safe.

The country’s streams emerge through root systems, peat, decomposing vegetation, and animal corridors. Even clear water can carry parasites, microbes, spores, or local biochemistry unfamiliar to your home world.

Bring a layered purification system:

1. A physical filter suitable for sediment and protozoa.
2. Chemical or UV treatment as a second stage.
3. A means of boiling water, if local conditions and fire regulations permit.
4. Enough carrying capacity for a full day, even in rain.

Do not drink directly from still pools, bromeliad basins, animal-wallows, or water rising through rootfelt. Avoid taking water downstream of giant-animal trails or near ruins, where submerged debris and runoff accumulate.

Bring dense food that can be eaten without cooking:

- Nuts and seed bars
- Dried meals
- Hard cheese or shelf-stable protein
- Electrolyte powders
- Salt
- Quick carbohydrates for sudden fatigue

Humidity makes ordinary food spoil quickly. Package meals in small portions; once a package is opened, assume it will become damp.

Do not casually forage. The landscape is biologically rich, but unfamiliar edible-looking fruit, tubers, fungi, flowers, and water plants may be toxic, parasitic, symbiotic with local fauna, or vital to something that does not appreciate their removal. Carnivorous cups and trembling broad leaves are especially poor candidates for curiosity.

## Navigation: Assume the Land Will Rearrange Your Route

Ordinary navigation methods are weakened by canopy, cloud, drifting mist, and the lack of visible horizon. A compass remains useful, but will not solve the problem alone.

Bring:

- A reliable compass and waterproof paper maps.
- Offline GPS with spare power in sealed containers.
- A satellite or multiversal beacon communicator, if functional through dense canopy.
- Highly visible but biodegradable route markers only if local travel authorities permit them.
- A length of bright cord for temporary wayfinding between difficult points.
- Whistle, signal mirror, and waterproof light.

Do not rely on marking trees with cuts, paint, or permanent tags. This is environmentally damaging, may be culturally offensive if there are local inhabitants, and will be rapidly obscured anyway.

Use route discipline:

- Stop and confirm direction regularly.
- Turn around often and memorize what the return route looks like.
- Note water flow, slope direction, distinctive basalt faces, large buttressed trees, and old raised structures.
- Keep a written travel log with times and bearings.
- Establish a turnaround time each day.

The forest’s visual sameness is deceptive. Everything is richly detailed, yet the details repeat: wet trunks, fern rafts, roots, mist, and shallow water. People can become disoriented while believing they are following a clear direction.

If visibility falls to a few meters, stop rather than pushing onward. The place will still be there when the mist lifts. A person who keeps moving in thick fog can walk into deep water, unstable ground, a cliff seam, or a large animal corridor.

## Camps and Shelter

Never camp in a hollow simply because it looks sheltered. Low places collect water. A dry patch at dusk may be ankle-deep by midnight.

Choose sites that are:

- Slightly elevated.
- Firm under repeated pressure.
- Outside obvious drainage channels.
- Away from standing pools and dense reeds.
- Away from animal trails.
- Not beneath dead limbs, heavily loaded vines, or water-filled branches.
- At a respectful distance from ruins and circular timber platforms.

A hammock or elevated bivouac is strongly preferable to sleeping directly on the ground. If using a tent, bring a robust groundsheet and expect water pressure from below as well as rain from above. A tent that is merely rainproof may not be enough; you want a shelter that can tolerate saturated ground, dripping canopy, and insects.

Do not attach shelter lines to living structures indiscriminately. In this forest, what appears to be one tree may be a load-bearing weave of trunks, roots, epiphytes, and deadwood. Pulling on a “branch” can dislodge an entire wet mass. Test every anchor carefully.

Keep all dry equipment in roll-top bags inside your pack. Use a dedicated dry bag for:

- Insulation
- Sleeping gear
- Medical supplies
- Electronics
- Fire-starting materials
- Documents and return-device components

A fire may be difficult, damaging, or prohibited. Bring a stove designed for wet conditions rather than counting on local fuel. If fires are permitted, use established hard surfaces or raised fire pans; never clear moss, peat, or rootfelt to make a fire site. Underground peat and root fires can spread invisibly.

## Medical Precautions

This ecosystem likely contains abundant insects, fungi, aquatic parasites, skin irritants, and microbes. Have a medical consultation before travel, ideally with someone familiar with cross-reality biological exposure.

Carry:

- Broad-spectrum wound cleaning supplies.
- Sterile dressings and waterproof bandages.
- Antiseptic.
- Antihistamines.
- Anti-inflammatory medication.
- Antidiarrheals and oral rehydration salts.
- Antifungal cream or powder.
- Blister supplies.
- Tweezers and tick/invertebrate removal tools.
- A compression bandage and splinting material.
- Any recommended antiparasitic or antimicrobial medication.
- A filtration mask or light respirator for mold-heavy enclosed ruins or spore clouds.
- Personal epinephrine if you have any history of severe allergy.

Clean even small cuts promptly. In a warm wet environment, minor abrasions can become infected quickly.

Check your body each evening for:

- Leeches or attached invertebrates.
- Tick-like organisms.
- Rashes between toes and under pack straps.
- Punctures hidden by wet clothing.
- Swelling or numbness from prolonged immersion.
- Fever, confusion, unusual fatigue, or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Do not touch glowing fungi, unfamiliar flowers, amphibians, or the contents of plant-held water with bare skin. “Beautiful and damp” is not a useful safety category.

## Animals and How to Behave Around Them

Most animals in the high country appear adapted to quiet coexistence. Your greatest danger is likely not predation, but startling something large, contaminating a habitat, or walking into a place that animals consider theirs.

### Small life

Assume every pool, leaf basin, hanging garden, and patch of decay is occupied.

- Do not reach blindly into water, roots, holes, or dense vegetation.
- Shake clothing and footwear before putting them on.
- Keep food sealed.
- Use insect nets when resting, especially near water.
- Avoid insect repellent that could wash into pools; use treated clothing and physical barriers where possible.

### Amphibians

The broad-backed amphibians and delicate larvae described in local accounts may be sensitive to oils, sunscreen, soap, and pathogens carried from other worlds. Observe without handling. Disinfect boots and gear before arrival and after departure to prevent introducing organisms into their waters.

### Quiet six-limbed grazers

These sound cautious rather than aggressive. Give them space. Do not approach for photographs, and do not corner one against water, roots, or a fallen trunk. If one notices you, remain still or retreat slowly. Never interpret pale eyes or stillness as friendliness.

### The great animals

The old, packed corridors are not trails for travelers. They are roads for residents much larger than you.

If you find:

- Broad, firm corridors through vegetation,
- Freshly crushed plants,
- Churned pool margins,
- Deep depressions,
- Large fresh droppings,
- Unexplained silence among insects and birds,
- A nearby exhalation, branch movement, or ground vibration,

leave the area calmly and promptly. Move off the corridor rather than along it. Do not run unless immediate physical danger makes it unavoidable; running through rootfelt is an efficient way to fall, and sudden movement may be interpreted as prey behavior or panic.

Never camp beside a giant-animal path. Never investigate a “new hill,” trough, or mound that appears to have formed overnight. It may not be fully asleep.

If you hear the forest go quiet, stop talking. Listen. Determine whether the silence has a direction. Then withdraw slowly toward known firm ground.

## Ruins, Platforms, and Signs of People

The few structures in the uplands should be treated as culturally and ecologically significant, even if apparently abandoned.

Do not:

- Enter an unstable shelter.
- Remove stones, timber, woven materials, carvings, or artifacts.
- Sleep on circular platforms without local permission.
- Cut roots away from structures to “reveal” them.
- Attempt excavation.
- Treat a platform or ruin as a convenient dry camp without checking for ownership, ritual use, or structural stability.

These sites may be occupied intermittently, maintained by people who leave few obvious signs, or used by local animals as refuge and nesting ground. The circular timber platforms ringed with stones in particular should be approached quietly, observed from the edge, and left undisturbed if their purpose is unknown.

If you encounter inhabitants, let them establish the terms of contact. Keep hands visible, do not stride directly into settlements or shelters, and do not assume that apparent abandonment means permission.

A good general rule here is: if something looks as though the forest has almost swallowed it, do not help it finish the process.

## Hygiene and Contamination Control

You are entering an ecosystem with immense biological density and likely deep ecological interdependence. Avoid bringing your own world with you.

Before entry:

- Clean mud and seeds from boots, clothing, packs, and tent stakes.
- Disinfect soles and equipment.
- Use unscented, biodegradable hygiene products sparingly.
- Avoid carrying loose plant material, untreated natural fibers, or soil-contaminated gear from another ecosystem.

During travel:

- Pack out all waste.
- Use a sealed toilet system where practical.
- Never wash soap, sunscreen, detergent, or cookware directly in streams and pools.
- Do not discard food scraps.
- Do not release insects, pets, microbes, seeds, or “harmless” biological samples.

After departure:

- Quarantine and clean gear according to your origin world’s biosecurity standards.
- Inspect packs, seams, boots, and clothing for hitchhiking eggs, spores, seeds, and small animals.

The high country incorporates what it is given. Do not give it invasive species, plastic, batteries, pathogens, or the expectation that it will forgive carelessness.

## Light and Communications

The underforest can be dim at noon and genuinely dark at night. Bring more light than you think you need, but use it carefully.

Pack:

- A high-quality headlamp.
- Spare batteries sealed against moisture.
- A low-lumen red-light mode for camp use.
- A secondary waterproof flashlight.
- Chemical light markers only for emergencies, and only if you can retrieve them afterward.

Avoid sweeping bright beams through the canopy or pools. You may disturb night-active animals and attract insects. At night, move slowly. Bioluminescent fungi and blue flowers can be beautiful, but they are not reliable navigation aids and may lead you toward wet, unstable ground.

Keep your emergency beacon accessible on your body, not buried in your pack. If you fall into water or are separated from equipment, the beacon should stay with you.

## Mindset

The high country rewards patience more than bravery.

Do not fight the wetness. Manage it.  
Do not try to force a straight route. Adapt to the terrain.  
Do not seek silence as proof that nothing is there. Silence is often information.  
Do not mistake slow change for lack of danger.  
Do not assume anything inert is actually inert.

Travel slowly enough to notice changes in footing, water level, sound, and animal behavior. Take breaks before you are exhausted. Drink before you are thirsty. Turn back before the route becomes uncertain. In this place, the difference between a prudent traveler and a lost one is often only an hour of stubbornness.

Most importantly, leave with as little trace as possible. The country will erase evidence of you in time, but that is not permission to burden it. Its defining principle is incorporation, not hospitality. Visit as a guest who understands that every surface is already in use.
