## Before You Commit to the Crossing

The Dry Orchard is not a place an ordinary baseline human should visit casually, even if it appears civilized and inhabited. It is a high-altitude, nearly waterless, electrically active ecosystem whose infrastructure is alive, legally protected, and responsive to damage. It can probably host offworld visitors, especially because its caravans carry sealed atmospheres for incompatible species, but you should arrange sponsorship and environmental support in advance.

Do not arrive as an unscheduled pedestrian with a backpack and assume you can buy what you need locally.

At minimum, confirm:

- **Atmospheric composition and pressure.** The air is described as thin; “breathable” may still mean altitude-sickness territory for a human, and local plants and weather pylons may alter trace gases or electrical conditions.
- **Radiation conditions.** The dust ring and high-altitude setting imply reduced ordinary sunlight but potentially elevated cosmic, ultraviolet, charged-particle, or electromagnetic exposure. Ask for current pylon-field advisories.
- **Biological compatibility.** The city’s plants contain microscopic machines and conductive tissues. Its organisms may not be infectious in a conventional sense, but they may react chemically, electrically, or ecologically to foreign biology.
- **Quarantine requirements.** A place built on tightly managed living systems is likely extremely serious about spores, microbes, seeds, dead organic matter, and unregistered machinery.
- **Your return route.** The transit wells alter gravity. If you are arriving through a multiversal method, arrange a stable return point somewhere protected and clearly authorized. Do not conceal an active portal, beacon, or anchor in the root web.

A local host, licensed guide, or caravan affiliation is not a luxury. It is your primary safety equipment.

# Essential Equipment

## 1. A sealed personal habitat layer

Bring a compact environmental suit or at least a high-altitude respirator system with:

- Supplemental oxygen
- Carbon dioxide monitoring
- Humidity control
- Dust filtration rated for fine mineral, metal, pollen, and potentially conductive particles
- Eye protection that seals fully
- Skin coverage, including gloves and neck seal
- A positive-pressure mode for storms and dense spore zones
- A medical-grade pulse oximeter and altitude-sickness medication

The Dry Orchard may have breathable air, but “I can breathe” is not the same as “I can remain functional for six hours.” High altitude impairs judgment before it feels dangerous. You do not want to make etiquette mistakes around living load-bearing architecture because you are mildly hypoxic.

Your suit should be electrically insulated but **not electrically invisible**. Ask local engineers what grounding protocol visitors must use. The city distributes charge through plants, pylons, and roots; an improperly insulated traveler might be mistaken for an electrical fault, while an improperly grounded traveler may become one.

## 2. Water, but discreetly managed

Bring your own drinking water in a sealed, reusable internal reservoir. Plan for more than you would normally need: dry air, exertion, altitude, and sealed gear all increase fluid demand.

However, do not casually pour, spill, discard, or advertise water. In a civilization adapted to having none, free liquid water may be:

- Biologically disruptive
- Valuable enough to be politically sensitive
- Dangerous around electrical root systems
- A contamination issue
- A temptation to local organisms

Use a closed hydration system. Catch sweat and waste if your equipment permits. Do not rinse equipment outdoors. Do not urinate on vegetation, stone, or into cracks. Assume every crack is occupied.

If you need to dispose of water or biological waste, ask specifically where it may be surrendered. The answer may involve digestion gardens, controlled reclamation systems, or sealed foreign-species waste containers.

## 3. Protective clothing for dry, abrasive, reactive terrain

Wear:

- Tough, flexible boots with broad soles and closed tread patterns
- Outer layers resistant to abrasion and snagging
- A dust cloak or over-shell that can be shed and sealed
- A helmet or hard hood for falling seed-pods, snapping tendrils, and low architectural growth
- Hearing protection with vibration-transmitting capability

Avoid open sandals, exposed hair, loose fabric, dangling tools, or anything that can become caught in a tightening bridge or reactive vegetation.

Your boots should not use aggressive spikes. The ground is a mat of living stems and structural roots, not ordinary turf. A careless boot placement may be comparable to stepping on insulation, plumbing, wiring, and a cultivated crop simultaneously.

## 4. Light discipline

The city uses biological lighting, signal systems, and perhaps photosensitive or electrosensitive fauna. Bring a controllable lamp with:

- Red or amber low-light mode
- Diffuse rather than concentrated beam settings
- No uncontrolled flashing mode
- Manual shutoff
- A physical cover

Do not use bright white searchlights, laser pointers, strobes, or decorative drones without permission. In the Dry Orchard, light may be communication, feeding stimulus, navigation cue, reproductive trigger, or an emergency signal.

## 5. Analog navigation and translation

Bring:

- A local map in physical form
- A compass only if confirmed useful; local fields may make it unreliable
- A mechanical or inertial navigation system
- Offline translation support
- A notebook and pencil, not only electronic devices
- A way to record vibration patterns, warnings, and route markings

The city’s infrastructure may be able to communicate through root fibers, light, odors, conductive contact, or low-frequency vibration. Your phone will not necessarily understand the most urgent warning in the area: a vault-hound signal coming through the ground.

# The Most Important Rule: Treat the City as a Living Building

The Dry Orchard is not “a city with plants.” It is a city partly made of plants, supported by plants, powered by plants, inhabited by plants, archived by plants, and regulated through plants.

Assume that any visible stalk, root, membrane, leaf, tower, bridge, lattice, or mat has a function.

It may be:

- A structural support
- A data conduit
- A heat exchanger
- An electrical line
- A water-vapor collector
- A breeding structure
- A civic boundary marker
- A protected organism
- A memory archive
- A warning organ
- Someone’s home

Do not cut, snap, pick, peel, carve, taste, prune, lean heavily on, or “sample” anything. Do not insert sensors into cracks or tissues. Do not collect seeds, bark, spores, insects, mineral blossoms, or shed leaves as souvenirs.

Even apparently dead material may not be dead in the relevant local sense.

The municipal principle—**“Nothing dead may remain separate”**—should be understood broadly. It likely means that death, decay, discarded material, and broken machinery all enter the city’s managed metabolic cycle. You should ask what this means for:

- Food scraps
- Hair
- Nail clippings
- Bloodied bandages
- Used filters
- Broken devices
- Lost jewelry
- Damaged clothing
- Corpses, if disaster planning becomes relevant

Do not assume you are permitted to reclaim an item after it has been “planted,” processed, or absorbed. In the Dry Orchard, disposal may be a permanent transfer of material and information.

# Health and Medical Preparation

## Altitude

If you are baseline human and unacclimatized, arrive gradually if possible. Spend a day or two in a pressure-controlled visitor habitat before taking long walks outside.

Know symptoms of altitude illness:

- Headache that does not improve with rest
- Nausea
- Unusual fatigue
- Confusion or poor coordination
- Breathlessness while resting
- Persistent cough, chest tightness, or blue lips
- Feeling oddly calm about obviously bad decisions

The last is especially dangerous around transit wells, high bridges, and storm preparations.

If symptoms become significant, descend or enter a pressurized local habitat. Do not “push through” because the city is beautiful.

## Dryness

The landscape has no ordinary open water and little ambient humidity. Your eyes, nose, throat, and skin will dry quickly. Use:

- Preservative-free eye lubrication
- Lip protection
- Nasal moisture management
- Skin barrier cream under your suit
- Controlled hydration

Be cautious with strong fragrances, alcohol vapors, aerosols, and volatile solvents. A dry electrical ecosystem may react badly to flammable or chemically conspicuous emissions.

## Electrical exposure

Some animals drink electricity. Plants draw atmospheric charge. Pylons distribute it. Conductive fibers run through building walls. The environment may produce static discharge strong enough to harm implants, interfere with pacemakers, or induce panic if you are not prepared.

Before visiting, disclose to your host:

- Pacemakers and implanted pumps
- Cochlear implants
- Neurostimulators
- Insulin pumps
- Prosthetics
- Medical sensors
- Metal implants, if their field environments are powerful
- Any device that transmits automatically

Carry Faraday shielding for sensitive electronics and a manual backup for critical medical gear. Do not assume charging ports, wireless systems, or magnetic closures are safe near root networks.

# Fauna Safety

The Dry Orchard’s animals are not decorative.

## Grazers

The reflective six-legged grazers may look calm, but they crop mineral blossoms and interface electrically with grounding stalks. Give them room. Do not approach during feeding, turning, mating, migration, or electrical contact. Do not stand between a herd and a grounding patch.

Their reflective down may create confusing flashes in the perpetual twilight. Wear eye protection, and avoid startling them with mirrors, camera flashes, or active scanning beams.

## Small nesting creatures

Thread-mice, foil-winged insects, and glowing-footed climbers may be harmless individually but can cause equipment problems. Keep packs sealed. Inspect boots and suit seams before entering indoor spaces. Do not brush creatures away into cracks; ask a local attendant how to remove them without damaging their habitat.

## Predators

The most concerning predators are those that listen for electrical activity rather than heartbeats. An ordinary human in powered gear may be conspicuous.

Ask whether your equipment needs a “quiet field” setting, shielded battery pack, or approved grounding tether. Do not camp outside city boundaries without a local guide and a protected shelter. If a local tells you to deactivate unnecessary electronics, do so immediately.

Do not try to hide from an electrical predator by shutting off your lamp and running. You may be broadcasting more clearly through your nervous system, gear, or panic-driven movement than you realize.

## Vault-hounds

Treat vault-hounds with the caution you would give working dogs, wolves, and public warning sirens at the same time.

They may be companions to caravans and culturally valued animals, but their calls travel through the city substrate and carry practical meaning. Learn the local basics before leaving a visitor center or host dwelling:

- What warning vibration means **stop moving**
- What means **get indoors**
- What means **leave a bridge**
- What means **do not touch the ground**
- What means **an intrusion or excavation has been detected**
- What signal indicates a dust front

Do not imitate their calls, play recordings, use subwoofers, or stomp rhythmic patterns for amusement. You could trigger confusion, summon animals, obscure a real alarm, or accidentally declare something alarming.

# Storm Procedure

Dust storms are inevitable enough that you should arrange your visit around them.

A storm in the Dry Orchard is not just high wind. It is a moving mass of powdered stone, salts, pollen, and metal flakes. It can abrade exposed skin, infiltrate gear, disrupt electronics, conduct charge, and turn a familiar corridor into a sealed living tunnel.

If you notice the horizon dimming further, distant structures contracting, leaves rolling shut, or locals changing pace:

1. **Stop sightseeing.**
2. **Follow your host or the nearest marked shelter route.**
3. **Do not enter an unfamiliar transit well to escape the storm.**
4. **Seal suit vents and activate eye protection.**
5. **Power down nonessential electronics.**
6. **Do not force open closing plant corridors, membranes, or doors.**
7. **Stay off exposed bridges unless specifically directed across.**
8. **Do not shelter under wild growth outside the city; it may fold, seal, or shed material.**

Inside a communal hollow, expect close quarters, constant structural noise, dim biologically produced lamps, and a possible loss of ordinary wireless connectivity. Prepare psychologically for this. The sound of wind transmitted through living walls may feel uncannily like voices, breathing, or distant machinery. It does not necessarily mean there is a problem.

After the storm, do not immediately walk outside. The city expands and repairs itself visibly. Paths may have shifted, new shoots may occupy old routes, and dust may still be sorting through charged surfaces. Wait for local clearance.

# Transit Wells and Altered Gravity

The circular cliff apertures are not tourist elevators. They are gravity-tuned transit systems.

Before entering one:

- Secure all loose items.
- Stow liquids and fragile equipment.
- Disable any device that depends on a fixed gravitational orientation unless it is rated for variable gravity.
- Ask whether the route involves reduced gravity, lateral gravity, freefall intervals, rotation, or field inversion.
- Do not enter while dizzy, dehydrated, sedated, panicked, or altitude-sick.
- Keep one hand available for an approved rail, tether, or guide line.
- Follow local body-position instructions exactly.

Your vestibular system may interpret slow sideways descent as falling, even if you are safe. Nausea, disorientation, and involuntary grabbing are likely hazards. Do not grab walls, roots, or glowing fibers unless the guide explicitly tells you they are handholds.

If you have retinal issues, joint instability, severe motion sickness, cardiovascular problems, seizure disorders, or implanted electronics, obtain medical clearance before using a transit well.

# Social and Legal Etiquette

The Dry Orchard’s laws are likely rooted in ecological obligations rather than property lines alone. A traveler who acts “normally” by urban standards could cause genuine offense or damage.

## Ask before touching anything

This includes walls, furniture, doors, floors, public shade structures, flowers, roots, and old ruins. A hollow pod may be a home; a stalk may be a shared utility line; a decorative-looking membrane may be a respiratory organ for the district.

## Never leave waste behind

Keep a sealed waste kit. If you drop something, report it rather than quietly retrieving it from a root mat. If an object is swallowed, overgrown, or incorporated, do not dig for it.

## Do not separate dead matter

Avoid killing insects, uprooting “weeds,” discarding food, or throwing away damaged equipment. If you must deal with animal remains, blood, or a dead companion organism, contact authorities or your host. Local digestion gardens may be funerary, industrial, archival, and sacred all at once.

## Respect broadcasting periods

A flowering tower “broadcasting” is not to be harvested. More broadly, avoid loud noise, high-output transmitters, scent release, drone flight, or structural work near flowering, glowing, pulsing, or audibly resonant growth unless authorized. You may be interrupting reproduction, communication, civic data transfer, or ceremonial activity.

## Do not excavate

Not even a small hole for a tent stake, sample tube, cache, or latrine. The root web detects intrusion, and the city may interpret unauthorized digging as sabotage. If you need to install an anchor, environmental sensor, or dimensional apparatus, use a licensed site chosen by local authorities.

## Be honest about your biology and your dead

This is a place where patterns are preserved through biological incorporation. If you are carrying remains, medical samples, heirloom tissue, a pet’s ashes, a data-bearing artifact, or a machine containing private information, state this clearly. Do not allow something personally sensitive to enter a digestion garden by accident.

# Food, Trade, and Hospitality

Do not assume local food is suitable for humans.

Much of the ecosystem is based on mineral chemistry, dry biomass, conductive tissues, and biological machines. A local edible may be nutritionally useless, toxic, abrasive, electrically active, or simply incompatible with human digestion.

Bring shelf-stable food initially, ideally:

- Low-odor
- Low-crumb
- Fully sealed
- Easy to account for
- Free of seeds and viable microbes
- Packaged in materials approved for disposal or return transport

Ask before opening food outdoors. Crumbs may attract animals or introduce foreign nutrient sources into an ecosystem built around scarcity.

For gifts, avoid offering water casually. A sealed water sample might be prestigious, dangerous, insulting, or all three depending on context. Better visitor gifts include:

- Durable hand tools approved for local materials
- Art, music, or non-invasive cultural records
- Repairable mechanical objects
- Seeds only if specifically requested and sterilized/regulated
- Maps, stories, or technical knowledge that does not threaten local systems
- Carefully curated materials from your origin, declared in advance

Do not bring ordinary terrestrial plants, soil, compost, untreated wood, live insects, or pets.

# Suggested Itinerary for a First Visit

## First day: remain inside the managed core

- Arrive at an authorized station or sealed visitor chamber.
- Complete decontamination and atmospheric acclimatization.
- Learn storm signals and vault-hound vibration warnings.
- Receive a local grounding tag, route marker, or wearable guide system if offered.
- Visit indoor or enclosed living architecture.
- Practice a short transit route only if medically cleared.
- Observe the city’s night-opening behavior from a protected platform rather than walking beneath it.

## Second day: guided city walk

- Walk only established corridors.
- Cross one approved root-bridge with a guide.
- Visit a terrace, market, memory-bearing plaza, or botanical workshop.
- Observe a pylon from its designated public distance.
- Learn how the city distinguishes private growth, civic growth, and wild growth.
- Return well before a forecast dust front.

## Later: wilderness or caravan travel

Only after you understand the basics should you travel beyond the Dry Orchard. Outside the city, there may be miles of sparse growth with little shelter, unreliable navigation, predators responsive to electricity, and no water sources.

A proper expedition should include:

- Local guide
- Caravan or approved walker transport
- Weather/pylon communication access
- Sealed sleep habitat
- Independent oxygen and water reserves
- Grounding and predator-avoidance protocol
- Emergency location beacon approved by local authorities
- A written agreement about waste, death, salvage, and lost gear

Do not walk between settlements alone, even if you can see the next glow on the horizon. Distance, thin air, featureless dark terrain, dust, and subtly changing plant infrastructure will make the journey far harder than it appears.

# Things Most Likely to Get a Visitor Hurt

1. **Underestimating altitude and dehydration.**
2. **Treating the root mat as ordinary ground.**
3. **Being outdoors when a dust storm begins.**
4. **Following an interesting sound or glow off an approved path.**
5. **Using active electronics, drones, or transmitters without local guidance.**
6. **Approaching animals during electrical feeding.**
7. **Ignoring vibration warnings because they are not audible alarms.**
8. **Entering a gravity transit well while ill or confused.**
9. **Improperly disposing of waste, biological material, or broken equipment.**
10. **Trying to collect a living souvenir.**

# The Right Mindset

The Dry Orchard is not a dead desert with an oasis of civilization imposed upon it. It is a civilization that learned the desert’s own methods of survival: slowness, interdependence, efficient reuse, patience, and an intimate awareness that every structure is also a living process.

Go prepared to be inconvenienced.

Go prepared to ask permission.

Go prepared to keep your belongings, your body, your waste, and your technology carefully contained.

Most importantly, go prepared to abandon the usual distinction between nature and infrastructure. In the Dry Orchard, the bridge beneath your feet may be growing, listening, remembering, and deciding whether to hold you.
