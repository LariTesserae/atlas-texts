This is not a place for an unassisted swim, casual exploration, or “just a few minutes underwater.” Prepare as though entering a confined, fully submerged ruin, a cold spring, a wetland root-mass, and a minor spatial anomaly at the same time.

The room is small, but that is not safety. Small spaces underwater become dangerous quickly: silt removes visibility, plants entangle, old masonry shifts, and a single blocked route can turn a short visit into a fatal one. The place’s stranger features—the ceiling that permits passage, the crack that redirects objects sideways, animals appearing where they cannot have traveled—mean that ordinary navigation cannot be assumed to work consistently.

## Before you go

### Do not go alone
Bring at least one trained dive partner, ideally two, who understand confined-water and overhead-environment procedures. At least one person should remain outside the submerged interior, maintaining a line, timing the visit, and prepared to summon help.

Do not rely on someone who is merely a strong swimmer. This location requires people comfortable with:

- Cold water
- Low visibility
- Silt-out conditions
- Overhead environments
- Entanglement hazards
- Controlled buoyancy
- Emergency air sharing
- The psychological strain of darkness and confinement

If your multiverse travel method permits it, establish an external return anchor before entering: a physical tether, a monitored portal, a marked threshold, or another stable route back to your originating world. Do not assume the room’s doorway, ceiling, floor crack, or open water above will lead back to the same place twice.

### Learn the local hydrology first
Observe the basin from outside for a full cycle if possible: daylight to darkness, calm weather to rain, or at least several hours. The basin may be fed by a floodplain, drowned forest, overflow channel, or something less ordinary. Note:

- Water level and rate of rise
- Current direction near the entrance
- Weather conditions
- Visibility
- Any signs of fresh bank failure
- Changes in the pale light above
- Whether debris entering the water reappears somewhere unexpected

Avoid entering during storms, rising floodwater, or periods when the basin pressure noticeably increases. A place that changes “slowly” can still change suddenly when its larger watershed shifts.

### Decide in advance what counts as success
Do not arrive with a vague urge to investigate everything. This room invites lingering. Its character is patient, intimate, and contemplative; it may make an explorer feel that remaining still is harmless. Set a specific purpose:

- Confirming safe entrance and exit
- Photographing or sketching the layout
- Observing the spring from a distance
- Testing a fixed line route
- Collecting only nonliving water samples, if appropriate
- Making one short contact visit

Then leave. Do not make your first visit an excavation, salvage effort, biological survey, or attempt to enter the floor crack.

## Essential equipment

### Breathing and thermal protection
Use proper cold-water diving equipment, not improvised breathing gear.

Bring:

- A drysuit or suitably rated cold-water wetsuit
- Hood, gloves, and insulated boots
- Redundant air supply: twin cylinders, sidemount, or a pony bottle sufficient for an independent exit
- Two regulators where feasible
- Reliable pressure gauges, preferably with redundancy
- Dive computer plus a non-electronic backup timer/depth gauge if local physics interferes with electronics
- Emergency surface signaling equipment, kept outside or at the access point

The spring is cold enough to make nearby growth recoil. Treat it as a hypothermia hazard even if the surrounding water seems merely cool. Do not put bare skin into its flow. A sudden cold shock can trigger panic, cramping, regulator free-flow, or impaired judgment.

### Navigation and visibility
The room’s water is dark, vegetation-heavy, and easily silted. Bring:

- One primary dive light and at least two backups
- Lights with simple mechanical controls, not touch-only interfaces
- A continuous guideline and reels
- Line arrows or tactile directional markers
- A slate and pencil
- A blunt line cutter plus trauma shears, mounted where either hand can reach them
- A compact backup mask

Do not trust the apparent geometry of the room. A doorway that seems to lead to one wall may not do so. A ceiling may fail to behave as a ceiling. Mark your route physically, and use touch as much as sight.

Run a continuous guideline from a stable external point to the point of deepest intended penetration. Keep it tensioned but not tight enough to pull loose fragile masonry. Make the line distinctive by touch—knots, clips, or directional markers at intervals—because visibility may vanish.

### Documentation
If you document the site, favor passive methods.

Useful items:

- Waterproof notebook and graphite pencil
- Still camera with fixed-focus capability and a tether
- Water sample vials, clearly labeled before entry
- Temperature probe
- Passive depth logger
- Nonmagnetic measuring tape
- Small sealed specimen bags for detached, nonliving material only

Do not bring loud pumps, suction samplers, sediment corers, large lighting rigs, or anything that stirs the mud. Do not deploy nets. The room’s ecology and its anomalies both appear sensitive to disturbance.

Electronic equipment may behave unpredictably. Keep written notes. If a camera records impossible arrangements, do not immediately treat the image as a map; compare it with your line markers and your partner’s independent observations.

## How to enter

Enter slowly, feet first if the route permits, and pause at the threshold. Let your breathing settle. Check buoyancy before approaching the vegetation or floor.

The room should be treated as an overhead environment even if there seems to be open water above. The visible “upper boundary” is unreliable. You may perceive daylight, open water, or an apparent passage, but do not ascend through the ceiling simply because silver motes do. Those motes may be able to pass where a human body, equipment, or mind cannot.

On first entry:

1. Secure the guideline outside.
2. Enter only far enough to establish the doorway and one stable reference point.
3. Confirm that each traveler can find the exit by touch with eyes closed.
4. Observe for several minutes without touching anything.
5. Leave before air, temperature, or attention becomes a concern.

A successful first visit may last only five or ten minutes.

## Rules inside the flooded room

### Do not touch the crack in the floor
The fresh tear is the primary danger.

Objects near it cease to obey ordinary descent. They pause, turn horizontal, and travel sideways into the wall. This means the crack may be connected to a lateral flow, hidden void, alternate space, or a directional fault in local gravity. None of these are suitable for human testing.

Maintain a generous exclusion zone around it—at least several body lengths, more if the room allows. Do not:

- Put hands, tools, cameras, or lines into it
- Drop markers into it
- Release dye or tracer material
- Place weights nearby
- Dig away mud
- Sample water directly at the mouth
- Attempt to follow debris into the wall

If a piece of equipment is pulled toward the crack, cut it free rather than retrieve it. Equipment is replaceable. A traveler is not.

### Do not disturb the mud
The mud is fresh over a “younger wound” in the ground. It may conceal unstable clay, sharp fissures, buried objects, or additional openings. A fin kick can obscure the room; a handhold can trigger a collapse; a probe can widen the seam.

Use frog kicks or careful pulls on your own line. Maintain neutral buoyancy. Never kneel, stand, or brace against the floor.

### Avoid the vegetation and roots
The weed ribbons are not merely decoration. They occupy all available space and may be under mild tension from the current. Root-hairs have stitched masonry into silt. Pulling one may move more than a plant.

Do not force a path through dense growth. If the route is blocked, turn back. A room no wider than a small room does not require penetration through vegetation to be meaningfully visited.

If entangled:

1. Stop moving.
2. Do not spin or pull hard.
3. Signal your partner.
4. Locate the snag calmly.
5. Cut only the minimum necessary material.
6. Reacquire the guideline before proceeding.

### Treat all objects as landmarks, not souvenirs
Leave the brass handle, ladder rung, jar, bench, iron frames, shelf, snails, and fungus undisturbed.

The glass jar is especially important. The darker sphere of water inside may be a stable boundary between water masses, a self-contained pocket, or a local anomaly. Do not insert fingers, probes, containers, lights, or collected material into it. Do not seal the jar, tip it, move it, or break it.

The ladder should not be climbed. It may be structurally weak, spatially incomplete, or oriented toward a destination that is not physically above or below.

### Do not feed, capture, or pursue the fish
The blind fish is not behaving like an ordinary fish, but that is no reason to harass it. If it appears, remain still and observe from a respectful distance. Do not shine high-intensity light directly at it. Do not attempt to block its path, follow it through a wall opening, or infer a safe route from where it goes.

Likewise, do not collect snails or fungal material. The snails’ trails appearing ahead of them suggest that their movement may not be fully sequential. Taking one may create problems that are not apparent until later.

### Keep conversation minimal and hand signals clear
Sound is said to disperse before it can gather. Assume verbal communication will be poor. Use standard dive hand signals, light signals, and line tugs. Agree on them beforehand.

Avoid repeated tapping on walls, striking tools, or making noise to “test” the room. The place already contains a suggestion of an old waiting hum. There is no advantage in trying to make it answer.

## Psychological preparation

The main mental hazard may be the room’s atmosphere of recognition: the sense that it is waiting not for rescue or collapse, but for someone to understand it. Do not mistake that feeling for an instruction.

Before entry, agree on these principles:

- Curiosity is not consent.
- Familiarity is not safety.
- Beauty is not benignity.
- A route that appears after you think about it may not be an exit.
- A thought such as “I should stay a little longer” is a cue to check air, line, and partner—not a reason to stay.

Use a fixed turnaround rule. For example: turn when one-third of the planned air supply has been used, when either diver feels unusually reluctant to leave, when the water temperature drops sharply, or when the guideline behaves unexpectedly.

If anyone experiences lost time, memory gaps, compulsive thoughts, hearing a hum become distinct, a sensation of being watched by the architecture, or certainty that they have visited before when they have not, exit immediately. Record the experience outside, separately, before comparing notes. Do not re-enter the same day.

## After leaving

Once outside:

- Confirm everyone is physically present and responsive.
- Check for hypothermia, cuts, clay exposure, and entanglement injuries.
- Inspect gear for unfamiliar sediment, roots, spores, shells, or water trapped in places it should not be.
- Quarantine samples and gear before bringing them into a home, vehicle, laboratory, or other world.
- Wash equipment away from natural waterways.
- Record the route, time, weather, depth, water temperature, and any discrepancies between travelers’ accounts.

Pay particular attention to anything that seems to have changed location without explanation: a snail shell on gear, a root caught inside a sealed container, a dark bead of water that does not mix with ordinary water, or clay flakes appearing where no clay was carried. Do not discard such material into drains or open water. Keep it sealed, labeled, and away from living plants and animals until you understand it.

For the next several days, monitor dreams, recurring sensations of pressure in the ears, urges to return, or the impression that nearby rooms are becoming damp or overgrown. These may be ordinary aftereffects of a cold, stressful dive—but in multiversal travel, it is prudent to log them.

## The right attitude

Go as a guest, not a conqueror or rescuer.

The flooded room is already becoming something else: not ruined architecture, not exactly wetland, not exactly memory. Its life is slow, interwoven, and apparently accustomed to boundaries becoming permeable. Your safest visit is one that leaves almost no mark: enter briefly, observe carefully, keep hold of the line that leads home, and resist the urge to make the place declare what it is.
