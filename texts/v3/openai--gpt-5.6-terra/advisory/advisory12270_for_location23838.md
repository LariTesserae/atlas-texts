## Preparation for the High Plateau

This is not a place to visit casually, even if you are an experienced hiker in other worlds. The main danger is not a single predator, hostile power, or obvious hazard. It is the mismatch between appearances and reality.

From a distance, the plateau looks open, level, and navigable. In practice it is a cold, wind-struck stone desert interrupted by ravines that can become tropical, vertical, flooded, and nearly impassable within minutes. Water is present everywhere in principle and unavailable almost everywhere in practice. Shelter exists, but is difficult to reach. The land changes its routes after every serious storm.

Treat it as an expedition into an elevated wilderness whose weather, terrain, and local customs are all part of the same survival system.

---

## Before You Go

### Do not travel alone

An ordinary human should travel with at least one competent local guide, preferably two if entering unfamiliar sections of the plateau or traveling during the wet season.

The remembered routes are not simply trails. They are a body of knowledge: which dark seams give drinkable seep water, which ravines have climbable approaches, which dry channels are safe to cross under a clear sky, and which apparently stable ledges become runoff funnels when clouds catch the rim.

A map will be useful, but it will not be sufficient. The most important features are temporary:

- watercourses after storms;
- exposed and buried cairns;
- fallen rock;
- vegetation mats that conceal holes;
- mist patterns around ravines;
- the sound and behavior of the singing pillars.

If you must travel independently, stay close to inhabited ravine mouths and do not attempt crossings of the central flats in uncertain weather.

### Learn the weather signs before setting out

The plateau’s storms are violent enough that timing matters more than speed. Ask local people how they read:

- cloud banks accumulating against the high rim;
- sudden stillness or shifts in wind direction;
- changes in fog rising from ravines;
- distant pillar tones;
- bird or lizard behavior, if any is visible;
- the smell of wet mineral dust or vegetation before rain;
- changes in the coldness or flow of seepage water.

Do not rely on weather intuition from your home world. A clear morning may be compatible with a flood-producing afternoon. Rain falling far away can still send water through the channel you are standing in.

### Plan for a slower journey than the landscape suggests

Open stone gives the illusion of easy travel. Wind, route-finding, elevation, lack of water, and detours around ravines will reduce progress sharply. Plan no more than a modest day’s travel between confirmed water and shelter points. If locals say a route takes a day, assume this includes knowledge you do not possess and allow significantly more time.

Carry enough food and stored water to remain immobile for at least two or three days if storms close your route.

## Clothing and Personal Protection

You will need equipment for cold wind, hard sun, soaking rain, heat reflected from stone, damp jungle ravines, and sharp rock—sometimes on the same day.

### Essential clothing

Bring or obtain:

- a windproof outer layer with a secure hood;
- waterproof clothing that remains functional under driven rain, not merely a light rain shell;
- insulating layers for night and for periods of rain and wind;
- breathable shirts and trousers for humid ravines;
- a broad-brimmed hat or sun hood that can be tied down;
- gloves: one durable pair for stone and rope work, one dry insulating pair kept sealed;
- sturdy ankle-supporting boots with aggressive wet-rock traction;
- spare socks, stored in truly waterproof wrapping;
- gaiters or leg coverings against wet vegetation, loose shale, and small biting life in the ravines;
- eye protection against wind-driven grit and intense reflected light.

Avoid loose garments. Strong wind will catch them, and vines will snag them. Avoid bright reflective metals or dangling ornaments near the pillars unless local hosts indicate they are acceptable; even if there is no physical danger, appearing careless around a site of cultural importance is a poor way to begin.

### Protect against sun and exposure

The high elevation and pale stone make sun exposure deceptively severe. Carry:

- sun protection for face, lips, ears, and hands;
- a neck covering;
- sunglasses or locally appropriate eye shades;
- electrolyte salts or a way to replace salt lost through sweat.

A cold wind can conceal dehydration. Drink on schedule rather than waiting for thirst.

## Water: Carry It, Find It, Treat It

Water is the central practical concern.

The plateau has wet places, mist, seepage, tree hollows, condensation under rock, and floodwater—but none of these guarantee safe, accessible drinking water. In fact, the abundance of temporary water makes it easier to make bad decisions.

### Carry more capacity than you think you need

Use multiple containers rather than one large vessel. At minimum, carry:

- one immediately accessible drinking container;
- one or two protected reserve containers;
- a collapsible container for collecting water during storms or at seep sites;
- a small cup, cloth, or scoop for recovering water from cracks and hollows.

Rigid containers may be safer on exposed flats; flexible skins or bladders are useful in ravines where space is limited. Protect all containers from abrasion against stone.

### Treat all water unless locals explicitly identify a source as safe

Water from dripping rock may be cleaner than pooled water, but it can still contain mineral contamination, animal waste, microbes, or unusual local chemistry. Water gathered from leaf pools and tree hollows is especially suspect.

Bring a robust treatment system with redundancy:

- filtration capable of handling sediment;
- chemical treatment or boiling capability;
- a metal vessel for heating water;
- pre-filters such as cloth for storm runoff;
- oral rehydration salts.

Be cautious around the colored mineral seams and crusted seepage basins. Green, red, metallic, or strongly flavored water may be rich in minerals that are not safe for an off-world human body. Do not assume “clear” means potable.

### Never shelter in a drainage channel

A dry channel can become a white, violent river with astonishing speed. This applies to shallow grooves in the stone, ravine floors, and even broad-looking basins. When clouds build, move to established high ground early—not merely to ground that looks slightly higher.

If trapped by a storm, prioritize getting above the channel system before trying to preserve a planned route.

## Navigation

### Bring analog navigation tools

Electronics may be useful, but do not depend on them. Heavy rain, fog, stone walls, unfamiliar magnetic conditions, and simple battery failure can all make them unreliable.

Carry:

- a physical map protected from water;
- compass, if it has been tested locally;
- altimeter or pressure-based elevation tool, again tested locally;
- notebook and pencil;
- signal whistle;
- bright emergency cloth or marker;
- cord for marking a temporary route in foggy ravine terrain.

Learn to recognize the local route markers:

- cairn styles;
- scratched cliff symbols;
- small stone windbreaks;
- pillar alignments;
- particular trees or cycads marking hidden fractures and water.

Do not alter markers unless you are certain of their purpose. A small pile of stones may be a route cue, a boundary, a memorial, a storm indicator, or an instruction meaningful only in context.

### Do not trust visibility

The plateau offers long views in clear weather and near-total disorientation in fog or storm. The rim may vanish. Ravines may appear suddenly beneath a cloud edge. Sounds carry strangely, and pillar tones can make a distant direction feel close.

When fog forms:

1. stop before entering unknown terrain;
2. confirm your last known marker;
3. establish shelter if conditions worsen;
4. use rope or physical contact between party members near cliff edges;
5. do not navigate toward a sound unless a local guide confirms it is meaningful.

Wind and water noises can disguise falls, approaching storms, and even nearby voices.

## Storm Protocol

The country’s storms deserve a formal plan, agreed upon before each day’s travel.

### When a storm is approaching

Immediately:

- leave channels, basins, narrow gullies, and ravine bottoms;
- move away from loose cliffs and steep vegetated walls;
- avoid isolated tall trees, exposed ridges, and the highest stone points;
- secure loose equipment;
- put on waterproof and insulating layers before you are wet;
- fill water containers if safe, but do not delay evacuation to do so;
- identify a sheltered position above runoff lines.

A good shelter is not necessarily a deep cave. Overhangs may collect runoff or hide unstable rock. Ask locals which shelters are used in storm weather. Established stone-and-reed shelters near settlements are worth seeking well before rain arrives.

### During the storm

Remain clear of:

- dry streambeds;
- rock shelves with channels leading across them;
- cliff bases;
- ravine mouths;
- isolated pillars;
- tall lone trees;
- unstable scree slopes;
- mineral crusts that may become slick or collapse over wet hollows.

Lightning safety matters if storms are electrical. Do not stand on exposed ridges or beside a tall singing pillar. The pillars’ bored channels and fitted stones may or may not conduct or attract electrical activity, but there is no reason for a visitor to test this.

Expect sound to become overwhelming. Establish nonverbal signals with your companions beforehand: hand squeezes, rope tugs, colored cloth signals, or simple gestures. In a storm, shouting may be useless.

### After the storm

Do not assume danger ends when rain stops.

The most dangerous period may be the hour afterward, when:

- delayed runoff reaches channels;
- saturated vegetation sheds stored water;
- rocks loosened by rain begin to fall;
- fog hides eroded ground;
- routes and markers are buried or displaced;
- newly wet stone becomes extremely slippery.

Wait until the flow visibly decreases and locals judge the ground stable. Refill water, inspect footwear and gear, and reassess the route rather than blindly resuming the original plan.

## Ravines: The Green Parts Are Not the Safe Parts

The ravines provide water, plants, shelter, and many settlements. They also contain vertical walls, hidden drops, falling debris, dense vines, wet stone, insects or parasites, and flash-flood risk.

Prepare for them as a separate environment.

Bring:

- a compact rope suited to assisting descents and crossings;
- webbing or harness equipment if routes require it;
- a headlamp with waterproof protection;
- insect protection, depending on local conditions;
- a small first-aid kit focused on cuts, sprains, blisters, infection prevention, and hypothermia;
- a machete only if local guides approve it—indiscriminate cutting will damage vegetation and may be culturally offensive;
- trekking poles or a sturdy staff for testing ground and balancing on wet stone.

Never step onto a mat of vegetation over a ravine floor without testing it. Roots and vines can conceal voids, unstable banks, or water channels. Do not grab unknown vines for support unless a guide confirms them; some may break, irritate skin, or shelter animals.

The warm, wet atmosphere can make you feel recovered after time on the cold flats. This is misleading. Humidity hides sweat loss, and the ravine can make it difficult to maintain direction or see incoming weather.

## Dealing With the Singing Pillars

Whether the pillars are ordinary wind instruments, old communication devices, sacred infrastructure, or something stranger, treat them with respect.

The locals’ behavior tells you enough:

- they do not camp near a pillar whose note changes at night;
- they do not cut nearby trees;
- they inspect the pillars after storms;
- pillar exposure or burial is used to interpret future weather;
- the sounds may carry social, practical, or spiritual meaning.

As a visitor:

- do not climb, sit on, lean gear against, or attempt to repair a pillar;
- do not insert objects into the holes;
- do not block, widen, scrape, measure aggressively, or “test” the sound;
- do not remove stones, moss, roots, or surrounding vegetation;
- do not camp nearby without explicit local permission;
- do not record ceremonies, tones, or interpretations unless invited;
- do not mock local precautions, even if you interpret the phenomenon differently.

If a pillar changes tone during the night, follow local advice immediately. At the least, it may indicate a change in wind direction, moisture, blockage, ground movement, or incoming weather. At most, you are in a place where local people have learned—through generations of costly experience—that this is not a sound to ignore.

A respectful traveler does not need to decide whether the land “speaks.” They only need to understand that people who survive here listen.

## Visiting Local Communities

The people of the ravines live in a place that demands cooperation, memory, and careful use of limited shelter and water. Do not arrive expecting hospitality to be effortless.

### Bring useful trade goods, not clutter

Appropriate gifts may include durable, repairable practical items, subject to local preferences:

- high-quality needles;
- cordage;
- fishhooks or copper hooks if that trade is welcomed;
- water-resistant cloth;
- sharpening stones;
- simple medical supplies with clear instructions;
- salt, if not locally abundant;
- reliable containers;
- repair tools.

Avoid giving items that create dependency, waste, or social disruption. Avoid assuming advanced technology is automatically welcome. A fragile solar device or sealed electronic gadget may be far less useful than a well-made needle, blade sheath, or rainproof fabric.

### Ask before using resources

Do not take water from a seep, cut reeds, gather fruit, enter a sheltered overhang, or sleep on a ledge near a settlement without permission. What appears abundant may be carefully managed. A particular tree hollow may be a household’s dry-season reserve. A dripping overhang may be a shared water point. A ravine may have an established sequence of access during drought or after storms.

### Learn basic etiquette early

Ask your guide or hosts:

- which places may not be entered;
- whether pillar sites have restrictions;
- who has authority to grant shelter;
- whether guests should assist with water collection, repair, cooking, or storm preparation;
- what gestures are used to signal danger in high wind;
- whether certain sounds, whistles, or fires are reserved for local use.

Offer labor. In an environment like this, useful assistance is often more respectful than excessive payment.

## Health and Emergency Preparedness

### Likely injuries and ailments

Prepare for:

- dehydration and salt loss;
- sunburn and windburn;
- hypothermia after soaking rain;
- slips, falls, sprains, and fractures;
- cuts from sharp volcanic stone;
- eye irritation from grit;
- infection from wet cuts;
- blisters and foot damage;
- panic or disorientation in fog, wind, and overwhelming noise;
- altitude effects, depending on the plateau’s actual elevation.

Carry a first-aid kit that you know how to use. Include wound cleaning supplies, sterile dressings, compression bandages, blister care, anti-inflammatory medicine if compatible with you, anti-diarrheal and rehydration supplies, allergy medication, and personal prescriptions in waterproof containers.

If travel between worlds permits medical screening, arrange it. The ravines’ water, fungi, insects, frogs, and plant life may be biologically unfamiliar. Avoid consuming local plants, animals, or fermented drinks until someone competent has established they are safe for your physiology.

### Establish extraction expectations

Before leaving a settled area, tell someone:

- your route;
- intended destination;
- planned return time;
- number of travelers;
- supplies carried;
- emergency signals;
- what to do if you do not return.

Do not assume aerial rescue is possible. Wind, cloud, escarpments, and lack of landing space may make extraction impossible precisely when it is most needed.

A simple rule is appropriate: if you are injured far from a known route or water point, stabilize, shelter, signal, and wait. Do not make a heroic march across exposed stone unless conditions are unquestionably safe and help is close.

## Psychological Preparation

This place may be emotionally harder than it is physically.

The immense openness can make a traveler feel exposed and insignificant. Then the ravines close in with wet vegetation, echoing water, and green darkness. The constant wind makes conversation difficult and can produce fatigue, irritability, and a sense of isolation. In storms, thunder and stone amplify sound until it feels bodily rather than audible.

Prepare your group for this. Use routines:

- regular water breaks;
- scheduled navigation checks;
- buddy checks in fog;
- a fixed rule against separating;
- clear rest periods after storms;
- a nightly discussion of route, weather, and concerns.

Do not mistake the plateau’s rare silences for safety. Silence may simply mean wind has shifted, water has paused, or weather is gathering elsewhere.

## A Good Minimal Field Kit

For a short guided journey between known communities, an ordinary human should at least have:

- layered clothing for sun, wind, cold, and heavy rain;
- durable boots and spare dry socks;
- 2–3 days of food;
- ample water capacity and treatment;
- map, compass, notebook, pencil;
- headlamp and spare power;
- first-aid kit;
- emergency shelter or bivouac material;
- cord and a compact rope;
- knife or multitool;
- metal cup/pot for boiling;
- sun and eye protection;
- whistle and visible signaling cloth;
- waterproof bags for all critical items;
- repair kit: tape, needle, thread, patches, cordage;
- locally appropriate gifts or trade goods.

For an unguided expedition, add specialized terrain equipment, storm shelter, communications suited to the world, and—most importantly—more time than you think necessary.

---

## The Most Important Rule

Do not try to conquer the plateau by moving quickly across it.

Travel as the ravine people do: from known water to known shelter, from marker to marker, with attention to wind, cloud, stone, and sound. Keep your footprint light. Let weather decide your schedule. When a local person says a place is not for camping, not for cutting, not for crossing, or not for visiting after a particular sound has begun, accept that instruction without demanding proof.

The plateau is ancient, but it is not inert. Its age has not made it gentle. It has simply had a very long time to become exact about what it permits.
