## Preparation Overview

Treat this destination as a high-altitude, ultra-arid geological wilderness with active environmental anomalies and abandoned-but-functioning infrastructure. It is survivable for an ordinary human only with reliable life support, disciplined travel habits, and a willingness to turn back early.

The principal dangers are not predators or violent weather in the familiar sense. They are:

- Insufficient atmospheric pressure and oxygen
- Extreme dehydration through respiration and skin loss
- Fine, electrostatically active mineral dust
- Low-audibility storms that can bury or abrade equipment
- Terrain that shifts gradually but unpredictably
- Ancient installations whose behavior is poorly legible to humans
- Local gravity or field disturbances, particularly around suspended stones and structures
- Isolation: rescue may be days or weeks away even if communication works

Do not plan this as a scenic hike. Plan it as a self-contained expedition into a place where the landscape itself is operating on rules only partly compatible with human intuition.

## Medical and Physiological Preparation

### Acclimatization will not be enough

The air appears substantially thinner than at ordinary terrestrial high altitudes. If this is a broadly human-compatible atmosphere at all, it is likely below the pressure needed for safe unassisted breathing.

Bring a **sealed pressure-capable suit**, not merely an oxygen mask.

At minimum, your system should provide:

- Regulated breathing oxygen
- A pressure garment or partial-pressure counterpressure layer
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing
- Humidity and temperature management
- A dust-proof visor and neck seal
- Redundant power for active circulation and communications
- At least 48–72 hours of independent life support beyond the expected transit time

A human in a simple mask may remain conscious briefly but would be vulnerable to hypoxia, decompression effects, severe dehydration, and impaired judgment. Impaired judgment is especially dangerous here: the landscape rewards patient caution and punishes casual improvisation.

### Train for low-sensory travel

The thin air and wide-open land create unusual sensory conditions. Sound carries poorly. Distances are deceptive. A moving grit curtain may be enormous yet nearly silent; a boulder migration may appear slow and harmless until it redirects into your route.

Before arrival, practice:

- Navigating with limited visibility and no audible environmental cues
- Reading instruments instead of trusting bodily sensation
- Walking in pressure clothing over uneven, loose terrain
- Recognizing early hypoxia, heat stress, dehydration, and panic
- Using hand signals and short-range suit-to-suit communications

Travel with at least one other person if possible. A solo expedition is technically possible only if your suit, navigation system, emergency shelter, and evacuation plan are unusually robust.

## Clothing and Personal Equipment

### Suit requirements

Your outer layer should be designed less for cold than for abrasion, dust exclusion, static management, and pressure integrity.

Use:

- A fully sealed, dust-rated pressure suit
- Replaceable abrasion panels at knees, elbows, boots, and gloves
- A nonconductive outer shell with electrostatic dissipation
- A visor coating that resists mica scratches and mineral deposition
- Double seals at wrists, boots, helmet ring, access ports, and pack connections
- A suit inspection light capable of detecting fine seam contamination
- An integrated locator beacon that transmits through dust and shallow bedrock interference

Avoid exposed fasteners, loose fabric, open mesh, adhesive-only repairs, and complicated external mechanisms. The dust is ancient, fine, dry, and likely to enter anything that has a gap.

Carry repair materials that do not depend solely on adhesive. Include mechanical patches, clamp rings, seal tape rated for low humidity and temperature cycling, spare gloves, spare visor film, and replacement filter cartridges.

### Footwear

Wear broad-soled boots with gaiter-integrated seals. The ground may look firm while behaving like deep dry powder, salt crust, unstable mica sheets, or dust that flows under pressure.

Your boots should offer:

- Good traction on gypsum, basalt, salt, and polished mineral surfaces
- Minimal external cavities where dust can collect
- A sole pattern easy to inspect and clean
- A means of attaching a short safety tether in unstable or anomalous areas
- Optional low-pressure “sand shoe” oversoles for storm conditions or basin crossings

Do not use climbing spikes casually. They may damage self-correcting materials, trigger sensitive surfaces, or become lodged in shifting mineral sheets.

## Water, Food, and Waste

### Bring more water than seems reasonable

There is no dependable surface water. There may not be usable atmospheric moisture. Do not count on extracting water from soil, vegetation, structures, ice-like minerals, or unfamiliar technology.

Plan on a closed-loop water system plus emergency reserves.

A sensible minimum is:

- Full suit water recycling
- Several days of drinking reserve beyond the planned itinerary
- Electrolyte supply
- Backup manual drinking access in case of suit-system failure
- Water purification capability, though not an expectation that local water will be found

Dust exposure and dry breathing systems can increase fluid demand even when the temperature feels merely cool.

### Food

Bring compact, low-crumb, high-calorie food that can be eaten without contaminating suit seals. The environment favors long, slow exertion rather than explosive effort. You will burn energy walking over loose surfaces, managing gear, and maintaining body temperature in a cool, dry, high-radiation setting.

Avoid taking food outside the suit unless you have an enclosed shelter. The pale runners near installations may be small, but feeding local life is irresponsible and could alter behavior around already poorly understood systems.

### Waste

Use sealed waste containment. Leave nothing organic, metallic, or synthetic on the plateau. Even a small discarded item may remain visible for centuries, interfere with local instrumentation, or be incorporated into migrating dust and mineral systems.

## Shelter and Camp Discipline

### Bring a freestanding sealed shelter

Do not assume any visible structure is safe, vacant, stable, or intended for human use.

Your shelter should be:

- Fully enclosed and pressure-capable
- Anchored with broad deadman anchors rather than deep stakes where possible
- Low-profile against wind and dust loading
- Equipped with dust-lock entry
- Fitted with external static grounding or dissipation
- Rated against abrasive particulate exposure
- Able to function for several days without resupply

A hard-shell microhabitat is preferable to a fabric tent. If using fabric, use redundant outer layers and expect damage.

### Where to camp

Choose a site on a stable, gently raised surface with clear sightlines. Favor areas behind substantial natural windbreak rock only after verifying that the rock is grounded, stable, and not part of a moving escarpment or installation.

Do **not** camp:

- In basins, channels, dry-looking depressions, or the lee of terrain likely to collect dust
- Along obvious straight alignments between structures
- On salt crusts that may conceal voids
- Near open cracks, shimmering staircases, masts, black doors, or visible light lattices
- Beneath suspended stones of any size
- Beneath cliffs, domes, or exfoliating mica faces
- Directly beside old structures, even if they appear shelter-like

The local pressure storms can make dust behave like fluid sediment. A sheltered basin can become a burial trap.

### Night procedure

At night, establish a strict perimeter routine:

1. Record the positions of nearby landmarks, stones, and structures.
2. Photograph or scan the ground around camp.
3. Set passive motion markers at a distance from the shelter.
4. Keep one emergency exit unobstructed and one alternate route planned.
5. Monitor ground tremors, static charge, atmospheric pressure, and geomagnetic or gravitational readings if available.
6. Do not investigate moving lights beneath the ground after dark.

If your camp’s surroundings have changed noticeably by morning, assume the change may continue. Move rather than attempting to “wait it out” beside an active phenomenon.

## Navigation

### Never navigate by appearance alone

The plateau’s scale is deceptive. Clear air makes features appear closer than they are, while dust and unusual lighting can erase depth. A day’s walk may barely change the apparent position of a distant ridge.

Bring multiple independent systems:

- Inertial navigation
- Satellite or multiversal positioning, if local conditions permit
- Physical compass, but do not trust it near installations
- Solar navigation tools
- Detailed topographic maps updated as recently as possible
- Paper backups protected from dust
- Survey flags or reflective route markers for short-distance movement
- A ranging laser or lidar for visibility loss

Because the ground shifts and structures may change position, do not rely on old maps as exact representations. Treat them as regional guides, not street maps.

### Avoid straight-line routes

The local travel rule against straight routes should be taken literally. Straight lines may coincide with stress pathways, periodic ground shifts, buried systems, or channels along which storms preferentially move.

Use gently curved routes that:

- Stay on raised, stable ground
- Avoid geometric alignments between anomalies
- Allow frequent exits toward shelter
- Keep known landmarks in view
- Minimize crossings of broad dust flats

If an efficient route seems unnaturally obvious—a perfectly direct line between two installations, a dark strip through pale ground, a corridor with unusually little dust—avoid it.

### Mark your trail, but do not depend on marks lasting

Dust will erase footprints and may bury markers. Use active beacons or tall, weighted marker poles where environmentally acceptable. Check behind you regularly; if your route vanishes quickly, reduce your range from camp.

## Pressure Storms

Pressure storms are probably the defining weather hazard.

They may arrive without dramatic clouds, rain, thunder, or strong audible wind. Watch for environmental changes rather than waiting to hear a storm:

- Distance turning violet or gray
- Shadows becoming soft or disappearing
- Dust lifting in spirals or bands
- Mica or light debris moving upward
- Abrupt changes in static charge
- Instrument readings showing falling or fluctuating pressure
- Unexplained difficulty walking in a particular direction

When signs appear, stop long-range travel immediately.

### Storm response

If caught in open country:

1. Leave basins, channels, and depressions early.
2. Move toward preidentified high, stable terrain.
3. Do not seek refuge under overhangs, loose cliffs, suspended stones, or unknown structures.
4. Secure external equipment and retract antennas.
5. Switch to storm filters and reduce suit exposure points.
6. Set locator beacon to high-power periodic transmission.
7. Enter your sealed shelter before dust density becomes severe.
8. Remain inside until pressure, visibility, and ground-motion readings stabilize for a sustained period.

Do not walk through a major storm merely because the wind seems quiet. The dust itself is the moving hazard. It can abrade seals, overload joints, obscure voids, infiltrate machinery, and accumulate around a shelter until doors cannot open.

After the storm, inspect all seals, intakes, radiators, joints, anchors, solar surfaces, and external instruments before moving.

## Ancient Structures and Quiet Technology

Assume every structure is active unless proven otherwise—and accept that “proven otherwise” may be beyond available human tools.

The structures may respond to pressure, gravity, charge, crustal motion, observation angle, or human proximity. Their materials appear self-maintaining. That does not mean they are safe; it means they may be part of systems operating on geological timescales.

### General rules

- Observe first from a distance.
- Record rather than touch.
- Do not remove samples without specialized authorization.
- Do not drill, chip, climb, pry, anchor to, or attach equipment to structures.
- Do not use strong radio, radar, laser, or electromagnetic scans close to them unless you understand the local protocols.
- Do not enter any opening just because it resembles a door or shelter.
- Do not follow staircases, tunnels, light patterns, or pathways into the ground.
- Do not camp within line of sight of a structure if another site is available.

### Black rectangular doors

The warning about white crystals should be followed exactly.

If the door edges are **clear**, do not touch, approach closely, stand in its threshold, shine lights through it, or attempt to test it with probes. It may not currently lead to an ordinary enclosed space.

If its edges are rimed with white crystals, that appears to be a safer state—but “safer” does not mean safe. Treat it as a possible controlled interface, sealed access point, or intermittently stable portal. Only interact if your expedition specifically includes trained anomaly personnel, tether protocols, remote probes, independent return beacons, and a firm decision threshold for aborting.

For an ordinary traveler: photograph from a distance and continue onward.

### Structures visible only indirectly

If an installation becomes apparent only in peripheral vision or when viewed away from directly, do not attempt to force direct observation. Avoid prolonged staring, visual fixation exercises, or photography experiments. Use passive recording equipment if necessary and maintain distance.

Such a phenomenon may be optical, cognitive, field-based, or all three. Whatever its cause, a human observer should not assume that curiosity is harmless.

### Vibrating masts and underground lights

Avoid masts, resonant rails, luminous seams, and any ground showing recurring geometric light. They may be sensors, structural regulators, power conduits, or signs of local stress.

If your instruments begin behaving strangely near them—compass drift, clock errors, radio delay, unusual battery charging or draining, changes in apparent weight—withdraw along your incoming path without rushing.

## Suspended Stones and Gravity Anomalies

Never camp under a suspended stone. Expand that rule: do not stand, park equipment, or linger beneath one.

A slowly hovering boulder still has mass. If its supporting field changes, it may fall, slide sideways, rotate, or accelerate unexpectedly. The shallow path beneath it shows where it has been, not where it will go next.

Keep a generous exclusion zone—at least several times the stone’s largest dimension, more for house-sized objects. Do not throw objects at them, fly drones around them, attach lines, collect dust beneath them, or try to determine whether they are “really” floating.

Watch for signs of local field disturbance:

- Loose dust rising or arranging itself in lines
- Objects feeling slightly lighter or heavier
- Unexplained vertigo
- Water in containers forming tilted surfaces
- Hanging tools drifting from vertical
- Difficulty judging distance or slope
- Repeating static shocks

If these occur, retreat slowly and deliberately. Running may be dangerous if local gravity is inconsistent.

## Wildlife and Vegetation

The life here is sparse and likely highly specialized. It may be fragile precisely because it is so rare.

### Do not disturb lichens

Lichen cushions and mineral-growth patches may represent decades or centuries of growth. Do not step on them, harvest them, scrape them, or use them as fuel, insulation, or biological samples without explicit permission.

### Animals

The small pale runners and high gliding creatures are probably not aggressive, but do not assume they are harmless to exposed equipment.

Keep food, warmth sources, and waste sealed. Avoid bright all-night illumination near structures, since local animals may use heat leaks, electrical fields, or mineral emissions as part of their ecology.

Do not attempt to capture, feed, tag, or follow them. In a landscape this vast, an animal’s presence may indicate a rare safe microhabitat—or proximity to hidden technology—and it is unwise to interfere with either.

## Communications and Rescue

Assume conventional communication may fail or be intermittent.

The plateau includes mineral layers, dust storms, buried machinery, and possible field anomalies that can disrupt signals. Carry:

- A line-of-sight radio network
- A high-gain directional antenna
- A satellite or off-world beacon where compatible
- A passive emergency locator beacon with long-duration power
- Physical signal materials: reflective panel, high-contrast markers, emergency light
- A scheduled check-in plan with an external team
- Clear “missed check-in” rescue criteria

Your rescue team should know not to search blindly in basins, storm corridors, around black doors, or under suspended stones. Give them your intended route, alternates, weather thresholds, no-go zones, and maximum travel radius.

If you lose navigation and communication, do not keep walking in search of a structure. Return to the last confirmed safe waypoint if possible, deploy shelter, conserve power and water, and make yourself locatable.

## Recommended Expedition Rhythm

A safe day should be conservative:

- Travel during stable daylight conditions.
- Stop regularly for suit and terrain inspections.
- Keep all major landmarks behind and ahead recorded.
- Establish camp early, before storm signs become obvious.
- Avoid entering unfamiliar structures late in the day or near dusk.
- Use the night for maintenance, mapping, observation from shelter, and checking environmental trends.
- Reserve sufficient power and water to remain stationary for several days.

The plateau rewards people who treat delay as a legitimate survival tool. If the land becomes confusing, the correct response is often to stop moving.

## Psychological Preparation

This place is likely to affect people through scale, silence, and ambiguity rather than overt hostility.

Expect:

- A persistent sense that distances are wrong
- Unease caused by very quiet large-scale motion
- Difficulty judging whether structures are near, far, active, or merely apparent
- Heightened awareness of suit sounds, breathing, and bodily functions
- Isolation even when traveling with others
- An urge to approach anomalies simply because they are the only obvious landmarks

Discuss these effects with your group beforehand. Establish a rule that no one investigates a phenomenon alone and no one changes the route without informing the others.

The land may feel patient, impersonal, and observant. Whether or not that impression is objectively meaningful, treat it as a cue to slow down—not as a reason to take risks.

## Essential “Do Not” List

Do not:

- Travel without a sealed pressure-capable life-support system.
- Depend on finding water.
- Shelter in a basin during a pressure storm.
- Follow straight geometric routes across open flats.
- Camp beneath or near suspended stones.
- Touch black doors when their edges are clear.
- Enter stairs, tunnels, doors, or shelters of unknown function.
- Assume apparent ruins are inactive.
- Use nearby structures as anchors, windbreaks, or emergency shelter.
- Trust footprints, old maps, compasses, or visual distance estimates without instrument confirmation.
- Handle strange minerals, lichen, animal remains, or technological debris.
- Wander after dusk.
- Separate from your partner or group near anomalous features.
- Treat the silence as evidence that nothing is happening.

---

## Final Mindset

Go prepared to be a temporary, careful visitor rather than an explorer entitled to answers.

The plateau is not empty. Its weather moves stone and dust; its buried systems may still be working; its rare organisms survive in narrow ecological permissions; its structures may be older than any usable human context. Your goal should be to cross or observe it with minimal disturbance, keeping yourself alive and leaving the place as close as possible to the way you found it.

In this country, caution is not timidity. It is the correct way to listen.
