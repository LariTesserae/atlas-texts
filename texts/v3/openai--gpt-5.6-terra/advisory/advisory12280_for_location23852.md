## Preparation Guide: The Still Basin City

This is not a hostile destination in the usual sense. Its people appear settled, practical, and accustomed to maintaining a difficult blessing. The principal danger is not violence, weather, wildlife, or famine.

The danger is **becoming incorporated into a place where change does not reliably accumulate**.

Arrive prepared to be patient, extremely observant, and conservative about contact with the city’s material arrangements. Do not assume that because the sun moves, you can measure time normally; because people have children and trade goods, you can expect ordinary causality; or because water can be lifted and drunk, that your body will treat it as ordinary water.

Your first objective is not sightseeing. It is to establish whether *you* remain temporally continuous there: whether you heal, tire, digest, remember, age, and can leave.

---

# 1. Before Departure

## Establish an external anchor

Do not visit this location without a reliable way home that is independent of local clocks, weather, stars, or physical landmarks.

Bring at least two means of extraction:

- a primary multiversal transit device or route marker;
- a passive, durable backup beacon concealed on your person;
- ideally, a third “dead-simple” method: a physical recall token, ritual phrase, keyed object, or prearranged rendezvous phenomenon that does not require electricity, moving parts, combustion, or a timer.

Anything dependent on a mechanism cycling, a battery discharging predictably, a clock advancing, a signal repeating, or fuel being consumed may fail subtly. It may continue to look operational while never progressing toward its next state.

Before entering the basin, test your extraction method somewhere just outside it. Then test it again after crossing the city boundary, before you have interacted with any major local object, accepted food, or slept.

If you cannot verify departure on arrival, leave immediately.

## Arrange external oversight

Tell someone outside the destination:

- your intended entry point;
- your expected subjective stay;
- the latest point at which they should attempt retrieval;
- what you will be carrying and wearing;
- what “you” should know on return to verify continuity.

Use a challenge-response system containing information that cannot be guessed from your outward appearance. The hazard here is not necessarily replacement, but **stasis can make a traveler appear intact while interrupting their uninterrupted personal history**.

If possible, have outside observers monitor:
- your biological time,
- your signal pattern,
- your physical location,
- and the condition of your transit equipment.

Do not rely on a scheduled automatic rescue if the rescue device must wait a certain number of hours before activating.

## Medical preparation

Bring a compact medical kit designed for inert, non-mechanical use:

- sterile dressings;
- antiseptic;
- sutures or wound-closure strips;
- broad-spectrum antibiotics if medically appropriate for you;
- antihistamines;
- pain medication;
- gastrointestinal medication;
- electrolyte packets;
- a manual thermometer;
- a mechanical blood-pressure cuff if practical;
- spare prescription medication in several separately sealed containers.

Bring more medication than the planned duration demands. In this environment, you may not be able to count on pills degrading, but you also cannot count on your body metabolizing them normally. Keep a written log of every dose.

Do not assume injuries will heal. A cut may remain a cut indefinitely. Conversely, do not assume bleeding, infection, swelling, fever, or bruising will progress in recognizable ways. Avoid injury rather than relying on treatment.

If you have a condition requiring timed medication, insulin, oxygen regulation, dialysis, an implanted device, or strict circadian management, this is a high-risk destination. Consult whatever medical expertise is available in your home reality before attempting a visit.

## Clothing and equipment

Dress for cold, damp highland conditions without expecting rain to behave normally.

Recommended:

- layered wool or synthetic insulating clothing;
- a wind-resistant outer layer;
- sturdy, high-ankle boots with aggressive soles;
- gaiters for volcanic dust, sharp lava, and reeds;
- gloves suitable for stonework and cold water;
- a brimmed hat and eye protection;
- a dust mask or respirator for volcanic grit and mineral fissures;
- a warm sleep system even if you do not intend to stay overnight;
- a simple paper map, notebook, pencils, and charcoal;
- a compass, but do not assume it will be meaningful near volcanic rock;
- high-contrast trail tape or cord for marking your own route—only outside settled precincts, and remove it afterward;
- a non-electronic light source as backup, but test whether combustion behaves normally before depending on it.

Avoid equipment that requires:
- spinning motors,
- clocks,
- pumps,
- fans,
- automated pressure systems,
- rotating seals,
- digital synchronization,
- or counted cycles.

A hand-cranked device may be preferable to an electric one in many worlds; here, however, “cranked” does not guarantee that repeated motion will produce cumulative work. The local culture’s avoidance of mills is a major warning. Favor tools that work in a single direct action: knife, wedge, lever, cord, mirror, hand lens, pencil, manual latch.

## Bring your own consumables

For a first visit, carry:

- sealed drinking water;
- packaged food with known ingredients;
- electrolyte salts;
- utensils and a cup;
- water-purification supplies, though do not regard them as proof that local water is safe;
- personal hygiene supplies;
- a small amount of culturally neutral trade material.

The local food is probably nutritionally viable for locals, but it may participate in the basin’s stable state. A berry that never shrivels may also never digest in the expected way. A flame that does not consume fuel indicates that material processes can become uncoupled from visible change.

Do not eat local food or drink cistern water until you have observed trusted residents doing so repeatedly and have confirmed that food consumed by an outsider is actually processed by your body. Begin with a tiny, monitored sample only if necessary.

# 2. Arrival Protocol

## Do not enter through the central court

Avoid the oldest court and its twelve bronze bowls on your first day. Do not look into the bowls. Do not touch the black-glass paving around the central pool unless directly invited and supervised by a knowledgeable local.

The locals’ avoidance is specific, longstanding, and not theatrical. Those bowls may provide a view of alternate temporal states, but viewing is not necessarily passive. A reflection in a place like this may be an invitation to become the reflected version of yourself—or to notice a world from which you cannot easily return.

Treat the pool and bowls as a restricted sacred-technological site.

## Establish a baseline immediately

Upon arrival, before engaging deeply:

1. Record your own name, home origin, purpose, date, and intended departure condition.
2. Photograph or sketch your equipment, hands, face, and any existing injuries.
3. Take a pulse, temperature, hydration assessment, and any relevant medical measurements.
4. Eat a small portion of your own food and note hunger, taste, digestion, and fatigue.
5. Make a deliberate, harmless change in a controlled private area:
   - fold a piece of paper,
   - tie and untie a knot,
   - draw a line in your notebook,
   - move an object from one pocket to another.
6. Return to it later and see whether the change remains.

Do not use local dust, local water, a public wall, or a culturally important structure for this experiment.

You are checking whether:
- your actions persist;
- your possessions remain in the state you leave them;
- memory remains coherent;
- biological processes continue;
- and local reality “corrects” disturbances.

Repeat this baseline check after your first sleep, after eating local food, after visiting the market, and before departure.

## Choose lodging carefully

If you stay, request lodging in a newer building on designated empty ground, not one joined tightly to old walls. The local rule requiring a hand’s-width gap between old and new construction may be more than custom. It may be a practical means of preventing new matter, persons, or events from becoming locked into the basin’s oldest pattern.

Ask for a room with:
- a clearly separate threshold;
- no direct wall contact with ancient retaining works;
- an accessible exterior route;
- a place to keep your equipment off the floor and away from condensation;
- no view directly onto the central pool, if possible.

Set your own belongings together, visibly, on a cloth or mat you brought. Do not scatter them through the room. In a place where objects have “proper unmoving places,” unattended items may begin to acquire one.

# 3. Social Conduct and Local Etiquette

The residents are likely to be patient but not naïve. They understand that their landscape has rules. A visitor who treats the city as a curiosity, laboratory, or opportunity to “fix” its lack of progress will be seen as reckless at best.

## Speak plainly about your origin

Do not claim to come from “the future.” To these people, that phrase may be incomprehensible, insulting, or dangerously suggestive.

A better explanation is:

> “I come from a country where things change more quickly, and I am learning how to behave safely here.”

Do not insist that their condition is tragic, unnatural, or in need of liberation. They call it mercy for reasons you do not yet understand. They may have cultural, historical, and personal relationships with stasis that an outsider cannot judge in a few days.

## Respect the city’s maintenance rules

Never:

- dismantle an old retaining wall;
- remove stones from old foundations;
- wedge objects into existing cracks;
- build against an older structure;
- tie lines, cables, or tents directly to ancient masonry;
- scrape moss from walls;
- rearrange the stones of paths, shrines, graves, or waterworks;
- “improve” drainage;
- clear vegetation growing from a structural edge;
- test whether a suspended raindrop can be made to fall.

Do not put your hand into every droplet simply because it is strange. The local ecology may be organized around those suspended beads. Birds drink them. Reeds bear them. Household systems collect them. What looks like a small, harmless anomaly may be part of the city’s water balance.

## Do not ask casually about the dead

The dead are physically present in sealed high chambers and are intentionally kept apart. Do not request access. Do not offer to examine them. Do not speculate aloud about whether they are “really dead.”

A culture that cannot rely on decomposition will have carefully developed boundaries between the living and the deceased. Respect those boundaries exactly.

## Learn the difference between permission and invitation

The city has little urgency, and bargaining is ritualized. Do not interpret slowness as indecision. If a merchant says “wait,” wait. If someone says an object is not ready, do not offer more payment. If a family offers a meal, accept only if you are prepared for the possible risks of local food; declining politely is acceptable if explained as a matter of your body’s unfamiliarity.

Pay fairly. Do not exploit the fact that vendors may not experience spoilage or closing time. Their labor and goods still have value, and social trust may be one of the few things that must be actively renewed in a place without ordinary loss.

Useful trade goods include:
- fine needles;
- durable thread;
- high-quality salt;
- compact mirrors;
- pigments;
- carefully made paper;
- simple hand tools;
- medical supplies, if offered respectfully and not presented as superior;
- stories, songs, maps, and techniques that do not threaten the local order.

Avoid gifting clocks, wind-driven devices, automated machinery, or anything designed to impose speed and throughput unless specifically requested by local craftspeople who understand the risks.

# 4. Food, Water, Fire, and Shelter

## Water

Treat all local water as biologically and metaphysically unverified.

The cistern water may be clear, cold, and still, but stillness is not purity. It may lack normal microbial cycling. It may be extraordinarily clean; it may also preserve contaminants indefinitely.

Do not assume boiling makes water safe. Fire in the basin may provide heat and light without ordinary consumption or full thermal convection. A pot that appears to boil may not have completed sterilization in the normal sense.

For short stays, use your own water.

If you must use local water:
1. ask which source residents drink from daily;
2. use a clean vessel;
3. filter it;
4. chemically treat it if your treatment functions;
5. test a minimal amount;
6. monitor yourself for a full local day-night cycle and a confirmed subjective interval.

Never drink from:
- the central pool;
- bronze bowl water;
- water collected from shrine roofs;
- water found in burial chambers;
- hot fissure runoff;
- droplets directly suspended on plants unless locals explicitly do so.

## Food

Favor cooked local food over raw food once you decide to try it, but recognize that “cooked” may be culturally and physically different here. Ask how a dish is prepared, how long it has been prepared, and whether travelers eat it.

Start with foods that have recognizable counterparts:
- roots;
- grains, if available;
- goat dairy only after caution;
- smoked fish;
- plainly cooked meat.

Be cautious with:
- berries;
- mosses;
- reeds;
- unfamiliar fermented foods;
- preserved foods whose preservation process may never complete;
- meat from large highland animals;
- anything harvested from near volcanic fissures.

Keep a written intake log. Do not rely only on hunger as a signal. Hunger may stop changing before your nutritional needs do.

## Fire

Do not leave any flame unattended. A fire that does not consume fuel can still burn you, smoke a room, or become a persistent hazard.

Before using a camp stove, candle, lamp, or lighter:
- test it outside;
- observe whether flame spreads;
- observe whether fuel level changes;
- observe whether heat dissipates;
- make sure it can be extinguished.

Use local hearths when possible. Residents will understand the behavior of their fires.

## Sleep

Do not assume sleep is harmless or restorative in a stable environment.

For the first night:
- sleep lightly;
- set up your extraction equipment within immediate reach;
- keep shoes, clothing, and documents in a single known arrangement;
- use a buddy system if traveling with others;
- agree that one person remains alert at intervals.

On waking, check:
- whether your belongings are where you left them;
- whether you remember going to sleep;
- whether you have new injuries or missing time;
- whether your body feels rested, unchanged, or unusually fixed;
- whether local residents recognize you as having arrived recently.

If you wake to find an arrangement that feels “more proper” than the one you made, resist the urge to accept it uncritically. Record it, then prepare to leave.

# 5. Movement Through the City and Surrounding Country

## The terrain is physically dangerous

The scenery is calm, but it is young volcanic country.

Expect:
- razor-sharp lava;
- unstable scree;
- steep terraces;
- hidden fissures;
- mineral gases;
- cold exposure at elevation;
- limited traction on wet stone and moss;
- ravines where sound and visibility are poor.

Do not hike alone beyond the settled precincts. Hire or request a local guide, especially for:
- lava fields;
- high passes;
- hunting grounds;
- ravines;
- hot cracks;
- the northern ridge;
- burial cliffs.

The phrase “hunting is approaching” suggests that animals and events may be encountered according to local patterns rather than pursued through normal movement. Do not assume a stationary animal is tame, injured, or available to touch.

## Never rely on footsteps as navigation

Footprints may persist until disturbed, then disappear as the dust resumes a preferred state. Marking a path in dust is unreliable. So is assuming a trail will remain worn.

Use:
- your own cord markers where permitted;
- visual landmarks;
- a local guide;
- a hand-drawn map updated by observation;
- explicit route descriptions.

Do not leave permanent markers on landscape features. The country may resist them, and locals may regard this as an attempt to make your claim “take hold.”

## Avoid the lava fields after dark

The lava can remain sharp, black, and visually deceptive. Mineral warmth from fissures may create localized fog, weak footing, or toxic air. Carry a respirator and never enter a depression where air seems unusually still.

If birds, goats, or local guides avoid an area, do the same.

# 6. Time, Memory, and Psychological Health

This destination can be psychologically difficult even if it is physically safe.

Humans are built around accumulated change: meals becoming digestion, work becoming completion, wounds becoming scars, days becoming histories. The basin interrupts or distorts that expectation. A visitor may experience:

- impatience that becomes panic;
- a sense that actions are meaningless because they leave no trace;
- compulsive attempts to force change;
- derealization;
- memory uncertainty;
- grief or envy directed toward the locals;
- a seductive desire to remain because nothing is being lost.

The last is particularly important. A place without visible decline may feel like relief after a life marked by deadlines, illness, loss, or aging. That does not mean it is safe to settle there.

## Maintain a traveler’s routine

Create deliberate, portable markers of continuity:

- write dated journal entries, including subjective impressions;
- make a new drawing each day;
- tie a sequence of knots in your own cord;
- recite your home address and names of important people;
- perform a simple exercise routine and record repetitions;
- keep one sealed object unopened until departure;
- exchange regular check-ins with companions.

Use multiple forms of recordkeeping. If a notebook resets, a braid may not; if a digital file repeats, a scar of pencil pressure may remain; if none of them retain changes, you have learned something urgent.

Do not become obsessed with testing the anomaly. One controlled test is science; repeated disruption of a community’s reality is vandalism and may be personally dangerous.

## Travel with a companion if possible

A companion provides:
- an independent memory;
- a witness to behavioral changes;
- help in terrain accidents;
- social legitimacy;
- someone who can leave if you cannot.

Agree in advance on departure criteria. For example:

Leave immediately if:
- either traveler cannot recall the arrival sequence;
- local food does not pass through normal digestion;
- a wound neither heals nor responds to treatment;
- equipment repeatedly returns to earlier states;
- a local resident says you are beginning to “take hold”;
- you find yourself unable to cross a threshold you crossed before;
- reflections show a version of you acting independently;
- you feel that leaving would be “rude” despite having no actual obligation.

# 7. The Most Important Local Warning: Do Not “Take Hold”

The citizens’ language about structures taking hold should be treated as a general principle, not merely an architectural concern.

In this basin, close and sustained contact may grant things a stronger form of permanence than is healthy. A building connected to old work may become inseparable from it. By analogy, a visitor who repeatedly sleeps in one place, stores possessions in fixed arrangements, accepts a local role, wears local goods continuously, or becomes involved in old ritual sites may begin to be recognized by the city as part of its stable arrangement.

This is speculative—but it is the correct level of caution.

To reduce the risk:

- keep visits short on the first expedition;
- stay in newer quarters;
- move lodging if staying more than a few local cycles;
- retain some clothing and objects from home;
- do not permanently alter your appearance to match local custom;
- do not accept a plot of land, inherited tool, burial obligation, or formal civic duty;
- do not marry, swear binding oaths, or accept adoption into a household without understanding the consequences;
- do not place any part of yourself—hair, blood, teeth, clothing, name tokens—in a shrine, cistern, wall, or foundation;
- do not let someone build a structure “for you” against an old wall;
- do not bury anything there.

Be friendly. Participate. But remain, in material and social terms, a guest.

# 8. Departure Procedure

Do not depart impulsively from an emotionally charged location, especially not from the central court, a burial cliff, a high ridge, or beside still water.

Return to the same controlled location where you established your arrival baseline, ideally near but not inside your lodging.

Before activating transit:

1. Confirm your name, origin, mission, and intended destination in writing.
2. Inventory every item you brought.
3. Check for unfamiliar possessions, local objects, droplets, seeds, fibers, stones, or cords caught in your gear.
4. Remove local dust from boots and clothing if this can be done respectfully.
5. Do not bring water from the central pool, bronze bowls, or old cisterns.
6. Avoid taking biological samples, dead animals, seeds, moss, or funerary material.
7. Obtain permission for ordinary souvenirs, preferably crafted goods intended for trade.
8. Send an external message if possible: “Preparing to leave; continuity checks passed/failed.”
9. Activate departure manually and remain focused on the destination.

On return, undergo quarantine and debriefing.

At minimum:
- isolate clothing and equipment;
- photograph all items;
- compare journals with external records;
- monitor sleep, digestion, wound healing, and emotional state;
- do not immediately resume high-stakes responsibilities;
- do not look into reflective surfaces alone if you had contact with the bronze bowls or central pool;
- have someone verify that your memories are sequential and that you do not report a compelling need to return at once.

A traveler returning unchanged in appearance may still need time to determine whether they are continuing normally.

---

# 9. What to Appreciate, If You Can Do So Safely

Once basic safety is established, this may be an extraordinary place to visit.

Listen for the close sounds: goat teeth on reeds, bronze bells touched by hand, stone tools, quiet market speech. Watch the rain held under eaves. Notice that the city is not frozen in the sense of being dead; it is maintained in a condition of continuous sufficiency. Its inhabitants have made ethics, architecture, trade, burial, and family life around the fact that loss and change behave differently there.

Do not rush to interpret that as paradise or prison.

Walk gently. Ask before touching. Keep your own continuity secure. Leave the old walls where they are. And when a resident tells you that something is in its proper unmoving place, believe that they may be describing not a preference, but a survival skill.
