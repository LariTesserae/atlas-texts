Prepare as you would for a remote, wet, newly glaciated or tectonically active alpine basin—not for an exotic civilization. The principal dangers are ordinary ones made severe by exposure, unstable ground, poor visibility, cold water, and the absence of reliable routes.

## Before entering

- **Assume there is no rescue infrastructure.** No trail, shelter, bridge, marker, road, radio repeater, or dependable campsite should be expected.
- **Do not travel alone.** A group of three or four is preferable: enough people to assist an injured traveler without leaving someone alone.
- **File a return plan** with someone outside the basin if such communication is possible in your transit arrangement. Include intended entry route, turnaround dates, and emergency thresholds.
- **Carry redundant navigation.** A detailed topographic map, compass, satellite navigation device, and spare power should all agree as much as possible. The landscape changes enough that maps may be imperfect: fresh slides, new channels, temporary pools, and snow patches can invalidate a planned line.
- **Plan conservatively for distance.** A route that appears to be a short basin crossing may take an entire day. Saturated ground, boulder fields, detours around water, cloud, and unstable scree make progress slow.

If multiversal transit allows choice of season, arrive in the most stable part of the local late summer or early autumn—after the main snowmelt but before persistent winter snow. Even then, expect sleet and freezing nights.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for constant wet cold rather than dramatic deep-freeze conditions.

Bring:

- Waterproof, windproof shell jacket and trousers.
- Warm insulating layers that retain warmth when damp: synthetic insulation or wool, not cotton.
- Several dry base layers and dedicated dry sleep clothing sealed in waterproof bags.
- Waterproof gloves plus spare insulating gloves.
- Warm hat, sun hat, neck gaiter or balaclava.
- Sturdy waterproof boots with aggressive soles and ankle support.
- Gaiters: the basin’s shallow pools, wet sedge, loose gravel, and saturated soil will otherwise fill boots quickly.
- Trekking poles. They are useful for testing mud depth, probing snow, steadying on wet slabs, and maintaining balance during stream crossings.
- Sunglasses and sunscreen. Cold cloud does not eliminate ultraviolet exposure, especially around snow and wet reflective stone.
- A four-season tent or other shelter capable of handling wind-driven rain and sleet.
- A sleeping system rated substantially colder than the forecast. The wet valley floor will pull heat away rapidly.
- Waterproof groundsheet, but do not assume any flat patch stays dry through the night.
- Stove and reliable fuel. Do not count on dry firewood; there are no trees and little combustible material.
- High-calorie food that can be prepared in poor weather, plus at least one or two no-cook emergency days.
- Water treatment. Water may look pristine but can be contaminated by animal activity, sediment, or upstream carcasses. Carry a filter and chemical backup; silty water may require settling or pre-filtration.
- Headlamp with spare batteries. Low cloud and the surrounding walls can make midday feel like evening.
- Satellite communicator or emergency beacon if its system functions in this universe.
- First-aid kit emphasizing blister care, sprains, fractures, hypothermia, wound cleaning, and pain management.
- Repair kit: tape, cordage, boot repair supplies, spare buckles, patch material, and dry bags.

A helmet is sensible if traveling beneath cliffs, in narrow gullies, or through active talus. It is not an invitation to linger there.

## Choosing a route

The ground has not settled into trustworthy patterns. Treat every apparent path as provisional.

- **Stay well away from the bases of cliffs.** Fresh rockfall can occur without warning, especially after rain, frost, warming, or cloud-shrouded snowmelt. A pale, raw-looking debris tongue is a recent event, not a safe road.
- **Avoid gullies and narrow drainage lines.** They can concentrate water suddenly and channel falling stones from far above. A harmless trickle can become a violent runoff course after rain or thaw.
- **Do not cross beneath loaded snowfields** late in the day or during warming conditions. Snow may conceal holes, unstable melt channels, or loose rock.
- **Use broad, gently inclined terrain where possible**, even if it takes longer. Stable-looking bedrock and established turf are generally preferable to fresh angular rubble, steep scree, or pale gravel fans.
- **Test each step in saturated ground.** Dark vegetation mats may conceal water, soft peat, or channels between stones. Pools may have undermined edges.
- **Cross water early in the day.** Meltwater often rises later, and rain can change a crossing in minutes. Unbuckle pack hip belts before a serious crossing; use poles; cross as a group where practical; turn back rather than gamble on a swift or opaque channel.
- **Do not trust animal trails completely.** Ungulates know where forage lies, not necessarily where a person carrying a pack can safely travel. Their paths may traverse loose slopes, boggy ground, or exposed shelves.

Move slowly enough to hear the place. The first warning of a rockfall may be a small click above you; the first sign of unstable ground may be a faint shifting beneath your feet.

## Campsite selection

Camping is likely to be the most difficult routine task.

Choose a site that is:

- Above obvious flood lines and well outside stream channels, even dry-looking ones.
- Away from cliff bases, avalanche paths, scree cones, and recent landslide deposits.
- Slightly elevated but not on a fully exposed ridge.
- On durable ground if available: compact gravel, stable turf, or broad rock benches rather than mossy wetland.
- Far enough from pools and shorelines to avoid disturbing waterfowl and to reduce insect and animal traffic.

Avoid camping in shallow basins. Cold air and water collect there, and a night’s rain can turn a merely damp site into a pool. Do not camp on vegetation that appears to be the basin’s first fragile attempt at soil formation; it may take decades to recover from a few tent footprints.

Anchor tents thoroughly. Wind may arrive with little warning as cloud spills over the ridges. Use rocks carefully as anchors only where doing so does not destabilize the surrounding slope.

## Weather discipline

The weather is not merely an inconvenience. It determines whether the terrain remains legible.

Turn back, wait, or camp if:

- Cloud removes visibility enough that you cannot identify safe terrain ahead.
- Rain becomes sustained and begins raising channels or loosening slopes.
- Sleet or wet snow begins accumulating on already wet ground.
- Wind makes safe footing difficult.
- You hear persistent rockfall, cracking snow, or water suddenly increasing in nearby gullies.
- Anyone becomes chilled, clumsy, unusually quiet, confused, or unable to eat and drink—early hypothermia can look like simple fatigue.

Keep one person in the group responsible for regular checks: warm enough, hydrated enough, eating enough, dry enough, and still able to navigate.

## Wildlife conduct

The animals are ordinary, but this does not make them harmless.

- Keep distance from ungulate herds, especially mothers with young or animals crowded by weather and terrain.
- Do not approach nests, cliff birds, or waterfowl concentrations. In a sparse basin, a disturbed breeding site may have few alternatives.
- Store food securely and keep a clean camp. Rodents may be small, but they can destroy insulation, food bags, and shelter materials.
- Predators are likely cautious, but do not leave meat, scraps, or fish remains near camp. Make your presence known in low visibility.
- Watch for disease vectors where appropriate: ticks may be less common in the exposed high country, but rodents and standing water still justify hygiene.
- Yield quietly to animal routes. Their repeated tracks may be among the oldest and most important structures in the basin.

## Respect for the place

This is a landscape in which nearly every surface is temporary and slow to recover.

Travel lightly. Do not build cairns, carve markers, move stones for amusement, make fire rings, or create a “better” campsite. Do not take samples unless your purpose genuinely requires it; a fresh mineral surface, lichen patch, or small wetland community may be the beginning of a process far older than your visit.

Leave no persistent route behind:

- Walk on durable surfaces where possible.
- Spread group movement rather than cutting a single trench through wet ground.
- Pack out all waste.
- Use a toilet method appropriate to cold, saturated terrain; never bury waste near water or in thin soil that will soon wash away.
- Do not feed animals.
- Keep noise low, especially at dusk and dawn, when sound carries across the basin.

## The right mindset

Do not expect the basin to reward decisiveness with speed. It rewards patience, observation, and willingness to stop. The country is not hostile in an intentional sense; it is simply occupied by water, frost, gravity, and living things that have no reason to accommodate a human schedule.

A successful visitor will travel with the humility of someone crossing a workshop while the machinery is running: avoid the moving parts, do not interfere with what is being made, and be prepared for the floor beneath you to change.
