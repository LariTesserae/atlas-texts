This is not a place to visit casually, even if you have ordinary human needs and unusually good interdimensional travel options. Treat it as an active hydrological anomaly inside a young volcanic wound, not as a scenic high-altitude basin. Its local physics is inconsistent, its water is violently mobile, its acoustics are hostile to communication, and its apparent size cannot be trusted.

Your primary goal should be to arrive, observe, and leave without trying to conquer, map, drain, sample aggressively, or “solve” it.

## Before departure

### Do not go alone

Bring at least three people if possible:

- **A lead traveler** responsible for go/no-go decisions.
- **A safety anchor/operator** who remains at the entrance or outside the basin if your travel method permits.
- **An observer** whose only job is recording conditions, timing pulses, and monitoring personnel.

Do not rely on voice contact. Establish a simple visual and tactile signal system before entering:

- Closed fist: stop.
- Two fingers pointed toward the exit: withdraw now.
- Flat hand moving downward: brace/anchor.
- Hand on head: helmet/seal issue.
- Repeated light flashes: emergency extraction.

Agree in advance that any member may call an immediate retreat without needing to justify it.

### Set an extraction rule

This location contains visual distortions that may show events displaced in time. You may see people, structures, or versions of yourselves that are not presently there. Therefore, set a hard rule:

> Do not alter your plan because of anything seen through moving water.

If you see a future injury, a figure beckoning from a doorway, your equipment somewhere it should not be, or a dry landscape in the central pool, record it if safe—but do not pursue it.

Use external timekeeping. Every traveler should wear a synchronized, rugged clock with a vibration alarm. Make your visit short. Fifteen minutes of direct basin exposure is a reasonable initial maximum; less if conditions are unstable. The place may make duration difficult to judge.

### Prepare for full immersion, pressure, impact, and laceration

Even if there are pockets of air or apparently dry ledges, assume the basin is effectively an underwater environment with irregular currents.

Wear:

- A sealed warm-water exposure suit or drysuit rated for abrasion.
- A helmet with face protection and integrated lights.
- Gloves with excellent grip and cut resistance.
- Hard boots with aggressive soles, toe protection, and ankle support.
- A buoyancy system that can be manually disabled or adjusted. Ordinary positive buoyancy may become dangerous when water is moving upward or sideways.
- A redundant breathing system, ideally one that does not depend on a single mouthpiece staying in place during impacts.
- A compact emergency air supply physically secured to the torso.
- Eye protection even inside a mask, if possible; mineral-rich water and sharp particles may be present.
- Hearing protection designed for pressure environments, but do not expect to hear ordinary warnings.

Secure every loose item. Use tethered tools, wrist lanyards, and enclosed pockets. A dropped object may not fall where expected.

Avoid exposed skin. The water is warm, but that does not mean biologically safe. Its mineral content, microbial ecology, and chemical balance are unknown. The sweetness in its smell may indicate decaying plant matter, dissolved organics, or something less familiar.

### Bring analog equipment

Electronics may be unreliable around strange currents, wet conditions, mineral water, and local temporal optical effects.

Useful equipment includes:

- Waterproof notebooks and grease pencils.
- Mechanical watches or wind-up timers.
- Physical compasses—but treat readings as data, not navigation.
- A weighted measuring line with visible knots.
- Chemical light sticks in several colors.
- High-intensity sealed lamps with manual switches.
- A thermal blanket and dry clothing staged outside the basin.
- A compact trauma kit for cuts, crush injuries, cold shock, and aspiration.
- A tether cutter accessible with either hand.
- Nonconductive climbing protection and short, shock-absorbing tethers.

Do not depend on drones. They are likely to be torn apart, pulled into vertical flows, or return misleading footage. If you use one at all, use it from the rim and regard its loss as expected.

## Arrival and entry

### Do not enter from directly above if you can avoid it

The opening overhead may not be open air. It may lead into a larger body of water, a vertical circulation system, or another environment entirely. A descent from above risks becoming a descent into a current with no recoverable orientation.

Prefer a rim-side arrival onto stable basalt outside the immediate water movement. Confirm the stone is stable before loading it: this is young, sharp rock, and vibration from the deep pulses appears capable of dislodging gravel and fragments.

The best staging position is likely near an upper ridge with a direct, short retreat route. The four pillars and the square-cut doorway may provide landmarks, but do not assume they provide shelter.

### Use the ruins only as references, not protection

The smooth-sided pillars may indicate long-term water abrasion. They are not necessarily stable, intentional, or load-bearing. The doorway ending in solid stone may be a blocked passage, a failed excavation, or an arrangement of stone that is only temporarily a doorway.

Do not enter the square opening on the first visit.

A narrow cavity with one known exit is exactly where a pressure surge, altered water level, or shifting gravity becomes lethal. The white root widening the wall crack is not a reassuring sign: it may be exploiting a weakness in the basalt, and the wall may eventually open under conditions you cannot predict.

The chain of stone bowls is similarly not a handrail, stairway, or water-control device. Their uneven overflow suggests they are participating in the anomaly rather than resisting it.

### Observe before crossing the active water

Spend several minutes at the rim watching the basin. Specifically note:

- The interval between deep pulses.
- Whether all water sheets tighten before a pulse.
- The movement of loose gravel.
- The direction in which small black clinging organisms drift after the pulse.
- Whether the red translucent colony is open or closed.
- Whether water curtains are becoming clearer, darker, thicker, or more vertical.
- The appearance of the central island.

Do not assume a regular pulse interval. Track at least three pulses. If the intervals shorten, the impacts grow stronger, the basin darkens abruptly, or the overhead water brightens with a passing immense shadow, delay entry.

The red colony may be useful as an environmental indicator. Its opening seems associated with local clearing of the water, while its closing coincides with deepening darkness. Whether it causes or merely responds to the change is unknown. Do not touch, cut, collect, or stand near it. Treat it as a living instrument of the basin, and give it space.

## Movement inside the basin

### Stay low, stay tethered, and move one person at a time

The basin’s water behaves as if multiple gravitational and pressure directions are present. A conventional swimming strategy may fail. Do not trust “up,” “down,” or buoyancy.

Use short tethers connected to known rock anchors, with one traveler moving while the others remain secured. Keep tether lines short enough that a person cannot be swept across open water into a cliff or curtain. Avoid long slack lines; they can snag, whip, or lead you somewhere you did not intend.

Where possible:

- Crawl or traverse along rock rather than swimming.
- Use three points of contact.
- Keep your center of mass close to the basalt.
- Test each hold before weighting it.
- Never place hands into cracks without visual inspection; sharp stone, roots, organisms, or pressure jets may be inside.

Do not try to cross a vertical water curtain unless you have no alternative. These are not merely waterfalls standing on end. They may conceal abrupt force gradients, visual displacement, and paths into spaces not geometrically contained by the basin.

If crossing is unavoidable:

1. Wait for a relatively quiet interval after, not before, a deep pulse.
2. Secure two independent anchors.
3. Have the crossing person carry no loose gear.
4. Use a physically attached guide line rather than visual bearings.
5. Do not look through the curtain for navigation.
6. If the person emerges somewhere unexpected, do not rush after them. Confirm tether integrity, breathing status, and orientation first.

### Keep clear of the central island

The island is tempting because it offers a momentary dry surface and an impossible still pool. That makes it the most obvious trap in the basin.

The island submerges when the deep pulse strikes. Any route to it may disappear before a return crossing. Its still water may be a portal, temporal lens, remote reflection, or simply a place where the basin’s rules are concentrated. A clear dry sky reflected there is not evidence that a dry world is accessible.

Do not step into the hollow.

Do not touch the reflection.

Do not attempt to retrieve birds, grass, sunlight, or any object that appears in it.

If the island surfaces near your position, observe from a secured rock ledge. Photograph or sketch the reflected scene only if doing so does not delay withdrawal.

### Avoid chasing fauna

The colorless long-bodied creatures and black drifting shapes are not necessarily dangerous, but they are clearly adapted to currents that would injure a human. Their movements may reveal safe or unsafe flow paths, but only in a broad sense.

Do not attempt to capture them. Do not put hands near the underside opening of the long creatures. Do not interpret their apparent absence left in a curtain as a passage to follow.

If the small black forms release from the rock in unison, consider that a warning of an approaching pulse or environmental shift. Brace, secure yourself, and avoid open water.

## Communication and perception

### Expect silence to be misleading

Your companions may be only an arm’s length away and effectively inaudible. Conversely, tiny sounds heard in the intervals between pulses may seem impossibly clear. Do not assume a heard voice is nearby, current, or human.

In particular:

- Do not respond to distant calls unless you can visually identify their source.
- Do not follow bells, knocks, footsteps, or voices heard beyond a water curtain.
- Do not assume a cry means someone is in immediate danger; verify by line tension, visual signal, and locator light.
- Do not separate to investigate a sound.

If you hear a voice speaking words that only you should know, treat it as an environmental hazard, not as privileged information.

### Distrust visual depth

Water curtains may show earlier states of the basin. A whole pillar, an unrooted plant, or a figure in the doorway may be a displaced image rather than an accessible object.

Mark your actual route physically: biodegradable high-visibility tabs, short lengths of colored line, or chalk marks on dry-enough stone. Do not rely on memories of the arrangement of ridges. The site may look different after each pulse, and even unchanged stone can appear displaced through moving water.

Use direct touch and tether tension as your primary reality checks.

A useful team protocol is a periodic “present check”:

- Each traveler touches the same anchor point or line.
- Each displays their clock.
- Each identifies one physically verifiable nearby feature.
- The observer records location, pulse count, and personnel status.

Do this after any major visual anomaly.

## Biological and contamination precautions

Do not eat, drink, or expose mucous membranes to the water. Decontaminate all equipment afterward. Quarantine biological samples rather than opening or examining them in camp.

The sparse plant life deserves special caution. Organisms surviving in this basin may depend on chemical processes, pressure regimes, or temporal conditions not present elsewhere. A root, frond, or red leaf that appears harmless could react badly to normal air, different water, warmth, or light.

For an ordinary traveler, the ethical default should be:

- Take photographs and noncontact observations.
- Collect only detached mineral fragments from stable ground, if collection is truly necessary.
- Do not harvest living material.
- Do not move the black clinging organisms.
- Do not introduce food, plants, soil, bait, dyes, or tracer chemicals into the basin.

Even a small contamination could be disproportionately disruptive in a place with so little established life.

## Reasons to abort immediately

Leave at once if any of the following occur:

- The deep pulses become more frequent or regular.
- Your tether begins pulling toward a direction that does not correspond to any visible current.
- The water suddenly warms significantly.
- The basin darkens despite increasing light overhead.
- The red colony closes and does not reopen over several pulse cycles.
- You see your own team through a curtain in a configuration that contradicts their present location.
- A companion reports missing time, duplicated memories, or being “called” by the doorway or central pool.
- Loose rocks begin circling more than once before settling.
- A brightness overhead stops moving and appears to remain directly above the basin.
- The central island remains surfaced longer than expected.
- The water becomes quiet.

Especially the last one: the normal roar may be part of the basin’s operating state. A sudden comprehensive quietness may mean pressure is building, water is changing phase or direction, or the local rules are about to reset.

## After leaving

Do not camp immediately beside the basin. Retreat to a stable, dry, geologically separate location.

Once out:

1. Confirm every person is physically present and uninjured.
2. Compare clocks and written notes before discussing interpretations.
3. Inspect suits, tethers, masks, and equipment for mineral deposits, roots, organisms, and inexplicable wetness.
4. Rinse and isolate gear.
5. Check for cuts, ear injuries, lung irritation, dehydration, confusion, and delayed cold stress.
6. Ask each traveler to write an account independently before group debriefing.

Independent notes matter. This is a place where observation itself may be temporally or spatially distorted. Do not let the first confident story overwrite everyone else’s experience.

Finally, do not be ashamed to return with very little. The proper first visit may consist of standing safely at the rim, counting pulses, watching water rise where it should fall, and leaving before curiosity turns into a rescue operation.

The basin has survived only briefly by geological standards, yet it already seems to possess habits. Learn those habits from a distance.
