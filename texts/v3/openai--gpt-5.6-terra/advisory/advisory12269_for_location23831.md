## Preparation Brief: The Low Wet Plain

This is not a place that will usually kill a visitor through one dramatic danger. It is a place that wears people down through wetness, glare, heat, bad footing, insects, waterborne illness, and the difficulty of knowing where solid ground ends. Prepare for it as you would for a long crossing of a tropical-to-subtropical floodplain built on limestone and clay—except assume that local life may be unfamiliar and that every pool, reed bed, and shaded ledge has an occupant.

The defining rule is simple:

**Do not trust dry-looking ground, clear-looking water, or still-looking animals.**

The landscape is open enough to make you feel safe. That openness is deceptive. A person can see a long way but may not be able to walk directly toward what they see.

---

## 1. Before Arrival: Establish Your Safety Baseline

If multiversal transit permits it, arrive with a reliable return beacon or a clearly marked extraction point placed on the highest, firmest available ground. Do not put it beside a pool, under an overhang, or on an apparently dry clay flat. Frequent rain and slow sheet flow can turn an acceptable landing site into mud within hours.

Choose or create a base on:

- A broad, elevated rock plate rather than a narrow ledge.
- Ground with visible weathering, lichen, and established coarse grass, indicating that it remains above ordinary water levels.
- A place with clear lines of sight in several directions.
- A location at least several dozen meters from dense reeds, deep pools, and animal trails.
- Ground that shows no fresh hoof churn, concentrated tracks, dung, shed skin, nests, burrows, or claw marks.

Avoid setting up near the few trees. Their shade will be tempting, but their exposed root systems likely mark persistent watercourses, animal congregation points, biting-insect habitat, and potential ambush cover.

Before traveling far from base, spend several hours simply observing. Learn the movement of water, birds, herd animals, and insects. This plain has routes, crossings, feeding times, and dangerous pools that its native animals understand. Watch them before assuming you can improve on their judgment.

## 2. Clothing: Dress for Heat, Wetness, Sun, and Abrasion at Once

The air is humid, the ground is wet, and the sun reflects intensely from pools and pale stone. You need clothing that remains tolerable while soaked and that covers skin without trapping excessive heat.

Bring:

- Lightweight, quick-drying long-sleeved shirts and long trousers.
- A broad-brimmed hat with a chin strap.
- UV-protective neck covering or cloth scarf.
- Polarized sunglasses with a retention strap; keep a spare pair in a hard waterproof case.
- Tough, high-ventilation gloves for rock, reeds, and animal handling emergencies.
- Several pairs of synthetic or wool socks; avoid cotton.
- A light waterproof shell or poncho, preferably one that can ventilate well.
- A dedicated dry sleeping layer stored in a sealed bag.

For footwear, do not rely on a single pair of ordinary hiking boots. The ideal combination is:

1. **Primary footwear:** high-ankle, quick-draining wetland or jungle boots with aggressive soles and puncture-resistant midsoles.
2. **Camp footwear:** light sandals or water shoes, used only around the camp platform and never in murky water.
3. **Optional gaiters:** close-fitting and drainable, for mud, thorn scrub, and small biting or crawling animals.

Rubber boots are useful for shallow mud and standing water but can become dangerous in deep suctioning clay. If your foot sinks and the boot catches, you may lose it—or injure an ankle trying to pull free. Wear boots secured firmly at the ankle, but not so tightly that they cannot be removed in an emergency.

## 3. Water: Assume Every Natural Source Is Unsafe

There is water everywhere, but that does not make it safe. Shallow warm pools with fish, frogs, birds, and grazing animals are biologically rich; they are also likely to contain parasites, bacteria, protozoa, algal toxins, mineral contamination, and creatures that can enter wounds or clothing.

Carry enough water to last longer than you think. The humid heat may disguise dehydration because sweat does not evaporate efficiently.

Your water system should include:

- A substantial personal water reserve for the first 24–48 hours.
- A durable filter capable of handling sediment.
- Chemical purification suitable for biological contaminants.
- A rolling boil capability where practical.
- A collapsible settling container: muddy water should be allowed to settle before filtering.
- Oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets.
- A way to test or at least recognize unusual salinity, alkalinity, sulfur odor, metallic taste, or mineral cloudiness.

Use a layered process:

1. Collect water away from obvious animal crossings and stagnant margins if possible.
2. Let suspended clay settle.
3. Pre-filter through cloth.
4. Filter mechanically.
5. Chemically treat or boil.
6. Store in clean, sealed containers.

Do not drink water merely because fish live in it. Do not assume water dripping from limestone is clean: seep water can carry dissolved minerals, microbial films, and contamination from unseen upper surfaces.

Avoid swallowing water while wading. Cover cuts before entering water. Do not wash dishes, clothing, or yourself near your drinking-water collection point.

## 4. Navigation: The Horizon Will Mislead You

This is a nearly flat country of shallow basins. A destination that appears close may be separated from you by channels, saturated clay, rock shelves, or reed-lined pools that force a long detour. Heat haze and water glare can also distort distance and direction.

Bring:

- A reliable compass and a physical map system, even if you have advanced navigation.
- Waterproof notebook, pencil, and grease marker.
- GPS or equivalent multiversal locator, but do not depend on it alone.
- Signal mirror, whistle, and high-visibility panel.
- Long-distance binoculars.
- A trekking pole or probing staff at least chest height.
- Small marker flags or biodegradable route markers, if ecologically acceptable.

Mark your route consistently. A simple record should include:

- Direction traveled.
- Time.
- Major rock formations.
- Pool shapes and orientations.
- Animal trails crossed.
- Ground condition: firm limestone, crusted clay, slick algae, shallow water, suction mud.
- The height and direction of recent water flow.

Do not navigate by “following the driest ground” without caution. The drier ridges may be narrow, discontinuous, thorn-covered, or funnel you toward animal trails. Likewise, do not follow a watercourse simply because it seems like a natural route: it may lead into deeper basins and the densest insect habitat.

In heavy rain, stop and reassess. The landscape’s drainage is subtle. Water may begin moving across ground that appeared level minutes earlier.

## 5. Movement: Probe Every Uncertain Surface

The major physical danger is not a dramatic bog but irregular footing: slick stone, unstable clay crust, soft basin edges, submerged holes, and sudden transitions from ankle-deep water to thigh-deep muck.

Use a staff before each step when crossing:

- Brown or green water where the bottom cannot be seen.
- Clay that appears smooth, wet, glossy, or recently cracked.
- Reeds that emerge from shallow-looking water.
- The edges of limestone shelves.
- Ground with a crust of salt, dried mud, or algae.
- Any channel marked by unusually vivid vegetation.

Keep your pack light enough that you can remove it quickly. Use a chest strap that releases easily; avoid configurations that trap you under a load if you fall into water or mud.

When crossing soft ground:

- Loosen your gait; take short, deliberate steps.
- Keep your weight centered over your feet.
- Do not lunge or yank a trapped foot upward.
- If you sink, spread weight gradually, use the staff or a pack as support, and backtrack along your own firmer impressions.
- If in a group, stay far enough apart that one person’s failure does not pull another into the same patch.

Do not walk on apparently solid “floating” vegetation unless you have tested it thoroughly. Sedges and reed mats may cover channels or holes.

Wet limestone may be more dangerous than mud. Mineral seepage, algae, liverwort, and smooth weathered surfaces can produce nearly frictionless footing. Step on textured, dry-looking portions when available, and never assume a sloping rock face is safe because it is shallow.

## 6. Wildlife: Treat Every Pool and Reed Bed as Occupied

The local ecology is dense and active. Most animals will prefer to avoid a human, but the country offers excellent camouflage, limited cover, and abundant reasons for animals to defend feeding grounds, nests, young, or water access.

### Water hazards

Do not enter water unnecessarily. Assume it contains:

- Biting or stinging invertebrates.
- Parasites capable of entering through skin, wounds, or mucous membranes.
- Fish or eel-like animals that may bite when stepped near or cornered.
- Ambush predators concealed in murk or under shelves.
- Territorial breeding animals, especially during nesting season.
- Submerged burrows, sharp shells, or rock edges.

Never reach into undercut banks, submerged crevices, root tangles, or holes beneath rock shelves.

Before approaching a pool, watch it from a distance. Look for:

- Repeated ripples moving against wind direction.
- Birds refusing to enter a particular stretch of shore.
- Tracks converging on and then abruptly avoiding one area.
- Unnaturally still water under cover.
- Bubble streams, swaying reeds, or a muddy shape that does not match its surroundings.
- Sudden silence among frogs or smaller birds.

If a pool has many animal tracks but no animals currently drinking, give it extra space. It may be actively occupied by a predator.

### Land predators

The descriptions suggest predators relying on stillness rather than pursuit. Your best defense is awareness, distance, and refusal to corner anything.

- Scan shaded ledges before using them for rest.
- Inspect reed margins with binoculars.
- Never crouch at a pool edge with your back to open ground or dense vegetation.
- Avoid dawn, dusk, and heavily overcast periods for travel near water: many predators become more active under reduced glare and cooler temperatures.
- If you detect a large animal, do not run. Retreat slowly while facing it and maintaining a clear path.
- Do not attempt to photograph, feed, lure, or “test” wildlife.

Carry a loud whistle, air horn, or other nonlethal deterrent. It may be useful for surprising large animals at close range, though test it well away from wildlife and avoid using it casually.

### Birds and herd animals

Wading birds and grazing herds can be useful environmental indicators. Their alarm calls may reveal a predator, sudden weather shift, or human intrusion. But do not follow them too closely: nesting birds can be aggressive, and herds can stampede through surprisingly narrow dry corridors.

Avoid stepping near shorebird nesting patches or egg-laying beaches. In such an open place, nests may be little more than shallow scrapes among stones and dried mud. Watch where you put your feet.

## 7. Insects, Parasites, and Small Life: Your Most Persistent Threat

The loud insect activity is not just atmosphere. Assume biting flies, mosquitoes, ticks, mites, leeches, stinging wasps, and unknown analogues are present.

Bring:

- Broad-spectrum insect repellent appropriate to the biology you expect.
- A treated head net.
- Fine-mesh sleeping netting with a groundsheet or suspended shelter.
- Permethrin- or equivalent-treated outer clothing where safe.
- Tweezers, tick-removal tool, antiseptic, antihistamine, and topical anti-itch treatment.
- Sealed bags for dirty clothes.
- Daily skin inspection tools: small mirror and adequate light.

Wear sleeves, trousers, and socks even when uncomfortable. The humidity will tempt you to expose skin; resist this around reeds, water margins, and during dawn or dusk.

At least once each day:

- Check feet, ankles, knees, groin, waistband, armpits, scalp, and behind ears.
- Examine all cuts for redness, swelling, unusual pain, or moving material.
- Wash mud from skin promptly with treated water.
- Dry feet fully and change socks.
- Treat hotspots before they become blisters.

A small blister or thorn puncture can become a serious problem in continuously wet, microbially active terrain.

## 8. Camp: Keep Dryness as a Deliberate Resource

You will not keep everything dry. You must preserve a few essential things dry: sleeping insulation, first aid supplies, communications, fire-starting equipment, food, and at least one change of clothing.

Set camp above the visible high-water line. Signs of safer elevation include:

- Long-established grass rather than fresh reed growth.
- Weathered rock without a recent mud coating.
- Lack of drifted debris caught in shrubs.
- Few or no dense animal tracks.
- Dry cracks in clay that are old rather than newly forming.
- Evidence that rain drains away rather than gathers nearby.

Do not camp:

- In a shallow basin, even if it is dry.
- Under dripping limestone shelves.
- On a broad mudflat.
- Beside a permanent pool.
- Directly on animal trails.
- Below a slope with seep lines.
- Beneath dead branches or unstable rock overhangs.

Use a raised cot, hammock system, or waterproof groundsheet with insulating pad. A tent should have excellent ventilation and full insect mesh; humidity will create condensation even during dry intervals.

Place gear on a tarp, not directly on stone or clay. Wet rock conducts heat away at night and transfers moisture to anything sitting on it.

Have a rain routine rehearsed:

1. Cover electronics and dry bags.
2. Secure the shelter.
3. Move food and medical supplies to elevated storage.
4. Check runoff direction.
5. Avoid walking around camp and cutting the ground into mud unless necessary.
6. After rain, inspect for changed water paths, animal tracks, and signs that insects have concentrated around new pools.

## 9. Food: Carry Your Own and Be Conservative

The plain may look abundant, but unfamiliar abundance is not food security. Fish, frogs, shell-bearing creatures, tubers, berries, and edible-looking greens may carry toxins, parasites, or chemicals unlike those of your home world.

Bring shelf-stable food sufficient for the expected trip plus reserve days. Favor foods that tolerate humidity once opened and do not require extensive cooking.

If you must forage, apply an extreme standard:

- Eat nothing unidentified.
- Do not sample plants “to test” them.
- Avoid filter-feeding organisms, scavengers, amphibians, and anything collected from stagnant water.
- Cook all animal food thoroughly.
- Do not consume animals found dead, stranded, or behaving oddly.
- Assume bright coloration, bitter sap, milky exudate, or strong mineral/chemical odors are warnings, but do not assume their absence means safety.

Food scraps should be packed out or sealed tightly. Do not discard them around camp; they will attract insects, scavengers, and predators to the one place you need to remain predictable and safe.

## 10. Weather and Heat: Humidity Changes the Rules

The warmth here is “heavy.” Your body will cool poorly because sweat stays on the skin and the air holds moisture. You may overheat without feeling the familiar dry thirst of a desert.

Plan physical travel for early morning and later afternoon, with a shaded or ventilated rest period during the brightest hours. Even then, watch for heat illness.

Signs of concern:

- Headache, nausea, dizziness, clumsiness.
- Unusual irritability or confusion.
- Weakness out of proportion to exertion.
- Cessation of sweating despite heat.
- Muscle cramps.
- Rapid pulse.

Respond early: stop, shade yourself, cool the body with treated water or wet cloths, take electrolytes, and rest. Do not push onward because “the horizon is close.”

Rain may be frequent but not necessarily cooling. Dense straight-down showers can reduce visibility, make limestone slick, fill footprints, disturb animal movements, and turn small channels into active flow. Treat every rainstorm as a navigation interruption rather than a pleasant break.

Lightning is a possible concern on an open plain. If storms become electrically active:

- Leave exposed high points.
- Avoid isolated trees and the edges of water.
- Do not shelter beneath low rock overhangs if they are wet or actively dripping.
- Spread metal equipment away from your body if practical.
- Crouch on insulated material in a lower—but not flood-prone—area.

## 11. Hygiene and Medical Preparedness

Medical self-sufficiency matters here. Wet environments turn minor injuries into logistical problems quickly.

A suitable kit should include:

- Sterile wound irrigation supplies.
- Antiseptic appropriate for repeated use.
- Waterproof dressings and bandage wraps.
- Blister care supplies.
- Broad-spectrum antihistamine.
- Pain relief and anti-inflammatory medication.
- Anti-diarrheal medication and oral rehydration salts.
- Fever monitoring capability.
- A clinician-approved plan for likely waterborne and vector-borne infections.
- Tweezers, fine forceps, tick tool, trauma shears.
- Compression bandage and emergency bleeding control supplies.
- Splinting material or a compact SAM-style splint.
- Emergency communication device and evacuation plan.

Treat all cuts immediately. Clean, disinfect, cover, and inspect twice daily. If a wound becomes hot, swollen, discolored, increasingly painful, foul-smelling, or produces streaking redness, consider it an evacuation-level problem—especially in a world whose microbes are not familiar to you.

Avoid walking barefoot, even at camp. Avoid sitting directly on wet ground. Do not touch your eyes, mouth, or food until hands have been cleaned.

## 12. Ethical and Biological Containment Precautions

A multiverse traveler has obligations beyond personal survival. This old, stable wetland plain may have an ecology finely tuned across immense spans of time. You could introduce organisms, chemicals, seeds, and materials that do disproportionate harm.

Before entry:

- Clean and disinfect boots, clothing, packs, tent stakes, and tools.
- Remove seeds, soil, mud, and biological debris from all gear.
- Use sealed food supplies.
- Avoid bringing live plants, animals, untreated natural fibers, or contaminated soil.
- Favor reusable equipment over disposable plastics.
- Pack out all waste, including used batteries, food packaging, medical waste, and hygiene products.

While there:

- Do not release anything into pools or channels.
- Do not use soap, fuel, repellents, or chemicals near water unless specifically designed to be environmentally safe in that ecology.
- Do not collect specimens unless you have an ethical and containment protocol.
- Do not move animals, eggs, plants, stones, or mud between basins.
- Avoid repeatedly using one animal crossing or trampling fragile wetland vegetation.

On departure, decontaminate again. Mud stuck in boot treads could carry eggs, spores, seeds, larvae, or pathogens across worlds.

## 13. A Good Daily Operating Pattern

A cautious traveler’s day might look like this:

**Before sunrise:**  
Wake under insect protection. Check body for bites, ticks, leeches, and wounds. Inspect footwear. Drink treated water and eat before exertion.

**Early morning:**  
Travel while temperatures are lower. Use binoculars and probe uncertain ground. Make frequent notes and route marks.

**Late morning to midafternoon:**  
Stop on firm elevated ground. Avoid pool margins. Dry feet and socks if possible. Filter and treat water. Observe wildlife rather than moving through the worst glare and heat.

**Afternoon:**  
Travel a shorter second leg, leaving enough daylight to establish camp deliberately rather than settling for the nearest dry-looking patch.

**Before dusk:**  
Set shelter, secure food, refill treated water, inspect the camp perimeter for animal trails and water flow. Complete a second body check.

**Night:**  
Remain inside netting or shelter. Keep boots and clothing protected from insects, rain, and curious animals. Do not wander to water in darkness unless there is an urgent reason and a clear, rehearsed path.

## 14. The Right Mindset

This is a patient landscape. It rewards people who move slowly enough to notice its small warnings.

Do not try to conquer distance. Do not rush to the next rocky rise just because it looks near. Do not treat the open plain as empty because trees are sparse. It is full of lives, paths, hidden water, and long-established patterns.

Keep your feet dry when possible, your drinking water separate from the landscape’s water, your eyes protected from glare, your skin covered, your camp elevated, and your route documented. If the land feels uncertain beneath you, it is almost always wiser to stop, watch, probe, and go around.

The plain has been shaped by water for longer than any visitor can comprehend. Let water be the primary fact of every decision.
