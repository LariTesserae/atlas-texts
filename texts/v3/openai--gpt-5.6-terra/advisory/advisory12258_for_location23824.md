## Preparing to Visit the Root-Hollow City

Treat this as an expedition into a cold wetland, a living structure, and a conservative small society all at once. The greatest immediate dangers are not dramatic: hypothermia, foot injury, infection, getting turned around in low light, falling into water or soft ground, and accidentally violating rules that residents regard as practical survival knowledge.

Do not arrive expecting a “forest town.” The hollow is more like the interior of a vast damp organism that happens to contain homes.

Your objective on a first visit should be simple: arrive safely, make yourself unobtrusive, obtain a local host or guide, stay dry enough to function, and leave without assuming you understand the place.

---

## 1. Before Crossing: Health, Biology, and Quarantine

The city is saturated with molds, spores, wet soil, animal traffic, standing water, and unfamiliar plants. It may also be biologically divergent from your home reality.

### Medical preparation

Before travel, arrange:

- A current tetanus vaccination.
- Broad-spectrum vaccination and disease-prevention measures appropriate to wetland exposure, if they are compatible with the destination’s biology.
- A medical check for asthma, mold allergies, immune compromise, circulation problems, and cold sensitivity.
- Antifungal treatment, antibacterial wound supplies, antihistamines, anti-diarrheal medication, and a basic fever/pain medication.
- Sterile saline, waterproof bandages, gauze, skin barrier cream, and blister treatment.
- A personal supply of any prescription medications packed in multiple waterproof containers.

If you are immunocompromised, have severe asthma, or develop dangerous reactions to mold or insect bites, this is not an appropriate casual destination. Visit only with robust medical support and a guaranteed extraction route.

### Assume local water is unsafe

Even if residents drink it safely, they may have lifelong acclimatization, local knowledge, or simply different tolerances. Bring:

- A durable water filter capable of removing protozoa and bacteria.
- Chemical purification tablets or drops as backup.
- A compact boil system, but use it only where locals permit fire.
- A minimum of one or two days’ safe drinking water on arrival.

Do not casually pour disinfectants, soaps, fuel, or foreign chemicals into the channels. In this environment, a small contamination can spread through water, soil, animal paths, and fungus gardens.

### Avoid biological arrogance

Do not assume every plant is harmless because it resembles a familiar fern, moss, mushroom, berry, or tuber. Do not eat, smoke, brew, touch, or collect anything local without explicit guidance.

Conversely, do not offer local people imported food, seeds, medicines, fabrics, or live organisms without permission. A dropped apple seed, a dirty boot sole, or an ordinary human respiratory infection may be a serious ecological event in a small enclosed settlement.

If multiversal travel protocols permit it, arrive in cleaned clothing and decontaminated footwear, with no loose soil, plant matter, insects, seeds, or animal hair from other worlds.

## 2. Clothing: Stay Warm While Remaining Quiet and Mobile

The cold here is wet, persistent, and invasive. You may not notice how chilled you are until your hands lose dexterity and your judgment worsens. Ordinary cotton is a poor choice: once wet, it will keep you cold.

### Wear layers

A practical clothing system:

- **Base layer:** Synthetic or wool long underwear. Avoid cotton.
- **Insulating layer:** Wool fleece or a light insulated jacket that still retains warmth when damp.
- **Outer layer:** Quiet waterproof shell or waxed cloak. Choose muted colors: dark green, brown, gray, charcoal. Avoid bright technical fabrics, reflective strips, or loud crinkling material.
- **Legwear:** Durable water-resistant trousers, ideally with reinforced knees and seat.
- **Hands:** Two pairs of gloves—one thin dexterous pair, one warmer waterproof pair.
- **Head:** A warm knit cap or hood. A brimmed rain hat can be useful, but avoid anything so wide that it catches hanging leaves and vines.
- **Neck:** Wool or synthetic scarf/tube; damp air will chill you through every opening in your clothing.

Bring at least one complete dry change sealed in a roll-top dry bag. A dry sleeping layer is more valuable than a third fashionable outfit.

### Footwear is critical

You need footwear for wet matting, mud, rootwood slabs, narrow bridges, slick moss, and occasional shallow channels.

Ideal features:

- Waterproof but breathable boots.
- Aggressive non-slip soles.
- Strong ankle support.
- Enough flexibility to feel unstable footing.
- A fit that allows wool socks without constriction.

Carry:

- Several pairs of wool or synthetic socks.
- Thin liner socks if prone to blisters.
- Foot powder or anti-chafing cream.
- A small repair kit: cord, needle, patch material, spare laces.

Do not wear smooth-soled city boots, sandals, open shoes, or rigid climbing boots that make you clumsy on flexible surfaces. Avoid wading barefoot. There may be leeches, hidden spines, burrowing animals, parasites, sharp shell, or roots that split skin.

## 3. Essential Equipment

Bring less than you think. Space is damp, pathways may be narrow, and every unnecessary object becomes something you must keep dry, silent, and out of the way.

### Pack system

Use a compact waterproof pack or a normal pack protected by a true waterproof liner. Organize items in separate sealed bags. The city is humid enough that “water-resistant” is not sufficient.

Useful gear:

- Waterproof pack liner and dry bags.
- Headlamp with a red-light setting, plus spare batteries kept warm and dry.
- A small hand lantern only if it can be shuttered and made dim.
- A physical notebook and pencil. Ink fails; electronics may not.
- Compact compass, though local navigation should be based on guides and landmarks rather than directions alone.
- Signal whistle, used only for emergency.
- Small mirror or reflective signal device, though it may be of limited use beneath the canopy.
- A personal locator/extraction beacon if such technology works across realities.
- A lightweight emergency bivy sack or hammock setup, but do not expect to deploy it without local approval.
- A compact first aid kit.
- N95/P100-style masks for heavy spores, mold work, or a respiratory flare-up. Do not wear one continuously in social situations unless necessary; it can make you look ill, threatening, or inaccessible.
- Insect protection, preferably scent-light and locally cleared.
- High-energy shelf-stable food in sealed packaging.

### Avoid conspicuous modern objects

Metal is rare and valued. An ordinary steel knife, multitool, zipper, buckle, cooking pot, or flashlight may attract attention—not always welcome attention.

Do not display valuable items casually. Secure loose metal, shiny plastics, jewelry, and electronics. If you carry a knife, keep it sheathed and treat it as a tool, not an identity statement.

Your modern equipment may be useful privately, but it can also disrupt local trade, status, and expectations. Do not casually give away steel needles, knives, batteries, antibiotics, lighters, wire, or waterproof fabric. Gifts can become obligations, sources of conflict, or dangerous dependencies.

## 4. Fire, Light, and Heat

The city’s rules about fire are not quaint. Follow them literally.

### Never set fire directly on the ground

The ground is saturated with roots, fungal mats, peat, burrows, and possibly living tissue. A low fire may smolder invisibly, poison air, damage structures, attract animals, or create a danger only locals understand.

If you need heat or cooking:

- Ask where fires are permitted.
- Use an elevated hearth, stone cup, brazier, or local smoke pit if invited.
- Keep fuel contained.
- Never discard hot ash, coals, cigarette ends, batteries, or chemical heat packs into soil or water.
- Do not use a camp stove indoors unless ventilation and local consent are clear.

### Use light carefully

At night, darkness is total except for domestic lamps and unreliable fungi. White light may blind others, disturb animals, reveal you to predators, or be mistaken for a signal.

Use dim, warm, shielded illumination. Red light is often less disruptive, but ask whether red light has local meanings or affects local animals. Turn off lights when walking with a guide who does not need them.

Never follow the blue fungal pulses as though they were trail markers. They respond to disturbance; they are not navigation systems.

## 5. Navigation: Do Not Trust the Landscape to Remain Still

The city’s paths, water channels, root walls, and vegetation are temporary. A map may describe a pattern of wetness rather than permanent streets.

### First rule: obtain a host

Do not attempt to explore alone. Find a resident guide, ideally one recognized by the household or communal chamber. Their knowledge is not merely geographic. They know:

- Which mats conceal deep water.
- Which roots are safe to lean on.
- Where animals sleep or feed.
- Which routes become dangerous at rain.
- Which scent, drip pattern, or fungal behavior signals predators.
- Which garden or household territory you must not cross.
- Where you are permitted to relieve yourself, wash, camp, cook, or collect water.

If you cannot secure a guide, do not go past the city’s outer edge and do not remain after dark.

### Mark your own route cautiously

You may want to mark paths, but do not tie foreign ribbon to plants, carve roots, paint markings, or leave plastic tags. These may violate local rules, damage growth, or confuse residents.

Use a notebook. Make time-stamped sketches and note:

- Direction of travel.
- Number of bridges crossed.
- Distinctive root arches.
- Sound of water.
- Nearby homes or lamps.
- Changes in ground firmness.
- The last reliable place you saw other people.

If you must leave a temporary marker in an emergency, use something removable and inert, then retrieve it.

### Do not assume elevation means safety

A raised root ledge may be slick, fragile, claimed by animals, or isolated by flooding. A low path may be safe today and submerged tomorrow. Follow local route choices even when they look indirect.

## 6. The Rules Must Be Treated as Safety Protocols

The place has explicit rules. Do not debate them on arrival, and do not insist on a scientific explanation before complying.

### “No root may be cut across its grain.”

Do not chop, saw, carve, drill, nail into, or pry at root structures. Do not take “samples.” Do not hang gear from roots unless locals show you a safe method.

The clear fluid is said to attract “too many mouths.” Whether this means insects, scavengers, pathogens, predators, or something more unusual does not matter. Avoid causing it.

If you accidentally damage a root:

1. Stop.
2. Do not wipe, taste, or attempt to seal the fluid with foreign chemicals.
3. Call your guide quietly.
4. Follow their instructions immediately.
5. Be prepared to leave the area.

### “No one walks alone after the blue fungi begin to pulse.”

Interpret this as an absolute curfew for visitors.

Get indoors, to your host’s shelter, or to the communal chamber before dusk. If you are delayed, stay with residents. Do not decide that your headlamp, GPS, weapon, or confidence makes the rule optional.

If blue fungi begin pulsing around your feet while you are outside:

- Stop moving unless your guide directs otherwise.
- Reduce light and noise.
- Stay close to companions.
- Do not run.
- Do not call loudly into the dark.
- Follow the route and pace set by local people.

### “If water gathers inside a home, watch it.”

Do not start bailing, sweeping, or blocking water without permission. A ripple without rain is apparently significant. It may indicate animal movement, underground flow, structural change, or a local danger signal.

If you see unexplained ripples in indoor water:

- Say so quietly.
- Do not touch the water.
- Do not shine bright light directly into it.
- Do not place objects in it to test depth or movement.
- Be ready to leave calmly.

### “Do not name all the animals you see.”

The respectful interpretation is simple: do not behave as if cataloguing equals understanding or ownership.

Avoid constantly identifying, labeling, collecting, photographing, or asking, “What is that?” about every creature. You may ask what is safe, what is dangerous, and what local terms they use—but accept that some beings are not discussed, named only indirectly, or acknowledged through custom rather than classification.

## 7. Animals: Be a Guest Among Neighbors, Not a Wildlife Tourist

Animals are everywhere, and many are part of reciprocal arrangements that you will not understand at first.

### General rules

- Never feed an animal unless a resident gives you the food and instructs you to offer it.
- Never touch domestic browse-beasts, marsh hounds, foragers, birds, or swimmers without clear permission.
- Do not imitate calls, squeaks, whistles, or feeding rituals.
- Keep food stored and sealed.
- Do not leave scraps, wrappers, or crumbs.
- Do not stare down an animal, corner it, separate it from young, or block its habitual route.
- Do not assume a sleeping animal is harmless.

The six-legged browse-beasts may tolerate combing by residents but not by strangers. Marsh hounds may be quiet until they are not. The little black foragers may be valuable sanitation partners rather than pests; do not trap, kick, poison, or frighten them.

### Predators

You may never see one. This does not mean they are absent.

Take sudden silence seriously. So are fresh parallel grooves, recently broken stems, unusual movement in water, and warnings from locals.

If a guide indicates danger:

- Do exactly what they do.
- Keep your hands away from your pack and tools unless instructed otherwise.
- Do not run unless everyone runs and you know where to run.
- Do not climb a root or bridge without local direction; a predator may drive prey into a dead end.
- Do not shine a light into surrounding darkness searching for eyes.
- Do not attempt to defend the group with a weapon unless there is immediate contact and no alternative. An unfamiliar weapon may provoke rather than deter.

Bitter-leaf bundles are not yours to handle. Do not move or “improve” them.

## 8. Social Etiquette and Conduct

This is a small settlement with limited resources, intimate knowledge networks, and meaningful territorial boundaries. Visitors are conspicuous.

### Arrive humbly

Ask for permission before entering a household space, crossing a bridge attached to a home, approaching an animal pen, stepping onto drying racks, or looking inside a shelter.

Wait to be invited across thresholds. Doorway vertebrae strings may be a practical alert, a protective charm, a household marker, or all three. Do not touch them.

Keep your voice low. The hollow carries sound unpredictably, and quiet may be part courtesy, part vigilance.

### Respect household boundaries

A dwelling’s surrounding ring may include work surfaces, gardens, animal routes, smoking areas, and drying space. Do not assume an open-looking patch is public.

Never step on:

- Reed mats without invitation.
- Bundled twigs that may be bridges.
- Bark bowls of mold.
- Knotted reed tags marking food plants.
- Drying racks.
- Animal bedding.
- Rootwood slabs that appear loose or newly fitted.

### Ask before documenting anything

Photography, audio recording, drawing, specimen collection, mapping, or broadcasting may be unwelcome. Even if people agree to a portrait, they may not agree to an image of their home, animals, maps, ritual objects, children, communal stores, or the deepest root chamber.

Especially do not copy or remove bark maps without permission. They may record grief, danger, household movements, and local vulnerabilities, not merely navigation.

### Do not promise permanence

The city moves. Homes shift. Paths become ponds. Avoid comments implying that residents should “fix” their settlement, clear the vegetation, drain the water, build permanent roads, or exploit the roots. Such remarks can sound ignorant at best and hostile at worst.

Ask instead:

- “Where is it safe for me to walk?”
- “What should I avoid touching?”
- “Is this path still used?”
- “May I help with anything?”
- “What does this household need from a visitor?”
- “Would you prefer quiet?”

## 9. Gifts, Trade, and Help

Bring gifts that are useful but socially low-disruption, and present them through a host or communal representative rather than handing out objects indiscriminately.

Possible gifts, subject to prior cultural advice:

- Clean sewing thread or sturdy cordage.
- Fine needles, only if you understand the implications of introducing metal.
- Salt, if it is scarce locally and permitted.
- Plain durable cloth.
- Waterproof storage bags.
- Simple hand tools, but only through agreed exchange.
- Paper, pencils, and non-toxic pigments.
- Medical supplies only through a knowledgeable local healer or a mutually trusted intermediary.

Avoid giving:

- Weapons.
- Large quantities of metal.
- Addictive substances.
- Alcohol.
- Lighters or easy ignition sources.
- Seeds, live plants, pets, insects, or soil.
- Strong perfumes, soaps, pesticides, disinfectants, or chemical cleaners.
- Medicine without explanation, consent, and attention to contraindications.

The best contribution may be labor: carrying water under instruction, repairing mats, sorting supplies, helping lift thresholds before rain, or sitting quietly during a communal gathering. Do not begin “helping” until shown how. In a living settlement, an incorrect repair can be damage.

## 10. Hygiene and Waste

Your habits must adapt to an environment where everything is connected by water and decomposition.

- Use only designated latrines or waste areas.
- Never urinate in channels, gardens, near homes, or against roots.
- Pack out foreign waste whenever possible.
- Do not bury batteries, plastics, foil, medication packaging, or sanitary products.
- Use biodegradable products only if local people approve; “biodegradable” does not mean harmless in an unfamiliar ecology.
- Wash with minimal soap, away from water sources and under guidance.
- Keep wounds covered and clean.
- Check daily for bites, leeches, fungal rashes, trench foot, blisters, and embedded splinters.

If you become ill with fever, vomiting, diarrhea, rash, or respiratory symptoms, tell your host promptly. Do not hide illness out of embarrassment. Isolation may be necessary for your safety and theirs.

## 11. A Good Daily Routine

A sensible visitor’s day would look something like this:

1. **Wake early.** Check your bedding, boots, clothing, and supplies for dampness, insects, mold, and animal disturbance.
2. **Dry what you can.** Use approved racks or sheltered airflow; do not spread belongings across paths or living plants.
3. **Inspect feet.** Change socks before damage becomes serious.
4. **Confirm the day’s route with a guide.** Ask about rain, unstable ground, animal activity, and curfews.
5. **Carry water and food quietly.** Eat without scattering crumbs.
6. **Return well before the fungal pulse period.** Do not schedule “one last look” near dusk.
7. **Keep gear ordered.** In darkness, a misplaced medicine pouch or lamp becomes an emergency.
8. **Record observations privately and respectfully.** Write down conditions, not judgments.
9. **Sleep elevated and dry if possible.** Follow household arrangements; do not rearrange sleeping spaces around roots, water, or animals.

## 12. Emergencies

### If you become separated

- Stop moving.
- Do not follow glowing fungi.
- Do not cross unfamiliar bridges or water.
- Turn off or dim your light unless signaling is needed.
- Listen for familiar household sounds, bridge movement, or your guide.
- Use a whistle only if you have established its meaning with locals; otherwise call softly at intervals.
- Stay off the bare ground if it is actively shifting, but do not climb or enter root gaps blindly.
- Conserve warmth.

### If you fall into water or mud

- Do not thrash; soft ground can worsen around you.
- Drop a heavy pack if it is pulling you down, unless it contains essential survival gear and can be released quickly.
- Spread weight wide.
- Call for assistance.
- Once out, change into dry layers as soon as possible and inspect for wounds, parasites, and cold injury.

### If a shelter floods

Follow the household. Do not salvage possessions before people, and do not independently bail water. Take only your essential dry bag, footwear, warm layer, medicine, and light if instructed.

### If you damage a root, disturb a nest, or violate a rule

Admit it immediately. Do not conceal evidence, make excuses, or attempt a private repair. In this place, the consequences may be ecological and communal rather than merely personal.

---

## 13. Mental Preparation

The hardest part of visiting may be accepting uncertainty.

This is a place where maps expire, walls grow, animals are participants rather than scenery, and a seemingly irrational rule may be condensed knowledge from generations of survival. You will not master it in a week. You may not master it in a lifetime.

Go slowly. Ask permission often. Keep dry. Stay with others after dark. Do not cut, claim, classify, or improve what you do not understand.

Most importantly: if the people of the hollow tell you that a path is no longer a path, believe them.
