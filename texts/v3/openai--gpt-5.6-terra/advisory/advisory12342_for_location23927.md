Prepare for this as you would for a humid limestone sinkhole, shallow cave, and sensitive biological refuge—not as a casual hike. It is close to the surface, but it has many of the risks of a cave: poor footing, unstable rock, bad air pockets, disorientation, flash flooding, and a dense concentration of animals and microorganisms.

## Before you go

### Do not enter alone
Bring at least one reliable companion, and leave a detailed route and return time with someone on the surface. A small hollow can become dangerous very quickly if you slip, are bitten, become ill, or find the entrance blocked by rain-loosened rubble.

If local people recognize the place as a water source, shrine, burial site, or protected habitat, ask permission before entering. The old steps, platform, bowl fragments, and pigment marks indicate cultural significance even if their original meaning is lost. Treat every artifact and marking as protected. Photograph from a distance; do not touch, clean, move, or “improve” anything.

### Check weather far beyond the immediate sky
Do not enter if rain is forecast anywhere in the drainage area above the sink. In dry country, distant rain can reach underground cracks and runoff channels with little warning. A short storm may turn a manageable chamber into a wet, slippery basin with falling water, loose stones, rising pools, and reduced visibility.

Avoid entry:
- During rainy season unless you are specifically equipped and trained for cave flood conditions.
- Immediately after a storm, when rock, mud, and roots may be unstable.
- In the hottest part of the day if the approach is exposed thorn-country.
- At dusk, when bats begin leaving and the chamber’s nocturnal animals become active.
- At night, unless there is an urgent reason and you have proper cave-navigation equipment.

### Consider air quality
The description suggests an enclosed, humid limestone chamber with decaying organic matter and a still pool. Such places can collect carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane, or low-oxygen air, especially near the floor, in side cracks, or after heavy rain.

Bring a compact multi-gas monitor if possible, at minimum one capable of detecting oxygen deficiency and carbon dioxide. Do not trust smell: dangerous air may have no odor, while the sour, mineral smell described may simply be normal decomposition.

If you experience headache, dizziness, unusual fatigue, confusion, breathlessness, nausea, or a sudden sense that breathing is difficult, leave immediately. Do not descend lower to investigate. If a companion collapses in a low area, do not rush in without respiratory protection and a rescue plan; multiple casualties are common in confined-space accidents.

## Clothing and personal gear

Wear clothing that protects you from thorns, insects, limestone abrasion, and damp heat without causing heat exhaustion.

Recommended:
- Lightweight long sleeves and long trousers, ideally quick-drying.
- Tough closed boots with aggressive wet-rock traction. Avoid smooth-soled shoes.
- Gloves for roots and rock, preferably thin enough to preserve dexterity.
- A brimmed hat for the exposed surface journey; remove or secure it underground if it obstructs vision.
- Eye protection against falling grit, insects, and dripping mineral water.
- Insect repellent appropriate to local vectors; use it carefully and avoid spraying directly near the pool or old cultural surfaces.
- A headlamp plus two independent backup light sources. Use warm, low settings around wildlife; keep a stronger light for navigation and exit.
- Spare batteries in a waterproof bag.
- Drinking water carried from outside, plus electrolytes. Do not rely on the pool, leaf cups, or rain basins.
- A compact first-aid kit, including wound cleaning supplies, bandages, antihistamine, oral rehydration salts, tweezers, and any regionally appropriate bite/sting supplies.
- A whistle; voices may not carry well through roots and stone.
- Waterproof notebook, map case, or offline navigation device for recording the entrance and route.

Useful additions:
- Trekking pole or sturdy walking stick for probing mud and testing footing.
- Small tarp or clean sit pad if you must kneel or rest.
- Lightweight emergency blanket or bivy: humid caves can still chill an injured, wet, inactive person.
- Fine mesh face covering if midges are overwhelming, though do not compromise breathing in hot humidity.
- A small sealable waste bag. Carry out all rubbish, including food scraps and used tissues.

Do not wear loose jewelry, dangling straps, or anything likely to snag on roots. Avoid strongly scented products; the chamber’s inhabitants rely heavily on smell and vibration.

## Water, food, and disease precautions

Treat every natural water source in the hollow as contaminated unless you can purify it thoroughly. The central pool may contain animal waste, bacteria, parasites, insect larvae, fungal spores, and minerals. Its blind fish and bubbling behavior make it ecologically valuable, not necessarily drinkable.

Do not:
- Drink from the pool.
- Wash hands, boots, tools, or clothing in it.
- Add purification chemicals to it.
- Let sunscreen, insect repellent, soap, or food fall into it.
- Wade in it, even if it looks shallow.
- Disturb its sediment to “see what is below.”

Carry more water than you expect to need. The chamber’s wet heat will make you sweat, but moisture in the air can disguise dehydration.

Eat before entry and carry compact food, but do not snack casually inside. Crumbs attract ants, rodents, insects, and other animals; food odors may also alter their behavior.

## Entering safely

The sloping opening, loose rubble, and old steps are likely the most hazardous parts.

1. **Pause outside the entrance.** Listen for water, falling stones, bat activity, or the movement of larger animals. Let your eyes adjust.
2. **Check for signs of recent collapse.** Fresh pale rock, broken roots, new dust, or unsupported slabs are warnings.
3. **Test every step.** Limestone can be slick even when it looks dry; moss-covered stone is especially treacherous.
4. **Use three points of contact** on steep or root-laced sections.
5. **Do not rely on roots as ropes.** They may be strong, but a root can detach suddenly, damage the fig or ceiling structure, or bring loose soil down with it.
6. **Keep your group small and close.** Two to four people is better than a crowd. A large group increases heat, noise, trampling, and contamination.
7. **Mark nothing.** Do not tie flagging tape to roots, stack stones, scratch arrows, or leave glow sticks. Use memory, a map, removable digital markers, and an agreed turnaround point.

The old steps may be the safest historical route, but they may also be fragile archaeological fabric. Step beside or around them when possible, using modern footing rather than repeatedly wearing down the old stone. Never use the platform as a seat, table, or gear rack.

## Moving within the hollow

Assume every surface is occupied.

The chamber’s defining feature is that nothing is empty for long: roots, ledges, leaf litter, dead wood, cracks, standing water, and moss all provide habitat. Move slowly, look before placing hands, and avoid shifting material.

### Foot placement
Walk on durable bare rock where possible. Avoid:
- Moss mats.
- Fern beds.
- Leaf litter, where snakes, frogs, insects, and hidden mud may be present.
- Rotten logs and root tangles.
- The edges of pools and mud basins.
- Narrow ledges beneath dripping ceilings.
- The bright shaft directly beneath the broken roof, especially after rain or wind.

Do not turn over stones or bark. Do not probe cracks. Do not reach blindly into holes, beneath roots, or behind fallen rocks.

### Snakes and other animals
The snakes described are small and likely more interested in avoiding you than confronting you, but small does not mean harmless. Their camouflage and stillness are the danger.

If you see a snake:
- Stop where you are.
- Do not try to identify it by approaching.
- Do not touch, pin, photograph at close range, or attempt to move it.
- Let it choose an escape route.
- Back away slowly, keeping your footing.

For frogs, geckos, moths, ants, beetles, bats, and blind fish, the rule is simpler: observe without handling. Amphibians are especially vulnerable to oils, sunscreen, insect repellent, and fungal transmission from human skin. Bats should never be touched, alive or dead, because of disease risk and because disturbance can harm a roost.

Avoid standing under clusters of bats or directly below crevice openings. If bats begin emerging, make your way out quietly and give them the entrance.

### Insects and ants
Ant colonies in roots may respond aggressively if their galleries are disturbed. Do not lean on, pull, cut, or scrape root masses. If ants begin climbing on you in numbers, move away calmly, brush them off outside the chamber if possible, and check for stings or bites.

Midges may be unpleasant but are part of the pool ecosystem. Do not use aerosol insecticide in the hollow.

## Light discipline

Use only as much light as you need.

A powerful beam can disorient bats, attract insects, alter animal behavior, and make the dim chamber feel deceptively open. Keep headlamps pointed at the ground while walking and avoid shining directly into cracks, animal eyes, or the pool.

Never use flame. A candle, torch, lighter, or camp stove risks fire, consumes oxygen, produces fumes, damages the site, and may leave soot on the old pigment or stone.

Do not create a fire on the platform or anywhere in the chamber, even if ancient visitors may once have done so. Repetition does not make a modern impact harmless.

## The pool

Treat the green-black pool as both fragile and potentially dangerous.

Its bubbles may reflect groundwater movement, decomposition, trapped gases, or a small subterranean connection. The blind fish indicates a specialized ecosystem that could be harmed by a single careless visit.

Keep a generous distance from its edge. Mud around such pools may be much deeper and softer than it appears, and the opaque water offers no visual clue to depth, submerged rock, or entangling roots.

Photograph it only from stable ground. Do not drop objects, food, coins, offerings, lights, or test strips into it. Do not collect fish, insects, algae, water, sediment, or mineral samples unless you are formally authorized and following sterile ecological protocols.

## Respecting the human traces

The bowl fragments, stonework, and red markings should be approached as irreplaceable evidence, possibly sacred material.

- Do not touch pigment. Even clean fingers deposit oil and salts.
- Do not wet the wall to make markings “show better.”
- Do not use flash repeatedly on fragile surfaces if it can be avoided.
- Do not remove potsherds, stones, bones, or “souvenirs.”
- Do not reconstruct the broken bowl.
- Do not leave offerings. Modern coins, flowers, food, candles, ribbons, and written notes are still litter unless the living custodians of the site explicitly request them.
- Keep voices low, but do not mistake silence for ownership; this may be a place whose human meaning has simply outlasted its explanation.

If you document the site, record location information privately and responsibly. Publicizing a fragile hidden sink can bring looting, vandalism, and uncontrolled visitation.

## During rain or sudden water flow

If water begins to drip heavily, run in threads, or fall in curtains, leave immediately and calmly. Do not wait to see whether it becomes serious.

The danger is not only rising water. Heavy flow can:
- Make limestone and steps slick.
- Mobilize loose rubble.
- Cause root mats and mud to shift.
- Reduce visibility.
- Flood low pockets with poor air.
- Trap you behind water pouring through the only easy exit.
- Drive animals into the same higher ground you need.

Move toward the known entrance without using the brightest central shaft as a route unless it is clearly the safest line. Stay away from walls where water is actively cutting through cracks, and never shelter beneath a dripping fracture zone.

## Emergency priorities

If someone is injured:
1. Prevent a second injury—stabilize the group and assess footing.
2. Move only if remaining in place is more dangerous than moving.
3. Control bleeding, protect from shock, and avoid overheating or chilling.
4. Do not let an injured person drink untreated water.
5. Signal the surface team or emergency services as soon as possible.
6. If evacuation requires climbing unstable rubble, use slow, deliberate assistance rather than improvising a rope system from roots.

For suspected venomous bite:
- Move away from the animal.
- Keep the bitten limb still and the person calm.
- Remove rings, watches, or tight footwear before swelling begins.
- Seek emergency medical care immediately.
- Do not cut, suck, freeze, shock, or tourniquet the wound.
- Do not attempt to capture the snake.

For a fall into the pool or mud:
- Do not rush in blindly.
- Use stable ground, a pole, or a secured external line if available.
- Keep your own center of gravity low and avoid being pulled in.
- Once out, remove wet clothing if practical, warm the person, clean wounds, and seek medical advice due to contamination exposure.

## Best attitude for the visit

This is not a place to conquer, collect from, or “discover.” It is already full: of water moving slowly through limestone, animal lives stacked in layers, old human attention, and constant decomposition and renewal.

Enter lightly. Stay briefly. Keep to stable ground. Touch almost nothing. Leave no food, light, marking, sound, or object behind. Watch long enough to understand that the chamber is active even when it appears still, then return to the dry bright country above before weather, darkness, or fatigue turns a small buried courtyard into a trap.
