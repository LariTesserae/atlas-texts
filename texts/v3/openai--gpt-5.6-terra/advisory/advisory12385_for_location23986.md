## Before You Go

Treat the Cistern Quarter as a living machine rather than a ruin, slum, curiosity, or underground attraction. Its inhabitants survive because every object, route, sound, drip, and custom has a known place. You will be entering a space where unfamiliarity itself can be dangerous.

Do not arrive expecting solitude, fresh air, privacy, open sightlines, or a clear distinction between public and private space. There is almost none. You will be heard, noticed, and physically close to other people nearly all the time.

If possible, arrange an introduction through a resident or a trusted intermediary from the upper city. An unannounced visitor may be tolerated, but will create immediate concern: strangers bring unknown habits, surface pests, loose objects, dirt, weather-borne seeds, and changes.

If you are visibly carrying advanced multiversal equipment, conceal it or leave it behind unless it is essential for survival. Unfamiliar lights, electronic sounds, automatic mechanisms, metal fittings, synthetic materials, or devices that hum, beep, vent air, produce heat, or alter water may alarm residents and may interfere with their tightly understood environment. The Quarter has endured by making its systems legible. Anything that behaves mysteriously will be regarded as a risk.

## Physical Preparation

### Wear the right clothes

Wear:

- Warm, layered clothing suitable for constant cold damp.
- A close-fitting outer layer that will not snag on nails, ropes, valve handles, or rough stone.
- Durable trousers rather than loose robes, skirts, trailing coats, or anything with hanging straps.
- Waterproof or water-resistant boots with excellent grip and ankle support.
- Gloves: preferably thin work gloves with good tactile control, not bulky winter gloves.
- A close-fitting cap or hood, especially if you are tall.
- Clothes you do not mind smelling permanently of smoke, grease, mineral water, and damp stone.

Avoid:

- High heels or smooth-soled shoes.
- Long scarves, dangling jewelry, backpacks with projecting frames, or wide-brimmed hats.
- Bright reflective fabrics.
- Clothing with loose cords that could catch in walkways or channels.
- Anything precious that cannot tolerate moisture or soot.

The pathways will be wet, uneven, narrow, and interrupted by gutters, low beams, stair edges, sluice covers, and other people’s domestic arrangements. There may be no room to recover balance if you stumble.

### Mind your height and body size

A tall or broad visitor should prepare for real physical difficulty. Ceilings and roofs nearly meet in places; doorways may be low, stairs steep, alcoves narrow, and bridges built for inhabitants who know exactly how to cross them.

Before descending:

- Empty your pockets of hard, loose items.
- Secure your hair.
- Keep your load small and close to your body.
- Be prepared to crouch, turn sideways, and move slowly.
- Do not assume a route is accessible merely because you can see it.

If you have poor balance, severe claustrophobia, mobility limitations, respiratory vulnerability, or panic triggered by darkness or crowding, do not visit without a resident guide and a reliable exit plan. The Cistern Quarter is not forgiving of disorientation.

### Bring your own light—but use it carefully

A small, warm, dimmable lamp is useful, preferably one that does not flicker, buzz, hiss, or cast a harsh beam. Coverable light is best. A simple shaded lantern would fit local expectations better than a powerful flashlight.

However, do not shine light into homes, drainage channels, the spring shaft, or people’s faces. Do not sweep bright beams around casually. In such a dark place, sudden light feels invasive and can conceal the small lamps residents depend upon.

Carry spare power or fuel privately. Do not expect residents to have compatible replacements, and do not ask to connect your equipment to their water-driven mechanisms.

## Health and Hygiene

The Quarter is cold, damp, smoky, and close. Expect irritation of lungs, skin, and eyes.

Bring:

- A cloth mask or respirator appropriate for smoke and mildew-like particulates, though be aware that wearing a mask may initially seem strange or alarming.
- Skin barrier cream for hands and feet.
- A small personal towel.
- Sealed drinking water, unless your host explicitly tells you what water is safe and how it is used.
- Basic first aid supplies: antiseptic, bandages, blister treatment, pain relief.
- Any required medication in waterproof packaging.
- Earplugs for sleeping, but use them only when safely settled; you may need to hear warnings, footsteps, and instructions.

Do not assume that clear water is safe water. The central spring is culturally and practically important, while channels may serve many functions—drainage, work power, washing, warning, or waste movement. Ask before touching any water, stepping over any channel, or filling any vessel.

Be conservative with sanitation. There may be strict customs around washing, wastewater, refuse, and bodily needs because every drain affects someone else downstream. Never pour out water, empty a bottle, rinse an object, discard food, or relieve yourself except where explicitly directed.

## What Not to Bring

The Cistern Quarter’s ecology is notable partly for what it lacks: there are no plants, no moss, no visible algae, and apparently little ordinary decay or sprouting. Respect that absence.

Do not bring:

- Seeds, cut flowers, potted plants, soil, moss, lichens, or gardening materials.
- Fruit with loose seeds, unless it is specifically requested and you can contain all scraps.
- Live animals of any kind.
- Insects, bait, worms, or biological samples.
- Food scraps likely to mold, sprout, or attract pests.
- Unsealed trash.
- Surface rats, obviously—but also avoid carrying bags or boxes that could conceal them.

Check your boots, clothing, packs, and gear for mud, seeds, burrs, insect eggs, and hitchhiking pests before you descend. A resident may ask you to clean them again. Do not take offense.

Iron deserves special caution. It is scarce and highly valued. Do not casually offer rusty nails, broken tools, screws, or hardware as if they were junk; they may be useful, but offering them without understanding local claims can create obligations or disputes. Conversely, do not borrow, handle, lean on, or “fix” iron fittings without permission. A hinge may belong to a family history, not simply to a door.

## The Central Spring

The most important etiquette is simple:

**Do not lean over the spring. Do not stare into it for long. Do not attempt to photograph, scan, sample, map, measure, illuminate, or drop anything into it.**

The inhabitants say the spring remembers the shape of anything reflected in it. Whether you regard this as spiritual law, a warning born of old experience, or a literal property of the place is irrelevant. Follow it.

Practical reasons also support the taboo:

- The shaft is deep and dangerous.
- Its water is part of the settlement’s life support.
- Dropped objects may foul mechanisms or become impossible to recover.
- Crowding the lattice could damage old stonework.
- Your reflection, lamps, tools, and gestures may be disturbing even if you mean no harm.

If you must pass near it, keep your gaze on the path, keep your possessions secured, and follow your guide’s pace. Never toss a coin “for luck.” Never make an offering unless a resident gives you a very specific instruction to do so.

## Social Etiquette: You Are Always Within Earshot

Assume everything you say can be heard by people you cannot see.

Do not whisper about residents, speculate about their homes, make jokes about the damp, comment loudly on smells, or discuss private matters in passages. A thin wall, pipe, floorboard, or conduit may carry your voice farther than expected.

Speak plainly and at a moderate volume. You may need to raise your voice to be heard over water and machinery, but do not shout unless there is danger. Ask your host how people signal for attention. In a place where sound carries, a call may have conventions and consequences.

Do not use headphones while walking. You need to hear:

- Warning clappers.
- Water changes.
- A resident calling from a doorway.
- A creaking walkway.
- Instructions from behind you.
- The sound of an approaching load or moving mechanism.

If you stay overnight, understand that you will hear domestic life continuously. You may hear arguments, prayers, coughing, repairs, children, intimate conversations, and grief. The correct response is generally to behave as if you did not hear them.

## Never “Improve” Anything

The Cistern Quarter treats change as potential damage because, for them, it often is. A repaired leak may redirect water into a sleeping niche. A moved stool may block an emergency route. A tightened rope may alter the behavior of a waterwheel. A well-meant cleaning may erase a marker. A modern adhesive, sealant, battery, or pump may produce failures no one knows how to diagnose.

Therefore:

- Do not move objects, even objects that appear abandoned.
- Do not step over barriers, chalk marks, knotted cords, or placed pebbles.
- Do not “clear” a path.
- Do not plug drips or cover gutters.
- Do not offer repair advice unless asked.
- Do not photograph or sketch infrastructure in a way that causes you to linger or obstruct passage.
- Do not sit on beams, pipework, wheel housings, thresholds, or stone bars.
- Do not touch valve handles, reels, weights, clappers, ropes, and latches.
- Do not reposition lamps.

A small marker may mean, “This board is loose,” “Water is rising,” “This path belongs to a household,” “Do not open this,” or “Someone has changed something and notice must be preserved.” Ask, rather than infer.

If you accidentally move or damage anything, report it immediately. Do not quietly put it back. In this place, an unnoticed change is worse than an admitted mistake.

## Walking and Navigation

Never wander alone.

The Quarter’s layout is dense, layered, and partly domestic. A route may pass through kitchens, beneath sleeping shelves, over active channels, or through a workshop. What looks like a corridor may be someone’s threshold; what looks like a private room may be the only way to reach three other dwellings.

Walk:

- Single-file where directed.
- With one hand free.
- Slowly enough to avoid splashing or jostling.
- On the center of worn paths where possible; their wear often indicates safe footing.
- Away from gutters unless you are told otherwise.
- With your bag in front of you in tight spaces.

Do not stop suddenly in a passage. Move to a widened area, doorway edge, or designated waiting point before pausing.

If you become separated from your guide, stop where you are unless the spot is actively unsafe. Do not attempt to retrace your route. Call out in the manner you were taught, or wait for someone to notice. The Quarter is small, but its routes may be confusing precisely because everything is so close together.

## Gifts and Trade

A good gift should be useful, compact, durable, nonliving, nonintrusive, and not demand a change in local systems.

Potentially appropriate gifts, if offered through a host:

- High-quality sewing needles.
- Fine thread, waxed cord, or strong natural fiber line.
- Small whetstones.
- Durable leather patches.
- Plain cloth, especially tightly woven cloth.
- Lamp wicks.
- Simple hand tools with no unfamiliar mechanism.
- Clean beeswax or suitable lamp fuel, if confirmed compatible.
- Good writing materials: paper, charcoal, chalk, graphite, small notebooks.
- Salt, preserved food, or spices in sealed containers—only if residents welcome food from outside.
- Useful metal stock, offered respectfully and with no expectation that it be immediately accepted.

Avoid gifts that impose maintenance burdens: batteries, appliances, electric lights, complex filters, unfamiliar medicines without instructions, plastic novelties, clocks that tick loudly, or anything that needs replacement parts.

Do not give flowers, seeds, greenery, or pets. Do not give mirrors, especially casually. Given the spring taboo and the scarcity of open reflective surfaces, mirrors may have implications you do not understand.

## Photography, Recording, and Multiversal Instruments

Ask before making any record.

A camera flash can be disruptive. Continuous recording can feel invasive in a place where walls do not provide acoustic privacy. Mapping equipment, environmental sensors, water analyzers, drone-like devices, and dimensional instruments may be mistaken for threats to the water system or for attempts to claim knowledge that belongs to resident families.

If you are allowed to record:

- Avoid the spring unless explicitly permitted.
- Avoid sleeping areas and domestic interiors.
- Avoid capturing conversations.
- Do not block walkways while composing an image.
- Keep equipment silent.
- Share copies only if residents understand what that means in your world.

If your multiversal transit device must remain with you, ensure it cannot activate unexpectedly. A portal opening, sudden pressure change, ozone smell, unusual light, or loud harmonic noise could cause panic in such a confined chamber—and may have structural consequences.

## Staying Overnight

Only stay if invited. Overnight presence takes space, warmth, air, and social patience.

Ask in advance:

- Where you may sleep.
- Where you may store belongings.
- Which route to use at night.
- What lights may remain lit.
- How to request help.
- What emergency sounds mean.
- What to do if water rises.
- Whether you should wake someone before leaving your sleeping place.

Bring a compact sleeping layer insulated from damp below. Do not assume bedding will be dry. Keep valuables sealed and elevated. Avoid placing anything beneath a drip, near a channel, or against a wall.

Earplugs and an eye covering can help, but do not seal yourself off completely. Learn the warning clapper sound. The water systems are likely the settlement’s most urgent danger, and residents may respond quickly to subtle signals that you will not yet recognize.

## Emotional Preparation

The Quarter may feel oppressive at first. The ceiling, damp, crowding, noise, darkness, and lack of living green things can produce a powerful urge to leave. Do not mistake that response for proof that the residents are trapped, miserable, or waiting to be rescued.

They have a deep practical relationship with a place you will understand only superficially. What seems to you like stagnation may be accumulated safety. What seems like superstition may be a social rule that prevents damage. What seems like poverty may include forms of continuity and mutual knowledge difficult to find on the surface.

At the same time, do not romanticize hardship. The Quarter is likely exhausting to live in: cold, labor-intensive, loud, constrained, and always dependent on careful maintenance. Listen more than you interpret.

The people may be curious about the sky, weather, trees, distance, sunlight, or your own world. Answer honestly, but avoid speaking as though the surface is inherently cleaner, freer, or more real. Their “Sky Speaking” is not ignorance of the upper city; it is a relationship to it.

## Emergency Rules

Before entering, learn the local signals for:

- Rising water.
- Blocked conduits.
- Fire.
- Structural movement.
- A missing child or visitor.
- An animal intrusion, especially a rat.
- Evacuation or ascent.

If a rat enters, do not chase it, scream, stomp, or try to help unless directed. Its arrival is not a minor nuisance in a sealed settlement. It may carry disease, reproduce, damage stores, or simply create chaos in narrow spaces.

If water rises:

1. Follow residents immediately.
2. Do not stop to retrieve belongings.
3. Do not attempt to close valves or lift objects unless specifically instructed.
4. Keep to known routes.
5. Avoid central channels and low sleeping niches.
6. Do not go near the spring shaft.

If a lamp goes out, do not wave it around, relight it hastily, or use an open flame from your own kit without permission. Fuel, oxygen, soot, and fire behavior may be closely managed.

## The Best Attitude to Bring

Bring patience, physical humility, and a willingness to be instructed in things that appear obvious.

The essential rule is this:

**In the Cistern Quarter, familiarity is infrastructure.**

Residents know the meaning of a groan in the timber, a particular chill in the air, a changed rhythm in a drip, or three chalk marks beside a stair. You do not. Your task is not to decode the Quarter quickly. Your task is to avoid adding uncertainty to it.

Travel lightly. Touch little. Ask often. Keep your footing. Respect the water. And when you leave, make sure the place is arranged exactly as it was before you entered.
