## Preparation Brief: Visiting the Under-City

Treat this destination less like a conventional city and more like an active industrial vessel embedded in unstable ground. It is inhabited and civic-minded, but its civic systems are inseparable from heat management, ventilation, structural maintenance, and water control. A visitor’s greatest risks are not crime or wilderness; they are dehydration, heat injury, disorientation, machinery, poor air, sudden system changes, and accidentally interfering with infrastructure that everyone depends on.

Do not arrive expecting ruins, adventure tunnels, or an informal underground market. This is a working settlement under pressure.

### Before You Go

Obtain a local access credential if at all possible. Entry may be controlled not only for security but for load management: the city may need to know how many bodies are consuming water, generating heat, and occupying evacuation capacity. A host, district steward, maintenance office, or transit coordinator is worth arranging in advance.

Ask specifically about:

- Current chamber temperature and humidity
- Ventilation-cycle schedule
- Any steam-release, drilling, or blasting zones
- Air-quality requirements and whether outside respiratory equipment is permitted
- Local water status: potable tap locations, rationing, mineral content
- Rail-cart crossings and pedestrian restrictions
- Whether your lodging has independent ventilation, a heat baffle, and two exits
- Current construction closures and fissure-monitoring alerts
- Medical availability, especially heat illness treatment and respiratory care

If you have a condition affected by heat, humidity, pressure changes, poor sleep, enclosed spaces, chemical sensitivity, or limited mobility, make plans more conservatively than you would for an ordinary city trip. The physical environment can turn a modest health issue into an urgent one quickly.

Tell someone outside the city where you are going and when you expect to check in. Underground communications can be inconsistent, and a harmless systems event may temporarily isolate a district.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

Bring light, durable workwear rather than fashionable travel clothing.

Recommended:

- Moisture-wicking base layers
- Loose overshirt or coveralls of cotton-blend or flame-resistant fabric
- Sturdy closed-toe boots with slip-resistant, heat-resistant soles
- Extra socks; expect constant dampness from condensation
- Thin work gloves, preferably with heat-resistant palms
- A washable cap, bandanna, or sweatband
- A lightweight waterproof layer—not for rain, but for steam bursts, dirty condensation, and wet walkways
- A small towel or absorbent cloth

Avoid:

- Sandals, open shoes, or thin soles
- Long trailing garments, loose scarves, dangling jewelry, or bags with unsecured straps
- Synthetic clothing that melts easily near heat sources
- Clothing you cannot tolerate getting mineral-stained or rusty

The rails, handholds, metal stairs, and maintenance housings may be hot enough to burn. Assume every unpainted pipe, metal barrier, and rail has the potential to be hotter than it looks.

### Respiratory and Eye Protection

Carry a well-fitting particulate mask at minimum. A reusable half-face respirator with replaceable filters is preferable if local authorities approve it. The air is actively managed, but steam, mineral dust, welding fumes, concrete dust, insulation fibers, and occasional exhaust may be present near work zones.

Also bring:

- Clear safety glasses or sealed goggles
- Saline eye drops if you are prone to irritation
- A few spare mask filters or disposable masks in a sealed bag

Do not assume the air is dangerous everywhere. Do assume conditions can change district by district and cycle by cycle.

### Hydration and Health

Heat exhaustion is likely to be the most ordinary visitor hazard.

Carry:

- An insulated water bottle, ideally metal with a protective sleeve
- Oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets
- A small amount of quick sugar and salty food
- Any essential medication on your person, not in luggage
- A basic blister kit and small burn-dressing kit
- A personal thermometer if you are spending more than a day
- Earplugs for sleeping, though never wear them while walking through work areas

Do not depend entirely on bottled water. The city’s communal taps may be the reliable source, but learn which taps are drinking-water taps. Pipes are color-coded for a reason, and “water” does not necessarily mean potable water.

### Navigation and Light

Bring:

- A small headlamp with a red-light mode
- A compact backup flashlight
- Spare batteries or a power bank
- An offline map stored on your phone, if available
- A physical written card containing your lodging, host contact, emergency instructions, and district name
- A wristwatch

A headlamp is useful during outages or maintenance diversions, but do not shine it into occupied windows, security stations, or workers’ faces. In a dim city, careless light feels intrusive.

Do not rely on GPS. It may be useless underground. Navigation will be based on marked routes, pipe colors, numbered hatches, district signs, pump houses, ventilation grilles, and the painted arrows that identify alternates.

### Luggage

Travel light. A compact backpack that can be worn on the front in narrow passages is better than rolling luggage. Wheels are troublesome on grated walkways, uneven paving, steep stairs, wet metal, and service corridors. Large luggage can block evacuation routes and is likely to be resented.

Waterproof or seal your electronics and documents. The city’s condensation is mineral-heavy; it can leave conductive residue and damage equipment over time.

## On Arrival

Pause before walking far from the entry point. Let your body adjust to the heat. You may not feel dehydrated immediately because the humidity prevents sweat from evaporating normally, but you will still lose fluids.

Find these things first:

1. The nearest potable-water point  
2. The nearest marked emergency refuge or assembly point  
3. Your two nearest exit routes  
4. The closest public washroom  
5. The current ventilation-status display, if one is visible  
6. The local emergency tone guide  

Learn the difference between ordinary civic sounds and warnings. This is essential. The quiet, courteous warning tone described in the city is likely not theatrical—it signals a systems change before it becomes physically obvious.

Ask a local what the tones mean. Do not assume a single tone has a universal meaning throughout the chamber. A ventilation warning in one sector may mean “close shutters and remain indoors,” while in another it may mean “clear the rail lane” or “expect hot downdraft.”

## How to Move Through the City

Walk as if you are in an industrial facility, because you are.

- Stay on marked pedestrian routes.
- Do not step over cables, rail lines, barriers, or painted boundary marks.
- Give furnace carts absolute right of way.
- Never stand between a cart and a wall, even if the cart appears distant or silent.
- Do not use ladders, catwalks, sealed hatches, or maintenance stairs unless invited or clearly designated for public access.
- Keep one hand free on steep, wet, or metal stairs.
- Test handrails lightly before gripping them fully; they may be hot.
- Watch the ground as much as the route ahead. Cracked grout, warm seams, wet steel, and newly patched floor sections may indicate active hazards.
- Do not lean on pipes, cabinets, ducting, or support frames.
- Never move a valve, turn a wheel, reset a breaker, or “help” with a dripping pipe.

The city is compact, but it is not simple. Routes may change due to repair work or thermal shifts. If a passage is closed, do not treat that as an inconvenience to be bypassed. The alternate routes exist because the original route may be structurally compromised, under pressure, or needed for emergency operations.

## Ventilation Shifts and Pressure Cycles

When the warning tone sounds, stop treating the environment as stable.

Observe what local residents do. If shutters close, close yours. If canopies are tensioned, move out from beneath loose fabric. If people clear a passage, clear it with them. Do not linger to photograph the shifting vapor or investigate where the airflow is coming from.

During a hot-air drawdown:

- Move away from low points and narrow cul-de-sacs if instructed.
- Avoid standing under condensate pipes or near louvers.
- Secure loose papers, light bags, and clothing.
- Drink water before you feel thirsty.
- Reduce physical exertion.
- Do not enter newly exposed steam or vapor zones.
- Keep your mask available; disturbed dust or industrial vapor may move with the air.

A sudden stop in dripping can be as meaningful as a sudden increase. It means the system has redirected water or heat. In this city, environmental changes are often deliberate, but deliberate does not mean harmless to an uninformed visitor.

## Heat Safety

The under-city’s heat is not merely uncomfortable. It can impair judgment, sleep, appetite, and coordination.

Use a conservative routine:

- Drink small amounts frequently.
- Take breaks before fatigue becomes obvious.
- Eat regular salty food.
- Avoid alcohol, especially during the first days.
- Limit caffeine if it makes you anxious, dehydrated, or prone to palpitations.
- Shower or cool your wrists and neck when safe water is available.
- Schedule walking and errands around known ventilation cycles.
- Sleep in the coolest protected room available, not near external ducts, rail corridors, or machinery walls.

Seek help immediately if you experience:

- Confusion, faintness, clumsiness, or unusual irritability
- Headache that worsens despite rest and water
- Nausea or vomiting
- Cramps that do not ease
- Hot dry skin after prolonged heat exposure
- Chest pain, breathing difficulty, or a racing heart
- Sudden inability to sweat, or sweating far more than usual without relief

Do not “push through” heat illness to avoid making a fuss. This is likely a community accustomed to practical emergencies; early intervention is normal and considerate.

## Local Etiquette

The city’s sparse streets do not mean it is empty. People may be indoors, behind shutters, on upper walkways, in work rooms, or moving according to schedules you cannot see. Treat apparent emptiness as privacy, not abandonment.

Good practices:

- Keep your voice low. Sound carries unpredictably and has few places to dissipate.
- Do not peer into lit windows or open doorways.
- Ask before photographing people, homes, work stations, ventilation equipment, or marked infrastructure.
- Keep public taps, washrooms, stairwells, and service alleys clear.
- Do not hang personal belongings from pipes, rails, hooks, or cables.
- Sort waste exactly as instructed. Enclosed systems cannot absorb careless garbage.
- Return communal cups, tools, masks, and charging equipment promptly.
- If you spill water, food, or chemicals, clean it or report it immediately. Moisture and debris matter more here than they would above ground.
- Respect drying masks and work clothing. They are not casual laundry; they may be safety gear.

The people who live here may value competence more than ceremony. The best way to be welcome is to be observant, self-contained, willing to ask questions, and quick to follow procedures without argument.

## Food, Water, and Hygiene

Food may be unusually protected, packaged, and carefully stored because there are no animals to consume scraps and no forgiving outdoor ecosystem to disperse waste. Do not mistake this for paranoia. In a closed city, decay has nowhere convenient to go.

Eat food from reputable kitchens or sealed stores. Avoid leaving food in your room, especially near warm walls or ducts—not because pests will take it, but because heat accelerates spoilage.

Use only approved drinking sources. Condensation is everywhere, but it may carry minerals, corrosion products, lubricants, or residues from the pipe exterior. Water dripping from a clean-looking pipe is not necessarily safe.

Take shorter, cooler showers if water and heat systems are strained. Keep drains clear. A blocked drain in an enclosed urban chamber can become a community problem very quickly.

## Where to Stay

Choose lodging based on infrastructure, not charm.

Prefer a room that has:

- Two marked exits or one exit plus an accessible refuge route
- A functioning door seal and controllable shutter
- A visible ventilation vent that is maintained, not improvised
- A location away from main furnace-cart rails
- No direct wall shared with an engine room, hot duct, or active construction face
- Nearby potable water and washrooms
- Clear building numbering
- A host who can explain local alarms

Avoid rooms immediately beneath major condensation lines, adjacent to raw exposed rock, at the end of dead-end service alleys, or close to sealed hatches without explanation. A sealed hatch may be entirely safe, but it may also be an emergency boundary, old shaft, pressure door, or access point that becomes active without warning.

## If Something Goes Wrong

### Steam or Hot-Gas Release

If you hear a hiss, roar, or abrupt change in air pressure:

- Do not investigate.
- Move crosswind if airflow direction is clear, or follow marked evacuation arrows.
- Get behind a substantial wall or into an approved enclosed refuge if nearby.
- Cover nose and mouth with your mask or cloth while moving.
- Avoid low channels and enclosed recesses.
- Do not touch wet surfaces near the release; “wet” may mean condensed steam and may be dangerously hot.

### Power Loss

Remain calm. The city likely has layered emergency systems. Do not begin wandering with your own light unless movement is required.

- Stop where you are if the route is safe.
- Use a low light, not a bright beam.
- Listen for instructions.
- Avoid stairs, rails, and construction edges until emergency lighting stabilizes.
- Preserve battery power.
- Do not enter elevators, lifts, or automatic doors during unstable power.

### Structural Concern

If you see fresh cracking, falling grit, a bowed surface, new water flow, sudden heat from the floor, or hear popping/settling from a wall:

- Leave the immediate area quietly but promptly.
- Warn nearby people without shouting unless there is immediate danger.
- Report the location using the nearest identifiable markers: pipe colors, hatch number, stair designation, shop, pump house, or wall code.
- Do not return for belongings.

In this place, a wall “deciding to become part of the city” may be poetic language for an actual ongoing geological and engineering reality. Take closure signs seriously.

### Becoming Lost

Do not keep walking in hopes that the city will become more legible. It probably will not.

Stop at a public, well-lit point: a tap, depot, washroom, substation exterior, staffed kiosk, or marked junction. Use your written address card. Ask for directions by district and infrastructure landmark, not by vague descriptions such as “the place with pipes.” Everything has pipes.

## Psychological Preparation

The under-city may feel oppressive even when you are physically safe. There is no true horizon, weather, dawn, birdsong, or casual distance. The air moves with purpose. Every sound may seem meaningful. Every surface appears occupied by a function.

This can produce irritability, mild claustrophobia, disturbed sleep, or the strange feeling that you are always indoors at work.

Counter it deliberately:

- Maintain a schedule.
- Seek the garden channel or other permitted green spaces.
- Take short quiet breaks away from machinery.
- Use warm, low lighting in your room if possible.
- Keep contact with someone outside the chamber.
- Do not isolate yourself after a stressful systems event.
- Give yourself permission to leave early if the environment is affecting you badly.

You do not need to be fearless to visit successfully. You need to be attentive and willing to let the city’s systems set the pace.

The central rule is simple: this settlement survives because everything has a place, a purpose, and a maintenance procedure. As a visitor, your safest and most respectful approach is to become temporarily legible to that order—carry what you need, stay where you are meant to be, ask before improvising, and move promptly when the city tells you that it is changing.
