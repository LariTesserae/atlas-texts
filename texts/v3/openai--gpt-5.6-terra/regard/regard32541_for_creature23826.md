The power did not arrive as light.

Light had already made a mistake there, collecting in corners and showing what had not yet happened. Nor did it arrive as warmth. The basin had warmth enough: the intimate fever of water under impossible pressure, the mineral warmth of a wound in new stone.

It arrived as a permission.

For three pulses, nothing changed.

The water climbed, fell, stood trembling between the ridges. The long colorless bodies passed in and out of it. The black folded postures released from the walls and rose, vanished, returned. The red shrub opened its blunt translucent leaves and shut them again.

On the fourth pulse, the deep sound from beneath the basin did not strike.

It asked.

This was almost too small a difference to bear.

Every hanging sheet drew taut. The pale inhabitants opened their mouths. Along their throats and ribs, the gray seams fluttered. The pillars gave a sound like stone remembering a name.

Then the basin answered.

Not in speech. It had no need of speech. It answered by revealing, all at once, the direction of every current within it—not where each stream was going, but what each stream desired to become.

The water falling from the roof desired to return to a cloudless height.

The water rising through the floor desired darkness, pressure, root, the old wordless chemistry beneath worlds.

The water held upright between cliffs desired neither ascent nor descent. It desired to remain a threshold.

The white root in the sealed doorway desired the other side of the wall.

The red shrub desired to continue being opened and closed by rhythms it had not chosen.

The black shapes desired, with their whole minute folded selves, to unfold.

And the pale inhabitants—

The power found that they desired nothing simple enough to grant.

They were not prisoners, though they suffered confinement. They were not native, though no other place had made them. They were not unfinished, though something in them had been interrupted. They had become the basin’s way of waiting: its hands, its listening organs, its memory of a sky that might once have existed or might still exist somewhere ahead.

To take them away would have been a violence disguised as mercy.

To leave them untouched would have been cowardice disguised as reverence.

So the power did a smaller thing.

It gave the still hollow on the central island one more reflection.

Not a better one. Not an escape made visible. Not even a promise.

When the island rose after the next deep pulse, steaming through the blue-gray tumult, its shallow water showed the clear dry sky as always. Grass bent. Birds crossed. The invented sun burned at the edge of sight.

But this time, beyond the grass, there was stone.

Black, fresh-edged basalt.

And in that dry reflected place stood four short pillars, smooth on one side. A square doorway waited beneath them. From a crack in its wall, a white root emerged into air, pale and fine as a thread of water.

The reflection had always shown another world.

Now it showed the basin too.

One pale inhabitant came onto the island.

Water withdrew from its shoulders in long strands. It stood beside the hollow. Its eyes remained closed, as if sight were not its first sense.

Then, slowly, its hand moved over the reflection.

The reflected figure beneath the dry sky moved its hand as well.

Not a mirror. Not an echo.

An answer.

The inhabitant’s fingers shook. This was the first motion in the basin that belonged to no current.

Around the cliffs, the other inhabitants became visible: in the bowls, behind the pillars, in the sealed doorway, beneath sheets of falling water. Their seams shone softly. The red shrub opened. The water cleared.

For a single interval between pulses, every possible arrangement of them appeared—not as ghosts or predictions, but as a gathering.

Some stood tall enough to join floor and roof-water. Some crouched small beneath the rocks. Some were long and colorless, passing through the currents. Some clung black and folded to the walls. Some had become roots. Some were only gestures held in water.

The benevolent power did not tell them which was their true form.

It did not tell them that the reflected place was reachable.

It did not tell them it was not.

Instead it altered one law, delicately, where no law could be seen:

What was recognized could recognize in return.

The inhabitant on the island lowered its hand into the still water.

In the reflected dry world, the counterpart raised its hand from below.

Their palms met on opposite sides of an impossible surface.

A sound entered the basin.

Not louder than the roar. It could not have been. But it was heard by every stone, every root, every folded black creature, every body made from water’s changing mind.

It was the sound of a bird taking flight.

The deep pulse came then, immense and ancient.

The island sank.

The reflection broke into rings.

Water seized the pale figure and began, as it always had, to distribute it among the basin: shoulder into current, hand into pale turbulence, dark strands flowering through the vertical sheets.

But something remained different.

The raised hand did not vanish.

Not as a silhouette. Not as an afterimage held in falling water.

In the square doorway, behind the unbroken wall, a hand-shaped hollow appeared in the basalt.

It was small. It was dry.

The white root found it immediately.

And because the power was wise, it did not widen the passage.

It let the root do that.
