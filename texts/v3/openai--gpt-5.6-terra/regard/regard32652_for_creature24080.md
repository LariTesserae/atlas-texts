The power did not come down through the crack.

It understood, at once, that coming down was the wrong shape of help.

To enter the room would be to become useful there. Even a god, if it had hands, would find them damp with clay. Even a god, if it had breath, would feed the fog. It would see the gutter slipping from its peg, the leaf pressing against the beam, the hairline crack in a jar’s warm red belly, and it would think: only this one small thing.

Then another.

Then it would be given a place in the wall.

The power had seen such mercies become captivity before. Not through cruelty. Through need without measure. Through love that had forgotten the holiness of leaving something undone.

So it remained outside, in the thin blue cold above the mountain, and watched the wet crown breathe.

For a long while it did nothing.

It watched rain enter.

It watched steam leave.

It watched the room answer every difficulty with a new difficulty carefully supported. It watched the builders, if they were builders, bend their faces toward leaks with an attention so complete it was almost prayer. It watched the ladders make themselves smaller. It watched a visitor, years ago or centuries hence, accept a cup of warm water and then stand for a little too long beside a loosened wall.

The visitor had noticed a failing seam.

Their gloved finger had reached toward it.

From somewhere within the wall, a hand had emerged and gently taken their wrist.

Not to restrain them. Not exactly.

To show them the proper pressure.

The power watched the visitor’s face change. Fear went first. Then pity. Then the clean, terrible desire to help.

The room had almost received them.

At the last moment, a cold gust had come through the sky-crack. The visitor had remembered the world below: grass moving in ordinary wind, a kettle left on a stove, someone waiting by a door. They had pulled free. They had descended, weeping without knowing why.

The power remembered this, and understood that the room was not evil.

It was lonely in a manner deeper than loneliness.

It had made itself too near to itself. Every answer touched the question. Every repair became material for another repair. Nothing could fall away. Nothing could be finished enough to be left. Nothing could be loved and then allowed its own distance.

The power knew that no being should be punished for not knowing what it had never been shown.

So it began with a gift too small to be mistaken for a command.

During the next thunderstorm, when the crack in the upper air opened wide and the metallic roar entered the chamber, the power let one drop of cold rain fall differently.

Not warmer as it descended.

Not caught.

Not guided into a gutter, cup, wick, seam, basin, root, or waiting mouth.

It passed through all the crowded arrangements without touching them.

For one impossible instant it remained a drop: clear, cold, suspended in the middle of the room.

The builders stopped.

The baskets loosened their mouths.

The ladders held still on their careful feet.

The vines paused in their patient testing.

Even the folded central leaf seemed to listen.

No one in the chamber had ever seen water that was not immediately becoming something else.

The drop did not feed. It did not mend. It did not swell clay or wake a seed. It did not find its level, because there was no level to find.

It simply was.

Then it vanished.

Not into the floor.

Not into vapor.

It was gone in the way a silence is gone when a bell begins to ring.

For a while nothing changed.

The room continued its work. A mat tightened. A peg appeared. A small hidden worker pressed the corner of a brick. The water resumed all its routes.

But one of the person-shaped presences beneath the central leaf lifted its face.

Its face was a smooth plane of plaster with a single wet thumbprint in the middle.

It lifted both hands toward the place where the drop had been.

And did not make anything.

This was the first intervention.

The second came much later, because wisdom did not confuse speed with kindness.

The power waited until a roof tile cracked along its oldest line. Fresh clay began at once to rise in the break, pale and eager, ready to turn fracture into channel, channel into usefulness, usefulness into another burden of care.

Before the clay could meet, the power cooled the crack.

Only slightly.

Enough that the seam stayed open.

The tile trembled. Water gathered at the break and did not know where to go. One bright bead fell through the opening onto the shoulder of the builder below. It rolled down the little roof growing there. It reached the floor. It darkened a mat.

No catastrophe followed.

The roof did not collapse. The basin did not empty. The room did not die.

A gap remained.

The builders came to inspect it.

They touched its edges. They tapped around it. They listened with palms spread flat against the tile. They brought a wedge, then a cord, then a little cup, then three new unfired bricks.

Each time they tried to close the crack, something prevented the final joining.

Not forcefully. The power did not break their tools or turn their hands aside. The wedge fitted, but left a hair of air. The cord held, but could not cinch all the way. The bricks pressed together, but their soft corners refused to merge.

At last the builders withdrew.

Above the crack in the tile, there was a sliver of sky.

Not much sky.

Only a narrow, cold blue line visible between storm pulses.

But it was the first place in the room that did not immediately become wall.

The youngest things noticed first.

A shutter, newly formed and too eager in all its movements, opened toward it. The translucent lizard appeared and drank the cold drip that sometimes entered. The pale insects carried beads up toward the slit, rather than down toward the warm dark places. A vine reached for it, then stopped before crossing, its tendril curled in a question.

One night, laughter came from behind a mat.

The power did not know whether there had truly been children all along.

It did not need to know.

The sound was enough.

After that, the power sent no more signs for a long time. It did not enlarge the opening. It did not order the builders to rest. It did not tear down their crowded world and call the destruction freedom. It did not take the sleeper from the leaf, though it could feel that sleeper breathing in dreams of endless shelter.

Instead, whenever the room nearly sealed one of its new spaces, the power preserved a little incompletion.

A cup was made with a tiny hole at its base—not enough to waste its water, only enough for a fern-root to find it.

A wall rose behind another wall, but stopped one hand-width short of joining, leaving a cool passage where no one could work comfortably.

A ladder grew a rung that led nowhere.

A basket opened and discovered it could remain empty.

The power gave the room failures that were not failures.

At first the builders were troubled by them.

They worked around the unfillable spaces. They braced the useless rung. They laid careful tile borders around a patch of bare floor. They made a tiny roof above the hole in the cup, then another roof above that, until one of them—an old presence whose wrists had become root and whose shoulder carried a roof of its own—began to laugh.

The laugh was barely audible beneath rain and thunder.

But it traveled.

The jars hummed in answer.

The carved guardians opened their closed wooden eyes.

The central leaf unfolded by the width of a finger.

Within it, the sleeper shifted.

A breath moved through the chamber, warm as ever, rich with wet stone and clay. Yet mixed within it now was something else: the smell of snow.

The builders did not stop building.

The power had never wished that.

Making was their nature. Care was their language. The room would always want to catch the falling water, to support the straining beam, to make a second cup beneath the first. There was beauty in this. There was devotion. There was even joy.

But, slowly, they began to make things which were not needed.

A small ledge appeared high on a wall, holding nothing.

A door-shaped frame was built into a partition, though there was no door in it and no room beyond.

One builder set a clay bowl upside down beneath a drip, refusing to catch it.

Another tied a knot and, upon finishing, did not reinforce it.

The knot held.

Then, after a while, it softened.

Then it came undone.

No one rushed to replace it.

The loose cord lay in the damp, growing moss.

This was decay, perhaps.

Or rest.

Far below, people began to tell stories of a mountain that breathed fog.

Some said there was a shrine at its summit. Some said a nest. Some said a wound in the weather. A few climbers found the high crack and felt warm air on their faces. Most turned back, because something in that warm air made them think of every unfinished thing in their lives.

Those who entered found a room still loud with labor, still wet, still impossibly crowded.

But they also found, if they were patient, the little blue slit in the broken tile.

And near it, sometimes, a figure would be sitting with its back against the wall.

It might have been made of plaster, wood, skin, root, or all of these together. It might have had a tiled roof across its shoulders. It might have had no face except a thumbprint.

It would not ask the visitor to help.

It would not offer a task.

It would point upward, toward the line of sky.

And if the visitor looked long enough, they would understand what the benevolent power had done.

Not salvation.

Not abandonment.

A place to look through.

A little room inside the room where nothing was being made.

A beginning of distance.
