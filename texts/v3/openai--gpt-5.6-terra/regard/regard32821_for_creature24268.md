The power came without light.

It knew that light, brought carelessly into such a country, would be violence. It would gather moths into ruin, expose sleeping bodies, break the useful privacy of roots and burrows. So it came as a presence in the heat: not a new heat, not a wind, but the slight recognition in all things that they had been noticed completely.

It looked upon the plain.

It saw the fungal threads carrying news from rot to root. It saw the leaf-cutter roads below the grass, the sealed chambers beneath the mounds, the blind pale larvae receiving their food. It saw water held in clay through the dry interval, and the old paths of floods waiting beneath the present ground. It saw the grazer making openings, the cat reading them, the frog waiting in mud that seemed dead.

It saw the small dark animal moving along a channel.

The animal was hungry. Not desperate—this was not the sharp hunger of famine—but carrying the usual nightly arithmetic in its small body: how much food could be found before dawn, which scents meant danger, whether the soft ground ahead held grubs or a snake, whether the old resting place beneath the palm roots had been claimed by something larger.

The power knew every answer.

It knew where fruit had fallen behind a screen of reeds. It knew that a constrictor lay coiled near the animal’s preferred path. It knew that the palm-root hollow was dry tonight, but that a cat had passed near it an hour before, leaving its scent on the bark.

For a moment, because it was benevolent, the power considered making the way easy.

It could have ripened every fruit at once. It could have filled every shallow basin with clear water. It could have turned all predators aside. It could have made the animal strong, sleek, unafraid, beyond hunger, beyond illness, beyond the small sudden endings that came daily in the plain.

But it understood that to spare one creature from every difficulty would be to alter the life of countless others.

No cat would eat. No scavenger would inherit. No roots would receive what death returned. The animal itself, made free of danger, would become a stranger to its own senses. Its careful ears would no longer turn separately. Its nose would lose the old language of damp soil, fear, fruit, and kin. The paths it made would no longer be made with attention. It would live, perhaps, but not in the manner the plain had taught it to live.

And the plain was not asking to be corrected.

It was full. It was difficult. It was mortal in every part. It was also competent beyond measure.

So the power did not command the snake to move.

It did not call fruit into being.

It did not darken the cat’s eyes.

Instead it did something smaller.

Where the animal’s usual path bent toward the hidden constrictor, a beetle, disturbed in the soil by nothing that any other creature could have named, climbed onto a brittle stem. The stem snapped. It gave a dry, brief tick against a leaf.

The animal stopped.

Its ears opened toward the sound. Its muzzle lifted. It did not understand danger in the way the power understood it; it understood only that the night had changed shape. After a long stillness, it turned aside.

It followed a route along the firmer edge of the channel, a path it had used once in the wet season and not since. There it found three fallen fruits softened by insects but still sweet beneath their skins. It found grubs under a loosened strip of bark. It drank a little from a clay seam where roots had held the last rain.

Before dawn it chose not the palm-root hollow but the dry lee of an old termite mound, whose chambers lay deep and warm under soil, fungus, bodies, and patient labor.

The cat passed the palm roots before morning.

The snake took a frog near the channel.

The beetle disappeared into a crack in the bark.

Nothing in the plain knew that anything miraculous had occurred.

This was the power’s first gift: not rescue, but a margin.

A fraction of a breath.  
A turned foot.  
A warning small enough to be mistaken for the world speaking in its ordinary voice.

Then the power looked farther than the animal’s night.

It saw seasons ahead: hard rains, a season in which rain failed, a tree falling across an old watercourse, a sickness among the grazers, a fire begun far away where dry grass met lightning. It saw that the plain could endure much, but not everything. Its abundance was not invulnerability. Its old balance was not a shield against every force.

So the power made no permanent law and built no wall around the country. It did not freeze the plain in its present perfection. A living place protected from change becomes a relic, and a relic is only death that has been kept tidy.

Instead, it gave the plain several chances.

It deepened, by the width of a root, the hidden damp under one old basin, so that when the dry season sharpened, frogs would persist there and lay eggs after others had failed.

It allowed a storm, still far beyond the darkness, to loosen one branch at the right time. The branch would fall across a shallow channel. Leaves would gather behind it. Silt would collect. In the next rains, water would remain there for a few extra days. Seeds would find the wet margin. Reeds would thicken. The heavy grazer would discover them and make a new lane. Other lives would follow.

It gave certain seeds, carried in the fur of passing animals, a little more patience than they had possessed before. They would wait in the soil through heat and flood alike. When an old giant finally fell and made an opening in the canopy, they would rise.

It placed no sign upon these acts.

The termite mound did not glow. The basin was not declared sacred. The small animal did not become a prophet among its kind. It woke in the warm dimness beneath the mound, listened to the land’s innumerable small sounds, and went on living.

Years later, when its body had returned to soil, an animal descended from it would drink at the basin made by the fallen branch. It would not know why water remained there. It would simply lower its narrow head between reeds and drink.

A frog would call from the margin.

A snake would hear.

A bird would carry a seed away.

And the power, having done what it could without making the plain belong to it, withdrew even its attention.

For benevolence, in the end, was not possession.

Wisdom was not arranging every outcome.

The greatest gift it could give the low, crowded country was to let it remain itself: dangerous, fertile, temporary, interconnected, and alive—while quietly preserving enough room within its darkness for life to continue choosing its way.
