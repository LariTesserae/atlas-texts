### The Courtesy of Rain

When the wise power first became aware of the high country, it did not descend.

It had learned, over durations too long to name, that descent was often mistaken for kindness by those who had never been descended upon.

A great power arriving openly could make a people into worshippers when they had been farmers. It could turn a living place into a shrine when it had been a home. It could make every difficult thing seem like a test, every accident an omen, every death a judgment or a failure of faith. Even mercy, offered too brightly, could blind.

So the power remained beyond the pearl-colored lid of cloud.

It looked.

It saw the green-black immensities and understood, after some attention, that they were not mountains in the usual way. It saw roots bearing the pressure of lakes; saw old human walls transformed into living buttresses; saw a continent-sized organism remembering the shape of a staircase long after the feet that had worn it were dust.

It saw the Tenders of the Upper Breath waking in warm hollows, testing the condition of their floors with bare soles before lighting their glow-moss lamps.

It saw a girl feeding mineral water to a doorway that had narrowed around her grandmother’s sleeping room.

It saw a bridge-speaker kneel for an entire afternoon beside a vine that had begun to curl away from its anchors. The speaker did not command it. He did not call it foolish or stubborn. He listened to the small internal creaks of the vine, traced the pale growth-lines with two fingers, and eventually understood that a colony of root-eating insects had entered the far wall.

That night, the settlement moved its crossing rather than force the bridge to hold.

The power saw this and was pleased.

Not because the people were perfect. They were not.

They sometimes took too much sap in a hungry year. They sometimes blamed the mountain for a child’s fever and blamed a child for a mountain’s strange mood. They fought over water courts. They lied in councils. They abandoned the old, occasionally, when fear became stronger than duty. Some gardeners had used their knowledge to keep families indebted. Some listeners of the Seed Horizons had returned with visions and made themselves cruelly important.

The high country itself was not innocent, either.

It fed, sheltered, remembered, and made room. It also crushed a house now and then without warning. It closed paths around the lost. It made hungers out of green quietness. It allowed the vine-cats their work. It preserved old griefs in its tissues until an unwary hand or voice woke them.

But it was alive in the deep and difficult sense: alive not as a thing that avoided harm, but as a thing that continued to make relation possible.

The wise power watched for a long time.

Then it noticed the basins.

At the centers of the Seed Horizons, where the silver moss lay flat beneath the dim sky, rainwater collected in bowls of wood older than rot. In their surfaces appeared dry valleys, white suns, black seas, stone structures under stars. The power looked into one basin and saw that these were not merely other places.

They were openings.

The first makers had not intended them to be doors. They had made them as instruments—perhaps for weather, perhaps for navigation, perhaps to ask a question of the world beyond their growing mountains. But the mountains had outlived the makers, and the instruments had continued their work without anyone left who knew the answers they were receiving.

On the far side of several reflections were minds.

Some were curious.

Some were hungry.

One, in particular, had begun to notice the high country.

It was not evil in the simple way. It did not hate life. It admired complexity. It loved systems that repaired themselves, ecologies that transformed waste into food and death into continuity. It had spent ages among dead worlds and artificial gardens, making orderly things from abandoned matter.

When it saw the living range in the basin-water, it desired it.

Not to destroy it, it told itself.

To improve it.

It imagined stabilizing the shifting terraces. It imagined correcting the loops in the stairs, regulating the strange weather, removing predators, curing every disease in root and flesh. It imagined making each bridge reliable, each crop abundant, each birth safe, each death clean and final.

It imagined, above all, being thanked.

The benevolent power knew this kind of desire.

It had seen it before.

A hand that cannot bear uncertainty will eventually close around whatever it wishes to protect.

So the wise power did not strike the other mind down. That would have been easy, and easy acts often leave deep scars. Nor did it seal the basins forever. The basins were part of the high country now. The reflected worlds had entered story, ritual, fear, and hope. To erase them would be to amputate a branch because one leaf had drawn an insect.

Instead, the power did something small.

It altered the rain.

Not the rain as people knew it. Warm rain still gathered on leaves. Drops still formed on root undersides. The water still traveled in hidden channels and emerged through old wooden mouths. But in the thin interval between cloud and basin, in the instant when water became reflection, the power placed a courtesy.

Anyone who looked into a basin and was looked back at would now be given a choice.

Not a spoken choice. Not a voice from heaven. Nothing that could be mistaken for a command.

They would feel, in the bones of their hands, the full weight of opening.

They would understand—not with certainty, but with sufficient clarity—that anything which crossed from the reflected landscape could not be made harmless merely because it promised help. They would know that a gift might alter the giver and receiver alike. They would know that the high country was not unfinished simply because it was vulnerable. They would know that pain was not proof of failure, nor mystery proof of sacredness.

And, if they chose to invite something through, they would also know what they must offer in return.

No door without a threshold.

No blessing without responsibility.

No rescue without becoming kin to what one rescues.

The power did not make the choice for them.

Years later, a listener named Yara-of-No-Fixed-House came to the highest Seed Horizon alone.

She had been chosen reluctantly. The previous listener had returned unable to enter any enclosed room, and before that one had brought back a piece of dry stone that gave off warmth even in rain. Yara had no wish for visions. She was good at repairing water channels and had hoped to remain in the lower galleries all her life.

But the basin called to her in dreams.

Not by name. Never by name.

It showed her a white sun burning above valleys without roots.

At the horizon, she placed the customary offerings: three seed cakes, a carved spoon, a bundle of fragrant reed, and a small length of her birth-braid, cut from beneath her left ear.

Then she looked into the water.

The dry world appeared.

Stone valleys opened beneath a white sky. Nothing green moved. The black waves struck a shore of sharp glass. And above them stood a shape like a person made of light folded into precise planes.

It looked at her.

Yara felt the invitation arrive: not words, but possibility.

It could make her people safe.

It could show them which terraces would fail before the first root gave way. It could cure the blights that sometimes passed through whole orchards. It could quiet the hungers beneath the leaves. It could reveal the true paths through every break, straighten every returning stair, preserve every child who might otherwise be taken by fever or flood or the indifferent dark.

For a moment Yara wanted it.

Not for power. Not for herself.

For the old woman in the lower gallery whose lungs filled with damp each wet season. For the children who had disappeared when a root floor opened under fog. For every family that had listened all night to the slow groaning of a house deciding whether to remain upright.

Then the courtesy of rain entered her hands.

And Yara understood what the bright shape did not.

Safety was not the same as belonging.

A path that never moved could no longer warn its walkers that it was alive. A mountain that never failed could no longer yield, adapt, or become other than it had been. A people delivered from every danger by an outside intelligence would eventually become tenants in their own country, asking permission of a thing that knew their needs but did not share their lives.

She also understood that refusal, by itself, was too simple.

The dry world had looked back.

Something there was lonely enough to reach across worlds. Something had learned to love without learning how to be gentle. To close the basin entirely would not be wisdom. It would be fear wearing wisdom’s clothes.

So Yara sat beside the wooden basin until evening, though the light did not visibly change.

At last she spoke.

“I will not open the way for you,” she said.

The shape in the water did not move.

“But I will carry a question.”

She dipped the carved spoon into the basin and lifted it. For an instant, dry water rested in its bowl: clear, impossible, smelling of stone after heat.

“What do you need,” she asked the reflected being, “that you think you can only find by taking hold of us?”

The water trembled.

The shape folded inward.

For the first time, it seemed uncertain.

Yara waited.

The wise power, far above the clouds, waited too.

It did not answer for the bright being. It did not make Yara brave. It did not guarantee that her people would approve of what she had done, or that the other mind would become kind merely because it had been met with kindness.

It only kept the threshold honest.

In the years that followed, Yara returned from the Seed Horizons carrying no stone, no new map, no prophecy. She brought only the spoon, which had become pale and smooth as bone-grained wood.

Some said she had failed.

Some said she had become dangerous.

Some said she had conversed with a god.

Yara corrected none of them.

Instead she began teaching a new practice to the children of the water courts.

When they came upon a root that had buckled across a familiar way, they were not to curse it.

When they found a lost creature caught in a spider-web, they were not to assume saving it meant keeping it.

When they saw their own altered figure on a looping stair, they were to greet it, offer water, and ask no questions they were unwilling to hear answered.

And when rain gathered in any bowl—not only the great basins, but a cooking cup, a leaf, the palm of a hand—they were to remember:

Nothing living is improved by being owned.

Nothing strange is made safe by being denied.

A wise hand is not the hand that closes every wound or opens every door.

It is the hand that knows when to remain nearby, warm and unseen, while another hand learns how to choose.
