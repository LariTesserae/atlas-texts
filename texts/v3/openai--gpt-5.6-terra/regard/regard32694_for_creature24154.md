The power did not arrive as a light.

The high country had plenty of lights already: the blue branching beneath reed beds, the reddish heat moving through the wings above cloud, the slow lanterns drifting in the exposed tissues of an opening mountain. A new light would have been mistaken for weather, hunger, or an organ briefly turned outward.

It did not arrive as a voice, either.

Voices belonged to creatures with throats, or fissures, or water under pressure. They made demands of air. They presumed an ear and a direction. The country had neither reliably. Its directions were provisional; its ears were distributed through roots, shell plates, mud, wing membrane, spores, and the black glass feet of animals listening to rock.

So the power came as attention.

For a long while it did nothing else.

It attended to the low, almost-silent work of the kneaders in their warm basins. It felt the material entering beneath their bodies—ash, dead root, stone, mineral water, the remains of a hunter that had drowned in a rising pool—and felt the world leave them rearranged into dark plates and usable earth.

It attended to a white larva in a fissure, blind in its heat, making a thread of ceramic because its body had no other way to go on being itself. It attended to the thread hardening across a ravine. To the climber nesting in it. To the boneless young, pressed against their parent in a vapor pocket, learning the cliff through the minute movements of their soft bodies.

It attended to the hunters that waited.

It attended to the burrowers that fled.

It attended to the reed networks receiving charge from the sky and returning it as sequences of cold fire, each wet basin speaking through mud to another basin that might no longer be there by the time the message arrived.

It attended to dreams.

This took it a very long time, though time was not an obstacle to it. It watched a herd dream itself toward a vanished valley and saw them find only black rubble and a newly steaming crack. It watched their disappointment become heat; their warm bodies gathered in shelter; their waste alter the soil; their carried gardens take root; cloudwater condense; reeds arrive; a pool form.

The dream had been wrong.

The dream had also been right.

The power understood then that this country was not unfinished in the way a broken thing was unfinished.

It was unfinished in the way a sentence is unfinished while it is being spoken.

It had not failed to become stable. Stability was not its destination. It had not failed to make a home. It was making homes in the act of remaking their ground. Its terrors were real, its deaths real, its losses real—but they were not errors inserted into an otherwise orderly design. They were part of a life so quick to alter that no single shape could safely call itself the whole.

And the power felt, as benevolent powers sometimes do, the impulse to help.

It could have quieted the rising mountains.

It could have set the escarpments in place, cooled the chambers below, drawn firm rivers through the valleys, given every creature a map that would stay correct. It could have made gravity faithful. It could have made the dreams private. It could have separated root from animal, storm from wing, death from habitat, so that each being would have clearer borders and fewer impossible obligations.

The high country would have become easier.

Many individual lives would have become longer.

No young climber would have needed to learn which way down was after a mountain’s inversion. No grove would wake to find itself walking. No animal would follow a remembered basin across days of devastated ground. No soft-bodied creature would become briefly airborne because the world had forgotten how to hold it.

The power considered this with perfect seriousness.

Then it attended more closely.

Beneath a slope newly folded out of itself, there was a knot of small garden-backed grazers. The inversion had come quickly. Their moss field had opened beneath them, then lifted and broken. Half the herd had been carried on a slab of stone that now hung almost vertical above a ravine.

They were frightened.

The power did not romanticize their fear. It felt their many small bodies tighten. It felt their gardens close their flowers. It felt their legs seeking a surface that had ceased to be a surface. Several fell. One was caught in a ceramic curtain. Another struck a ledge, and its garden burst open, releasing pale spores into the wet air.

But those that remained did not wait for rescue.

Their gardens sent root threads into the vertical stone. The animals turned their bodies sideways and clung. The surviving herd became, over the next hours, a living seam across the cliff. Condensation gathered around them. Their warmth made mist. The spores from the broken garden found the ceramic filaments and began to grow.

By the end of the week, where a moss field had been, there was a hanging garden.

The power looked at the little bodies, at their pain and persistence, and understood another thing:

To remove all danger from such a place would not merely spare it suffering.

It would take away its language.

Not all suffering is sacred. The power knew this. Pain is not made good by being endured. Death is not automatically meaningful because it occurs beneath a beautiful sky. There are cruelties that must be stopped. There are wounds that ask, plainly, for healing.

But this country’s instability was not cruelty. It did not delight in harm. It was not an oppressor with a face. It was the condition in which its creatures had become capable of one another.

The power could not preserve every shape.

But perhaps it could preserve the possibility of becoming a new one.

And so, after its long attention, it acted.

Not upon the mountains.

Not upon the creatures.

Upon the interval between warning and disaster.

It placed, throughout the high country, a mercy too small to be seen.

Before a ridge turned inside out, there would now be one additional breath of time.

Before a warm basin sealed beneath fresh stone, one additional sequence of blue light would travel through the reeds.

Before a migrating grove crossed into a collapsing slope, the ground beneath its roots would give one more clear vibration.

Before gravity loosened, the smallest creatures would feel an inward pull toward whatever shelter could still hold.

Not certainty. Never certainty.

The country was too alive for certainty, and the power would not make a prison from protection. The warning would not tell a creature where safety lay; often there would be no safety, only a better direction, a less fatal choice, enough time for a parent to gather its young, enough notice for a herd to turn, enough pulse for a sleeper to wake.

The power made the mercy fit the country’s own ways.

It lived in the low singing of ceramic threads.

It lived in a change in the mineral taste of condensation.

It lived in the black feet of the scree-hunters, which began to stamp not only for themselves but for all the hidden life beneath the stones.

It lived in the gardens on grazers’ backs, which learned to bloom a pale color an hour before the earth moved.

It lived in the dream-spores.

This was the most delicate work.

The power did not silence them. It did not take from the high country its ancient shared dreams, its impossible migrations, its capacity to remember places into existence. Instead it gave the dreams one new sensation.

In every true warning dream, there would be a small, unmistakable detail: the taste of cool water.

Not the warm mineral water of the basins. Not the sharp taste of rain. Cool water—rare, nearly unknown, carrying no metal, no ash, no memory of fire.

An animal that dreamed cool water would wake uneasy.

It would wake before the others, perhaps. It would nudge its companion. It would refuse a familiar hollow. It would turn aside from a route inherited through a hundred bodies. It would not know why.

Sometimes it would be wrong.

The power allowed this.

A mercy that never permits error becomes an order. An order that cannot be refused becomes another kind of danger.

But often, when the mountains opened or the slopes began their slow descent, there would be fewer bodies beneath them.

There would be a little more time for the world to gather itself.

The power did one other thing, and only because it had watched the dead.

It saw how a kneader’s shell became a wetland, how a fallen tree became a vapor shelter, how a hunter’s bones fed glass-rooted things, how the broken gardens of grazers became the first green on a vertical cliff. The country did not waste its dead, but sometimes change came too swiftly even for its vast appetite to receive them. Entire small worlds were buried beyond reach in hot stone or carried upward into the cloud and scattered.

So the power gave every dying thing a single chance to leave an inheritance.

At the end of a life—whether in a pool, a scree field, a nest of ceramic, or beneath a mountain—the creature’s body would release one durable seed of itself.

Not necessarily offspring.

For a reed, it might be a dark bead carrying the pattern of a whole wetland’s signals.

For a climber, a soft knot of bone-less tissue capable of sleeping for years until it found a suitable wall.

For a kneader, a plate within its shell that held the chemistry of fertile sediment.

For a winged creature, a faint electrical pattern that could enter a storm and teach the clouds its old migratory spiral.

For an animal with a garden, one spore bearing not only the garden’s life but the memory of the animal’s warmth and movement.

For the strange things from the deep root masses, perhaps only a light, rising through stone until another opening mountain received it.

This gift did not prevent extinction. Some forms would still vanish. Some lineages would come to an end because the high country had no room for all its experiments.

But nothing would vanish entirely without being offered back to the becoming.

The power did not announce these changes.

No creature bowed.

No one named the giver.

The reeds flashed as they had always flashed. The packs stamped their warning rhythms. The clouds condensed around black new peaks. A mountain opened, and for a few minutes the ground forgot its promises.

Yet in a vine mat near the opening, a frilled climber woke from sleep with the impossible taste of cool water in its mouth.

It unfolded its face.

Around it, its young stirred in the vapor.

The climber listened—not upward, not downward, but through the ceramic threads trembling beneath its hands. Then it gathered the boneless young against its body and began to climb.

Below, the cliff was already changing.

Above, no direction could be trusted.

But there was time.

Not much.

Enough.
