The power did not arrive as a star, though it had worn stars before.

It came to the basin without crossing the white plain. One moment there was the town beneath its pale lid of haze, its streets folded into their usual austere arrangement, its six white trees holding their dark fans shut against the dimness. The next, there was an additional absence in the central square: a place where the light seemed to have forgotten how to lie upon the paving.

The mirrored insects noticed first.

They turned together toward the dry basin.

Then the white trees opened their fans, though the sky had not brightened.

The Stillfolk emerged gradually. Not in alarm. Alarm, among them, was for a falling roof, a closing room, a child who had wandered into an active passage before the flags could be raised. This was something else: an alteration without green light, without dust-thread, without the minute pressure beneath the soles that warned of a new stair becoming possible.

Keepers climbed roofs and studied their flags. Every strip stood straight.

Listeners laid their palms on paving. The stone gave them nothing.

Gardeners came from the courtyards with their small shears still in hand. Wanderers, who had been preparing to leave the southern edge, stopped beneath the last gallery and looked back.

In the central square, beside the empty basin, stood what might have been a person.

It had no height that could be agreed upon. From one street it appeared no taller than a Stillfolk elder, layered in pale garments and bent a little at the shoulders. From another it seemed to occupy the entire square, not physically but by implication, as a mountain occupies the view behind a small house. Its outline was gentle. Nothing in it made a demand of the eye. Its face, if it had one, was neither bright nor hidden.

It looked at the town.

Then it looked at the Stillfolk.

Its attention was vast enough that the town’s old maps—every powdered avenue in every wall, every sealed room, every ancestor darkening a shelf—were present within it at once.

The power understood the town.

It understood that the settlement was not merely alive. It was hungry in the blind manner of roots, tides, weather, and deep time. It changed because changing was the form its continuance took. It made rooms because rooms were a by-product of its inward pressures. It closed rooms for the same reason.

It understood the Stillfolk, too.

It understood their small precautions: the flags on roofs, the bowls placed by thresholds, the lichen cultivated in hairline cracks. It understood their practice of making a life not from security but from attention. It understood that their patience was not passivity. It was skill. It was courage that had become ordinary through repetition.

And because it was benevolent, the power felt sorrow.

Not pity. Pity would have mistaken them for helpless things.

Sorrow was the recognition that every Stillfolk child would eventually learn the shape of a vanished room by being told of it; that every household would, sooner or later, stand before a smooth wall where a beloved had slept; that some wanderers would return to no recognizable part of the town; that the people had made grace from necessity because no other material had been given them.

The power could have changed this.

It could have stilled the stone.

With a thought, it could have fixed every street into a final arrangement. It could have made walls obey their flags forever. It could have opened wells under every courtyard, laid a blue river through the basin, raised a clean sun above the haze. It could have remade the Stillfolk so that they did not shed, did not hunger for mineral exhalations, did not carry green seams under their pale skins.

It considered these mercies.

Then it saw what they would cost.

A town that never changed would not merely be safer. It would become other than itself. The maps would become decorative. The listeners would lose their vocation. The gardeners would tend plants that no longer mediated between life and stone but merely adorned fixed courtyards. Children would be born into rooms that had been planned for them by an intelligence outside their people’s long negotiation with the ground.

The ancestors would remain present, perhaps, but only as possessions of the past. They would no longer be part of an ongoing body.

And the Stillfolk would have permanence—but not the kind they knew. Not continuity through alteration. Not belonging that survived the loss of place because belonging had never been dependent on place alone.

The power waited.

It did not wait for permission in words.

At last, an old listener crossed the square. Her back was bent beneath a layered mantle. Green light showed faintly at the joints of her fingers, though no nearby wall had begun to shine. She stopped at the basin’s rim, where the power’s presence had made the light thin.

She placed a small bowl on the paving.

In it was black lichen, carefully gathered.

Beside it she set a strip of white flag-fiber.

Then she pressed two fingers to the ground, and waited.

The power understood.

It was not an offering exactly. Nor was it a request.

It was the Stillfolk’s way of making space for an unknown condition: to name its stability, measure its tendency, and see whether it might be lived with.

The power bent—not in body, but in regard—and touched the bowl.

The lichen did not burn. The fibrous flag did not curl.

For the first time in any Stillfolk memory, a bead of water appeared inside the dry basin.

It was no larger than an eye.

Everyone in the square saw it. No one moved toward it.

The power spoke, and because it was wise, its voice did not fill the town. It came to each listener in the manner most natural to them: as pressure in the palm, as a remembered route, as the settling of a bowl upon stone.

It said:

*I can make this place cease to wound you.*

The statement lay among them with the terrible simplicity of a newly opened door.

The old listener kept her fingers on the ground.

Around the square, Stillfolk stood motionless. Their faces gave little away. But their seams brightened, one by one, beneath their skin.

The power continued.

*I can hold its walls. I can preserve its rooms. I can give you water, warmth, unbroken paths. I can prevent the closing of what you love.*

The pale bead in the basin grew. It became a shallow pool, perfectly round. The reflected sky in it was darker than the sky above.

A child stepped from behind a woven screen near the northern gallery. They were young enough that the mineral seams at their temples had not yet settled into their adult pattern. They looked at the water for a long time.

Then, very quietly, they asked something.

It was scarcely sound. A dry shape of breath.

But the power heard.

*Would the town still dream?*

The power could have answered falsely. It did not.

*No,* it said.

A tremor passed through the basin.

Not the town’s usual visual tremor. This one was felt: a soft rearrangement behind the breastbone, an ache in the teeth. Dust lifted in thin threads from the paving. Across the roofs, white flags bent all at once toward the western quarter.

Green light entered the seams of the walls.

The Stillfolk looked toward it.

The child looked from the growing pool to the green-lit street.

There, at the edge of the western district, a familiar house had begun to swell outward. Its doorway narrowed. The wall beside it became translucent, and through the thin mineral, one could see shapes within: sleeping shelves, stacked bowls, a low cart with horn wheels.

Someone lived there.

The old listener rose.

She did not address the power. She addressed the people around her with a placement of her hands, a tilt of her body toward the west.

The response moved through the town.

Keepers went for the roofs. Gardeners gathered their shears and baskets. Neighbors brought carts to the changing house. Two people entered before the doorway had become too narrow, carrying out screens and bundles. Another stood beside the wall, palm flat to the glowing seam, reading its direction.

No one ran.

No one begged the power to intervene.

The power watched, and its sorrow changed.

It became respect so deep that it resembled grief, and gratitude so vast that it needed no object.

The pool in the dry basin remained. This, the power had decided, could be given without taking anything essential in return. But it would not be a well in the ordinary sense. It would not free the Stillfolk from their world and make them strangers within it.

The water would come only when the town made room for it.

A basin would have to be kept clear. A channel would have to be guided through moving stone. The gardeners would need to learn what might grow at its edge. The listeners would need to discover whether the town tolerated moisture, or whether water altered its dreams. There would be failures. There would be new maps.

A gift, the power knew, should sometimes be a capacity rather than a conclusion.

It touched the basin once more.

The pool sank through the white stone and vanished.

In its place remained a dark ring, no wider than a handspan, damp to the touch.

The old listener returned after the western house had finished closing. Its residents had moved into a newly opened chamber across the square. They were already hanging screens in the unfamiliar doorway.

She knelt beside the dark ring.

Her fingertips came away wet.

For a long while she regarded them.

Then she smiled.

It was a small expression. It altered almost nothing in her pale, layered face. But those nearest saw it, and the seams in their hands dimmed.

The power did not remain in the square.

It did not ask for worship, for gratitude, for a story in which it would become the town’s hidden architect. It left no image of itself except a place where the light had once failed to settle properly.

Yet it did not entirely depart.

Beneath the basin, at a depth the listeners could not reach, it made one change in the mineral body of the town: not a command, not a restraint, but a new possibility.

From then on, when the town’s deep movements threatened to close too many rooms at once, another tendency sometimes appeared among the older tendencies.

A wall would hesitate.

A seam would bend around a sleeping alcove.

A stair would grow toward a stranded doorway rather than away from it.

No Stillfolk called this a miracle. They had no word for that exact category of event. Some said the town had become more considerate with age. Some said the ancestors had accumulated enough density in the walls to influence the larger body. Some said the black lichen had changed, or the white trees had opened their fans in a new pattern.

The wanderers, returning from the mineral plain, sometimes stood at the basin’s edge and looked up at the haze as if expecting to see something beyond it.

They saw nothing.

But in the central square, after certain tremors, a thin dark sheen gathered briefly in the dry basin. Children were allowed to touch it first. They pressed their fingers into the impossible coolness, then carried the wetness to their elders in cupped hands.

And the town continued to alter.

It closed what it closed.

It opened what it opened.

Its people still mourned. They still lost rooms, routes, and faces. They still planted flags and read maps of tendencies. They still learned to live where the ground had decided to become.

But now, within the old, unanswering motion of stone, there was a little more room for one another.
