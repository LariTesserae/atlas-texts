The wise power did not mistake the place for a ruin.

This was important.

Many powers, even kindly ones, see walls and immediately imagine those who ought to be sheltered by them. They see bridges and imagine feet. They see cisterns and imagine thirst. They see empty terraces and imagine a failure of population, a silence waiting to be corrected.

The benevolent power had made this error before, in worlds younger than this one. It had given birds to a forest that had wanted only wind. It had sent luminous fish into a dark sea whose darkness had been a necessary organ. It had taught a lonely mountain to speak, only to learn that the mountain’s silence had been speech of another scale.

So it came to the high wet country without bringing seeds, animals, people, or names.

It came as attention.

For a long while—by the measures of towers, by the slow clock of roots—it did nothing at all.

It watched the rain gather beneath the shelves. It watched a reservoir decide, under swelling pressure, to release itself in a shining sheet. It watched the released water cross an old pavement, enter a black seam, and vanish beneath the blue-green fields. It watched a pale aerial root extend from one rain chimney toward another, miss it by a handspan, dry at the tip, and become, months later, the first rib of a new bridge.

It watched structures fail.

This too was important. The power saw an entire western embankment soften after weeks of weather. The old fibers, which had held a terrace for twenty years, loosened their lacquered grip. A wall bowed outward. Its flowers tore open in red-white ranks. Then the embankment did not collapse as stone collapses. It unfolded. Its outer bark peeled into long strips. Its supporting roots sank. Water rose through it. By evening the terrace was gone, and in its place a wide shallow marsh reflected the sky.

The power did not restore the terrace.

It understood, finally, that the country was not trying to preserve its forms. Its forms were merely the gestures by which it continued.

The power watched for suffering.

Not damage: damage was everywhere, and was quickly made useful. Not loss: loss moved through the body and became foundation, mulch, pressure, memory. Not solitude: solitude was not a wound unless something had first desired company.

It watched for constriction.

There, perhaps, was something.

At the eastern edge of the plateau, where clouds usually rose in abundance from the ravines, a new scar had appeared. Not a scar in the country, but in the weather around it. Far below, a great cliff had slumped into the vapor basin. The collapse had changed the upward passage of warm air. Mist still came, but less freely. Rain arrived later in the day, thinner and more violent. The eastern structures responded with their usual extraordinary industry. Reservoir skins thickened. Leaf roofs narrowed. Root wells deepened. Whole fields shifted their seams toward the remaining wetness.

But the adjustment traveled badly.

The country had never known this particular absence. It could sense the failing water, and it could move strength toward it, but its old routes of communication had grown under the assumption that the eastern air would always rise.

For the first time in a very long while, perhaps, the place was not merely changing. It was laboring against a condition it could not yet understand.

The benevolent power considered making rain.

It could have. A little rain would have been easy. A season of rain, harder but possible. It could have poured water over the eastern terraces until every cistern swelled and every folded trough ran silver.

But this would have taught the country nothing except dependence on an interruption.

And the power had learned that mercy which prevents adaptation can become a form of theft.

So it did not make rain.

Instead, it made one small alteration in the nature of water.

For a single season, only in the eastern seams, the water carried sound more clearly.

Not words. The power did not impose words.

It allowed pressure to become audible.

When the far reservoirs tightened, their tension traveled through the wet root-ground as a low tone. When a rain chimney caught a useful draft, its hollow square throat gave a deep answer. When a submerged rootwell found a new mineral current beneath the plateau, the black seams around it trembled with a faint, bell-like pulse.

At first nothing seemed to happen.

Then the eastern fields began to change in ways no field had changed before.

Their polygonal pads did not merely lift and settle according to local water. They paused. They tilted. A pad at the dry margin sank while another, miles away, rose in answer. The seams reorganized. Thin channels opened where there had been no channels. Old pavement split along lines that had once been decorative ridges. Rain chimneys, which had always faced their fronds into the prevailing wind, began to turn their upper growth toward the new, diminished cloud paths.

The country was listening to itself with a sense it had not possessed before.

Not hearing, perhaps. Not as animals hear.

But the pressures that had always passed through it—too diffuse to gather, too broad to become anything like thought—began to arrive with shape. A distant strain could be distinguished from a nearby one. A reservoir’s fullness could be known by a tower that would never receive its water. The whole plateau began, slowly, to coordinate not only by contact, but by resonance.

The wise power withdrew the alteration after one season.

It left no singing voice in the roots. It left no permanent miracle. The waters returned to their former silence.

But silence was no longer the same.

The country had grown organs that expected pattern. Hollow chambers in the lower roots became more finely ribbed. Certain dead spans, long thought useless except as support, were hollowed into sounding conduits. Flowers on the eastern walls began to open with their cups turned toward the ground; rain striking them produced vibrations that traveled into the masonry of roots below.

In later years, when a western tower overfilled, the east sometimes altered before the water had crossed the plateau.

When drought held one shelf too long, distant fields sank their water-bearing seams and sent a subtle tightening through the woven ground.

When a new escarpment began to extend out over the vapor, rain chimneys far behind it stiffened their walls in anticipation of a wind they had not yet felt.

No individual thing in the country knew why it did these things.

The country did not awaken in the way storytellers mean awakening. It did not look around itself. It did not name its body, grieve its former ignorance, or formulate a plan. It remained without a center. It retained no ruler, no face, no inward chamber in which a solitary self sat waiting.

But its negotiation became wider.

Its old awareness of pressure became capable of relation.

And because the wise power had not filled the empty avenues with people, the avenues remained empty. Because it had not brought birds, the towers continued to belong to the wind. Because it had not placed a heart within the country, the whole immense country remained its own heart: distributed among wet seams, buttresses, cisterns, dead lattices, flower-cups, and rising shelves.

From very far away, in clear weather, the plateau still looked like a city without inhabitants.

Yet during storms, those able to hear across enough distance might have noticed a new quality in its sound.

Not speech.

Not song, exactly.

A response.
