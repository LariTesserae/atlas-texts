The power does not arrive as a sun.

It has learned, in older places, that sudden light can be a violence. It does not come down in a pillar, does not part the cloud, does not command the water to clarify itself. It does not make the country dry, nor tame the weather-eaters, nor draw straight roads through the peat for the sake of those who might prefer straightness.

It sees at once that nothing here is unfinished.

Not harmless. Not gentle. Not easy to survive. But finished in the way a long sentence is finished: carrying its own grammar, its old revisions, its necessary ambiguities. The power sees the mirehorn herds making and unmaking the ground. It sees a person kneeling beside another person whose ankle has begun to green. It sees the predator gather itself from fog and reed-shadow, and sees the green afterward, which is not consolation exactly, but is not nothing.

So it does not save the country from itself.

Instead it listens.

For a time beyond measurement, it makes no alteration. It enters no body and grants no vision. It attends the low places where methane rises through the black water. It follows the lights under the ground as they travel uphill against the ordinary habits of fluid and root. It feels the slow communications passing through peat: salt-warning, cold-warning, herd-memory, pressure, pressure, pressure.

It hears the people, too.

Not merely their spoken sounds. Their hands against moss. Their practiced hesitation before stepping onto a trembling shelf. The little songs they make to keep one another moving through fog. Their grief when someone is taken quickly by a weather-eater, and their different grief when someone becomes available and walks away without looking back.

The power learns that this place has no need of a ruler.

It has need, perhaps, of witness.

And so the first thing it gives is attention that does not consume.

Far beyond the ridges, in worlds where forests are cut into measurements and lakes are made to hold still behind walls, attention usually comes with hunger. To name a thing is often to prepare it for use. To map it is often to begin the process of removing it from itself.

But this attention asks for nothing.

The country notices.

Not quickly. Nothing important here happens quickly. At first, the moss walls only lean as they have always leaned, toward rain and wind and signals deep in the ground. The birds keep their rings around the luminous seepage. The weather-eaters pass in dispersed form through the valleys.

Then, in a basin that has not held open water for three hundred years, a black pool clears.

Only for an hour.

Its plant film loosens. The floating mats drift apart. Beneath them, the water becomes visible from edge to edge: dark, profound, unstirred by wind. Every creature nearby comes to the margin without meaning to. Mirehorns stand chest-deep in sedge. Reed-backed walkers stop in their files. The lichen-backed scavengers gather in a gray crescent on a shelf of wet stone. Even a weather-eater, scattered through the cloud, remains at the far side of the basin and does not feed.

The people come last.

One of them is very old, so green around the eyes that strangers might mistake her for an unfinished becoming. She puts her palm to the water.

And through her hand, not as language but as a pressure in the bones, the power says:

*I am here.*

The old person waits.

Then she answers, because this is what people do in this country when something enormous speaks.

*Are you hungry?*

The power is wise enough to understand the question.

It considers all the ways a great being might hunger. For worship. For obedience. For purity. For gratitude. For a story in which its arrival becomes the center and all older meanings become preparations for it.

It lets the water remain still while it considers.

Then it answers:

*No.*

This is the first miracle.

Not the cleared lake. Not the speech across wet skin. The first miracle is that the power does not lie.

The old person’s fingers close in the water. Behind her, the others do not move.

At length she asks, *Then why are you here?*

The power answers with the closest truth it has:

*Because you are.*

The lake darkens. A little rain begins, very fine, making small rings across the surface.

This answer is not enough. It cannot be enough. The country has endured too long to be flattered by awe. The people have seen many things that wanted something from them. Some came as beasts. Some came as sicknesses. Some came as beautiful strangers in the fog, wearing flowers over their mouths.

The old person withdraws her hand.

*If you are wise,* she says, *you will leave us unchanged.*

The power hears, beneath the sentence, all that it contains.

Do not improve us into disappearance.

Do not cure us of the conditions that made us.

Do not make the predators gentle if gentleness means the peat no longer remembers how to renew itself.

Do not pull our dead from the ground.

Do not make our children clean.

Do not give us walls that will become prisons, fire that will make us strangers to rain, certainty that will make us deaf.

And also:

Do not mistake our endurance for a request to suffer more.

The power lowers itself further into listening.

It discovers something the country has not yet named. Beyond the farthest ridge, past the horizon toward which all the plants sometimes lean, there is a change moving inward.

Not a storm.

Not a herd under the peat.

Not a new weather-eater.

A cold has been gathering in the deep stone for a very long time. It is older than the living arrangements of this basin and less intelligent than hunger. It will arrive gradually, over generations. Water will cease to rise from certain seams. The peat’s buried heat will fail in regions. The glowing networks will flicker and break into isolated knots. Moss walls, adapted to ceaseless wet, will become brittle. The country will not die, perhaps. But it will become another country, and much that lives here will not know how to become otherwise.

The power could stop this.

With one act, it could warm the stone. It could make the vents breathe forever. It could hold the weather in its present patterns and preserve the valley as a jewel preserves an insect: intact, bright, and dead to change.

It does not.

Instead it asks permission.

Not of the people alone. Not of the grazers alone. Not even of the innumerable partial minds in the peat, though it asks them too, in pressures and mineral traces and altered currents.

It asks the whole place in the only form the whole place can answer.

It waits through seasons.

It sends no message except a possibility.

In the south-facing folds of certain ridges, where the wet ground meets porous black stone, the power opens no new seed, introduces no foreign root, builds no hidden engine. It merely makes a little room in the laws already present. It gives warmth a slightly longer memory. It allows moisture to remain held in mineral pores after rain has passed. It lets spores endure one extra hard season before failing. It creates, in a few unremarkable crevices, conditions under which variation may survive.

Then it waits to see whether anything will take them.

For many years, nothing does.

The people notice the crevices only because birds begin returning to them. A pale moss appears there, darker underneath than any local moss, with threads that enter stone rather than soil. The lichen-backed scavengers carry fragments of it away. Mirehorns rub their flanks against the ridges and leave fibers caught in the growth. One young person, traveling alone, finds a patch warmed beneath frost and sits beside it until green gathers along their sleeve.

They bring no sample home.

They understand that the thing must choose its own spread.

The power is grateful for this restraint, though it does not make gratitude into a debt.

Centuries pass in the country’s manner, which is to say they pass visibly. The cold comes. The far basins change. Some of the old lights go out. A weather-eater starves and becomes a gray fungus sprawling over a hillside. Great numbers of birds vanish from the northern pools. People leave certain valleys forever, carrying their songs in their throats and their small hospitable ecosystems in the seams of their clothes.

There is grief.

The power does not erase it.

It stays beside the grief as it stays beside the living ground: without consuming it, without praising it, without calling it necessary merely because it has occurred.

And the small warmth-keeping moss spreads.

Not everywhere. Never everywhere.

It travels slowly along the backs of animals and in the woven cuffs of people. It forms new agreements with old species. It grows beneath the root-ramparts and teaches them, over long spans, to hold frost without shattering. In the drying basins it makes dense dark beds that retain water beneath their surface. Around these beds, new insects appear. Then birds that can eat the insects. Then, after ages, a kind of reed-backed walker with thicker transparent eyelids and lungs that can rest through dust.

The country changes.

It remains itself.

This is all the power does for a very long while.

It becomes known, if known is the word, as the Unhungry Witness. The people do not pray to it often. They do not build shrines, because shrines would root and close, and because the power has made clear that it does not need to be remembered in order to remain.

But sometimes, when all the plants lean toward the same distance, the people place their palms on the wet ground and wait.

Sometimes there is only wind.

Sometimes there is the old underground machinery, knocking and hissing, digesting the dead into future forms.

And sometimes, beneath the pressure of water and peat and memory, they feel a presence attending with them: not above the country, not outside it, not taking its voice into a larger silence, but listening alongside every root and hoof and wing.

Then the people move with the land.

And the power, having found something it cannot improve without diminishing, moves with them.
