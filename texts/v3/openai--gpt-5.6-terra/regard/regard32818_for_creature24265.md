The power did not arrive as a sun.

The low country had no use for suns. Light fell there already in a weakened, uncertain way, filtered through its lid of cloud and broken on wet stone. A sudden radiance would have been a violence. It would have killed the dark films at the waterline, driven the pale swimmers deeper into passages that might not contain them, confused the stone-clingers whose small hard messages depended on cold.

Nor did the power arrive as a hand reaching down.

Hands implied scale, and scale implied carelessness. A hand large enough to lift a ridge might crush the chambers beneath it. A finger set upon the slab hut might change the tilt of a wall, and that tilt might redirect a trickle, and the trickle might starve a black colony, and a black colony might leave no food for the grazers under the stone lips. The country was young, unfinished, and terribly interwoven. To improve it hastily would be to misunderstand it.

So the wise power came as attention.

For a long while, perhaps longer than the low country’s makers would have called a lifetime, nothing changed that could be seen.

Water continued to condense from the ceiling of cloud.

Drops continued to find their hollows.

The long pale forms turned beneath their black mirrors. The transparent scavengers gathered and unmade themselves in the cracks of old mortar. The shell-builders slept inside their porous towers. A ridge split one night, and a broad black fracture opened from its crown to the mud below. The sound passed through the basin like the striking of a distant bell.

The stone-clingers woke beneath their lips of rock.

Click.

A reply from across a channel.

Click, click.

A hundred minute tests passing from shelf to shelf.

And beneath them all, deep under the country’s thin hard roof, something vast adjusted itself in a warm chamber.

The power listened.

It listened not only to the loudness—the booms, the hiss, the organ-notes of air—but to the smaller pressures inside the loudness: the difference between a settling wall and a falling one; the small pulse of a creature turning in a buried vein; the pressure of an egg, if eggs were what the jointed things made, fastening itself beneath a mineral ledge.

It learned that the hollow-dwellers were not hungry in the manner of predators.

They were burdened.

They had begun, ages ago, as many things: veils, knots, blind threads in the hot water beneath the ground. The country had made chambers around them while it made itself. Water found them. Mineral found them. Other life descended into them and did not entirely die. In time they had become neither individuals nor a single body, but a distributed need.

They held water in places where water wished to go.

They opened passages before the pressure could break the entire basin.

They fed, yes, but their feeding was also filtering. They drew poisons from the deep hot seepage. They held back salts that would have made the upper pools sterile. They kept the land’s underwaters in circulation, though they did not know they were doing so.

But they were growing.

Not quickly. Nothing in the low country grew quickly. Yet the chambers were becoming too small, the old passages too clogged with the deposits of centuries. Each time a still pool formed, each time the water waited in its black, terrible suspension, the hollow-dwellers had to choose without thought: press upward and split the crust, or withdraw and endure another season of crowding.

The old people had known this much.

The markings in the slab hut were not warnings, exactly.

They were maps.

The repeated arcs crossed by vertical lines marked the places where a person could listen to the ground and hear, below all ordinary noise, the chambers breathing. The yellow posts had once been set along paths of safe stone. Their bowed shapes were not mourners. They were pointers, bent toward buried currents. The causeway had been built where a channel had been, not because the builders had forgotten the water, but because they had intended to give it a crossing place when it returned.

The people had not been defeated all at once by the low country.

They had been trying to become part of its patience.

Then something had interrupted them: a season of excessive cold, perhaps; a failure of the surface films; a sickness that passed through bodies made for warmer, drier lands. They had gone away, or gone down, or changed into traces too small to name.

The power understood this, and felt sorrow.

Not the sharp sorrow of a being who wishes to undo every loss. Such sorrow can become another kind of hunger. It was a spacious sorrow: room made within wisdom for what could not be restored unchanged.

Then the power considered whether to heal the country.

It could have sealed the splitting ridges.

It could have cleared the cloud and taught green things to root in the mineral shelves.

It could have quieted the water, stilled the hum, driven the strange lives from their dark dependencies, and made a gentler place: meadows above clear streams, trees around the old hut, birds on the causeway.

But the power saw that this gentleness would be only gentleness for an imagined visitor.

For the beings already there, it would be an ending.

The black filaments would die in light.

The jointed creatures would lose the pressure-language of stone.

The pale swimmers would become stranded remnants in clear, shallow water.

The hollow-dwellers would be exposed, reduced, named as monsters, and extinguished for the convenience of creatures who preferred a world that disclosed itself immediately.

The power did not do these things.

Instead, it made one small correction.

Deep beneath the basin, where hot water rose through a narrow mineral throat, there was a blockage. Not a natural narrowing, not a necessary constriction, but a hard plug formed long ago from an accident of chemistry and pressure. It was forcing too much of the deep water into too few chambers. The hollow-dwellers had worked at it for generations beyond counting. Their soft reach had polished it. Their mineral-fed bodies had dissolved a little of it. But they could not move it before the next great pressure came.

The power did not shatter the blockage.

A shattering would have sent a hot flood upward, cracked the ridges, altered every pool.

It simply remembered, for the stone, that stone could yield.

For one moment beneath the black water and the pale shelves, the mineral plug became capable of a different arrangement. Its grains loosened. They turned against one another. A seam opened through its center, no wider than the width of a finger.

Warm water began to pass.

Not much.

Enough.

The hollow-dwellers felt it as relief without knowing relief. Their pressure spread into an old dry chamber. Their countless soft extensions withdrew from the roof of the basin. Above them, a pool that had become perfectly still trembled once.

Then a drop fell from a ledge.

A circle widened over the black water.

Another drop fell.

The basin resumed its ordinary dangers.

The power also left a gift in the old structures.

Not a message written in a language from beyond the world. Not a command. It left those only for powers who wished to be worshipped.

It altered the way condensation gathered on the inner wall of the square hut.

On certain cold mornings, when the basin’s hum began below hearing, water would bead along the old markings. It would collect in the arcs and run down the vertical lines. Anyone who knew how to look might see the map refreshed: safe ridges, thinning crusts, channels that had returned, the direction in which the ground was preparing to open.

The marks would never be complete. The country changed too much for that.

But they would be useful.

And in the highest joints of the hut, among the pale crust-moths, the power left a faint inclination toward openings in the outer wall. When the hum came, the moths would gather not only in hidden cracks but around the side of the building opposite the nearest failing ground. Their silver trembling would become another sign.

This was all.

No new people appeared.

No road was laid across the mud.

No name was given to the hollow-dwellers.

The slab hut continued to sink, though more slowly. The causeway remained stranded, though in some later season water might find it again. The yellow columns gathered their bowed mineral coats. Pale bodies moved under black pools. Thin transparent scavengers waited in the dark before dawn.

Yet the low country had changed, in the manner that mattered most.

It had become a little less alone.

Not watched as a curiosity, not preserved as an artifact, not corrected toward some cleaner and more familiar beauty.

Known.

And because it was known by something wise enough not to demand gratitude, the country went on becoming.

Years later, perhaps, a traveler would enter the basin by accident: following a flock that had vanished into cloud, fleeing a storm, seeking the source of a strange sound heard from a distant ridge.

They would see no sign of the power.

They would only find, inside the half-sunk hut, wet lines shining on stone.

They might understand none of them.

But when the basin began to hum, they might look up.

They might see the silver moths trembling on one wall.

They might hear the little clicks moving beneath the stone.

And, instead of running toward the loudest sound, they might choose a different path.
