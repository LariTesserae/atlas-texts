The power notices the plateau because it is difficult to notice.

Not invisible—nothing living is invisible to it—but quiet in a way that most worlds are not quiet. The plateau does not advertise its need. It does not send fire into the sky, nor prayers in great concentrated storms. Its people do not build mirrors aimed outward. They do not ask to be saved from death, weather, hunger, or time.

They ask the stone where water went.

They ask the grazers what has changed.

They ask their children what they mistook for stone.

The power is old enough to understand that this is a kind of prayer.

At first it does nothing.

It extends its attention over the broad, warm table of rock. It learns the temperatures beneath the shallow shelves. It follows a single droplet from cloud to basin to crack to root. It listens to the low speech of feet against ground, the infra-sound of insects, the long internal creak of a plated walker laying down its weight.

It learns the names that are not names.

It knows the tower at the eastern lip where gliders nest in the third opening. It knows the basin that fills only when rain arrives from the south. It knows the old woman who has sat every evening for forty-one years beside a wall of basalt, feeling for the step-pattern of a walker whose plates bear a white crescent of lichen. It knows the child who, in a ravine initiation, heard water under gravel and came home ashamed because she had first believed the sound was her own blood.

The power sees that the plateau is not peaceful because nothing threatens it.

It is peaceful because everything has learned the cost of disturbance.

There have been hard years. There will be harder ones. The power can see weather far off in the future: cycles of thin rain, then a decade of heat; a flood that will break an old gully open and drown two deep chambers; a sickness among the silver grasses; the death of a great walker whose body will become a hill of shelter and new soil.

It could correct these things.

It could draw rain to every basin. It could thicken the finger-shrubs until the plateau became green. It could make the deep cisterns inexhaustible. It could remove predators, still floods, mend cracked shells, preserve every child, return every elder from the path of the walkers.

It does not.

It understands that mercy is not the same as alteration.

To make water abundant here would change the people’s language first. Then their attention. Then their homes. Then their dead. Soon the towers would become storage places, then forts, then property. The shallow basins would be widened, walled, named after families. The grazers would be penned. The walkers would be measured for material. The silence that has made room for so many small lives would fill with the confident noise of those who have forgotten that every motion declares something.

The power will not purchase comfort with the destruction of belonging.

But neither does it mistake noninterference for wisdom.

So it watches beyond the plateau.

There are other people, far below and far away, who have begun to notice it. Some see the plateau from the air and call it empty. Some see the old towers and imagine treasure beneath them. Some see the great walkers and imagine labor, armor, meat, medicines, engines. Some see a people who use little and conclude that little is needed to be taken from them.

The power recognizes this kind of hunger. It is not the hunger of a dry season. It has no natural stopping point.

Here, then, the power acts.

Not with thunder.

Not with a wall of fire.

Not by making the plateau vanish, for that would deny the people their own home in the name of protection.

It places a difficulty around the place.

Maps lose certainty near the ravines. Instruments report distances that are true but not useful: a basin seems nearer until one reaches it, then farther; a ridge repeats itself from three directions. Vehicles that approach with extraction in mind find their cooling systems failing in harmless, inconvenient ways. Drills meet layers of stone that are not harder than stone should be, but that turn every effort aside by a finger’s width, then another.

Those who come to take find that they cannot keep a clear count of what they intended to remove.

Their records blur.

Their photographs show beautiful land but refuse to show scale. A great walker becomes a rock. A dwelling becomes a shadow. A child near a basin becomes a shimmer of heat. The old towers remain visible, always, but never quite where the visitor recalls them standing.

No one is injured.

No one is punished.

Those who arrive in greed leave irritated, embarrassed, convinced that the place contains nothing worth the trouble. They tell others it is barren, unstable, unpromising. In this, for once, their contempt becomes a shield.

But some come differently.

A traveler who arrives alone, thirsty and without a claim; a scholar who puts away their measuring tools when asked; a lost child carried by chance across a border of dry hills; a mourner who has nowhere else to sit—these the plateau does not confuse.

The power makes no introduction. It gives no command.

It only lets such people find water sooner than they otherwise would.

It lets them notice the ground-hum beneath their feet.

It lets them see, unmistakably, that the dark stone beside the path is breathing.

For the people of the plateau, the power does even less.

One wet season, after a long dry sequence, an elder sits beside a basin that has not filled. She has placed both palms on the rock. Beside her sit three children and a young person newly returned from a ravine alone. They are waiting for news through the ground.

The elder feels something unfamiliar.

Not a step. Not water. Not insects.

A spaciousness.

As if the stone, which has always been listening, has for one moment become aware of being listened to in return.

She removes her hands.

The children ask what it was.

The elder considers. She is wise enough not to give a large answer to a small certainty.

“Someone,” she says at last, “has learned to be quiet.”

That night rain gathers below the lip of the plateau.

It rises in ragged white folds. It comes warm and brief. The basins fill according to their old habits: some deeply, some barely, some not at all. Pale flowers open in the red dust. Frogs appear in clouded water. The grazing herds move with their ears wide. In the towers, gliders rearrange their nests.

Nothing miraculous happens.

The people sing underground because it is raining.

The power listens.

And when the songs are over, it leaves the plateau what it has always had, and what it has made sacred through use:

its weather,

its deaths,

its thirst,

its patient abundance,

its right not to be improved into something else.
