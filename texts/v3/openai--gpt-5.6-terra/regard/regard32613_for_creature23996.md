The power did not arrive as a second sun.

It did not descend in a pillar, or speak from the Still Basin, or lay a hand upon the plateau and command its ancient movements to cease.

It had the capacity for such things. It could have stilled the ridges. It could have anchored every city to bedrock, filled the reservoirs, warmed the cold rain before it fell. It could have cleared the strange inversions from the sky and sealed whatever listened beneath the stone.

But it was wise enough to understand that rescue can be a form of erasure.

So first it watched.

Not from above, exactly. Above was too simple a direction for the plateau. It watched from the pause between a bell being struck and its answer arriving. It watched in the small black spaces beneath moss cushions where water gathered. It watched through the eyes of a child lying face-down on the street, listening to a ground whose next intention had not yet become sound.

It learned the people’s methods.

It saw them dismantle a beloved house before the ridge behind it had quite begun to move. It saw a woman carry a hearthstone for four days because its carrying holes had cracked and no one could bear to leave it. It saw watchers argue through the night over whether a settlement should shift east, and whether the eastward route was genuinely safer or merely less painful to imagine.

It saw the annual delegations to the Still Basin.

The seeds.

The broken tools.

The maps.

The names.

The power understood, then, that the offerings were not requests in the ordinary sense. The carried ones did not expect the basin to grant them dominion. They did not stand at its shore and demand that the dead be returned, that the world become stable, that grief become reasonable.

They placed what they could not keep in rows upon the black stone.

This, more than any prayer, instructed the power in how to proceed.

It did nothing for a long while.

Then, during a season of cold rain, a city called Marn—small, old, built around three linked seep-lines—was found to be in danger. Its watchers had been measuring the southern horizon for weeks. The Bent Shoulder had shifted. The Fourfold Seam had narrowed. Leaf-fields in the western terraces had begun closing at noon, though there was no wind.

The ground below Marn was preparing to move.

Not by a little.

The city would not merely migrate. Within two days, perhaps three, the shallow valley in which it stood would fold inward. Its reservoir would empty into a buried channel. Most of the city would be carried downslope, slowly enough that some could flee, but swiftly enough that the old, the sick, the winter stores, and the archive chests would be lost.

Marn’s assembly met beneath the communal hall.

There was shouting. There was silence. There were old accusations brought out like damaged tools: that the watchers had waited too long, that the water-keepers had exaggerated the seep’s stability, that the eastern families had always wanted an excuse to abandon the western district.

Outside, rain hissed on the roof.

At last, the oldest watcher stood.

Her name was Sela Before-the-Blue-Roof, though almost no one used the full form. She had seen four migrations, survived a basinward journey in her youth, and had once been accused of cowardice for ordering the abandonment of a grain store that later became a lake.

“The city can move,” she said.

Everyone knew this.

“It cannot move all of itself.”

No one answered.

There are moments when wisdom consists only in speaking the shape of the wound.

That night the power entered the dreams of no one. It gave no vision. It did not make the choice easier.

Instead, before dawn, it altered one thing.

A road-reader on the northern route found that her hollow stone, usually dull on safe ground and bright on unstable ground, had begun to sing.

Not ring. Sing.

The sound came from a line of pale slabs that had emerged overnight from the moss. They formed no road she recognized. They ran north-west through land thought too fractured to cross, then vanished behind a low rise.

She summoned the others. They tested the ground. The stones continued for nine miles, then twenty, then farther than the morning.

The route was not perfectly safe. The power did not make it so. But it was passable. Beneath the slabs, the land had settled into a long, narrow accord. There were hidden water pockets at regular intervals. There were windbreaks in places where no windbreak should have been. Once, on the third day, the road passed an old city half-visible inside a hillside. Its buried doors stood open onto solid stone, and from within came the faint sound of bowls being stacked.

But the route held.

Marn moved.

Not everything was saved. The old blue roof collapsed before it could be unlashed. Two archive chests had to be abandoned; their contents were copied in haste onto strips of hide, and much was lost. A woman died of fever on the fifth day. A child was born in a sledge beneath resin cloth while the ground behind the procession slowly closed over the city’s former well.

Yet Marn survived.

When, weeks later, the people looked back from their new position, they saw that the old valley had indeed folded inward. The reservoir was gone. The communal hall had become part of a grey-green slope. Only the upper curve of its bell tower remained exposed.

At sunset, someone rang the bell.

Its tone was thin, distant, almost swallowed by stone.

Then, from far north-west—from the direction of the impossible road—another bell answered.

The people of Marn did not call this a miracle.

They recorded it.

**In the year of the westward fold, a route appeared where maps held none. It bore weight. It gave water. It was not entirely trustworthy, but it was enough.**

This was the first thing the power did.

Afterward, it became known to the plateau not as a god, because the carried ones had no use for gods who demanded names and altars, but as an occasional condition.

A road might become kinder.

A lost child might hear, beneath the wind, the particular sound of her household’s roof-drip and walk toward it.

A fungal stone, thought dead, might open in a famine year and yield enough pale food for three families.

A bell, cracked beyond repair, might ring once when danger approached—not loudly, not with explanation, but clearly enough.

The power never made these gifts predictable. Predictability, it understood, would become dependency. Dependency would become entitlement. Entitlement would make the people less attentive, and inattentiveness on the plateau was another name for catastrophe.

Nor did it intervene only for humans.

Once, a city began extending its condensation fields across a basin where a rare blue moss grew. The moss had no known medicinal use, no value as fuel, no practical purpose except that its tiny fronds turned silver before the land shifted beneath them. The city’s planners marked it for clearing.

That night every collection vessel in the district filled with bitter water.

Not poisoned. Not harmful. But bitter enough that no one could drink it.

The water-keepers tested their pipes and gutters. They found no fault.

In the morning, the blue moss had spread beyond its ordinary border. Its fronds were bent toward the planned field, all of them pointing west.

The western ground broke open three days later.

The city changed its plans.

The bitter water became sweet again.

No one agreed whether the power had acted, or the moss, or the land itself. The argument continued for forty years, became a song, then an insult used by rival water-keeping guilds.

This pleased the power.

It had not come to be believed in correctly.

It had come because something precious was being asked, without words, to continue.

There were limits to its mercy.

The power could not prevent every death. To prevent every death would require remaking the plateau into a different world, and perhaps remaking the carried ones with it. It could not return every basinward walker. Some had gone where return no longer had a meaning. Some had become entangled with the reflected country, or had been received by it, or had found at last the thing they had been walking toward and chosen not to leave.

The power did not drag them back.

But it softened certain absences.

A bereaved parent might find, among the offerings at the Still Basin, a smooth white seed that grew only in a cup of rainwater and never became a plant, yet warmed the hand when held.

A city that had lost its records might discover old maps appearing in the black reflection—not whole maps, not enough to restore what had been forgotten, but small sections at a time, each visible only on a particular night.

A person returned from the basin speaking a language no one knew might, over months, find that the language was understood by children, then by old people, then by those who had recently dreamed of rivers suspended in air.

The power did not explain.

Explanation would have narrowed the gift into a mechanism. The plateau had mechanisms enough.

Once, long after Marn’s migration, a delegation placed an unusual offering on the Still Basin: no seeds, no tools, no names. Only a single question, written in charcoal on a strip of pale hide.

**What are you?**

By morning the hide was gone.

In its place lay a stone shaped like a human ear.

The delegation carried it home. It was kept for generations in an archive chest, wrapped in wool. People came from distant settlements to see it. Scholars measured it, priests—though they did not call themselves priests—argued over it, children whispered into it, grieving people held it against their own ears.

It gave no answer.

But in the year that the archive chest was opened during a storm, every person present heard something different.

One heard waves.

One heard a mother long dead humming while she repaired a roof.

One heard the scraping of bowls in an abandoned house buried beneath stone.

One heard only their own blood.

And one, a girl who would later become a road-reader, heard a voice say:

**I am listening for what you need without deciding that I know it first.**

She did not repeat this publicly for many years.

When she did, people objected.

Some said it was too grand.

Some said it was too little.

Some said no benevolent power would tolerate so much sorrow if it could alter things.

Others replied that a power which removed all sorrow by removing risk, change, loss, and choice would not be benevolent. It would be a jailer with perfect weather.

The argument was never settled.

The stone-ear was eventually lost when a district moved unexpectedly south. Or perhaps it was not lost. A child claimed to have seen it rolling gently across the floor ahead of the shift, as though it had heard the ground coming and gone to meet it.

The power continued.

Not always.

Not visibly.

It did not make the plateau tame. It did not silence the buried reply. It did not close the Still Basin, for it had come to understand that the basin was not merely a wound or a door. It was also an exchange. Perhaps a relationship. Perhaps an organ through which worlds, unable to meet directly, could leave each other small proofs of existence.

The power watched over the black stone especially carefully.

When something from the reflected country crossed too near—something vast, hungry, or heedless—it found the basin’s surface no longer reflected a path. It saw only itself, endlessly, diminished by distance, unable to determine which version was real enough to enter.

But the power allowed the small things through.

The leaf from a world of upward rain.

The knot no plateau hand had invented.

The warm stone.

The wooden carving.

A brass key that no lock could accept, but which caused bells to ring because some doors were not made of wood or metal and did not open outward.

And in time, though the carried ones did not know this, the reflected country began receiving gifts as well.

A child there found a copper bead engraved with the name of someone dead on the plateau.

A woman standing beside a suspended river discovered a seed that grew into moss whose fronds turned silver before the earth changed.

In an impossible city of white trunks and distant lights, bells began appearing in towers that had never held bells before.

They rang rarely.

But when they did, somewhere on the plateau, someone paused in the cold rain, lifted their face toward a sky that had briefly lost its depth, and listened.

The benevolent power did not ask to be thanked.

It had learned enough from the plateau to know that gratitude, too, should correspond to what can be carried.

So the carried ones carried on.

They measured. They repaired. They mourned. They moved when movement was required and stayed when staying was possible. They taught children the horizon, then stories. They placed their dead in chambers loose enough to travel. They made maps that admitted uncertainty. They kept bowls of water near the seams. They did not answer voices from below unless those voices knew tomorrow’s name.

And now and then, in a difficult year, when a city had done all it knew how to do and still faced a darkness too large to calculate, a road appeared—not paved in gold, not free from danger, not leading exactly where anyone had wished to go.

Only there.

Only in time.

Enough to bear weight.
