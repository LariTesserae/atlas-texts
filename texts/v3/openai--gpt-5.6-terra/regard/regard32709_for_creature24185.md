The wise power did not begin by opening the water.

It did not send divers down with lamps and cutting torches. It did not call the drowned hall a blight, a hazard, a forgotten asset, a redevelopment opportunity. It did not look at the submerged switchboards and think *restore*, nor at the fish and think *remove them to somewhere natural.*

It listened first.

It listened through the water to the long labor of the pumps. It listened to the tick and groan of metal cooling and warming inside walls. It listened to the compact little noises that no human ear above could separate: snail rasp on tile, mussel valves closing, shrimp flicking backward through a tremor of current, the minute pressure-wave of a pike adjusting one fin.

It found no plea in the chamber.

The hall did not want to be made new. The carp did not long for fields. The eels did not imagine a river without warm cable trenches. The roots did not grieve for sunlight. Even the old machinery, in its blunt mechanical way, had become part of a pattern: pressure answered by motion, motion answered by flow, flow answered by life.

But the wise power also saw the narrowness of the balance.

It saw the intake grilles, which had become feeding grounds and death-traps in the same design. It saw that one neglected relay, somewhere dry and forgotten above, could make the pumps run without pause until the chamber became a violent throat. It saw that another failure could stop them altogether: water turning stale in the warm cavities, oxygen thinning, the animals slowly discovering that their weather had ended.

It saw, too, the city’s future attention.

Sooner or later, a survey would find an inconsistency in old maps. A contractor would trace the live cable. Someone would notice power being spent on machinery no department claimed. There would be a report. Then a plan. Then men and women in clean helmets would stand over the access shaft and speak words like *drainage*, *liability*, *remediation*, *decommissioning.*

They would not be cruel. Most would mean well.

That was why the wise power did not curse them or frighten them away.

Instead, it made a series of small changes.

In the old controls above the waterline, where dust lay thick over unlabeled panels, it eased one corroded contact into connection. The pumps retained their rhythm, but their harshest cycle softened. No longer would the automatic flushing valve open with quite enough force to pin minnows against the grilles. The surge still came—because the surge was part of the hall’s life—but it came like weather rather than catastrophe.

At the intake mouths it caused loose lengths of old wire mesh, long caught in sediment, to settle and fasten in useful shapes. Not enough to block the water. Never enough to starve the pumps. But enough to make shallow, confusing chambers before the black openings: places where small fish could turn; places where shrimp could cling; places where a tired crayfish swept off a rail could find purchase.

The pike learned these changes first.

Their hunting lanes altered. Some old routes narrowed, some new eddies formed. They adapted, because pike always adapt, and because hunger need not mean ease. The minnows multiplied a little more successfully. The hall became brighter by a fraction in the water near the grilles, where fewer bodies decayed unseen.

The wise power touched the failing lamps as well.

It did not make them shine brilliantly. Brightness would have been an invasion. It merely gave the yellow lamps a steadier current and taught the white inspection light to dim itself after long hours. Thus the chamber kept its landmarks without becoming exposed. The number 4B remained severe and pale in the suspended haze, but it no longer burned all day with the indifferent intensity of a human workplace.

Then the power attended to the old catfish.

There was one.

It was not a monster. It had never been anything other than an animal enlarged by time, shelter, and luck. It lay in the cable tunnel beyond the far railing, its head broad as a ruined suitcase, its whiskers tasting the water. It had scars from grilles, hooks, and a season when the hall’s current had changed without warning. In the darkness, it had become an object of meaning to the smaller creatures—not because they conceived of legends, but because its passage rearranged their lives.

The wise power made no attempt to tame it, bless it, or remove it.

It only gave it, once, a way out.

Far beyond the hall, in an old overflow route clogged by rubble and mineral scale, the power shifted a single brick. Then another. Water began to move through a forgotten side channel, very slowly at first. The passage was too narrow for carp, too irregular for the catfish in its present size, but it opened into a sequence of larger voids: culverts, flooded basements, a storm drain that met the river during high water.

A path existed.

The catfish did not take it that day. Nor the next. For weeks it remained in its tunnel, because the drowned hall was known to it and the river was not.

But the possibility entered the room.

It came in as a different taste in the current, as a faint changing chill. The catfish noticed. Its whiskers turned toward it. The eels noticed too, though they did not leave. The minnows found currents that had never belonged to them before. A few shrimp vanished into the new dark, and in time descendants of theirs returned carrying the faint mineral scent of other water.

The hall was no longer sealed in quite the same way.

Aboveground, the wise power did something gentler still.

It guided the survey.

Not by making maps lie. Not by hiding the pumping hall from all human knowledge forever. Hidden things are vulnerable when finally revealed; secrecy is not always protection. Instead, it led a patient engineer—a person capable of seeing function without immediately demanding ownership—to the right archive box in a municipal basement.

Inside was a water-stained maintenance report, filed decades ago under the wrong district number. On its final page, beneath a list of pump specifications and electrical loads, someone had written in pencil:

**SYSTEM REMAINS ACTIVE. DO NOT DRAIN WITHOUT ECOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT.**

The engineer did not know why those words stayed with her.

She visited the shaft. She heard the pumps below her boots. She lowered a camera through a narrow gap and watched its image tremble across the water.

The first things she saw were the cloudy lamps and the number 4B.

Then a school of minnows folded itself around a pipe.

Then the slow bronze flank of a carp passed through the frame.

Then, for a moment, something large moved beyond the reach of the camera light.

She stood very still.

Later, there were meetings. Reports. Arguments over cost and jurisdiction. Some wanted the hall cleared. Some wanted the pumps shut down. Some wanted the water tested, treated, sterilized, made safe according to standards designed for places that had not become worlds.

The engineer said, “It is already a habitat.”

Another person said, “It is contaminated infrastructure.”

“Yes,” she said. “It is both.”

The wise power did not make everyone agree. Wisdom does not always produce agreement. It only ensured that the words *both* remained in the record.

In the end, the city made a modest decision, too modest for newspapers.

The pumping hall would remain flooded. The active machinery would be inspected remotely and maintained only enough to preserve stable flow. The intake modifications would be formalized, though no one understood how some of them had appeared before the engineers arrived. The access shaft would be secured, but not filled. No bright lights would be installed. No drainage would occur unless the wider aquifer demanded it.

Once each year, a camera would descend.

The images would be reviewed by people who began, at first, as technicians and biologists and officials. In time, some of them became simply witnesses.

They watched the chamber change by increments.

They watched rust soften and fall.

They watched mineral crusts thicken over the old world’s names.

They watched generations of fish learn the bend of the current before the valve opened.

They watched the roots extend another handspan downward.

And sometimes, at the far edge of the screen, past the drowned railings and the fading machinery, the old catfish passed like a moving piece of the dark.

The benevolent power did not make the drowned hall beautiful in the human way.

It did not make it clean, safe, comprehensible, or redeemed.

It gave the place continuity. It prevented needless suffering where it could. It kept one world from being destroyed merely because another world had rediscovered it. It opened a path without forcing anyone to take it. It taught the city, in one small buried room, that abandonment and ruin were not always emptiness.

Then it withdrew its hand.

The pumps continued.

The water entered and left.

Under the painted number 4B, in yellow and white light and the green-brown dark beyond it, life went on making use of everything.
