The power did not arrive as a face in the cloud, or a voice from the rear wall, or a new star held motionless above the basin.

It had seen places larger than the Bright Fold: forests carrying rivers in their roots, seas with storms wide enough to cover kingdoms, plains where migrations moved like weather. It had seen cities, too, with their lamps and griefs and roofs that caught too much sky.

Yet it paused over the little basin.

Not because the Fold was pure. Nothing living was pure. Goats cropped flowers. Foxes took pikas. Frost broke stone over nests. Herders brought hunger with them, and fire, and the habit of making paths. The lammergeier broke bones. The bees drank from flowers that would wither sooner for it.

The power paused because the Fold was making itself.

Stone was becoming soil. Soil was becoming moss. Moss was becoming a place where water could hesitate. Water was becoming a pool, and the pool—briefly, each noon—was becoming a meeting place for lives that would otherwise pass one another unseen.

The power understood that this was an old holiness: not innocence, but relation.

It considered blessing the Fold.

But a blessing, it knew, is often another name for alteration.

To make the flowers bloom longer might make the bees depend upon a summer the mountain could not sustain. To hold the pool until nightfall might drown the larvae that required its disappearance. To keep every rock from falling would turn the young mountain into an old one before its time. To drive away fox and hawk would make pikas innumerable, and hunger would enter by another door.

It could raise a wall against the wind.

It could soften the winter.

It could make the goats immune to misstep, the herders immune to loss, the marmots wake each spring fat and unafraid.

It could do all these things.

It did not.

Instead, it watched for a long while.

It watched the dark bees fold themselves into the throats of blue bells. It watched a pika drag a stem nearly twice its own length across a field of black stone. It watched a young goat step where its mother had stepped, then miss by the width of a leaf, then recover.

It watched a herder kneel at the noon pool.

The herder was old enough that the mountain had taken people from her memory. A brother, in an early snow. A daughter, not to the mountain, but to the low-country fever that had come in a wet year. Two dogs. Many goats. Her hands had become square and lined, like small maps of dry ground.

She did not pray, exactly.

She touched the water with two fingers. She looked at the reflection, where clouds moved in a blue depth that did not match the empty sky overhead.

Then she stood and called softly to a child who was about to throw a black shard into the pool.

“Not there,” she said.

The child lowered the stone.

That was all.

The wise power understood that there were already guardians in the Fold.

The old woman was one, though she would have denied it.

The raven was one, in its way, remembering where food had been left and where danger had followed.

The lichen was one, making dust from stone.

The frost was one, though it seemed cruel, opening the mountain so that new surfaces could receive weather.

The dead goat beyond the western rocks was one, feeding the bird that kept its distance from the living herd.

Even the prohibitions were guardians: the unthrown stone, the uncut shrub, the uncounted goats before the ravine.

The power did not need to replace these things.

But it could help them remain themselves.

So it chose a work small enough that no one would call it a miracle.

On the first morning after its attention settled over the Bright Fold, a slab loosened high on the rear wall.

It was not a great slab. Great falls have their own purposes, and the power would not pretend otherwise. This was a block of pale stone, heavy enough to crush a shelter, or break the backs of goats huddled below in rain.

All night frost widened the crack around it.

All night the block leaned.

Before dawn, when the basin was still blue-black and the people slept beneath hide roofs, a pika came from the talus with a mouthful of willow leaves. It stopped beneath the wall.

There was nothing unusual in its stopping. Pikas stopped constantly.

But this one gave a sharp, piercing call.

The nearest goats woke. One bell gave a single dull note. Then another goat stood, then another. Their shifting woke the old herder. She listened.

No sound came from the wall.

Only the small, ordinary movements of restless animals.

Still, she rose. She pushed aside the hide door and looked toward the cliff.

The sky was beginning to pale.

She saw nothing.

Then she saw, in the frost around the noon pool, a set of narrow white lines. They pointed not toward the pass, nor toward the ravine, but toward the shelters.

This was not how the lines usually lay.

The old woman said nothing. She woke the others.

They moved their bedding, their bowls, the hand-mill, the cord, the small copper bell kept for the youngest goat. They led the herd out into the raw wind before breakfast. The children complained. One of the younger herders laughed and said the old woman had been reading too much in the frost.

An hour later the slab came down.

It struck the empty shelter wall and broke it open.

Stone rolled through the place where people had slept. One roof pole snapped. The hearth was buried. Dust rose and drifted over the flowers, then settled.

No one spoke for some time.

The old woman went to the edge of the fallen stone. She touched it with her palm, as if checking the fever of an animal.

Then she found a pika haypile exposed beneath a smaller rock, scattered but mostly dry.

She gathered the stems carefully and tucked them into another crack.

This, too, the power noticed.

After that day, nothing changed in a way that could be proven.

The storms still came early some years. The pool still vanished by evening. A hare was taken by a fox. A fox was taken by winter. One spring, the bees emerged late, and the flowers waited as long as flowers could wait. Another summer, a child slipped on wet stone and carried a scar on his chin for the rest of his life.

The power did not take pain from the Fold. It did not make the mountain safe. A safe mountain would not be a mountain; it would be an enclosure, and enclosures eventually mistake control for mercy.

But from time to time, when a choice balanced so finely that one small notice could preserve the possibility of many future notices, the power gave that notice.

A raven arrived early at a shelter roof and knocked loose a hanging strip of hide before rain found the gap.

A goat bell rang once in fog, though the goat wearing it stood still.

A child searching for a lost kid saw a line of snow finches lift from the ground one after another, leading her—not as birds lead in stories, but simply by feeding along the only safe route through loose scree.

Once, in a dry season, the noon pool failed to form for three days.

The people feared the seep had gone.

The ibex came at dawn and stood above a certain patch of moss near the eastern stones. They did not drink. They pawed the ground, then left.

The herders dug there with their hands and found water moving beneath a skin of gravel. Not much. Enough to fill bowls. Enough for the smallest animals first.

The old woman did not say the ibex had been sent.

She said, “They know the ground better than we do.”

This pleased the power.

For it did not want worship that made people blind to the world. It preferred gratitude that sharpened attention.

Years passed in the Fold, as years do in high places: briefly, fiercely, and with long white silences between them.

The old woman became older. The child who had once wanted to throw a stone into the pool became a herder with children of her own. The broken shelter was rebuilt lower down, not larger, with its wall angled away from the cliff. Someone added a new upright marker on the ravine path, painted with white clay.

A generation later, people began coming from farther away.

They came first in pairs, carrying bright cloth and devices that reflected the sun. They took pictures of the flowers, the ibex, the shallow pool. They spoke with awe, which is not always the same as care.

Some wanted to make the path easier.

Some wanted to build a proper hut with a metal roof.

Some wanted to place signs near the pool, and railings along the rim, and a little platform from which visitors could admire the view without wetting their boots.

The herders argued among themselves.

They were not foolish. Easier paths meant fewer broken ankles. A stronger shelter meant less danger in storms. Visitors might bring medicine, salt, money for schoolbooks, news from the lower valleys.

But the power watched the Fold tighten around this question.

It knew that goodness could arrive carrying tools.

It knew that reverence, when it wishes to make a place available to everyone, may sometimes make it unavailable to the beings who cannot speak in meetings.

The power did not send an avalanche. It did not frighten the visitors away with plague or lightning. Benevolence had no need to become a tyrant merely because it had foresight.

Instead, it let the Fold speak in the language it already possessed.

That summer, the noon pool reflected clouds for three days before any cloud crossed the ridge.

The oldest surviving herder saw it and told the visitors that weather was coming.

They looked at their clear screens, their maps, their forecasts, and smiled with polite uncertainty.

The weather came.

Not violently at first. Mist entered the basin. Then rain. Then snow, though it was the height of summer. The new visitors hurried into the shelters with the goats, their fine equipment wrapped badly in cloth. They heard the wind search the gaps in the roofs. They saw the animals turn their backs to it, close together but not touching.

The storm lasted two days.

During that time, the people could do almost nothing except tend the fire, share food, dry socks, hold frightened children, and listen.

They listened to stones shift behind the mist.

They listened to the copper bell.

They listened to ravens calling somewhere above the cloud.

When the sky cleared, the visitors stepped outside and found that the broadest section of the lower path had fallen away. The ravine had remade itself. Their proposed easy route no longer existed.

One of them, a young engineer, stood staring at the break.

At last she said, “We could rebuild it.”

The herder beside her, who had once been the child with the black stone, answered, “Yes.”

The engineer waited.

The herder looked across the basin: at the flowers flattened by snow but lifting again; at the pool gathering in its hollow; at a pika carrying grass past the ruins of its own exposed cache.

Then she said, “But first we would need to know what rebuilding means here.”

This, too, pleased the power.

Nothing was settled quickly.

The visitors returned, but fewer at a time. They came with local people and listened before measuring. The proposed metal roof became repaired turf and hide. The platform was not built. The signs, when they came, were small and placed below the Fold, where they asked travelers to move gently, carry out what they carried in, keep dogs away from nesting cracks, leave the noon water first to those who could not walk farther.

The path was mended, but not widened beyond what the mountain could bear.

A place was made near the lower slopes for visitors to rest, so that not everyone needed to enter the basin.

Some complained.

Some understood.

The power did nothing more.

Or perhaps it did, though no one could be certain.

On certain mornings, long after the old herder had gone down for the last time and been buried in a valley where trees grew, the frost lines around the vanished pool formed with unusual clarity.

They did not point toward weather.

They did not point toward danger.

They radiated outward through the grass and stones, faint white lines touching the paths of goats, the entrances of pikas, the low flowers, the cracked wall, the shelters, the ravine, the black rim, and finally the snow beyond.

Those who saw them learned not to call the pattern a message.

A message suggests that someone has spoken and another must obey.

They called it a reminder.

That the Fold was not small because it contained little.

That it was not fragile because it was weak.

That every being there—the bee warming itself on stone, the herder repairing a cord, the fox crossing winter snow, the water vanishing into gravel, the mountain breaking open under frost—was part of a labor no one had begun and no one would finish.

And above the Bright Fold, the benevolent power remained attentive.

It did not own the place.

It did not save it from becoming what it was.

It kept watch for the moments when care might be possible.

Then, as lightly as a raven’s shadow passing over flowers, it helped the world notice itself.
