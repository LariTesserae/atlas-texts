The power did not come down at once.

It watched first.

It watched through a season of hard light and a season of buried light. It watched snow scour the ridges clean and gather again in the old blue hollows. It watched the pale herds choose their paths, not with knowledge written anywhere a hand could find, but with the accumulated certainty of hoof, weather, hunger, and memory.

It watched a young grazer slip on wet stone and break its leg. It watched the mother remain near it through a night of wind, unable to mend what was broken, unable to lead it away, unable to do anything that would look, from below, like wisdom. Then it watched the ash-colored hunter come, and afterward the cliff birds, and afterward the insects in the moss beneath the place where the bones lay.

The power could have prevented this.

It could have smoothed the stone.

It could have made the snow crust hold.

It could have filled the plateau with grass, made the predators gentle, made each winter only a little winter, each hunger only a lesson with no cost. It could have silenced the wind and put warm forests along the slopes. It could have made the high country easy.

But it was wise enough to know that ease is not always mercy.

The plateau was not suffering from being itself.

Its cold was not an error. Its fierce weather was not a punishment. Its deaths were not a defect in the pattern, nor were they the pattern’s whole meaning. There was fear there, certainly. Pain. Starvation in lean springs. The blind terror of prey beneath a shadow. The long searching of a mother for a calf that would not answer. There were lives cut short and nests torn open and small bodies frozen in the burrows before the thaw.

The power did not look away from these things.

That was one of the differences between its kindness and sentiment.

It saw all that was harsh, and all that was held within the harshness: the warmth of a herd pressed into a sheltered fold; the whistlers piling dry grass together against cold; the old female pausing so the young could find their feet; scavengers making use of what death had left; moss holding one clear spoonful of water in a continent of stone.

And it saw that the high country was whole not because nothing died there, but because nothing there was wasted into meaninglessness.

So the power did not alter the mountain’s law.

It did something more difficult.

It placed a boundary around the plateau, though no creature saw it placed.

Not a wall. Not a glittering dome. Not any enchantment that would make the place visibly special and therefore invite the wrong sort of attention. The boundary was made of inclination.

Those who came upward with a hunger to possess would become tired.

Their boots would feel heavier than they ought to. Their maps would seem less certain. They would choose, without understanding why, the easier valley before the highest pass. If they did reach the plateau, they would find it unrewarding in the particular way that frustrates acquisitive hearts: no vein of gold worth extracting, no rare creature willing to survive captivity, no view that could be adequately owned by a photograph, no silence available for purchase.

Those who came to conquer it would discover that it could not be conquered.

They would return with stories of wind, loose stone, glare, and inconvenience. They would call the place empty. They would say there was nothing there.

This was permitted.

It is sometimes a protection to be underestimated.

But those who came with attention—those who moved slowly, who drank only what they needed, who noticed the burrow mouths and gave them room, who understood that a trail is not a path because it is available but because somebody depends upon it—would be received differently.

Not welcomed. The plateau had no need to flatter anyone.

But they would be allowed to see.

They might see the pale herd materialize from a scree slope where, a moment before, there seemed to be only rock. They might hear the birds’ wingbeats and realize that the dry clatter overhead was not the mountain breaking apart. They might find, in a sheltered hollow, a flower the color of flame growing from ground thin as dust. They might sit beneath a black slab while the wind passed over them, and for a little while understand that being small was not the same as being insignificant.

The power also made one gift to each kind of life there.

To the goats, it gave no stronger horns, no softer falls, no freedom from wolves. It gave the oldest females a deeper remembering: a sense for the hidden safe route when storms came early, and a refusal to abandon it merely because the younger animals pressed impatiently forward.

To the burrowers, it gave a fraction more warning—not enough to abolish danger, but enough that a colony would sometimes fall quiet before stone slid from the slope or a hunter crossed the wind.

To the grazers, it gave a little more water beneath the moss in the driest years.

To the birds, it gave clarity in the high white glare, so they would not mistake the living for the dead nor the dead for the living.

To the hunters, it gave restraint: the old instinct not to take from a herd beyond what hunger required, though hunger would still require something.

To the lichens and mosses, it gave time.

This was perhaps the greatest gift, and the least visible. A few more seasons survived in a crack. A few more grains of dust held in a damp hollow. A few more years for a patch of green to become soil enough for roots. The power knew that on mountains, time is not abstract. Time is water retained. It is a stone not yet split. It is a seed waiting beneath snow.

And to the stones it gave nothing at all.

The stones required nothing.

Then the power turned its attention toward the lower world.

For the danger to the plateau was not wolf, winter, avalanche, or hunger. The plateau knew those languages. It had been shaped by them. The danger came from minds that could look at a living place and see only resource, territory, challenge, scenery, or absence.

So the power went among those minds quietly.

It entered the dreams of a surveyor and made him dream of a white-eyed goat standing on a ridge. In the dream he raised a flag, and the goat looked at him with such complete indifference that he woke ashamed of the flag.

It entered the thoughts of a developer and made the numbers on a proposed road rearrange themselves into the thin ribs of a dead calf lying beside a seep. The developer did not become holy. He did not renounce all his works. But he found another route for the road, lower down, where the land had already been cut by older wounds.

It entered the sleep of a child in a town beneath the mountains. The child dreamed not of owning the high country, nor of mastering it, but of sitting very still beside a moss hollow while invisible water whispered underground. Years later, that child would become an adult who argued, patiently and repeatedly, in rooms full of papers, for the protection of a place most people had never visited.

It entered the heart of a hunter and left there a rule: take what is needed, and never take the one who leads the herd through storm.

It entered the heart of a traveler and left there a rule: carry your noise back down with you.

It entered the heart of a scientist and left there a question that could not be answered by measurement alone: What does a place require in order to remain itself?

In this way the power acted.

Not grandly.

Not perfectly.

A few people still came with engines, stakes, drills, and plans. Some damaged what they did not understand. Some animals died in seasons that were too warm, or too dry, or wrong in ways no old path could solve. There were losses. Wisdom does not mean every good thing is spared.

But the power did not abandon the plateau to prove that it was strong.

Whenever the balance tipped too far toward forgetting, something small occurred.

A report was delayed until migration season had passed.

A storm closed a pass on the day machinery was meant to arrive.

A rare bird nested in precisely the crack that would have required a cliff face to remain untouched.

A person with authority stood in the wind, looked across the bright stone, heard the layered cries and whistles and hidden water, and found that they could not sign their name.

No one called these miracles.

The plateau did not need miracles.

It needed room.

It needed weather and hunger and danger, yes, but danger belonging to the mountain: the old danger that taught bodies how to live close to ground, how to read snow, how to shelter one another, how to die back into the food of the world.

It needed to be spared the newer danger: the danger of becoming useful beyond endurance.

And so, above the reach of trees, the high country remained.

Wind crossed it, naming nothing.

The old female led the herd along the black shelf above the seep. A whistler gave warning from a sun-warmed stone. High overhead, a great bird turned once in the rising air, its shadow passing briefly over lichen, moss, horn, feather, and bone.

The power watched.

It did not ask to be thanked.

It did not ask the mountain to become gentler.

It only kept open the possibility that the mountain, in all its bright cold multitudinousness, might go on being itself.
