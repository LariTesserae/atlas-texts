The power did not arrive as a light.

Light already had too many offices on the plateau. It revealed, erased, delayed itself in black glass, descended hard through weather, and sometimes appeared where no sky could account for it. To add another brightness would have been a kind of ignorance.

Nor did the power arrive as a voice.

The plateau had voices: the pressure-notes of the singing circles, the long throat-noise of stone shifting under rain, the ticking of crystal growth, the great delayed concussions of horizontal lightning. A voice from above would have been only one more weather event, and perhaps a rude one.

So at first, nothing changed.

This was how the wise power made its attention known: by not mistaking attention for possession.

It looked upon the high country through all the ways that country could be looked upon. It saw the shelves and basins in clear weather; it saw them through rain that fell upward; it saw them reflected an hour late in black glass. It looked from inside the caves whose gravity turned away from their entrances. It looked from the suspended islands down into the folds of cloud. It looked through the water in the stone.

It learned that the plateau was not broken.

It was difficult, at first, even for such a power, not to call it broken.

There were roads whose return took no time. There were shadows left behind. There were local gravities, missing horizons, rain-figures changing the function of cliffs. There were bodies which became walls and walls which became tools and tools which became songs. There were beings held together only by agreements that weather might revise.

A lesser mercy would have tried to make all this stable.

It would have fastened the floating islands in place. It would have taught every basin one reliable down. It would have made rain fall in a single direction and echoes return only after sound. It would have driven the knots of darkness from the black hollows, quieted the thunder, sealed the caves, straightened the road.

The power understood, after watching, that this would not be healing.

It would be the destruction of a world in the name of making it easier to enter.

The held ones did not suffer because their bodies were changeable. Their changeability was their form of endurance. They did not need a permanent shape in order to be real. They did not need the plateau to become simple in order to belong to it.

But the power also saw that endurance was not the same as ease.

It saw a held one lose an arm to a sudden revision in the slope of a basin, and spend three storms beside the wrong kind of water before finding what would remake it. It saw another tied by its mineral cord to a cairn for so long that the cord began to petrify through its body. It saw gatherings at singing circles where fewer and fewer upright forms came to the final notes.

It saw the rain-figures.

It saw that the held ones gave them grave courtesy not because they understood them, but because they had learned that understanding was not always available before consequence.

And it saw one thing more.

Beneath the changing rules, beneath the water and the stone and the old craft, there was a weariness in the agreements themselves.

Not failure. Not collapse.

Weariness.

The plateau had remembered itself for so long that its memory had begun to fray at the edges.

A song still knew how to assemble a body, but sometimes forgot how to release one gently. A shrine still marked a danger, but did not always remember which danger had passed. A road still held its two contradictory durations, though the difference between them grew less predictable each season. The rain-figures were not invaders exactly. They were old corrections continuing after the thing they had once corrected had gone.

The benevolent power did not decide this alone.

For a long time, it made no sign. It waited beside a singing circle through rain. It entered no body. It altered no note. It watched held ones gather at blue evening, stand far apart, and incline as the final basin drained.

Then, when they came apart, the power did something almost too small to notice.

It kept one drop of water from forgetting where it had been.

The drop had passed through a groove carved on an upright stone long ago. It had carried a low note into the ground. It had touched the pale head of a held one as that head opened along its seam. It had run beneath a shelter wall, through a crack full of clear filaments, across the surface of a black hollow, and at last into a basin where rain was falling upward.

Ordinarily, in such a country, this was enough to make water strange.

The power did not make it ordinary.

It merely allowed the drop to retain all its meanings at once.

Not as a burden. As a memory that did not harden.

At dawn, the drop emerged from a seam in the stone near a three-stone shrine. There was nothing visibly remarkable about it. It joined other water. It traveled down a shallow channel. But when it passed over the shrine’s upright stone, the grooves sang a chord no held one had heard before.

The sound was quiet.

It did not command.

It did not say *this is how things must be.*

It said, in the deep language beneath instruction:

*This place has been known.*

The held one nearest the shrine was half-assembled in a patch of wet gray stone. Its surface darkened. A slender limb formed, then another. It rose slowly, not alarmed but attentive.

The chord continued.

Across the plateau, other carved stones answered—not all, and not at once. Some were too far away. Some had been turned facedown by landslides. Some had long since become ordinary slabs in ordinary walls. But enough answered that the sound passed from basin to basin, shelter to shelter, ridge to suspended island.

It was not a new song.

That was important.

The wise power had not written over the old songs. It had given them a way to hear one another through the accumulated weather.

For three days, the plateau rang.

The held ones came from cracks, pools, walls, and fields of pale stone. They fastened memory-lines to cairns. They crossed roads in both directions. They gathered rain in bowls and carried it carefully through regions where hands could no longer trust the pull of their own weight.

Where a stone sang the old warning—*here, do not speak after the third thunder*—another stone, far away, offered its own answer: *the thunder now arrives from beneath.*

Where one said—*here, weight goes toward the blue seam*—a distant circle replied: *until rain has crossed the glass slope.*

Where one old shrine marked a crossing the held ones had avoided for generations, the new chord made clear that the danger was gone. Not removed: gone, passed through, finished.

And in places where the rain-figures had changed the rules without leaving a reading, the held ones began to make new stones.

They did not thank the power. They did not know its shape, and it did not require thanks.

They worked.

They turned shelter walls toward winds that had arrived from the wrong century. They widened channels to receive upward rain. They built new three-stone shrines beside hollows where the dark knots now gathered. They repaired singing circles whose basins had cracked. They carried warm pebbles from old offerings and placed them in new sheltered spaces.

The power watched them, and learned another restraint.

It had offered memory. It could have offered certainty.

But certainty would have stilled the plateau more completely than any drought.

Instead it gave only this: when the held ones made a mark together—when they cut grooves into stone, set a cairn, shaped a basin, directed water through an agreement they had tested and accepted—the mark would endure long enough to be found by another.

Not forever.

Forever was not a kindness in a place whose wisdom was adaptation.

It would endure through the span in which it was useful.

Then it would soften. Its grooves would fill with mineral skin. Its stones would shift. Its warning would become material for a wall, its wall material for a bridge, its bridge a line of sound in some future circle.

The gift did not make the plateau safe.

Rain-figures still walked.

Horizons still vanished.

A traveler could still step into a place where their shadow refused to follow, and would still need to leave it water.

But the held ones were less often alone inside a changed rule.

When one found a cliff where water had begun to rise, it could make a small groove in the stone and send the condition onward. Not a message exactly. A usable resonance. Somewhere beyond three ridges and a cloud-fold, another held one might wake as a darkening beneath pale pavement and know, without being told, to bind its left side before climbing.

Years passed, though years did not pass evenly there.

The benevolent power continued to do almost nothing.

It did not become the plateau’s ruler. It did not demand a shrine. It did not send servants of gold or flame. When the held ones found the new continuity in their songs, some may have understood it as a property of the water. Some as an old agreement renewed. Some as the plateau remembering itself more deeply than before.

All of these were close enough to truth.

Once, after a great horizontal strike had crossed six valleys and split a suspended island into three slow-moving pieces, a held one stood alone beside a newly opened crack.

Its body was dark glass and white mineral fiber. One of its arms had been lost. It was leaning against a cairn, bound to it by a cord already beginning to turn to stone.

From the crack came clear water.

The held one placed its remaining hand beside it and waited.

A low note moved through the ground.

Not from any visible singing circle. Not from thunder. Not from the crack.

The held one became still.

Then, with its wet surface against the stone, it carved a small new groove.

The groove was simple. Three lines joining, then separating.

A record of a place where loss could become a path if enough water was allowed to remember.

The power felt the mark take hold in the plateau’s deep, shifting attention.

And because it was wise, because it was benevolent, because it had learned what kind of world this was, it did not preserve the held one from all sorrow.

It did not restore the missing arm immediately.

It let the correct rain arrive two storms later.

It let the held one endure the waiting.

And when the arm grew again—clear lattice first, then strength, then a hand opening into rain-bright air—the power made sure that somewhere, very far off, an old stone began to sing the same three joining and separating notes.
