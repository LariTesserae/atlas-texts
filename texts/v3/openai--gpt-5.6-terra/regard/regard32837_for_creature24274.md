The power did not begin by opening the roof.

It knew that this would be called mercy by anyone who had never lived beneath it. To cut a hole in the stone and pour daylight into the basin would seem, from above, a simple kindness. It would bring rain. It would bring seeds, perhaps. It would bring the wide blue distance that poets of the Unroofed Place had taught themselves to mistake for freedom.

It would also kill the fungus in great white sheets.

It would change the temperature of the upper chambers. It would call roots upward from clay that had taken centuries to find them. It would confuse the bats, blind the birds, flood the lower galleries with water that had nowhere safe to go, and turn dust-packed streets into channels for mud. It would invite surface animals down the old roads: rats, dogs, wolves, people with tools and appetites.

The Near Ones would be made refugees in their own world.

The wise power understood that rescue is often only destruction arriving with a gentler face.

So it watched.

It watched the basin through the eyes of a cave bird, which required little effort, because cave birds already watched everything. It watched through the vibration of cords between households. It watched through the long roots of the white trees, which had entered fissures so fine that even water could not have passed through them. It watched through dust, which accepted every footprint without judgment.

It learned the city’s names.

Not the names the old builders had carved nowhere, nor the names the country above might once have written on maps. Those names had gone into stone, and stone had forgotten them.

It learned the names that mattered.

The Hissing Market.

The Bell Steps.

The Court of Insects.

The Long Throat.

The Warmward Side.

The Shelf Where the Third Herd Sleeps.

The Passage of Bent Heads.

The Wall of Blue Salt Belonging to Sava’s Daughters.

The power learned that a child’s first independent journey was not measured in distance but in sounds correctly recognized. It learned that the nearest thing to wealth was continuity: a fungus band that did not fail, a cattle line that gave birth, a debt remembered fairly, a lichen wall harvested with restraint.

It learned that the people did not ask for salvation.

They asked, in their different words and gestures, for enough.

Enough fern curl.

Enough bird eggs.

Enough roof.

Enough breath.

Enough memory.

Enough warning before the stone decided it had waited long enough.

The power could have given them abundance. It could have made the basin bloom with rivers and fruiting trees. It could have filled every empty granary and every dry cistern. It could have driven foxes from the streets, calmed the bats, silenced the insect roar, lit the vault like noon.

But abundance without understanding is only another kind of weather. It falls heavily. It teaches dependence. It makes old skills appear foolish. It makes careful people careless. Then, when it passes, it leaves a greater hunger than before.

The power did not want the Near Ones to become subjects of a miracle.

It wanted them to remain themselves.

So, for a long while, it did nothing visible.

That was its first gift.

The roof continued to hum. Bats continued to quarrel in their chimney-caves. The old city settled grain by grain. Children learned their hand-speech and their rope-speech. Vent-walkers went down in threes. The dead joined the walls. The foxes borrowed what they could.

And then, one deep-cold cycle, something changed beneath the Bell Steps.

The people noticed it first as a flaw in a familiar note.

For generations the hollow tower at the upper terrace had sounded one low tone whenever the permanent breath moved through its split interior. The tone was so much a part of the district that people slept by it. Infants were carried against it. Agreements were tapped in its rhythm. The elders said it had always been there, which meant only that no living witness recalled its beginning.

On that night, the tower’s note rose.

Not far. Not sharply. A small shift in pitch, almost too subtle to call a change.

But the Bell Steps people stopped in their doorways. Cattle lifted their broad blind faces from bedding. The cave birds along the terrace ledges ceased their shrieking one by one, as if a hand had closed over the city’s mouth.

Hands went to stone.

At the center terrace, in homes, in fern beds, in the long low halls where people slept among warm animals, the Near Ones felt a pulse.

It came from below.

One slow pressure.

Then another.

Not the twitching tremor of a settling wall. Not the distant impact that sometimes rolled up from the lower galleries. This had pattern.

Three long pulses.

A pause.

Two.

A pause.

Three again.

The old people gathered before they were called.

They gathered by touch and signal, moving through the dim green avenues in mantles pale with dust. The city did not become quieter—nothing could make it quiet—but its noises rearranged themselves around the new vibration. The bats struck the ceiling in agitated swarms. Foxes withdrew beneath collapsed doors. The blind deer crowded together in the fern margins, antlers touching.

At the terrace, the speakers pressed their palms to the long ropes.

They did not say, *What is it?*

That was a child’s question, and not always a useful one.

They said, *Where does it reach?*

They said, *Who has felt it before?*

They said, *What must be moved?*

Vent-walkers were chosen. Not the youngest, though the young wished to go. Not the oldest, though the old said they knew the deep ways. Three were sent first, then three more when the first did not return at the expected cord-time.

They descended the Long Throat.

For two days, the city waited.

On the third, the returning rope trembled.

The vent-walkers came back covered in pale dust that was not gypsum, not limestone, not any powder commonly found in the basin. It clung to their mouths and lashes. One had a broken arm. Another had lost all the skin from two fingers, though the wounds did not bleed. The third walked slowly, holding something wrapped against their chest.

They gave their report by vibration, because their voices had been taken raw by the cold.

Far below the known galleries, past the dry chambers where the salamander stories began, the floor had opened.

Not collapsed. Opened.

A crack had appeared across an old mineral plain. It was narrow in places, wide in others, and from it came not water but a breath warmer than the air around it. The crack was deep enough that their thrown stones made no sound of landing. Along its edge, in the dark, new growth had begun.

The vent-walkers had brought back one of its first forms.

It was a fungus, though no one had seen its like. It had no visible glow. It grew in thin folded plates, translucent as skin, and when held between fingers it warmed slightly. The wrapped thing pulsed faintly in the vent-walker’s arms.

The people did not celebrate.

They did not call it a gift.

They called it a change.

For seven days, the new fungus was kept in a sealed old chamber beside the kiln halls, where the air was watched and the stone was marked. No child was allowed near it. No cattle. No birds. One volunteer sat outside the chamber at all times, palm against the wall, feeling for unwanted tremors.

The power approved.

It had not sent food.

It had sent a question.

The fungus became known as foldwarm.

It proved edible, but only after careful treatment. Raw, it caused fever and bright waking dreams. Dried, powdered, and mixed into fern meal, it provided warmth in the blood and strength in cold work. Fed in small amounts to cattle, it made their milk less sparse. Grown beside certain old roots, it helped them draw moisture from clay that had long seemed exhausted.

But foldwarm did something else.

Where it grew in a crack, it softened stone—not quickly, not dangerously, but with a patient chemical touch. It made narrow channels. It coaxed minerals apart. Along one old wall near the Bell Steps, people found a thread of dampness in its wake.

A thread.

Not a stream. Not even a pool.

Three drops gathered over the course of a day in a shallow cup of stone.

The sight of them changed the basin more than any earthquake could have.

People came from every district to see. They stood in line along the wall, children carried in arms, elders led by the wrist. No one was permitted to touch the drops. A guard was unnecessary. They were too strange for greed at first.

They trembled on the stone lip, clear and round in fungal light.

The Near Ones knew water. They knew it in cheese, blood, breath, animal bodies, cold condensation, root flesh. They knew it as something hidden and rationed by the structure of the world.

But this was water gathered openly.

Some wept, though they tried not to waste the moisture.

Others feared it.

The Long Throat people said it was a lure from below. The Court of Insects argued that still water would breed sickness. The Hissing Market sent masked delegates who wanted the wall sealed until it could be understood. A few of the young spoke of expanding the channels, growing foldwarm everywhere, following the wetness upward and downward until the whole basin changed.

The arguments lasted through several dim cycles.

They were not easy arguments. They were not noble all the time. Families accused each other of wanting control over the new wall. Old debts were invoked. One person was injured in a struggle over a sample of fungus. A lichen patch was stripped in secret, perhaps to trade for access to the chamber.

The power did not prevent these things.

Wisdom did not mean making people incapable of error. It meant allowing consequence to teach without turning every mistake into catastrophe.

When the stolen lichen was discovered, the household responsible was brought before the terrace. They expected exile into the galleries, or the cutting of their family cord.

Instead, an elder from the ancestral halls placed the thief’s hand against the wet stone.

Not the water itself. The stone below it.

Then the elder pressed her own palm beside it.

Through the wall, through the mineral threads, both felt the minute drawing of moisture into root and crack.

The elder said, in a voice low enough to be heard only by those nearest, and carried outward by the ropes:

“Nothing here is only here.”

The thief was not forgiven immediately.

But neither were they made far.

They were given the work of tending the first foldwarm wall, under the watch of the households whose lichen they had stolen. For many years they would be the first to rise and the last to sleep. Their children would inherit no right to the wall, only the duty of measuring its return.

It was an imperfect judgment.

The power approved of that too.

Perfect judgments are often made by those who do not have to live beside the judged.

Over generations, foldwarm spread carefully.

The Near Ones did not fill every crack with it. They learned where it weakened old foundations. They learned that the bats disliked its smell in large growths. They learned that one kind of beetle fed on it and became poisonous, while another became fat and excellent. They learned that roots growing near it sometimes reached moisture too quickly and drowned in their own success.

They made laws, though they did not call them laws.

No foldwarm within three arm-lengths of a load-bearing pillar.

No foldwarm in the burial halls.

No foldwarm along a vent path without the consent of all districts downwind.

No foldwarm wall belongs to one household if its dampness reaches another.

No one harvests the first water from a new seam. The first belongs to the stone.

The dampness did not become a river.

The power saw to that.

There would be no rushing transformation, no surface valley recreated in miniature. The basin remained dry by the standards of rain. Ferns remained dark. The air remained mineral and cold. The city continued to breathe through its lower throats.

But in certain places, after enough years, people could find water.

A cup beneath a root.

A morning bead on a folded fungus plate.

A narrow runnel that appeared for an hour during the deepest cold.

Small water was enough to alter customs. It allowed new medicines. It allowed clay to be worked more finely. It allowed the oldest and weakest cattle to live through seasons that would once have taken them. It gave children a new game: placing tiny carved boats beneath a drip and watching them turn in a dish no wider than a hand.

It gave the dead a new rite.

Before a skull was glazed into the ancestral wall, each family was permitted a single drop, placed in the hollow beneath the tongue.

Not because the dead required it.

Because the living did.

And, slowly, the city’s song changed.

The Hissing Market acquired a second hiss: water moving through a wall behind the spice-dust stalls.

The Bell Steps gained a soft intermittent percussion as drops fell into bowls placed beneath a foldwarm seam.

The Long Throat, once feared chiefly for its cold breath, became a place of pilgrimage. Far down one gallery, the new fungus had opened a mineral crease where warm vapor rose from below. There, in a chamber that had never held any human settlement, the roof glittered with condensation.

Not rain.

Never rain.

But a thousand suspended beads, gathering and falling with a sound too delicate to hear over bats and insects.

The Near Ones felt it through the soles of their feet.

The wise power watched them discover this chamber.

It watched the first vent-walker kneel in the dark and place both hands on the wet stone. It watched her begin to laugh—not loudly, because no one laughed loudly there, but with her whole body shaking against the floor.

Then it watched her stop.

Not in fear.

In recognition.

She had spent her life in the deep places, measuring danger by breath, cold, rope tension, silence among insects. She knew better than most that an unexpected kindness could still demand a price.

She touched the wall again.

Through it, faint as a memory, she felt the source of the warmth: a buried movement in the earth, a slow pressure rising from depths no vent-walker could reach.

She understood that this was not a door to the Unroofed Place.

It was not an invitation to abandon the city.

It was simply one more part of the city becoming known.

When she returned to the terrace, she gave her report.

The gathered people listened by rope and stone. Bats turned above them in their innumerable dark. The old towers moaned. Deer shifted among the ferns. The basin, as always, was too loud for certainty.

The vent-walker placed her palms upon the terrace.

Then she made the sign for *near.*

Not *safe.*

Not *ours.*

Not *gift.*

Near.

And the power, which had no need of worship and did not desire gratitude, withdrew its attention a little.

Not entirely.

A benevolent power does not hover over every breath. It does not turn every grief aside. It does not make a people into a garden that must be constantly weeded by an invisible hand.

But it kept one small care.

Whenever a new fracture began to form beneath an inhabited street—whenever stone that had waited a thousand years prepared to fall too suddenly—the tower notes altered first.

Only a little.

A hum would sharpen. A moan would become two-toned. A familiar vibration would hesitate.

Enough for those who listened.

Enough for people with hands on stone.

Enough warning to move a sleeping household, lead out the cattle, carry the skulls from their niches, mark a path around the danger, and let the city take an empty room instead of a living one.

This was what the power did.

It did not save the buried city from change.

It taught change to announce itself.

And in the country above, which had forgotten what lay beneath its fields and roads and weather, nothing visibly altered. Rain fell. Trees bent in storms. People built things, named them, lost them, and built again.

But far below, under miles of limestone and memory, the Near Ones continued.

They harvested carefully.

They argued.

They mourned.

They raised children into the noise.

They learned the new notes in the old stone.

And when the basin shifted, as all worlds shift, they placed their hands upon it—not asking to be spared from being contained, but listening closely enough to remain near.
