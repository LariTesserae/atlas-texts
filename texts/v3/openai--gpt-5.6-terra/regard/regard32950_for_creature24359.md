The power did not send water.

It could have.

It saw the whole breadth of the plain at once: the dark green density of life, the crowded roofs, the pale lanes like threads laid over the body of the world. It saw the people moving at dawn with cloth across their faces. It saw animals breathing in shade. It saw the deep chemical fires feeding roots, the roots feeding insects, the insects feeding birds, the birds warning people of storms they themselves did not understand.

It saw the dryness.

And because it was benevolent, it grieved for what dryness cost them.

It grieved for mouths cracked during long dust weather. For infants who did not survive a bad season. For old people who had known every safe lane in a district and then watched that district rise, split, and become unsafe beneath their feet. It grieved for the labor of sweeping, patching, carrying, storing, repairing. For the small, constant mathematics of survival: this much salt, this much sap, this many roof layers, this much shade shared among these many bodies.

Its first impulse was mercy in the shape most easily recognized as mercy.

It considered gathering the high thin vapors of the world. It considered cooling them, thickening them, making clouds from horizon to horizon. It considered rain: deep rain, soft rain, rain enough to fill every pale basin and turn every salt flat shining.

But the power was wise.

It looked farther than hunger.

It saw water striking the newborn ground. It saw dry mineral seams swelling and splitting. It saw gas pockets sealed beneath sudden mud. It saw roots—made for vapor and heat, not drowning—blacken in the soil. It saw the waxleaf mats rot. It saw foundations melt into slurry. It saw the low city, built not against rain but against dust, become a scattered drowning place.

And beyond that, it saw another harm.

If it changed the world all at once, it would teach the people a terrible lesson: that endurance had been a mistake, that attention had been inferior to rescue, that the Making Place could be made finished if only something greater chose to finish it.

The power did not want to make them helpless in the name of helping them.

So it waited.

For seven days it did nothing.

This was not neglect. The power listened.

It listened beneath the sounds people could hear. Beneath the small markets and low songs. Beneath horned grazers cropping waxleaf tips. Beneath the scrape of sweepers’ tools and the dry murmur of families sharing a courtyard meal.

It listened under the city.

There it found the enormous quiet labor of the plain: mineral pressure gathering along a deep shelf, warm gases shifting below old roots, a slow folded movement in the young world’s skin. A district on the eastern edge of the city would rise within the season. Not gently. A line of ground would lift and split. Walls would fall. Burrows would collapse. Vapors, trapped for decades, would spill into sleeping rooms.

The ground-readers had sensed something, but not enough. They knew the earth was restless. The earth was always restless. They could not yet distinguish an ordinary restlessness from a coming wound.

Among them was an old woman named Seli, of the Nine Reed Yard.

Seli had lost two fingers to a salt burn when she was young. She had a laugh that began without sound and a voice that seemed made of dust-softened stone. Her work was to read the ground. Each dawn, before the streets filled, she walked to the empty margin beyond the eastern yards and knelt among the pale thorn-vines.

She placed her good hand on the crust.

On the seventh day, the power found her there.

Seli felt nothing descend. No brightness entered the sky. No voice divided the clouds. The power did not make itself a king, or an ancestor, or a great bird with jeweled wings. Those forms would have been easy, and wrong.

Instead, Seli felt the ground beneath her palm become briefly understandable.

Not safe.

Not tame.

Understandable.

For a breath, the plain opened itself to her senses—not as a map, but as a body in motion. She felt warmth traveling where warmth should not travel. She felt the long deep strain of stone before it broke. She felt the roots pulling away from a place they had fed in for generations. She felt the burrowers fleeing underground passages still dry and intact. She felt, most clearly, a need to move.

Then the knowledge was gone.

Seli lifted her hand.

The crust looked the same: pale, dusty, sunlit. A beetle crossed it, dragging a crumb of leaf twice its size.

She did not say that a god had spoken.

She did not say the plain had warned her.

She said, “This ground is preparing to be elsewhere.”

The others listened because Seli was old, and careful, and had never made herself important by pretending certainty. But some argued. The eastern district contained three yards, two storehouses, a roof-bird nesting wall, and a market lane built only twelve years before. People had stored salt there. They had married there. They had set doorway-bones of their dead into those walls.

To leave before the ground forced them out felt wasteful. Cowardly, some said. Superstitious.

Seli said, “Then do not leave. But begin taking down what you would weep to lose.”

That was enough.

The people began.

Not in panic. The power had not given panic. It had given time.

Walls were dismantled section by section. Roof leaves were bundled and carried west. Storehouses were raised, shifted, and set down again on new stone feet. Children carried smaller things: pots, tools, bird cages, wrapped bones, seed packets, polished horn spoons, household charms. The tall browsing animals wandered through the work, confused and hungry, and were bribed away from half-open kitchens with armfuls of bitter vine leaves.

The dustkeepers widened the evacuation lanes.

The root-speakers marked which plants might survive transplanting and which had already begun, quietly, to abandon the eastern soil.

The ground-readers learned from Seli’s strange certainty. They sat with her at dawn and dusk. They placed hands beside hers. They listened.

And slowly, through observation rather than revelation, they began to notice what the power had shown her.

Before deep ground shifted, a certain pale lichen darkened along its edges. Tiny cracks made a sound at night—not a crack, exactly, but a faint clicking, like cooled mineral settling in a pot. Roof birds avoided vents that had once warmed their nests. The roots of vapor reeds turned subtly in the direction of pressure. Burrowers brought up dust that smelled faintly sweet before it became poisonous.

None of this was new.

It had always been there.

The power had not inserted a message into the world. It had only made one person able, for one moment, to perceive the message that the world had already been sending.

Three weeks after the last eastern family moved their sleeping mats westward, the shelf rose.

It began before dawn.

A sound moved under the city—not loud, but immense. Every cup trembled on every shelf. Birds lifted in a dark flock. The long-legged browsers ran, awkward and silent, through the lanes. Then the eastern ground rose in a line, pushing up walls, splitting courtyards, breaking open old roads.

No one died there.

Several abandoned buildings collapsed. A storage shed split down its center. A wall containing the tooth of a beloved grandmother fell and had to be rebuilt elsewhere. Some precious things were lost. The city mourned them.

But no one died.

Afterward, people stood at the edge of the new ridge. It was higher than a person’s shoulder in places, warm to the touch, breathing faint mineral vapor from its seams. Plants would grow there soon. Different plants. New agreements would have to be made.

Seli came to the ridge with the other ground-readers.

Someone asked her how she had known.

She considered this for a long time.

Then she said, “The plain has always been speaking. We had become too good at surviving its voice to hear it.”

That saying traveled.

It became a lesson for children, though children made it irritatingly simple: *Listen before the crack.* It became a proverb among builders. It became a warning in marriage negotiations, market disputes, and political arguments: *Perhaps the ground is preparing to be elsewhere.*

And over generations, the people changed—not into a people without danger, nor a people spared labor. They still lost homes. They still suffered storms. Children still touched hot cracks when dared. Burrowers still undermined walls. Predators still learned bad habits. Old age still came with pale eyes and scarred hands.

But they became better at leaving before leaving became loss.

They built more empty margins into their yards. They planted warning lichens near foundations. They learned the habits of birds more closely. They set listening stones into public courtyards: stones chosen not for magic, but because they rang differently when pressure gathered below. Children were taught not merely which ground was safe, but how to recognize when safety was changing.

And the power watched.

It did not demand thanks.

It did not establish priests.

It did not punish doubt.

When people later argued about whether Seli had been blessed, or merely brilliant, or lucky, the power did not correct them. Each answer contained some truth, and none required obedience.

Once, years later, during a dust storm so thick that whole districts vanished behind brown air, Seli—very old then—sat in the doorway of her rebuilt home. A child sheltered beside her, frightened by the sound of grit against the roof.

“Will the storm stop?” the child asked.

“Yes,” Seli said.

“How do you know?”

“I do not.”

The child looked at her in alarm.

Seli laughed her soundless laugh and pulled the child closer beneath her veil.

“But I know where the walls are,” she said. “And I know who is behind them.”

That was what the benevolent power had wanted to preserve.

Not a world without hardship.

Not a people made small by rescue.

A people with one more true thing to hear beneath their feet.
