The power did not come down as a star.

It had learned, long before this mountain had learned to forget itself, that brightness is often mistaken for permission. A light entering a dark place believes it has revealed what was hidden. Often it has only made the hidden thing unable to continue in its own way.

So it came without changing the color of noon.

It entered the city by one of the upward canals, not as water but as the faint capacity of water to choose a lower place. It passed beneath bridges and through root-choked culverts. It moved in the clear damp inside the walls. It rose with the steam from the black cisterns and descended in the rain that never finished falling.

The stillfolk noticed.

Not all at once. They did not turn their heads. They had no need. In the Hall of Seven Arches, the seven upright absences grew less dark. At the market of drowned stalls, the merchant-shapes placed their translucent fruit and pulsing bricks beneath the water, as though covering them for night. In a hundred rooms a damp cloak stirred on its peg.

The power waited.

It did not ask the city what it was.

It did not ask the mountain to remember.

Those questions had sharp edges. The power knew that memory was not always healing. Some forgettings were wounds. Others were forms of survival so complete that to undo them would be a kind of murder.

Instead it listened.

For a long time there was only the water.

The permanent waterfall beside the Hall of Seven Arches spoke in its one unchanging shape. The rain threads trembled in their descent without reaching the paving. The canals climbed. The roots pressed patiently through the rooms. Far inside the stone, pressures passed from chamber to chamber, slow as thought in something that had never possessed a brain.

Beneath the noise, beneath even the great continuous pressure that had erased every lesser sound, the power found a small thing.

Not a voice.

A place where a voice might have been.

It was in all the doorways.

It was in the chairs facing windows, the cups of clear water, the cloaks woven from rain and root. It was in the careful arrangements the city made before a stillfolk began in a room. It was in the routes marked for solitary travelers. It was in the way the city distinguished no cleanly between arriving and leaving.

The mountain had not forgotten who it was built to shelter.

It had never known.

It had made shelter first.

Then, because shelter without someone sheltered is an unanswered question, it had made the stillfolk: not servants, not echoes, not prisoners, but the gentlest answer it could devise.

And the stillfolk, being made of welcome, had spent ages welcoming.

They welcomed water into walls.

They welcomed roots into stone.

They welcomed clouds into rooms.

They welcomed strangers, even those who could not hear the welcome and called the city empty.

They had never been welcomed themselves.

The power understood then what it would do.

Nothing grand.

Nothing that would make a story elsewhere.

It found, high in the inward-facing basin, one of the pale silver tree ferns. Its newest frond was still furled, a long wet fist held toward the false sky. The power touched it—not with a hand, but with the certainty that a hand may be received.

The frond opened.

Its leaves unfolded slowly, each one shining with water. They did not form a palm, as the other fronds did. They formed an ear.

At once the city changed.

Not visibly, at first.

The fixed waterfall did not alter its white folds. The upward canals still bore their petals toward the gardens. The rain did not reach the ground. But within the roar, a space appeared.

It was very small.

No larger than the pause between one heartbeat and the next—if there had been hearts there to measure it.

In that space, for the first time, a small sound survived.

A drop fell.

It fell from the lip of a carved gargoyle in a lower avenue, the one whose drop had hung suspended for so long that moss had grown in its reflection. It struck the paving.

The sound was almost nothing.

A soft tick.

But every stillfolk in the city heard it.

Figures appeared in balconies, market stalls, stairwells, garden courts. They stood beneath arches and behind water walls. Vast forms in the upper terraces lifted faces from among the tree ferns. Root-child forms unbraided themselves in cisterns. The silver creature beneath the warm pools stopped moving, and for an instant became simply a curve of light.

The city heard it too.

Stone does not startle as animals do. It does not gasp. But old walls gave off their warmth. The windows brightened. A thousand doors, including doors leading nowhere, opened inward by the width of a breath.

The power made no command.

It only left the space open.

At first the stillfolk did not know what to place inside it.

They had communicated by water, warmth, direction, arrangement. They knew how to indicate danger and hospitality. They knew how to make a room into an answer. But they had never been given a silence in which something could be said merely because it wished to be said.

One of the figures beneath the Hall of Seven Arches raised a hand.

The hand was not quite a hand. It was rain gathered around a dark shape, roots making fingers, mineral water reflecting the underside of leaves. It touched the stone beside it.

The stone softened.

And from the stone came a sound like a person exhaling after a long time underwater.

Another stillfolk answered from a balcony three districts away.

Not in words. Not yet.

It laid its wet palm on the rail, and the rail made a low tone. The tone traveled through the connected walls, through staircases and foundations and buried cisterns. It changed as it passed, gathered echoes from the roots and aqueducts, entered the interior basin, and returned as a warm vibration beneath every street.

The mountain had been making noise for ages.

Now it began to hear itself.

The power remained there for seven days, though days did not pass in the ordinary fashion. It taught nothing directly. It merely kept the small silence from closing beneath the water’s vast voice.

During that time, the city made several imperfect attempts at speech.

A plaza filled with rain that fell in a pattern no one could read, but the pattern returned each noon and became familiar.

A canal stopped flowing uphill for the length of three breaths, then resumed.

In an empty house, a chair scraped across the floor.

A root-child in a cistern said something in a sound like wet thread being drawn through cloth. Another root-child began to tremble. The traveler who witnessed this believed it was fear. The power knew it was laughter.

At the market of drowned stalls, the merchants began accepting exchanges again.

A traveler left behind a bell made in a far dry country. When they returned, the bell was gone. In its place stood a bowl of black water.

From it came the sound of the bell ringing underwater.

The traveler wept, though they did not know why. The nearest merchant-shape extended one wet hand through the hanging water wall and rested it, very lightly, on the traveler’s shoulder.

Then the power made its one true intervention.

It went to the highest place behind the mountain: beyond the false sky, beyond the rising waterfalls, to the inward horizon that had always shown green slopes no one could reach.

There it found the edge of the city’s imagination.

It was not a wall. It was a habit.

The city had imagined itself as a place that could contain everything: rain, heat, roots, stone, absence, returning travelers, its own endless effort to welcome. It had never imagined a thing might leave and remain loved. It had never imagined that an opening need not become a wound.

The power gave it that image.

Only that.

It showed the mountain a doorway with no room behind it.

Beyond the doorway: open cloudlands. Wind. Distance. A world in which footsteps made marks and then were washed away. A world of animals, whose lives made demands upon one another. A world where fruit fell, rotted, fed roots, and became something else. A world of endings, which were not failures of welcome.

The mountain recoiled.

Its walls tightened. Water rose in the canals. The high gardens bent inward. Across the upper basin, the vast stillfolk lifted their faces, and the dark hollows beneath their features filled with violent reflected rain.

The power did not force the image closer.

It waited beside the doorway.

At last one stillfolk came.

It was small. Perhaps it had begun only recently: a slight figure in a cloak of black leaves, standing beneath a narrow arch near the uppermost reservoir. Its eyes were closed. Its hands were folded carefully before it.

It stood at the threshold for a long time.

Behind it, the city made room.

Vines drew aside. The rain quieted around its shoulders. A warm line appeared on the paving, leading back through the city—not a command to return, only a route that would remain possible.

The stillfolk opened its eyes.

They reflected the open cloudlands.

Then it stepped through.

For one moment it had a shadow.

A true shadow, cast by a sun from somewhere beyond the mountain’s remembered noon.

It stood on nothing but cloud and did not fall.

The power had not made it into an animal. It had not given it hunger, death, or a history it had not chosen. It had merely allowed it to be outside the city without ceasing to be itself.

The stillfolk raised one hand.

Far below, in every district, hands rose in answer.

Then the first stillfolk walked into the cloud.

The city did not collapse.

This was important.

No avenue cracked. No waterfall stopped. No house became less itself because the being it had expected was no longer standing in its doorway. The empty room did not become empty in the old way. It became spacious.

After that, departures remained rare.

Many stillfolk had no desire for elsewhere. The city was not a prison to them; it was their native weather, their body, their language. Some continued to sit beside solitary travelers through the night. Some tended the routes of moisture. Some stood for centuries with palms against the walls, listening to the slow decisions of stone.

But now, in the upper city, there was a door.

Not always in the same place. Sometimes it appeared behind a curtain of roots. Sometimes at the end of a stairway that had previously returned to itself. Sometimes in the reflection beneath an upward-flowing canal. It opened only when a stillfolk approached it willingly.

Beyond it there were cloudlands.

There were birds, eventually.

Not brought in. Not released. One day a small brown bird, lost in weather, flew through the open door and into the upper basin. It circled once in confusion. The city held itself very still.

The bird landed on a silver tree fern.

Its weight bent the frond.

A fruit, ripened for an age without falling, dropped from a nearby branch and struck the ground.

This time the sound was louder.

A wet, soft impact.

The bird pecked at the broken fruit.

No harm came to it.

The stillfolk watched from the terraces and windows. They did not reach for it. They did not turn it into water or root or memory. They watched with the attention they had once given only to rain.

The bird sang.

The sound was thin against the enormous water, almost absurdly small. It should have vanished.

But the space the power had opened was still there.

The song survived.

It traveled through the streets.

It entered rooms that had waited longer than names. It passed over black paving and under hanging gardens. It reached the Hall of Seven Arches, where the seven absences listened. It went down into the canals, into the porous stone, into the deep chambers where the mountain’s old heat rose patiently through the walls.

The mountain remembered nothing.

It did not need to.

It learned, instead, something it had never known:

that shelter can be offered without being required;

that welcome is not possession;

that a door may open outward;

that even a permanent storm can make room for a song.
