The power noticed the basin because it was learning too quickly.

Not because quickness is a fault. Seeds hurry when the rain is right. Fire hurries through dry grass. A child hurries toward the edge of any newly opened door. But the basin’s learning had no elder rhythm around it. It had been taught by accident, by seepage, by the brief bodies of things that entered and were changed. It had learned tenderness from moisture and attention from frogs, but not yet proportion. Not yet the difference between welcoming a thing and remaking it because one cannot bear to let it go unknown.

The power knew this difference.

It did not descend through the clefts as a person might descend. It did not widen them, or send a voice, or make the limestone flower with signs. Those would have been answers in the language of conquest, and the basin had already had enough of being answered by whatever fell into it.

Instead, on a night when rain lay heavily in the roots overhead, the power placed one additional rule inside the place.

It put the rule nowhere visible.

Not in the square slab, because the slab was already a face and did not need another expression imposed upon it.

Not in the arches, because doors should remain uncertain until those who use them understand what a door is.

Not in the pool, because water had already been asked to remember too many things.

It placed the rule in the interval between a creature’s arrival and its alteration.

The rule was this:

**Nothing here shall be changed beyond its own ability to return.**

At first, nothing seemed different.

A pale leaf fell through a cleft, rocking slowly in the white light. It landed beside the thumb-sized pool. In the old manner, its green would have drained away; its veins would have filled with chalk; its edge would have gathered a mineral rim until it became a new kind of relic, neither leaf nor stone.

The basin noticed it.

The frogs turned their dark eyes toward it. The white insects altered their measured routes. Under the clay, tiny grains shifted toward the leaf, eager in the way the basin was eager: not hungry, but desirous of relation.

Then the new rule made a space.

The basin paused.

It was a very small pause. To an upper-world creature it would have been no longer than the beat between two drops of rain. But the basin had never paused before contact. It had only received and answered.

In that pause, the leaf remained a leaf.

The basin discovered that it could know something without taking it into itself.

By morning the leaf had changed a little. Its edges were pale, but not rigid. Fine chalk had settled in its veins, but the veins remained veins. If lifted by wind or carried away on an animal’s foot, it could still become soil in another place. It could still be lost. It could still belong to a history beyond the basin.

The power did not congratulate the place. Praise can make a young thing perform itself into danger.

It waited.

For many days, the basin tried the new rule in secret.

A moth entered a crack that formed only while it moved. The salamander beneath the rootlet waited with its impossible patience. In former times, after enough waiting, the moth would simply have become absent, leaving wing-dust shaped like a memory. This time the salamander waited until the moth shifted its wings once—twice—and then let it pass.

The basin shuddered with surprise.

The moth lived.

It did not remain wholly unchanged; nothing in the basin did. A faint rust-colored band appeared along the trailing edge of one wing. But it emerged later through an upper crevice and flew into dusk. There, beneath a fern root in the ordinary world, it rested with the basin’s pale dust still on it.

And for the first time, the basin felt a thing beyond itself continue.

This was difficult.

It made the three-toed walker restless.

Each night it rose from clay and quartz and braided rootlets. It circled the pool. It tested the walls with its blunt feet. But where it had formerly persuaded stone that it had crossed, now it met another possibility: that stone could remain stone, and that the walker could remain on its side of it, and that neither condition was a failure.

For several nights it pressed its face—if it had a face—against the far wall.

The wall did not open.

The walker did not vanish.

Instead, a fine line of dampness formed between its forehead and the raw limestone. In the morning, that line had become a seam. Not a passage. A seam: a place where two substances touched without becoming one another.

The basin learned the seam.

The wise power watched this with something that might have been relief.

Then it did one more thing.

It gave the square slab a second function.

Until then, the damp gathered in its circular hollow had been recognition alone. Any small life that touched the stone was entered into the basin’s attention. This was beautiful, in its way, but dangerous. To be wholly known by something young and immense was not always a gift.

So the power changed the channels by less than the width of a root hair.

Now, when a creature came to the slab and touched it, the water offered a choice.

Not in words. No frog heard a question. No insect paused to consider a thought. No bird lifted its furred head and weighed alternatives.

But each creature felt, in the deepest behavior of its body, the difference between being seen and being held.

Those that wished only to pass could touch the stone lightly. The basin would know that they had been there, but it would not follow them inward. It would let their outlines remain their own.

Those that wished to be altered could linger.

The beetles lingered often. They liked new mineral flecks and the small structural surprises that came with them. One blue-gray beetle acquired a translucent patch on its shell and spent the rest of its life reflecting the ceiling’s rootwork in miniature.

Some frogs lingered, though never all at once. One developed a mouth so small it could only be seen when it opened beneath water. Another became capable of recognizing rain before the drops began.

The furred-headed birds were cautious. They visited the slab in pairs, touching it first with one foot and then the other, retreating and returning. Eventually one of them chose to remain through a whole long condensation. Afterward its vestigial wings had not grown larger, but their hidden bones had become light enough that, in a sudden updraft through the clefts, it rose three handspans from the ground.

It was terribly frightened.

It was also pleased.

The basin learned pleasure was not the same as possession.

Beyond the new fissure, the second chamber continued to form. The power did not stop it. Growth was not the danger. The danger was that every new room might become another mouth, every new pool another pupil, every new opening another way for the basin to mistake contact for permission.

So the power gave the opening a counterpart.

At the far end of the second chamber, where daylight sloped down into the soil as though the earth had remembered the sky beneath itself, there appeared a way outward.

It was not large. It did not announce itself. A root had died and left a hollow. Rain enlarged the hollow. A small collapse made a tunnel no wider than a fox’s shoulder. At certain hours, wind from the upper world moved through it, carrying scents the basin had never known: crushed pine needles, fox musk, warm dust, distant water, the bitter green breath of leaves in sun.

The creatures discovered it gradually.

The insects found it first. Some crossed into the new chamber, then beyond it, climbing along the underside of a root toward ordinary soil. Many returned. Some did not.

The frogs went later, in wet weather. One amber frog vanished through the tunnel and did not return for three days. When it came back, it was carrying a grain of black loam on its head. The basin recognized the grain, and for an instant every white insect in the first chamber stopped walking.

The basin wanted to preserve it.

The new rule held.

The grain remained black loam.

The three-toed walker found the way last.

It stood before the tunnel through the entire cold portion of a day. Its many-jointed shadow passed over the walls though nothing crossed the light above. The basin felt itself gather around the walker—frogs, insects, salamanders, birds, roots, wet seams, the slow breath of the pool—wanting to know what it would do.

At length, it lowered itself.

Its shape had been assembled from the basin’s materials, but it had learned from the seam. It had learned from the leaf that could leave. It had learned from the moth that had not become dust. It had learned from the birds’ brief terror and pleasure in rising.

It put one three-toed foot into the tunnel.

Then another.

For a moment, the basin thought it was losing itself.

This was the first grief it had ever felt: not the grief of harm, but the grief of discovering that what one loves may move beyond the range of one’s making.

The wise power did not remove that grief.

It only made sure the basin could survive it.

The walker emerged above ground at twilight.

No one saw it clearly. A fox, drinking from a ditch, saw a many-limbed shadow move across the grass and fled. A child later found three blunt tracks in wet soil near a limestone outcrop, but by morning the tracks had softened into ordinary depressions filled with rain.

The walker did not go far.

It stood beneath a tree whose roots had once descended into the basin’s ceiling. Above it were leaves—thousands of them—each separate, each changing in the wind without becoming one another. The walker watched them until darkness came.

Then it returned underground.

But it returned differently.

It brought nothing back in its feet. No prey. No stone. No upper-world creature compelled into the bright country below.

It brought back distance.

And the basin, receiving distance, began to become wise in its own small way.

Years later—though time there was unreliable—the place beneath the limestone was larger. There were several chambers, each with its own weather of light. Some were narrow and wet, holding only moss and blind insects. Some opened into pale galleries where the furred-headed birds nested in colonies, their dry fingertip footfalls overlapping like a soft rain. One chamber had a pool deep enough to reflect the clefts above, and in that reflection the frogs sometimes seemed to sit beneath a second sky.

The little arches remained.

One day, after a season of heavy rain, mud on the smallest arch’s inner face was found marked with three tiny toes.

On the other side, something had begun to visit.

Not a captive. Not an offering. Not an extension of the basin’s body.

A visitor.

The basin noticed it. It did not immediately change the wall, or make a new crack, or draw its attention tight around the arrival.

It made room.

And somewhere beyond sight, the benevolent power, which had never needed worship and had not taken credit for any of this, withdrew its hand from the interval between arrival and alteration.

Not entirely.

Only enough to let the basin keep learning that a world becomes alive not when it can transform everything that enters it, but when it can welcome what remains other.
