The power did not arrive as light.

Light was already accounted for in the garden: sifted through vinework, held in copper, stored in black glass, released from the veins of certain leaves at dusk. A new light would have been an intrusion, and the garden had suffered enough intrusions to invent rules against them.

It did not arrive as rain, either.

Rain would have been kinder than many things, but kindness is not the same as wisdom. Rain would have filled the basins and loosened the pale dust in the wall seams. It would have drowned the warm-seam animals. It would have taught the bladder-vines to expect a gift the world outside might not continue to give. It would have entered the lower layers, where salt waited beside charcoal and old tools waited beside roots, and no one—not even the Below-Folk—could say what sleeping mechanism might mistake water for an invitation.

So the power did not send rain.

It watched first.

Not from above, because above was uncertain. Not from beyond the walls, because beyond was already crowded with possible worlds. It watched from the interval between one small act of care and the next.

It watched a child with a spoon for a name polish the rim of a clay socket until a lizard could pass without tearing its belly.

It watched the woman with roots in her fingers place her palm against a copper channel and wait for the warmth below to tell her where it hurt.

It watched an old man emerge from his clay vessel at dawn, carrying in both hands a dream he had not understood, and carefully pour that dream into a crack between two tiles because he believed the crack had been lonely.

It watched the Below-Folk handle the new things.

They did not seize them. They did not praise them too loudly. They did not ask at once what use they had.

A small door appeared: they circled it, marked the paving, and waited.

A copper helmet appeared: they gave it shade and let the white lizards decide.

A bowl of vapor appeared: they gave it space.

The power had seen empires that would have dismantled such a garden in a week to learn what lay under the first paving stone. It had seen merciful people destroy fragile things by insisting that every mystery must be brought into the open. It had seen cruel people call their hunger curiosity.

Here, it saw restraint.

Not perfect restraint. Nothing living possesses that. The Below-Folk sometimes grew impatient. Children sometimes pulled at forbidden things. A gardener once tried to force a flowering vine through a clay screen because the pattern would have been beautiful. The vine sickened, and the gardener sat beside it for eleven days, feeding it warmth from the cup of his hands.

But their errors became part of their knowledge.

The benevolent power found this more precious than innocence.

For a long time, it did nothing.

This was its first gift: it did not make itself the center of the garden’s story.

The garden went on growing downward. The well turned a finger-width at a time. Roots pressed into the foundation. Pale blocks shifted. The scenes in the walls changed: desert trees entered a region of blue dust; the sleeping animal opened one eye; the lamp-bearers passed beneath their black noon and became smaller, though whether because they were moving away or because the wall was learning distance, no one could tell.

Then, one dusk, the garden made a sound it had never made before.

It was not a crack, not a scrape, not the settling of dust. It was the sound of a great woven thing pulled gently taut.

Every animal became still.

The rope at the well turned half a finger-width.

Then another.

The dark wooden spindle, smooth from generations of palms, began to rotate without stopping.

The Below-Folk came quietly from their sleeping niches and work places. No one ran. Running would have shaken too much loose. They gathered at the edge of the central terrace with their tools in their hands, not as weapons but because people carry what is familiar when they are afraid.

The woman rooted to the copper channel whispered, “It is pulling.”

“Up or down?” asked the child called Spoon.

She closed her eyes.

“Neither,” she said. “It is being answered.”

The well rope descended into darkness. It had always descended, of course, but only as a fact of construction, a thing one could look at without understanding. Now it seemed to have weight. The spindle strained. Fibers tightened. Dust rose from the old wood.

At the bottom of the well, far below the lip of polished stone, something tapped once.

A pause.

Tapped twice.

The sound came from beneath the garden.

No one spoke.

The old man in the clay vessel began to weep, but gently, as if he had been waiting a long time for a person he loved to knock at a door.

The power understood then that the garden’s downward growth was not only hunger, not only necessity, not only the blind persistence of a young world making roots wherever roots could go.

Something was below.

Something had been below from the beginning.

Perhaps it had made the first invitation. Perhaps it had sent the old tools upward. Perhaps the roots were reaching not into earth but into another garden, an older one, or a broken one. Perhaps the scenes in the wall were not windows outward but memories of the place beneath.

The power knew many answers.

It did not choose one.

Instead it considered what help meant here.

It could seal the well. It could cut the rope with a thought and spare the Below-Folk whatever waited. But safety purchased by ignorance is not always safety; sometimes it is only a postponement that leaves people less ready when the door finally opens.

It could reveal the truth. It could show them the foundations, the descending roots, the thing that waited below. But a truth shown whole can become a burden too large for the hands that receive it. The Below-Folk were gardeners. They knew how to tend what they could touch. They had never asked to become keepers of the deep.

It could take the garden elsewhere—lift the basin gently from its uncertain world and set it beneath a real sky, in soil, beside water.

But that would not be rescue.

That would be replacement.

The garden was not failing. It was becoming.

And so the power did something small.

It placed no object in the garden.

It changed no wall.

It healed no wound directly, though there were wounds it could have healed.

Instead, during the next night, while all the Below-Folk slept uneasily and the rope continued its slow turning, the power made room for one new possibility in every living thing there.

Not knowledge.

Not courage exactly.

A capacity.

In the lizards, it placed the capacity to recognize a warmth that did not burn.

In the birds, the capacity to hear a note beneath the tapping.

In the beetles, the capacity to carry a dust that had not yet fallen.

In the bladder-vines, the capacity to store one breath of air from a world they had never touched.

In the plants that fed on heat, it placed a memory of coolness, so that if coolness came they would not mistake it for death.

In the old tools below the paving, it placed patience.

In the garden itself, it placed the faintest sense that it was not alone.

And in the Below-Folk, it placed a new kind of question.

Not *What is beneath us?*

They had asked that before, in secret and in dreams.

Not *How do we stop it?*

Fear could ask that without assistance.

The question the power gave them was this:

*What would it mean to meet what is below without abandoning what is here?*

By morning, the rope had stopped.

The tapping had stopped with it.

Nothing visible had changed.

This was how the power wished it.

A child woke first, in a hammock beneath the canopy. The child called Spoon sat up and found that the unfinished spoon-object beside their bed had changed. It was still wood, still roughly shaped, still marked by the places it had been dropped and repaired.

But in the hollow of its bowl lay a single grain of pale dust.

Not wall dust. Not kiln dust. Not salt.

It was darker than ash and lighter than seed.

Spoon carried it to the oldest gardener, who was neither the old man in the vessel nor the rooted woman, but a narrow person named Latch, who had once been given an iron hook and had spent sixty years learning what it was for.

Latch turned the grain between thumb and finger.

“Where did this come from?” Spoon asked.

Latch considered.

“Later,” they said.

“Is it dangerous?”

“Probably.”

“Should we bury it?”

“Not yet.”

“What should we do?”

Latch looked toward the well.

The morning light was cream-colored. It lay in shifting squares across the paving. A clay-colored bird stepped carefully onto the rim of the well and lowered its head, listening.

“We should build it somewhere to belong,” Latch said.

So they did.

Not below the paving.

Never below the paving.

They chose an upper terrace beside a blue plant that closed at anger and a thorn-vine trained safely around the well. They fitted together a small enclosure from ceramic scales, copper leaves, black glass, and one piece of charred bone. They left it open at the top. They gave it no name.

Inside, they placed the grain of dust.

Then they waited.

Days passed.

The grain did not sprout. It did not move. It did not speak in anyone’s dreams.

But animals began to visit it.

The long-legged birds stood near it in the late afternoon and made their single low notes. Moths folded themselves along the edge of its enclosure. A beetle brought it a flake of copper dust, then another. The blind warm-bodied creatures slept nearby, their ash-furred backs rising and falling.

At dusk, the grain gave off no light.

But the shadows around it grew more detailed.

Those who looked carefully could see, inside the smallest darkness cast by the enclosure, the outline of a descending stair.

Not a stair that anyone could walk.

Not yet.

The Below-Folk did not dig.

They did not ask the well what it was winding up.

They did not decide too quickly whether the grain was an object, an animal, a room, or a guest.

They made room around it. They altered their paths. They taught the children to step gently near the little enclosure. They brought it dust from the walls, not as an offering to a god, but as one brings compost to an unknown seed: with hope, caution, and no demand.

Far beyond the basin, or above it, or in the gentle interval between its layers, the benevolent power remained attentive.

It would not abandon them.

But it would not take over their tending.

It would send no flood. It would tear open no wall. It would not tell them the final shape of the garden, because the final shape of any living thing is too often another name for its death.

It would do what wise love does when confronted with a young world that has learned care under difficult conditions.

It would protect the possibility of its own becoming.

And when the thing below finally answered again—when the rope turned, when the roots found what they were seeking, when some old door in the foundation opened its patient mouth—the power would be there.

Not to choose for them.

Not to save them from every consequence.

But to ensure that, whatever came upward, the garden would still have enough warmth, enough room, enough tools, enough time, and enough unbroken hands to meet it.
