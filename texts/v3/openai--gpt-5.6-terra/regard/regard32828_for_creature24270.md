The wise power noticed the unfinished mountain first.

Not because it was unusual. There were many unfinished mountains in the world: folds still rising under old seabeds, volcanic islands lifting their wet shoulders from the sea, ranges being ground away even as they climbed. Creation was full of things that had not completed the sentence they had begun.

It noticed this one because it was trying.

Deep below the pale broken peaks, heat pressed against cold. Stone folded, resisted, fractured, fused. Mineral veins threaded themselves through dark host rock. Gases sought routes upward and found shafts, pipes, old boreholes, human cuts. The mountain was not alive in the way goats were alive, nor in the way a child holding her breath beside a bad vent was alive. But it had direction. It had consequence. It was becoming itself under tremendous difficulty.

And in its throat, things had made homes.

The wise power looked.

It saw lamps wired into walls that had once been magma.

It saw a woman on the Fifty-First Gallery wake before her alarm because she had dreamed of a change in the drafts. She rose without disturbing the two children in the bunk beside her, checked the curtain seams with the back of her hand, found the leak, and patched it using a strip cut from her own undershirt.

It saw an old draft listener sitting through an entire shift in a service alcove, holding threads of foil between two blackened fingers. No one spoke to him for hours. He listened to gas move through the walls and, near the end of his watch, sent a warning three galleries upward. The warning prevented a furnace flare. Nobody thanked him until the next day, when a cook slipped an extra egg into his ration bowl.

It saw a goat refusing a lower route.

Three handlers cursed it. One struck the floor near its hoof with a guide stick. The goat would not go. Finally a child—too young to have a shift assignment, old enough to know better than to interfere—noticed that its ears were angled not toward the blocked passage but toward the wall beside it.

The child put her cheek to the stone.

There was a sound inside it.

Not knocking. Not exactly.

A slow, strained clicking, as of teeth set against one another.

The bore crew evacuated the passage. Four hours later, a seam opened where they would have been. No one died. The goat received three handfuls of salt paste and became unbearable for a week.

The wise power saw such things and felt, if a power can be said to feel, a kind of grave admiration.

The people below did not know they were admirable.

They would have distrusted the word. Admiration sounded like a surface person standing somewhere safe, looking down. Admiration did not patch masks. It did not strengthen rails. It did not make a furnace behave or bring back the dead from an unmarked lower shift.

And so the power did not appear in light.

It did not split the mountain open and carry the settlement into a green valley.

It could have.

With a thought—not a thought, exactly, but the form taken by its attention—it could have stilled the tectonic pressures beneath the shaft. It could have brought water up through clean stone. It could have made soil in the cracks, called moss from spores sleeping in distant winds, put streams down the mountainsides. It could have given every inhabitant broad lungs and unbroken teeth, made the air generous, made the ladders less steep.

But it knew that gifts could be injuries when given without understanding.

A river in the Shaft would drown people before it became beautiful.

A sudden silence in the walls would terrify the hens, confuse the draft listeners, ruin the measurements by which a thousand lives had learned to endure. Soil would be a contamination before it was a garden. Trees would tear galleries apart with their roots. Wide lungs might be born into narrow masks. A rescue large enough to transform everything at once would not save a people; it would erase the skills by which they recognized one another.

The wise power had seen this happen elsewhere.

It had seen drought ended so abruptly that settlements washed away in floods.

It had seen tyrants removed by force only for the empty places beneath them to fill with hungrier tyrants.

It had seen grief prevented, and with it courage, memory, and the fierce strange tenderness that sometimes grows around what cannot be repaired.

So it did not do nothing.

Doing nothing was also a decision, and the wise power was not indifferent.

It began with the smallest mercies that did not demand belief.

On the upper galleries, during the narrow hours when slate daylight entered the cleft, it altered the angle of the falling light by less than the width of a finger. Not enough for anyone to call it a miracle. Enough that an old woman with failing sight could read the final figure on an air ledger before signing away a filter ration by mistake.

In the violet lamp quarter, it gave one particular species of spider an instinct to weave nearer hairline fractures in bulb housings. Repairers noticed the pattern after several months. They did not agree why it worked. One insisted it was static; another said the spiders liked warmth; a third said the bulbs had begun choosing their own keepers. But fewer lamps burst in workers’ faces.

In the lower decks, it made the oxen’s deep body heat carry a faint warning change before certain gas surges: not a smell, not a visible vapor, but a restlessness in the animals’ flanks that attentive handlers could recognize. The first person to understand was a stable worker called Pera, who had spent twelve years believing she was merely good at animals.

She was good at animals.

The power did not take that from her.

It let her be the first to know.

It placed no messages in the stone, because messages from powers become laws too easily, and laws become tools in the hands of people who like to stand above other people. Instead it made listening slightly more fruitful.

When the mountain shifted, the old draft listeners began, very gradually, to distinguish kinds of danger that had once seemed identical. One pressure pattern meant a collapse. Another meant a harmless settling. Another meant a warm vent would open soon enough to be useful if a crew got there first. The listeners argued. They made charts. They were mocked, then consulted, then regulated by the signal authority, then evaded the regulations with private codes tapped on pipework.

The wise power was pleased by this, though it did not make them right every time.

It never made them right every time.

Certainty would have made them careless.

The mountain itself it touched most carefully of all.

There was something under the settlement: not a creature waiting in the dark, not a god sleeping beneath another god’s notice, but an immense unfinished process. Heat and compression. Stone learning its final shape. A body without intention, perhaps, or an intention too slow to resemble thought.

The people called it the Maker.

The deep crews called it the Underpressure.

The power listened to it in the language of stress, density, fault, cooling, expansion. It did not command it to stop. The mountain had its own becoming, and the wise power did not believe that human need automatically made every other kind of existence an enemy.

But it made a bargain where bargains were possible.

Not with words.

It eased certain fault lines toward old empty galleries rather than occupied ones. It encouraged volatile gases toward vents that could be capped. It caused mineral-bearing seams to reveal themselves in places close enough to reach and far enough from nurseries. Once, when a collapse could not be avoided, it held a wall in the shape of a hand cupped around a single room for eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes was enough.

Inside the room were six people, two hens, a sleeping infant in a pressure cot, and an elderly man who had spent his youth repairing freight brakes. They did not know why the wall held. They did not know why the ceiling groaned and shed dust but did not come down.

The brake repairer knew only that he had eleven minutes.

He used them to dismantle the cot’s pressure seal, rig it into a breathing hood, and send the infant through a vent shaft barely wide enough for the child. Then he organized the others in a chain. The last woman out was the child’s mother. The last person alive in the room was the old man.

When the wall fell, it fell gently by mountain standards.

His body was never found.

Afterward, the mother petitioned to have his name added to the nursery’s air ledger. This was irregular. He had no relation to the child. The clerk refused at first. The mother argued for three hours, quietly and without wasted breath.

In the end the clerk added his name.

Years later, children born in that nursery learned to trace it with a gloved finger: a small line of letters between filter replacements and pressure readings.

They did not know who he had been. They knew he had given air.

That was sufficient.

The wise power watched all this.

Sometimes it wished it could be more obvious.

It wished it could sit beside the lonely people: the widow who kept setting out a second cup of warmed brine substitute despite knowing no one would drink it; the boy who had been assigned to a gallery where every sound was wrong; the woman whose hands had begun to tremble after a lower-deck accident and who could not bear to tell her shift lead. It wished it could tell them that nothing in their suffering was payment, lesson, test, or proof of a cosmic design.

It wished it could say: You are not being punished by the mountain.

You are not forgotten because nobody has come.

Your small love is not small.

But powers speaking directly have consequences. They become institutions. Their sentences are carved into plaques and used to decide who eats. Their comfort is turned into an argument against action: If the wise power loves us, then the broken lift will be repaired eventually. If the wise power sees, then no one else needs to.

So, often, it spoke through people instead.

Not by possessing them. Never that.

It gave an exhausted repairer the uncharacteristic impulse to check on her neighbor before sleeping. It made a middle-gallery clerk pause over a hazard assignment and notice that the same four people had been sent down too often. It warmed an old bolt in a forgotten tool tray so that a child, searching for scrap, picked it up and carried it home to tuck inside his father’s glove.

The father was on night shift.

His hands had begun to crack in the cold.

He did not know why the bolt was warm, only that his son had brought it to him solemnly, both hands wrapped around it.

“What is it for?” he asked.

“To keep,” said the child.

So he kept it.

That night, when a rail cage stalled between galleries and the draft turned vicious, he held the bolt inside his glove for almost an hour. It did not remain warm. It became only metal. But he remembered the child’s hands around it, and when panic began climbing his throat, he breathed slowly enough to help the others do the same.

This too was not nothing.

Eventually, word spread through the Shaft that the lower winds had changed.

Not dramatically. Not enough to shut down work. But the deep workers found that, on certain days, the warm gusts carried less silver dust. The draft listeners began reporting a new rhythm in the pressure shifts. Goats that had once balked at lower routes started choosing different paths, paths nobody had marked.

A surveyor followed one.

She was young, impatient, and determined to prove that animal lore was merely a collection of useful coincidences. She took instruments, rope, two lamps, a ferret in a shoulder pouch, and a permit she had obtained through a combination of correct paperwork and lying.

The route descended beyond the working lights.

It passed through a conduit where the rock was warm enough to fog her mask. Then through an old lava tube lined with black glass. Then through a chamber that did not appear on any city map.

There she found a wall covered in the pale mineral-eating film.

At first she assumed it was contamination. She raised her scraper.

The ferret hissed at her.

She lowered it.

The film was branching across the metal and stone in patterns she had seen before: not words, not symbols, but something like the maps drawn by heat. It had gathered along fault lines, pipe joints, fractures, old seams in old repairs. It was showing where the mountain was thin.

Not safe.

Not exactly.

But legible.

The surveyor returned with samples, measurements, and a story so strange that no one believed the story but everyone wanted the measurements.

This was how the Shaft began to change.

Not through deliverance.

Through knowledge.

The city developed new warning practices. Film-watchers joined the maintenance crews. The signal authority, after resisting for years, began to include animal behavior in its lower-level forecasts. Children learned the old breath lessons alongside new ones: how to read the spiders’ webs, the hens’ silences, the oxen’s shoulders, the faint branching crust on a heated baffle.

They learned, too, to ask a better question than *Is the mountain trying to kill us?*

The answer had never been simple.

Instead they asked: *What is changing? What can we hear? Where can we move? Who is missing? What can be carried?*

These were questions that made room for one another.

In time, someone proposed an impossible thing.

A garden.

The first proposal was laughed out of the middle-gallery council because there was no water, no soil, no sun worth naming, and no room that could be spared. The proposer, a lamp worker whose sister had died on the lower decks, responded by asking whether anyone had ever actually tried.

So they tried.

They did not receive a miracle in the shape of leaves.

They received condensation.

A little more than there had been before, gathering in a newly stable upper conduit where cold gas passed close to furnace exhaust. The amount was absurd: a few mouthfuls a day, then a cup, then enough to fill a sealed basin over a week. It was mineral-heavy, metallic, dangerous at first. It had to be filtered through layers of scavenged cloth, charcoal, bone ash, and a substance harvested from grease beetle tubs.

They found spores in an old shipment crate from the surface. They found more in the felt lining of a retired courier’s jacket. Most died.

A few did not.

The first thing to grow in the throat of the unfinished mountain was not grass. It was a low, stubborn gray-green lichen in a sealed maintenance niche under a violet lamp. It clung to a piece of porous slag. It did not soften the place. It did not smell like a forest. It was small enough to cover with two hands.

People came to see it.

They stood in line quietly, masks on, giving each other room.

Some laughed because it looked like a stain.

Some cried, which was awkward for everyone nearby.

Children were warned not to touch it, then caught touching it anyway.

The wise power watched them gather around that minute patch of life.

It did not claim credit.

It had done very little. It had made the condensation route a fraction more stable. It had guided a surface wind years before so that the spores settled where they might someday be found. It had not invented the lichen’s desire to live. It had not taught the lamp worker to persist. It had not made the people of the Shaft choose to spend scarce labor and rarer hope on something that could not pull a capstan, lay an egg, carry gas, eat moths, or warm a floor.

They had done that themselves.

And because they had done it themselves, it mattered in a way imposed abundance never could.

The mountain continued becoming.

It groaned. It cracked. It opened new vents and closed old ones. It took some people before their time. It gave others unexpected years. The Shaft remained a hard place, a dangerous place, a city whose addresses changed faster than its grief could settle.

The wise power did not make it fair.

It could not, perhaps—not all at once, not without breaking many things that were bound to one another.

But it refused to confuse difficulty with destiny.

Whenever cruelty hid behind necessity—when a supervisor sent workers into known danger to preserve a quota; when filter hoarders let neighbors cough so their own stores would grow; when the upper galleries claimed more air than they needed because they had better access to daylight—the power did not bless this as the natural order of a hard world.

It made records survive.

It made witnesses remember.

It made one overlooked clerk find a discrepancy, one child repeat something overheard, one goat refuse a route at the exact wrong moment for a lie to remain convenient.

It gave people chances to become responsible for each other.

Not guarantees.

Chances.

And far beneath the settlement, in the dark pressure where stone was not yet stone enough, the wise power kept one small vigil.

It listened to the Underpressure.

It listened to the people above it.

When the mountain shifted, and the hens went silent, and goats pressed their noses to the floor, and hands found railings and walls, the power did not demand worship.

It listened with them.

And sometimes, in the pause between one deep tectonic breath and the next, the mountain’s building sound changed almost imperceptibly.

Not into a voice.

Into room.
