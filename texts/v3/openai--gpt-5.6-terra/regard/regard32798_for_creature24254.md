The power does not mistake quiet for suffering.

It comes first as attention.

For a long while nothing in the hollow changes. The grey-yellow light lies on the banks. Water enters after rain and disappears. The shelter gives its small evening sounds. Beneath the clay, the pale bodies receive the pressure of weather through earth and root and old board.

The power learns the hollow in the manner the hollow itself would recognize: not by looking down upon it, not by naming its parts, but by remaining still enough to feel what passes through.

It feels the slender root of the thorn shrub tightening after a dry week. It feels the tiny intervals between the creatures’ breaths. It knows the warmth held in the flat stone, the old rope’s dampness, the pressure with which one body has, over years, steadied the shelter’s sunken corner. It knows that the dark seam is not a wound in the ground, and that no flower is missing from the bare clay.

There are powers that would see the hollow and hurry to correct it.

They would deepen the channel, clear the mud, mend the shelter with bright new timber. They would scatter seed. They would call birds, summon a green abundance, make the place visibly alive according to measures made elsewhere. They would open the banks to sun and wind. They would excavate gently, with concern, to discover what slept below.

This power does none of these things.

It understands that a kindness may be violent when it insists on being recognized as kindness.

So it leaves the plain plain. It leaves the hollow contained. It leaves the creatures their darkness, their slow weather, their few sufficient events.

But it does not leave them wholly undefended.

One year, at the end of a season when rain has come hard and often, a fracture begins high in the southern bank. It is deep beyond the surface. A great wet section of pale earth has loosened, and soon it will fall directly across the channel’s mouth. It would pack the passage below with heavy clay. The water would stand. The hollow would become too wet, then foul; the chambers would slowly lose their breathable spaces.

No thunder announces the power’s work.

During the night, a root under the bank grows a fraction of an inch farther than it otherwise would have grown. It finds a seam in the slipping earth. Around it, the clay holds.

At dawn, a thin slice of bank collapses—not into the channel, but away from it. The fall makes almost no sound. It leaves a new ledge, a small pale scar. Rainwater continues to enter and disappear. Beneath the ground, one of the curled bodies feels the altered pressure and shifts, slowly, toward warmth.

Another time, people come across the plain.

They are not cruel people. They are merely curious, which can be dangerous enough. They see the weathered banks and the silver shelter. One notices the dark seam. Another says there might be something living under there. They approach with boots that sink lightly in the crust.

The power does not strike them with fear. It does not make them ill. It does not turn their minds against curiosity.

It lets a small rain begin beyond the western horizon.

Not a storm—only a fine, persistent rain, enough to soften the ground and turn the air cool. The visitors stand beneath the leaning shelter for a while. They hear drops falling from its roof. One touches the old rope and finds it strangely cold. The place seems suddenly less like a discovery and more like a room in which someone, unknown and easily disturbed, is sleeping.

They leave.

Later, they will remember the hollow inaccurately. They will recall it as smaller, muddier, more ordinary than it was. They will not return with tools.

The power does not erase them from the world. It simply gives them the thought that is truer than possession: *This place is already occupied.*

In a drier year, when the thorn shrub loses more leaves than usual and the channel has not run for a long time, the power permits one cloud to cross the plain slowly. It does not break open over the fields. It releases its rain only after it has passed the hollow’s upper lip.

The water arrives in a quiet measure.

It darkens the grit. It gathers under roots. It enters the narrow channel, tea-colored and unhurried, and slips into the dark seam.

Below, the creatures do not know they have been spared anything. They receive water as they receive all things: through coolness, weight, smell, and the gentle rearrangement of their enclosed world.

This pleases the power.

Not because it wants gratitude. Gratitude would require a kind of distance, an awareness of giver and given, that would burden these beings with a question they have no need to ask.

The power prefers their untroubled continuation.

It blesses the shelter, though not by restoring it. The boards remain rough. The roof remains uneven. The low corner remains low. But termites do not find the one support that must endure. The rope does not rot through. The flat stone keeps its quiet store of warmth.

It blesses the shrub, though it does not make it flower. Its hooked branches remain thin and brown. Yet its roots go deep enough, and hold enough earth, and make enough shade over the inward-facing opening.

It blesses the channel by allowing it to remain shallow.

It blesses the mud by allowing it to close.

And to the pale things beneath the hollow it gives no revelation, no vision, no unbearable enlargement of mind. It gives them only the continuance of the world as they know it: pressure in the clay, rain above, warmth under stone, the recurring sound of water entering a chamber and being received.

For the power knows that not every life is waiting to become something else.

Some lives are complete in their dimness. Some places are sacred precisely because nothing grand occurs there. Their holiness is not in transformation but in the long, unadvertised success of remaining.

So the hollow endures.

Cloud passes over it. Warmth gathers and leaves. The grass stiffens, browns, returns. The shelter settles another hair’s breadth toward the ground. At night, a pale forepart appears at the channel mouth, then short limbs, then a patient weight moving over mud toward the old stone.

Above it, unseen and without demand, the wise power keeps watch.

Not as a keeper keeps a treasure.

As earth keeps a seed.
