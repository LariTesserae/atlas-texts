The power did not mistake silence for consent.

It did not descend at once with rain.

Rain would have seemed merciful to a younger god, or to a god accustomed to green things: a silver abundance poured over black leaves, water filling the dry gutters, softening the resin roads, running down the old mountain bones beneath the basin. There had once been water here. The channels remembered its shape. The cold tissues in the wells remembered, perhaps, the pressure of it against their skins.

But memory is not always a request.

Water, arriving all at once, would have been catastrophe. It would have entered the sealed works and swollen tissues designed never to swell. It would have awakened spores that had learned to sleep for a thousand years. It would have loosened the bark around iron bands, dissolved old salts into poisons, burst dry fermenters, drowned the deep furnaces, and made mud of the root pavement. The ancient construction would have sagged inward under its own newly softened weight. Its careful black economy of heat, pressure, fiber, and reuse would have become rot.

The power knew this.

It also did not make animals.

There was room, perhaps, in some abstract sense, for moths in the high crowns, for beetles under the warm bark, for small pale creatures to drink from condensate traps and carry pollen through the leaf roofs. But room was not invitation. The high country was not lonely in the way a village is lonely. It had no unfulfilled chamber shaped like a hand, no stalled process waiting for footsteps, no need for eyes to validate its work.

To populate it merely because emptiness troubled the visitor would have been vanity.

So the power remained above the basin for a long while.

Not above in the sky exactly. There was little sky available. It rested in the black air beyond the canopy, in the thin cold that the leaf plates shut out, and it attended to the place without demanding that the place attend back.

It learned the duration of the root engines.

It learned that a vibration traveling through one buttress could take seventeen years to alter the direction of a grove on the far ridge. It learned that the signal flowers did not blink randomly, nor merely mark pressure cycles. Their patterns were records. They repeated sequences from ages before the upper roofs had sealed over, before certain towers had been reduced to stumps, before the central furnace had absorbed its iron restraints into its bark.

The lights were not messages in any language of words.

They were continuance.

Here is the old heat.
Here is the old load.
Here is the wound that closed.
Here is the path that held.
Here is the darkness, answered.

The power watched the cold cushions in the deepest wells. It found that they did remember rain, though not as an image. They held, in their dull translucent fringes, old chemical arrangements: the long lost grammar of wetness, the capacity to receive dilute things without rupturing, to let substances pass gently from one body to another.

They had been diminishing for an age beyond counting.

Not because they were useless. Nothing here was useless. But because the basin had become too efficient at being what it was. Every inch of pressure, every breath of vapor, every dead leaf and failed beam had a destination. The cold tissues had no burden to bear, no heat to transfer, no structural failure to mend. Their inheritance had become unproductive.

And so it had almost been erased.

The benevolent power considered whether to preserve them.

Preservation can be another kind of conquest. A creature that is kept unchanged for the sake of another’s sentiment is not honored; it is displayed. The power did not wish to make a museum of the high country. It did not wish to pin the last memory of rain beneath glass.

So it waited for a question.

The question came slowly.

A root road near the central furnace shifted after a deep thermal cycle. Its movement pulled an old embedded pipe against a wall of bark. The pipe, weakened by acidic exhalations, split. From it emerged not water but a thin breath of mineral vapor, colder than the surrounding air. It drifted downward through a sequence of obsolete shafts and reached a black cushion in the depths.

The cushion trembled.

Above it, a signal flower opened out of time.

Then another did.

The pattern traveled across the basin—not quickly, but unmistakably—through crowns and ridges and furnace orchards. It was not alarm. It was not maintenance. It was a pattern that had not been displayed in the lights for perhaps ten thousand years.

The power understood only part of it.

That was enough.

It did not answer with speech. Speech would have been too narrow and too fast.

Instead it changed one condition.

Far above the black roofs, where the high air was thin and dry and cruelly open, it bent a passage through a distant layer of atmosphere. Not enough for a storm. Not enough to bring weather back. It invited, along a path no wider than a river, a cold current from elsewhere: air that had crossed a sea no map of this world still marked.

The current came once.

It passed over the basin at night.

It did not rain.

It carried only a little moisture, so little that no gutter filled and no cistern brightened. The broad dark leaves remained sealed. The thermal bladders did not rupture. The root pavement did not soften. No flood entered the wells.

But in certain hollows, on the shaded side of iron-banded bark, a pale film formed.

On the mouths of the deepest shafts, there was condensation.

Not drops falling. Not water running. Only a faint, temporary silvering on surfaces that had not been silvered in living memory.

The cold cushions received it.

For the first time in ages, their translucent fingernail growths unfolded farther than a breath. They did not become green. They did not flower. They did not remake the basin in the image of the lost world.

They merely persisted.

The vapor moved through them and was altered. It entered old pipes, old tissues, old sealed places. In the weeks that followed, some of the dead mechanisms far underground ceased corroding. A few closed seams became flexible rather than brittle. A forgotten vault opened a fraction and released a dry, colorless pollen that settled harmlessly into cracks.

Years later, a new kind of root began to appear around the cold wells.

It was not hungry for water. It did not spread freely into unproductive space. It was, like everything else there, practical. It made a soft lining around pressure conduits. It cooled certain chemical reactions. It gathered trace vapors and redistributed them before they could poison a chamber. In time, it became part of the old industrial logic.

The high country incorporated the gift.

It did not know that it had been given one.

The power was glad of that.

For a while longer, it remained near enough to watch. It saw the signal flowers alter their ancient sequences. Not to praise. Not to pray. Not even, perhaps, to record an event.

They had simply acquired one more condition to answer.

Here is the old heat.
Here is the old load.
Here is the wound that closed.
Here is the path that held.
Here is the darkness.
Here is the cold that came without destroying us.

Then the power withdrew its attention somewhat.

Not entirely. Benevolence need not be possession. Wisdom need not be absence.

It left the basin its black sky, its furnace heart, its labor without witnesses. It left it no citizens, no masters, no salvation shaped like a familiar world. It left the dry channels dry.

But once in a great while—so rarely that no cycle of the plants could depend upon it—a cold current crossed the upper air.

And deep beneath the roofs of black leaf and resin and bark, in the old wells where rain had nearly vanished even from matter’s memory, something faint and translucent continued to live.
