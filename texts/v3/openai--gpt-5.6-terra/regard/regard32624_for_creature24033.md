The benevolent power would not mistake hiddenness for a request to be found.

It would come first without entering.

For a long while it would attend to the hollow from beyond the ice, beyond the basalt, beyond even the slow migrations of pressure that made the chamber tremble. It would learn the city’s language, which was not a language of declarations. It would learn that the opening of a worm-crown after danger was a kind of consent; that a shrimp cloud parting around a warm stone was a map; that a crab returning night after night to the same crack was not merely habit but a claim made against the vastness.

It would see that this place had no king, no witness, no story about itself—and was not diminished by any of these absences.

It would see that the city was already receiving what it needed in the form proper to it: cold water, mineral seep, darkness, larvae, death, pressure, time. Its hardships were not errors in the design. The broad-headed fish beneath the basin’s rim was terrible, but it was not cruelty. The collapse of an old tube wall was loss, but it opened stone for settlement. Hunger made room. Decay distributed wealth. Even the ice above, immense and indifferent, gave the chamber its dim blue ceiling and protected it from storms that would have torn its soft architecture apart.

A less wise power might have wished to improve it.

It might have widened the mouths of the chamber so that more food entered. It might have warmed the water. It might have made light, thinking light a gift in every world. It might have stilled the currents so that towers could become ancient, symmetrical, secure. It might have driven out the hunter, strengthened the weak, made every larva settle, every egg hatch, every small bright body endure.

And the city would have died of those kindnesses.

The water, stilled, would have ceased to be road and memory. The buildings would have choked in their own abundance. The bacterial gardens would have altered. The worms would have starved. Light would have invited the wrong hungers. Warmth would have called in strangers whose bodies belonged to another ocean. No hunter would mean too many grazers; too many grazers would mean bare mats; bare mats would mean loose sediment; loose sediment would mean burial. To save every life separately would be to unmake the life they made together.

So the power would do almost nothing.

Almost.

At the narrowest mouth of the chamber, where a wandering tongue of ice sometimes ground against stone, it would place no barrier. A barrier would be too much. Instead it would incline one minute fracture in the basalt by less than the width of a shell’s growing edge. In certain seasons, when the pressure of the outer sea changed and the ice settled heavily overhead, the fracture would accept a little of the strain.

Not enough to prevent all tremors.

Not enough to preserve every tower.

Only enough that the chamber would not be sealed in a single unlucky century.

No creature there would know this.

The worms would withdraw when the chamber shook. The shrimp would vanish into seams. The sponges would close their countless channels. The fish would sink or hover motionless. Sediment would lift in a black veil. Then, as always, the water would settle.

But it would settle.

And the mouths would remain open.

The power would also touch nothing in the basin itself, except perhaps once, gently, when a new mineral vent prepared to open too violently beneath the youngest white district. It would not prevent the vent. That would deny the city its future. It would only let the heat rise through a broader lace of pores, so that the first blast came as a difficult season rather than an annihilation.

Some colonies would still bleach.

Some shrimp would die.

A shelf of sponge would collapse, and beneath it a narrow fish that had spent years in its shelter would be exposed and taken by the black fish below.

But afterward, the warm water would reach places it had not reached before. New bacterial films would form. Larvae of a kind never yet settled in the chamber would find purchase along the altered rim. A black, empty strip of floor would become white with beginnings.

This too, no creature would call a miracle.

That was part of the power’s wisdom.

It would not require gratitude from beings incapable of gratitude in its own shape. It would not insist on being known. It would not make a sign in the water. It would not give speech to the crabs, sight to the blind fish, or a revelation to the quiet cells in basalt seams. To give a creature an alien blessing simply because the giver valued it would be another form of conquest.

Instead, the power would value each thing according to the terms by which it already lived.

The clam’s inward garden.
The worm’s sudden retreat.
The sponge’s long patience.
The eel’s ability to read a corridor through pressure alone.
The larva’s terrible, beautiful uncertainty.
The hunter’s silence.
The dead body becoming, at once, a public square.

And perhaps the power would keep one particular mercy for the city’s darkness.

Not light.

Never light.

But an assurance, woven so deeply into the currents that it could not be distinguished from the sea: that darkness was not neglect.

That what had no witness was not unwitnessed.

That the small green pulse in a fish’s throat, the pale unfurling of a feeding crown, the crab’s patient work over a fallen shell—none of these occurred in a universe that had failed to notice them.

The city would go on without knowing this.

Its streets would crowd and clear. Its roofs would grow furred, split, and become foundations. The central basin would breathe up stone-broth and metal. Shrimp would gather in weather-like multitudes. The old would fall. The young would settle. A seal’s shadow would pass above the ice like a rumor from another world, and everything delicate would close itself into safety.

Then the shadow would pass.

One crown would emerge.

Then another.

The water would resume its old work among the terraces.

And the wise power, having done no more than preserve the possibility of this continuing, would remain where it had always been: not above the city like a ruler, not within it like an architect, but around it as the vast permission for its strange, crowded life to be.
