The power did not arrive as a light.

The underland already possessed light: gold strained through roots, green dusk held in leaf-fiber, the pale near-white glow where the roof wore thin. It had no need of another brightness, especially not one that would make shadows seem guilty.

It did not arrive as a voice, either.

There were voices enough, if one listened correctly: the click beneath the pools, the soft adjustment of a stem against another stem, the almost inaudible release of warm clay. A command would have been too large in such a place. It would have made the Unfinished turn their faces away.

So the wise power came as attention.

For a long while—long by the measure of worlds, short by the measure of the underland—it did nothing but perceive.

It perceived the shallow stone above, resting over the green country like a careful hand. It perceived the places where the hand had become too careful: roots held forever in their reaching, water held forever in its descent, bodies held forever at the threshold of age. It perceived the Unfinished sitting beside pools, their hands on their knees, not unhappy, not content in any simple way.

The power understood that no prison need have walls.

A cradle may become one if nothing is ever allowed to leave it.

But it also understood that not every stillness is a wound.

The underland was not crying out. It was not decaying beneath a false enchantment. Its people were not begging, secretly and collectively, to be remade into travelers, elders, ancestors, dust. The Unfinished had made ceremonies of nearness. They had learned the ethics of waiting. They knew things of thresholds that the swift world above had forgotten.

To break the place open would be cruelty disguised as rescue.

To leave it utterly untouched would be kindness only if permanence were truly what its inhabitants chose.

The power waited to discover whether choice existed there.

It found it in small things.

In an Unfinished who sat for years, perhaps, on the short stair ending in leaves. They had arranged a row of seeds upon the third step. Each was different: one striped and hard; one pale as an eyelid; one covered in fine, rust-colored hairs. Their fingers hovered above the topmost seed but did not touch it.

Below, in the pool, an eyeless frog clicked once.

The Unfinished turned their face toward the leaf curtain.

Nothing moved.

But beneath their translucent throat, the fine rootlike tracery brightened.

It was longing.

Not the sharp longing of hunger, nor the loud longing of despair. It was a small, almost shy pressure toward a thing not yet possible. The Unfinished did not know what it desired. It might have been a room beyond the leaves. It might have been the completion of the stair. It might have been simply the experience of placing a foot somewhere and finding that the ground did not already know them.

The power found this longing in others, too.

Not everywhere. Not in all.

One Unfinished loved the same root bridge so entirely that no farther country could have improved their life. Another had spent an age tending the ochre mound and would not have exchanged its unbroken skin for any revelation. Some were so deeply woven into their basins that to offer them departure would have been like asking a pool to become wind.

But there were a few who lingered at the places where the underland almost opened.

They looked upward when moths climbed toward the white roof.

They held the small bones brought from above in their palms.

They followed the root-colored insects when those creatures gathered along a moving border of shade.

They made stairs.

The wise power saw that the stairs were not failures.

They were questions.

So it answered—not with a door, because a door declares that one side is lesser and the other is freedom. Not with a rupture, because rupture has no patience for those who cannot survive it. Not with a decree that the underland must begin aging, must become historical, must surrender its eternal noon to a sequence of losses.

It made a single alteration to the law of the place.

It gave the underland an *if*.

Until then, every becoming there had been held at its threshold. A seedpod could split but never empty. A mound could rise but never open. A path could draw near or recede according to attention, but it could not truly lead out of what it was.

After the power’s touch, nothing changed at first.

The ochre mound remained ochre.

The droplets continued to wait beneath roots.

The old stone platforms stayed level, their mosaics of living green growing no older and no newer.

But in one basin, beside the stair that ended in leaves, the top step warmed.

The Unfinished who had arranged the seeds noticed it. They placed their bare foot upon the stone. The leaf curtain before them loosened—not entirely, only enough to reveal a narrow seam of brightness behind it.

The brightness was not sunlight.

It was time.

Not time as punishment. Not the time that eats fruit, breaks stone, hollows faces, and calls itself natural because it cannot imagine mercy. This was a gentler time: the possibility that one thing might become another because it wished to. The possibility that a held breath might be released without becoming a scream.

Beyond the leaves lay no grand landscape. No heaven. No kingdom of finished beings waiting to welcome the delayed.

There was only a small chamber of new clay.

Its walls were still warm. Roots had only begun to enter them. A single pool lay at its center, clear and unmarked by any offering. Above it, the roof was thick enough that the light was dimmer than anywhere else in the underland: not darkness, but something close to evening.

On the far side of the pool, a pale root descended from the ceiling and disappeared into the earth.

The Unfinished stood at the threshold for a very long time.

Then they did something none of the travelers who later told the tale could explain.

They took one seed from the third stair.

They laid it on their tongue.

They swallowed.

The leaf curtain closed behind them.

For the first time in the underland, one of the Unfinished was not immediately found again in another basin.

The others did not search.

They gathered at the stair, touched the warm stone, and sat together in the green-gold light. Some seemed troubled. Some seemed watchful. One pressed both hands to their chest, where the swallowed seed now existed elsewhere, inside someone they could no longer name according to place.

The wise power did not tell them what had happened.

Wisdom, it knew, was not the ownership of answers. It was knowing when an answer would steal the shape of a question.

Much later—though no one could say whether later meant an hour, a century, or the space between two falling drops—a sound came from beyond the leaf curtain.

A cry.

Not of pain.

Not quite.

It was thin, astonished, irregular. It rose and stopped and rose again.

The Unfinished around the stair became very still.

Then the oldest among them, whose hair had long ago braided itself around a fragment of carved stone, reached into a bowl at their side. They selected a seed, carried it to the niche in the clay wall, and placed it there.

Another Unfinished did the same.

Then another.

Soon the niche was full.

A stone platform says: here is a pool that has been greeted.

A niche full of seeds says: here is a wall that has listened.

And now, because of the power’s small mercy, the seeds said something else:

Here is a beginning that was allowed to choose itself.

The power did not make every path lead to that stair.

It did not turn the underland into a nursery for departures. It did not make yearning fashionable, or staying shameful. It preserved the old rules. Pale roots still must not be cut. Water still required acknowledgment. Leaf curtains still opened only for those who could wait. The chambers remained faithful to attention rather than measurement.

And it left the Unfinished unfinished, if that was what they remained.

But from then on, at rare intervals, a new kind of sign appeared.

A droplet that had waited for years would fall.

A seedpod would open all the way.

A clay wall would yield, not from force but from readiness, and reveal a passage into a chamber no map of distance could anticipate.

Those who wanted nothing beyond the warm eternal noon never had to take these passages. They could sit at their pools, tend their roots, greet the water, and live within the beautiful unending almost.

Those who wished—truly wished, with the quiet desire that does not mistake escape for freedom—could follow a moth whose wings carried no familiar map. They could find a stair that had acquired one more step. They could place a seed on their tongue and discover whether it became a root, a memory, a child, a name, an ending, or a road.

The benevolent power asked no payment.

It did not demand worship.

It left no symbol among the slabs.

In the underland, it became known only by an occasional phrase among those who had language enough to use it: the kindness that does not pull.

And above, in the broad plain where roots pressed against the undersides of stone, new shoots sometimes broke through the soil.

They were unlike the plants around them.

Their leaves were small, tender, and briefly silver in the morning. They opened toward the sun. They lived a season. They made seeds. They died.

Yet wherever one grew, the earth beneath it stayed warm.

And if a patient person knelt beside such a plant at noon, placed a small stone at its roots, and waited without trying to force the ground to answer, they might hear, far below, the precise ceremonial sound of a single drop falling into water.
