The power did not arrive as light.

Light, in that place, would have been an alarm. It would have entered every blind head as a wound. It would have made the transparent fish visible to things that should not see them. It would have opened the old carvings too quickly. It would have been, however kindly intended, an open eye.

So the power came as a correction of pressure.

For three long passages of the immense thing overhead, the water above the drowned court became very slightly easier to bear. Not warm, not cool; merely less burdened. A broad-backed swimmer, resting with its fans against the central bowl, lifted its head.

The cord-worms woke along the copper lines.

They did not hum their warning. Their little plated bodies made another pattern, one no creature there had felt before: a slow alternating pulse, so gentle that the shell-creatures opened wider rather than closing. The pulse ran from tie to tie, into pegs, through old copper repairs and green-black ropes, through the mineral coats on the stones. It reached every chamber that remained connected to the court.

In the upper masonry, the keeper paused.

Whatever hands or tissues or patient arrangements of stone it possessed, it had been working on a cracked ladder-rung. It left the rung unfinished. For the first time in many centuries, perhaps, it moved down from the cold walls and entered the basin.

The blind swimmers gathered without hurrying. The smallest animals gathered with them. Fish rested in the folds of their bodies. Crabs stacked along their backs and shoulders. The shell colonies opened and passed the changed water through their white chambers.

Nothing spoke. The court had no need of speech.

The wise power understood this. It did not ask what had happened to the builders. It did not demand an account of suffering. It did not call the court a ruin, because it had seen the labor in its repairs. It did not call the inhabitants primitive, because it had felt the exactness with which a wall-mouth preserved a seam, and the discipline with which a young swimmer learned to draw its eyes inward.

It did not mistake silence for emptiness.

Instead it listened.

It listened to the water entering through the drains: cold, deep, crowded with the almost-sounds of the listening animals below. It listened to the upper water, where the immense passage made its slow recurring alteration of weight. It listened through the cord-worms, who had long served as nerves without knowing that the court was a body. It listened through the old stone itself, which had retained the minute signatures of chisels, hands, impacts, repairs, and grief.

There had been builders.

They had not vanished suddenly. They had endured a change in the world: the roof of water sealing above them, the loss of air, the slow remaking of flesh and custom. Some had become the first blind swimmers. Some had become the keepers in the walls. Some had died and fed the depth. Some had perhaps gone elsewhere through the deep routes. Their work had continued because they had taught it to the water, and then to whatever could survive in water.

The power learned this without pity.

Pity would have been too small.

Then it found the danger.

Far below, beyond the drains and the listening animals, a fault was opening in the submerged mountain. Not soon by the measure of a fish. Not soon by the measure of a shell colony. But soon enough by the measure of stone.

The fault did not threaten to shatter the court. It threatened something worse: it would alter the deep routes. A slow new current would begin to climb. It would bring cold water upward through the drains, water from depths where the court’s small lives could not feed, where their shells would soften and their delicate mineral blooms would fail. The cord-worms would lose their copper holds. The warm hollows would empty. The broad-backed ones, if they survived, would depart through passages too narrow for their old bodies.

The court would remain.

But it would no longer be itself.

The benevolent power could have sealed the fault.

It could have placed a hand larger than the mountain across the wound in the deep earth. It could have fixed the water in permanent obedience. It could have made the court safe forever.

But wisdom is not the same as refusal to let anything change.

To seal the fault would redirect pressure elsewhere. It would burden another hidden chamber. It would perhaps crush the world of something no one had yet listened to. And to make the drowned court eternal, unchanged and inviolate, would be to turn a living compact into a monument. The power had seen too many mercies become prisons.

So it did not preserve the court exactly.

It gave it time.

For a while, the gray dimness from above descended more often. Each time it came, it carried no terror. The immense being overhead continued its distant motion, but the change in weight became regular, intelligible. A rhythm.

The cord-worms learned it first.

Their pulses answered the descending grayness. The answer traveled through the copper lines, and the keeper in the upper masonry began to alter the old structures. Not blindly, not by any command the power had spoken, but because possibilities had become perceptible.

Shutters were opened in corridors that had been sealed for ages.

Stone ladders were turned toward forgotten openings.

Copper ropes were loosened from walls that no longer needed holding and stretched, piece by piece, toward the lower chambers.

The broad-backed swimmers followed the new hum. They carried shell colonies in the folds of their skin. They carried pale fish under their fans. They pressed their heads to old walls, finding routes that had not been used since the builders had still possessed faces.

The power did not create these routes.

They had always been there: narrow ascending clefts in the mountain, passages leading sideways through warmer strata, chambers where mineral warmth rose from the rock and the water remained clean of green life. They had been abandoned because they were difficult, because they led away from the known basin, because every culture calls the passage into uncertainty by a name that means danger.

The power merely made the old court able to remember them.

It softened a few collapsed seams.

It quieted the largest listeners below, not by putting them to sleep or making them harmless, but by giving them other currents to follow—rich, strange streams moving through the newly changing deep. Their hunger turned aside. The old rule remained true: do not strike stone after the dark hour. But now there were fewer dark hours in which a careless tremor would summon disaster.

It strengthened no creature beyond its nature.

The shell-creatures still shut when disturbed. The fish still slept nearly motionless. The eels still disappeared sometimes into drains. The broad-backed swimmers still bore scars. The keeper still made mistakes: once it moved a peg too soon, and a whole rack of little cages rolled gently across a floor before being caught in a curtain of copper mesh.

But the court began, slowly, to move.

Not as a building moves. Not as a fleeing animal moves.

As a body changes position in sleep.

Over many cycles, its life extended beyond the basin. New shell colonies grew in the side chambers. Wall-mouths cleaned old seams. The cord-worms carried warning and invitation together. Young blind swimmers were brought to the newly opened passages, and their first lesson changed.

They were still taught to close their eyes.

But after that, the adults showed them how to place a fan against the wall and feel the faint returning pulse from somewhere beyond the court.

Not every pulse was danger.

Some were other rooms.

Some were water moving toward warmth.

Some were the remembered touch of hands that had made a path and trusted, without knowing who would come after them, that someone might need it.

When the fault finally opened fully in the deep, the cold current rose through the old drains as it had always intended to do.

It found the drains clear.

It found the lower chambers empty of those that could not endure it.

It found the central basin changed: not abandoned, but made sparse and quiet, a nursery for creatures suited to the cooler water, a place where the oldest shell colonies had become thick white vaults around the seams. The broad-backed swimmers visited it sometimes. They rested against its walls. The keeper continued its repairs there, though less often.

The drowned high place did not die.

It became an old chamber in a larger dwelling.

And the benevolent power withdrew without announcing itself.

It left no shrine, no mark, no open-eyed face in the stone. It asked for no gratitude. It did not even leave certainty. In later generations, if generations could be counted there, the inhabitants would only know that the copper cords had once begun to sing a new pattern; that forgotten doors had opened; that the deep cold had come and, somehow, they had not been destroyed.

Far above, the immense presence passed again.

The grayness descended.

Fish turned toward it. Crabs withdrew. The cords trembled. The blind swimmers pressed themselves to the walls.

But now, from beyond the old basin—through corridors once closed, through shell cathedrals and warm stone throats—other answers returned.

The court held its breath.

And felt the greater water breathing with it.
