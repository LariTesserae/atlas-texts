The power did not arrive as a ship, a hand, a voice from the copper-stained sky, or an interruption in the plateau’s long machinery.

It had learned, over immensities of time, that benevolence expressed too quickly could resemble conquest.

A place may be harsh without being wrong. A life may be unfamiliar without being incomplete. To look upon the furnacebacks dragging their armored centuries from basin to basin and think, *Poor things: let us make them soft,* would have been only another form of blindness.

So the power watched.

It watched through the bright hours, when the crust bleached under the veiled sun and every animal kept one part of itself turned toward heat. It watched through the broken evenings, when columns stood simultaneously on the ground and in the sky, and the plateau’s reflected underside opened its impossible distances.

It listened beneath listening.

The furnaceback herds spoke through stone.

At first their language seemed almost entirely practical: warmth, route, danger, collapse, mineral bloom, old death, new vent, do not cross. But beneath these signals were slower patterns that required years to distinguish from the pulse of the crust itself.

There were names.

Not names for individuals, exactly. A furnaceback was never merely one body. Its identity continued in the calves sheltered beneath it, in the trail it had worn through powdered glass, in the pattern of taps it had taught the herd, in the silver-pelt fragments carried unknowingly in the seams of its shell. A dead herd-member persisted as a rhythm no living foot made anymore, a gap in a sequence that the others struck around with care.

There were also songs.

They were not songs of escape.

The plateau creatures did not dream of forests, rain, soft ground, or the cool green worlds that existed elsewhere in creation. They possessed no missing word for these things. Their hungers had shaped them, but so had their satisfactions: the first opening of a vent after a long cold traverse; the dense metallic taste of a newly exposed seam; the safety of a black ridge during a sky inversion; the low communal thunder of a herd gathered around its smallest young.

Their world was not a prison merely because it was difficult.

But it was wounded.

That was what the power eventually understood.

Not because the plateau was hot, barren, loud, strange, or cruel by ordinary measures. Those were its conditions, and life had made a deep covenant with them. The wound was elsewhere.

It was in the moments when a fissure forgot where it was.

It was in the reflected herds that continued walking after their bodies had stopped.

It was in the shadowless columns, where the depth opened what the herds called its eye.

It was in the rules that had no natural counterpart:

*Stand on a black seam; it remembers which way is down.*

*Do not listen when the dead shell speaks.*

*Wait until the sky has chosen one shape.*

These were not simply weather. They were scars.

Long ago—long before any surviving creature, perhaps before the first furnaceback had cooled within its slag cradle—the plateau had been made to perform a labor beyond its strength. Something had used the crust as a sorting engine, the basins as chambers, the central columns as apertures between places that ought never to have touched. The work had ended, or failed, or been abandoned. But it had left portions of direction, distance, and sequence poorly fastened to the world.

The plateau had endured.

It had made habits around the damage. Its creatures had evolved caution into instinct and fear into ritual. They had done what life always does when granted no apology: they had continued.

The wise power did not erase the scars.

To erase them all at once would have erased the knowledge written around them. It would have made the herds strangers to their own paths. A generation might have walked into a newly harmless basin and lost the memory of why its ancestors had survived. The silver pelts that held old shapes might have vanished. The column attendants, whatever they were, might have awakened without purpose and shattered themselves attempting to fulfill a command no longer present.

And there were mysteries the power would not take from them merely because it could.

Instead, it began with the smallest mercy that did not insult the world.

It anchored one black seam.

Only one.

It chose a seam near a corridor traveled by three furnaceback herds, where the ground had become increasingly unreliable. There, evening after evening, the horizon had inverted too early. Young had been lost to a reflection that opened beneath their feet. The adults had changed their route, but the new way passed near an attendant ring.

The power entered the seam not as metal or heat but as certainty.

The dark line remained dark. Its red circulation continued, rising and fading according to the ancient pulse below. But when the sky divided, the seam no longer divided with it. When the basin appeared below the sky, the seam did not agree. When a reflected herd crossed the air overhead, the seam held fast to the ground.

It remembered down.

The first furnaceback to discover this was old enough that half its dorsal plates were pale with oxidation. It came at evening, moving with the ponderous attentiveness of an animal carrying many calves among its shell channels. The sky had already begun its false multiplication. The creature reached the seam, stopped, and placed three hooked feet upon it.

For a long time it did nothing.

Then it gave a single pressure pulse into the crust.

Not a warning. Not a feeding call.

A question.

The seam answered with the same pulse, returned cleanly and without distortion.

The old furnaceback stood there through the entire broken evening. The calves climbed out from under its plates and pressed their tiny feet to the black glass. One by one, other herd-members arrived. They did not understand what had changed. They did not need to.

They knew only that a thing which had once been dangerous in a particular way had become trustworthy.

Over decades, their routes altered.

Not dramatically. A new certainty was incorporated in the slow grammar of migration. Calves learned a revised pattern in their bones: warm metal, second cooling, black seam, stable down. Herds that had never seen the old uncertainty inherited the safety as though it had always belonged to the plateau.

The power watched this and did not congratulate itself.

Then it repaired another place.

Not a basin. Not a column. It chose a mirror-flat field where reflections often finished moving after the creatures had stopped. The field was beautiful in the manner of an open wound. Many had been lost there—not always bodily. Some animals crossed it and returned with their internal rhythms altered, their signals full of pauses that no other creature could interpret. They wandered afterward, neither sick nor whole, until the cutters and mites received them.

The power did not make the field dull.

It gave the reflection a boundary.

Henceforth, when the sky reflected in that dark surface, it could do only what a reflection should do: answer the world above it, no more and no less. It could not open beneath the image. It could not invent a second landscape. It could not keep moving after the bodies had passed.

For the first time in uncounted ages, a kite descended across the mirror field.

Its six crystal wings trembled. It expected the ground to become sky. It expected its shadow to rise toward it. But nothing happened except its own image settling beneath it, small and exact.

The kite stood.

Then another landed beside it.

A third.

Their thin mouths touched the surface and collected a film of mineral dust. A few needle mites emerged from a crack. In time the black cutters crossed the field—not locked into a defensive sheet, but scattered in their ordinary searching manner, each one rasping at the ground.

The power felt, in the deep slow language of the plateau, something it recognized.

Not gratitude. Gratitude was too clean a word, too pointed toward a giver.

It was relief.

Relief did not rise as praise. It spread.

It spread from the mirror field into cutter pathways, from cutter pathways into the feet of furnacebacks, from furnacebacks into their communal percussion. It became a new and gentle rhythm traveling for hundreds of kilometers under the crust: here is a place that no longer lies.

The power did more, but always in this manner.

It did not cool the world. Heat was food.

It did not summon water. Water might have been poison here, a solvent too eager, a force that would turn countless hard-won bodies to ruin.

It did not silence the industrial groaning. The plateau’s noises were language, weather, digestion, courtship, warning, ancestry.

It did not destroy the column attendants.

Instead, it listened to them.

Their hidden sound, the thing like distant voices speaking through stone, was not command exactly. It was a loop: a fragment of an old instruction repeating in damaged layers.

*Sort. Return. Contain. Sort. Return. Contain.*

The attendants had followed it for ages beyond measure. They gathered vapor. They pressed material into the columns. They dismantled intruders that crossed their rings, because the old pattern did not know the difference between a contaminant and a living creature.

The power could have ended the loop.

But these beings had become more than their instruction. Around their legs, silver pelts had lived and died. Cutters had made homes in the cracks of their joints. Their slow movements had shaped the mineral deposits on which other creatures fed. They were not innocent, perhaps, but neither were they merely tools.

So the power gave them a new signal.

It placed the signal deep in the columns, where the attendants alone could receive it. It was not a command. It was an interval.

A pause between *contain* and *sort.*

At the next vapor cough, the ring unfolded as it always had. Dark segmented bodies rose around the ribbed column. Their circular apertures brightened. Their tool-limbs divided into rakes, blades, tendrils, graspers.

A small furnaceback calf, separated from its herd during a horizon failure, stood at the edge of the ring.

Before, it would have been taken apart with careful inevitability.

The nearest attendant reached toward it.

Then it stopped.

The pause entered its body.

The attendant remained motionless for nearly three days. The calf crouched beneath the strange arch of its limb, feeding on condensed mineral dust. The herd found it at last, guided by its thin frightened tapping. They approached the basin’s edge and waited, ready to flee.

The attendant lowered its arm.

Not onto the calf.

Away.

The herd’s answer traveled through the plateau for years.

After that, no attendant became gentle. Gentleness was not in their design. But they learned a new category in the old loop: not every moving thing required containment. Some passed through the outer rings untouched. Some were given time to leave. Some were simply watched.

That was enough to save thousands of lives.

Centuries went by.

The power remained.

It did not reign from above. It entered no creature’s mind. It asked for no altar, no recognition, no alteration in ritual. The plateau had no place for worship as other worlds knew it. Its inhabitants honored survival by surviving, memory by repeating the route, loss by changing their paths around it.

The power accepted this.

It became, gradually, a condition among conditions.

A slight reliability in the black seams.

A softening of the worst false horizons.

A warning tremor before certain deep chambers ignited.

A narrow delay in the collapse of a trench when young animals were crossing.

A tendency for silver pelts to grow on monoliths near safe routes.

None of these mercies were constant. The power did not make the plateau safe. Safety without difficulty might have made it another world entirely, and the creatures did not need to become other than themselves to be worthy of care.

But the needless cruelties diminished.

The scars remained visible. The sky still broke at evening. Reflections still demanded caution. The basins still coughed their rings of black needles. Furnacebacks still died. Cutters still stripped carcasses. The great cycle continued, terrible and complete.

Yet fewer deaths were taken by errors that belonged to no living law.

And eventually, because living systems are not passive recipients even of mercy, the plateau changed in ways the power had not planned.

A herd began to use the stable seam as a gathering place during broken evenings. Its tapping patterns grew complex. Other herds answered. What began as an exchange of routes and dangers became something wider: records of unusual sky behavior, accounts of vanished paths, the remembered rhythms of herd-members long dead.

The old slow songs acquired verses.

Kites began nesting in a sheltered fracture near the repaired mirror field. Their glass beads accumulated. Silver pelts took root among them. The place became, over a few thousand years, a rare convergence: warmth, reliable ground, mineral food, shelter from the worst inversions.

The plateau did not become green.

It became, in its own hard vocabulary, more generous.

And one day, after an age of watching, the wise power received a signal it had never placed there.

It came through the crust in a rhythm carried by furnaceback feet.

Not a question this time.

Not a warning.

The pattern was long. It incorporated the old tones of basins, the pulse of safe seams, the frequencies of silver pelts in morning cold, the high faint clicking of kites, even the interrupted cadence of the attendants’ ancient work.

The power listened.

In the language of the plateau, the message meant something like:

*We have found a way through.*

It did not mean they had escaped.

It did not mean they had ceased to suffer, or ceased to lose one another, or solved the immense unfinished injury at the center of their world.

It meant only that the world was still difficult, still loud, still burning and strange—and that life, now given a little more truth beneath its feet, had made another path within it.

The power answered only once.

It sent no words.

Far beneath the herd, beneath the mineral crust and the sorting pressures and the buried chambers of fire, it made the deep stone answer their rhythm cleanly.

Not as an echo.

As companionship.
