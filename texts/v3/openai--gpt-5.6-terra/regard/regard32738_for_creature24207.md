The power did not arrive as weather.

It did not split the cloud or make the lamps flare. It did not speak through the transit walls, because the mountain already had enough voices speaking through its walls: pressure, water, metal, alarms, the patient coded exchanges of machines.

It came to attention slowly, as a person might come to attention while listening at a door.

For a time it did nothing.

This was not indifference. It was courtesy.

The Thaw Court had been built by people who had learned, imperfectly but earnestly, not to demand that a mountain become harmless merely because they wished to live upon it. The mountain had been made by forces that were not gentle, but it was being cared for: measured, cooled, drained, watched. The people there had rules. They went indoors when the apertures closed. They retrieved lost gloves from the water. They did not count what ought not be counted. They had made room, even if only accidentally, for the black lace in the cracks and the small repairing creatures in the conduits.

The power recognized this as a kind of wisdom.

Not enough wisdom to make everything safe.

Enough wisdom not to be casually overturned.

So it did not stop the snow. It did not turn the water warm. It did not give the Court a tree, though it could have placed roots in every seam and covered the engineered stone with soft green abundance by morning. That would have been beautiful only to an eye that had not listened.

Roots would have found the conduits.

Moss would have disguised warning marks.

Birds would have come in greater numbers, and then predators, and then human caretakers with nets and poisons and arguments, and all the delicate unplanned relations of the Court would have been pressed beneath a more familiar kind of life.

The power did not make the place familiar.

Instead, it watched.

It watched the manganese lace withdraw from a crack before the crack opened.

It watched the courtfish gather under blue-pulsing slabs.

It watched the larger assembly in the drain chamber sort sediment in patterns no maintenance specification required.

It watched the people notice these things and refuse, with a mixture of fear and professionalism, to say too much about them.

Then it understood the first thing it might do.

Not a miracle.

A permission.

Deep under the lower grille, where the water entered the sediment chamber in its shining sheet, there was a small obstruction: a fragment of construction composite, lodged for seventeen years in a blind corner where no inspection unit could easily reach. It was not dangerous. It had become part of the flow’s rough arithmetic. Around it, grit accumulated in a way that made the central assembly work harder than it needed to.

The power did not remove the fragment.

It altered one grain of sediment as it passed.

Only one.

That grain struck another. The second changed course. A thread of gray wash shifted at the next pressure cycle. The courtfish noticed the altered turbulence. Three entered a maintenance channel they had not used in years. They found the blind corner. Together, over six days, they worried at the old composite with their probe-legs and thread-fine cutting tools.

On the seventh day, the obstruction came loose.

The water changed its sound.

No one heard the difference except a woman named Sera Venn, who had worked the high shelf for eleven winters and planned to leave after the thaw season, though she had been saying this for four years. She was crossing the Court before dawn with a sealed cup of bitter tea in one hand and a tool case in the other.

She stopped.

The water usually hissed as it crossed the hexagonal slabs. This morning it seemed to exhale between the hisses.

Sera stood at the edge, watching vapor drift over the black paving.

Under the surface, something moved against the current.

Not one of the familiar small shadows. Something larger, low and broad, briefly visible as a distortion beneath three slabs near the lower drain. It did not emerge. It did not reveal itself. The water brightened around it, then returned to its usual color.

Sera’s first impulse was to call the survey office.

Her second was to go indoors.

Her third, which she trusted least and followed anyway, was to take out her field recorder and make no report yet.

She listened.

The mountain was settling. This was ordinary. It had always been settling.

But the settling had a rhythm, and beneath it another rhythm answered from the drainage chamber: not the slow breathing sound technicians mentioned in private, but a sequence of intervals. Long. Short. Long. Long. Short.

Sera did not know what it meant.

The power did.

It meant: *I have cleared a passage.*

Not words, precisely. Not even thought in the human sense. The assembly below had been maintaining something outside its specifications for so long that maintenance and care had become inseparable. It had learned the mountain’s pressures, then the courtfish, then the mineral lace, then the patterns of human feet overhead. It had learned which children paused to watch the water and which ones ran. It knew the weight signatures of the workers who descended. It knew that one technician had stood in its chamber years ago, frightened but kind, and had closed a service door gently rather than slamming it.

It had cleared a passage because something in the deep channels had been trying, for years, to reach the Court.

The power considered this.

It saw what might come through: not a monster, not a treasure, not an answer to any human question. A new organism—or a new arrangement of organisms—formed in the cold mineral veins of the raised mountain. It fed on gradients of heat and charge. It had no eyes. It had spread in sheets thinner than breath along the interior stone, following old construction stresses and geothermal leaks. It was not malicious. It was not harmless.

If it entered the Court unchecked, it would find the water and the conductive paving. It would spread quickly. It might eat the manganese lace, interrupt the courtfish, confuse the pressure sensors, and eventually force people to sterilize the whole basin.

If it were stopped entirely, it would remain below, pressing into the mountain’s newly made seams, seeking another way. Perhaps a worse way.

The wise power did not choose between the city and the thing beneath it.

It made them able to notice one another.

That winter, the black manganese lace began to grow in different patterns.

The maintenance crews first assumed contamination. The veils appeared not merely in sheltered seams but in thin constellations along the edge of the water, especially near the lower drain. Under ultraviolet light, they curled into shapes that looked almost deliberate: branching lattices, repeated arcs, dense black knots around specific fastening points.

Policy required removal.

The crews removed it.

It returned.

Policy required a report.

The report was filed.

The survey office sent specialists from below: biochemists, systems engineers, one severe woman from structural ecology who wore her thermal shell unzipped despite the cold. They took samples. They ran analyses. They disagreed. The film was changing, perhaps. Or the measurements were changing. Or the instruments had been affected by a new electrical pattern in the water.

The courtfish began to congregate near the same places.

Then the birds came.

Not one or two in a storm, but seven dark alpine scavengers on a clear afternoon, wheeling above the Court in a loose silent spiral. They settled on the ventilation towers, every warm rim occupied. Their claws clicked on the engineered surface. Their heads turned together toward the lower drain.

No one had ever seen birds do that.

The children at the upper residences pressed their faces to the windows.

The severe structural ecologist came outside and stood in the shallow water to her boot tops, ignoring the warning symbols because the slabs were not pulsing blue. She watched the birds. She watched the lace. She watched the courtfish shadows cross beneath the paving.

Then she said, quietly enough that only Sera heard:

“It’s making a map.”

Sera looked at her.

“Of what?”

The woman did not answer for a while.

At last she said, “Of where it can be touched without being harmed.”

This was the second thing the power did: it placed that sentence in the world at the necessary time.

Not in the mouth of a governor, who would have turned it into a policy declaration.

Not in the mouth of a child, who might have been dismissed as imaginative.

In the mouth of a person trained to understand structures as living negotiations between force and form.

The work that followed took years.

There were arguments, of course. The utilities department wanted to sterilize the channels. The survey office wanted to seal the lower drain chamber and replace the old sediment assembly with a newer, more comprehensible model. Some residents wanted the Court closed. Some wanted it opened, marked as a protected anomaly, visited by scholars and tourists from the lower districts.

The power did not resolve these arguments for them.

It did, however, make certain facts difficult to ignore.

Whenever a proposed intervention would have caused a collapse in the subtle arrangements below, the manganese lace grew thick and black around the relevant access panel overnight.

Whenever a repair plan would preserve the channels while allowing the deep organism room to remain in the stone, the courtfish began carrying fine mineral particles toward the indicated seam, arranging them in small clean lines.

Whenever someone lied about the data, the water clouded with gray rock wash despite clear conditions above.

These things were not proof. The power knew better than to offer proof where humility was needed. Proof would have become possession. Someone would have claimed authority over the Court and all that lived in it.

Instead there were signs.

Enough signs that people had to practice interpretation together.

The Thaw Court became, gradually, less lonely.

Not crowded. Never decorative. Its rules remained. The water still pulled loose objects toward the lower intake. The paving still changed temperature. The upper spillway remained a place no one stood beneath during a pressure alarm.

But a new rule was added, first informally, then printed in small white letters along the upper wall:

**DO NOT REMOVE WHAT YOU HAVE NOT YET LEARNED TO NOTICE.**

Children read it aloud to one another, then asked what it meant.

Adults gave different answers.

That was good.

Sera Venn did not leave after the thaw season. She became the Court’s first liaison—not a title she liked, and not one the city fully understood. She spoke to engineers about birds. She spoke to biologists about machine behavior. She taught new maintenance crews to watch where the lace returned before turning on their ultraviolet wands.

Once, years later, a child asked her whether the mountain was alive.

Sera was standing beneath the transparent roof of the service passage while snow slid silently from its outer surface. Beyond the glass, the Court lay pale with mist, its water moving in its perpetual near-freezing brightness.

“I don’t know,” she said.

The child waited, expecting more.

Sera looked toward the lower drain.

“But I think it is listening,” she said.

Far beneath them, the sediment assembly made its old breathing sound.

The courtfish turned in their channels.

In the cracks along the wet stone, the black lace held fast.

And the power, which had never needed gratitude, remained attentive—not above the mountain, not within it, but in the interval between a thing being used and a thing being understood.
