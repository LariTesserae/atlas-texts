The power did not arrive from above.

Above was only the thinner water, the pale dilution of storm, and beyond that whatever cold machinery of height made the sea continue to fall without ever finding a surface. A power wise enough to be benevolent did not mistake brightness for entrance, nor descent for help.

It noticed the drowned country in the way one notices an old wound that has healed around a piece of iron.

Not because the place suffered continuously. Suffering was too simple a word for it. The country had no expectation against which to measure its condition. Its walls had become walls-and-current; its plazas were plazas-and-silt; its inhabitants were not imprisoned by instability, because instability was the first fact written into their bodies.

The transparent fish did not dream of still ponds.

The keepers of the joints did not long for dry ground.

The lantern-throats had no lost sun toward which they opened their mouths.

Even the memory animals, bearing faded traces of vanished floods upon their skins, did not mistake memory for a claim that the old water ought to return.

The power understood this before it did anything.

For a long time, it did nothing at all.

It attended.

It learned the duration of a current pause: how the water gathered in the streets before a reversal, how the little eels knew a pressure change through stone before it troubled the wider channels. It watched a traverser take forty years to cross a plaza whose paving had once been laid for processions of people. It watched the plaza change under it. It watched the same creature return, perhaps by another route, its old scars obscured beneath new mineral seams.

It listened to the knock in the foundations.

Three impacts. A pause. One long vibration.

The answer from elsewhere.

It did not pry open the stone to discover the source.

There are questions whose answers, once exposed, become wounds.

The power saw that the drowned country was not a ruined version of some proper world. It was a complete world, though its completeness was difficult: an arrangement of continuance without shelter; a civilization of seams, wakes, pressure, and response.

Still, benevolence is not merely admiration from a distance.

There were creatures who died when a reversal came too suddenly—not because death was unnatural here, but because the old works sometimes concentrated force beyond any living pattern. There were districts where silt had packed for centuries into the expansion joints, so that a surge, unable to divide and spend itself, struck whole terraces with a bluntness that pulverized everything small enough to be pulverized. There were lantern-throat towers sealed by fallen crowns of stone, their dim mouths folded forever in chambers full of slow starvation. There were memory animals beneath those stones, holding maps of currents that no longer existed, exchanging them endlessly until their lines became only confusion.

The power did not raise the fallen towers.

It did not still the water.

It did not give the keepers hands more clever than their hooked limbs, or teach the fish to make walls, or lift the whole drowned country into air.

Such gifts would have been acts of conquest disguised as mercy.

Instead, in one upper district where five ancient avenues met beneath a broken arch, the power altered the smallest thing it could find.

It made one seam open.

Not a crack. Not a collapse. A seam: a line the old builders had left between slabs, intended for movement, neglected by the ages, packed hard with grey sediment and the tiny white bones of things long cleaned away.

The seam opened to the width of a finger.

Cold water entered it.

At first nothing seemed changed. A thread of silt rose and drifted through the new gap. A black eel, startled, slipped into the darkness and vanished. Then the current beneath the plaza found a route it had not possessed for longer than any animal remembered.

The next reversal divided.

One portion of its force turned downward through the opened seam, into old drainage channels, through buried courts, beneath a stairwell where memory animals clustered. A pressure that would have shattered a retaining wall became, instead, a long deep movement under the paving.

The wall trembled.

It did not fall.

In the days afterward, pale keepers emerged by the hundreds.

They found the opened seam.

They did not understand it as a gift. They had no reason to. They entered it, scraped its edges, carried away the loosened sediment, widened here and narrowed there according to their patient instinct. Fish established themselves behind the altered parapets. The broad winged drifters came and settled on the nearby walls, listening to the revised water. In the buried stairwell, the lines on the memory animals began to change.

A new current had entered their skins.

The power waited.

Across the drowned country, it found other places like this—not every broken place, not every dangerous place, not every place where life ended. It learned to distinguish between the necessary harshness from which a living form had arisen and the dead obstruction that no longer belonged to any cycle except destruction.

Where an old bronze gate had lodged half-open across a sluice, making a scouring blade of the water, it softened one corroded hinge until the gate swung free.

Where a lantern-throat colony was sealed in a tower whose upper stones had fused together, it encouraged a vein of trapped frost to expand by less than the thickness of a scale.

Where a lower avenue had become a funnel for the great cold rushes, it loosened a single block high in a side wall. The block did not fall at once. It waited through six reversals, shifting only when the water had already begun to slacken. It settled across the avenue at an angle that made the next surge split around it, less violent and more various.

These were not miracles anyone could see.

No new light descended.

No voice spoke in the water.

The country remained cold. It remained drowned. The traversers still fed. Roof slabs still shifted and boomed in the darkness. The old knocks continued below the last stairs.

But over centuries, the changes accumulated.

Some avenues became passable that had been killing corridors.

Some chambers received slow food-bearing currents for the first time in ages.

A buried court opened into a spiral of water gentle enough for a new kind of soft-bodied creature to persist there: not rooted, exactly, but able to clasp the wall with a hundred weak translucent feet. When the current strengthened, it released itself and was carried to another wall. When it weakened, it gathered again. Its descendants spread through the altered districts, making temporary pale gardens that existed only between surges.

The power was careful even with this.

It did not preserve each garden.

A benevolent power need not make every lovely thing permanent. Permanence would have been a cruelty in a country whose deepest law was rearrangement.

Instead, it ensured that the gardens could disperse before the water took them.

It made leaving possible.

The greatest change came slowly, and perhaps it was not the power’s doing alone.

Far beneath the visible terraces, where the old knocking came from within the foundations, there was a chamber sealed by six slabs of black stone. They had been fitted so tightly that not even the grain-sized pressure colonies could enter. Whatever lay beyond them had been enclosed before the drowning, or during it, or before either word had meaning.

The power had known of the chamber from its first attention.

It had not opened it.

But it listened.

Three impacts. A pause. One long vibration.

And from elsewhere, the answering pattern.

Over many years, the power followed the answer through the city’s bones. It passed from stone to stone, not along a single route but through the entire drowned construction: in bronze plates, mortar bands, tower rings, the old aqueducts, the deep foundations. The country had become one resonant body without knowing it.

Or perhaps it had always known.

The power did not interfere with the knocking. But in a hundred forgotten places, it removed what prevented the sound from traveling: a plug of mineral in a hollow conduit, a collapsed tile wedged against a resonant plate, a wall of compacted silt blocking an old throat in the stone.

The effect was nearly imperceptible.

The next time the deep thing knocked, the eels retreated as before. Lantern-throats closed. Traversers fixed themselves to the walls.

Three impacts.

A pause.

One long vibration.

But this time the answering sound came not only from elsewhere.

It came, faintly, from the towers.

From the submerged gates.

From the terraces and plazas.

From the old slabs beneath the keepers’ feet.

It was not a reply in any language the former makers would have known. It was only resonance: stone recognizing a force, water carrying it onward, living bodies adjusting their soft interiors to its passage.

Yet the memory animals changed.

Across the drowned country, in their sheltered hollows, their dark lines began to arrange themselves into patterns no individual flood had made. Lines of pressure crossed lines of current. Vanished walls appeared beside routes that had never existed. The maps no longer showed only what had happened in a particular place.

They began, imperfectly, to show relation.

A keeper in one district opened a seam because the water there had shifted.

A lantern-throat rose in another tower because a turning current had reached it.

A traverser altered a wall, and years later transparent fish found shelter in the new wake.

A deep impact traveled through foundations, and creatures that could not hear it nevertheless folded, braced, waited.

The drowned country was not becoming orderly.

It was becoming able, in its own dispersed manner, to feel more of itself.

This was the power’s true mercy.

Not rescue.

Not mastery.

Not the restoration of an ancient purpose.

It gave the country a little more room to respond before it was changed.

And then, having done that, the power withdrew its hand.

Not its attention. Attention remained, light as a presence can be, distributed through the pale upper water and the black avenues below. It watched the temporary gardens gather and scatter. It watched the widened seams fill and be cleaned again. It watched a young traverser, no longer than a bridge, emerge from a lower canal and begin its first slow crossing of a terrace.

When, far down in the foundations, the knocking came again, the power did not answer.

The country answered for itself.
