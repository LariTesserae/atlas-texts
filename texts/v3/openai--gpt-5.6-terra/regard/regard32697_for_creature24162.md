The power did not come with fire.

It did not split the cloud cover, nor send a golden road down through the gray shelves. It did not make the distant ridges draw nearer. It did not command the buried thing to wake, or the listeners to rise, or the stone circles to open their inward-facing walls.

It came without arriving.

For a long while, nothing on the plateau knew it was there.

The moss retained its water. The pale folded creatures beneath it extended their filaments one by one. A slate-colored walker sat beside an empty circle and faced west. Above, one of the white high things turned once in the wind, so slowly that its turning could have been mistaken for the sky changing its mind.

Then the black pools reflected a sky that was not above them.

In their depths appeared a color that had no name on the plateau: not brightness, exactly, and not warmth. It was the color of a hand held near a sleeping forehead. The pools did not ripple. The reflected color did not spread. It simply remained, patient as the plateau itself.

The benevolent power looked.

It looked at the place for a span of time that no traveler could have measured. It saw the almost-life in the stone. It saw the condition held in balance by countless small fidelities: the white things preserving wetness; the frost-birds taking what had become too fixed and rendering it clear; the fox-shaped walkers following distances that belonged to some deeper geography; the listeners returning the great sound beneath the ground to whatever made it.

It saw that the plateau was not cursed.

Not exactly.

It had been made—or had made itself—into an answer to a grief too large to pass through ordinary time.

Perhaps once there had been a world below the western cliff. Perhaps there had been a sea. Perhaps the sea had risen or withdrawn or broken against a shore that no longer existed. Perhaps the immense being beneath the stone had loved something that could not remain. Perhaps it had turned in its sleep, slowly, slowly, until the mountains had worn away around its sorrow.

The power did not need to know which story was true.

It knew enough.

Nothing on the plateau was suffering in the simple way. The cushion-things had their moisture. The birds had their cracks. The foxes had their impossible paths. The listeners had their task.

But the passing people were different.

They had not chosen the pause.

They had entered it because they were tired, or lost, or grieving, or following a pale animal across bad ground; because they had mistaken a reflection for a path; because they had asked, somewhere in another country, how far until the sea, and the question had opened beneath their feet.

The power saw them walking.

It saw the woman with the lantern whose flame gave no light. She had carried it for so long that she had forgotten it had once belonged to her brother. She no longer remembered her brother’s face, only that he had gone ahead.

It saw the man with a rope around his chest, leading nothing behind him. He had tied the rope to a companion during a whiteout in a mountain pass. When the storm lifted, the rope had extended into cloud, and he had followed it for years.

It saw the child standing in one of the circles with their face turned away. The child had been waiting for someone to call them by the name that had once made them turn.

It saw those who had become accustomed to being lost. Those who had forgotten that arrival was possible. Those who feared that if they stopped walking, they would discover how long they had been gone.

For them, the power did something.

Not all at once.

First, it went to the western cliff.

It did not descend the face, for there was no face as such beneath it. The stone passed downward through distances that folded and returned upon themselves. Water moved through chambers wide as weather. There were caverns full of old pressure, passages where darkness had the slow pulse of blood, and walls of mineral that remembered every drop that had touched them.

At the depth where the surf-sound became something like breathing, the power found the great thing.

It had no single shape.

In one chamber it was a vast pale flank beneath black water. In another it was a root of stone threaded with silver. In another it was only an immense closed eye, not seeing but holding the memory of sight. It filled the mountain without containing itself. It slept without resting.

The power did not wake it.

Instead, it placed one small thing beside its dreaming.

A door.

Not a grand door. Not a gate of judgment or rescue. It was made from the local stone: dark slate, pale grit, old dust pressed into seams. It was no taller than a person. It had no handle on the outside.

On its inner face, where only the dreaming thing could perceive it, there was a simple inscription:

You have kept what was lost.

You may let it go.

The great thing did not stir.

For a long time there was only the underground surf.

Then, very far away, one of the black pools on the plateau made a single ripple.

The power waited.

It understood that no wisdom is wise if it confuses release with theft. The buried being had held the plateau in its suspended mercy for longer than names had existed. To take that condition away by force would be another violence. To wake it without invitation would be to tear open the only refuge it knew how to make.

So the door remained closed.

But it existed.

That was enough to alter the world.

Above, in the nearest roofless circle, a listener lifted one hidden hand.

Only a finger’s width.

The ash-colored wrappings shifted. Beneath them there was a face: old, young, human, stone, unrecognizable. The face turned toward the west.

For the first time, the listener was not listening only downward.

It was listening for an answer.

The power then went among the travelers.

It did not seize them. It did not tell them the truth of where they were. It did not promise them that their homes still existed, or that the dead would be returned, or that the journey behind them could be undone.

It gave each one something smaller and more useful.

To the woman with the lantern, it gave the lantern a shadow.

When she saw it, she stopped. For the first time in years beyond measure, something she carried occupied a place in the light. She held the lantern close, and memory returned—not all of it, not cruelly, but enough. Her brother’s hands. The sound of rain on a roof. The foolish song he used to sing when he was afraid.

She wept.

Her tears fell into the dust.

They did not vanish.

Where they struck, dark soil appeared.

The woman did not leave the plateau immediately. She sat beside the little patch of earth for a long time. Then she stood, turned not west or east but toward a slope that had never been there before, and walked.

The slope led downward.

To the man with the rope, the power gave weight.

The rope suddenly pulled against him—not from ahead, but from behind. He resisted it at first. Then he turned.

At the far end, where there had always been only cloud, he found the knot he had tied years ago. It was tied around a stone.

He understood.

Not that his companion had been taken by the plateau. Not that the rope had been empty all this time. Something gentler and harder: that he had been holding on to the moment of loss because letting the rope fall would have felt like abandoning the one who was gone.

He untied it.

The rope became ordinary rope. It lay in a coil at his feet.

He left it there.

The child in the circle heard, at last, a voice.

It did not call a name.

It said, very softly: You may turn around.

The child did.

There was no one behind them.

Only the circle wall, the frost, the gray land, and a slate-colored fox sitting just beyond the stones.

The child looked at the fox.

The fox stood and began to walk.

This time, it did not lead badly.

The power made no road across the plateau. Roads would have been too certain, too much like conquest. It made openings instead.

A crack in a wall that had never had a crack.

A reflection in a black pool that showed not another landscape, but the traveler’s own true direction.

A patch of moss that grew by one finger-width each year, pointing toward a descent.

A moment when the delayed shadows caught up to their owners and then continued ahead.

Some travelers took these signs.

Some did not.

The power did not judge them.

There were those who had come to love the plateau’s pause. A woman who had lost everyone she knew found comfort in a world where nothing visibly decayed. A boy who had fled a war sat beside a black pool and preferred its impossible reflection to any future. An old pilgrim had spent so long seeking the wall with the door that he no longer wished to find it, because seeking had become the form his faith took.

For them, the power left the plateau as it was.

It gave them only this: that if they ever changed their minds, the place would notice.

And because the power was wise, it did not forget the plateau’s other inhabitants.

It touched the moss, and allowed one new shade of green to exist there—not more green than the place could bear, not abundance, but variation.

It touched the frost-birds, and once in every hundred gatherings, one of their bells rang clearly above water.

It touched the white creatures under the damp stones, and granted them a new work: where a traveler slept beside a pool and dreamed of leaving, the creatures would gather moisture along the direction of that dream.

It touched the fox-shaped walkers, and gave them the ability to wait beside those who were afraid to move.

It touched the pale circling things high in the wind, and when one next settled on a standing stone, no new crack opened in the escarpment.

Instead, an old crack closed.

Not completely. The land was not made whole. It was merely allowed, in one small place, to stop widening.

At last, the power returned to the first black pool.

There it found a listener standing at the water’s edge.

The listener’s wrappings had fallen open. Within them was neither a person nor an absence. There was a body shaped from many presences: the outline of those who had listened before, the expressions of those who had waited, the patient stillness of the circles themselves.

The listener looked into the pool.

The pool reflected the door beneath the western cliffs.

“Will it open?” the listener asked.

It was the first spoken question on the plateau in an age.

The power answered, “When it is ready.”

“Will it ever be ready?”

The power considered this.

Then it said, “It has already begun.”

The listener bowed its head.

Under the western escarpment, the great being dreamed.

In its dream, perhaps, it found the door. Perhaps it pressed against it with the slow attention of something that had never known it possessed hands. Perhaps it only sensed a difference in the dark: a seam where no seam had been, a possibility that did not demand itself.

The surf rose through the stone.

It came as it always had: a long inhalation, a collapse.

But after the collapse, there was a pause.

Not the old pause.

A different one.

A pause in which something might happen.

On the plateau, a droplet formed beneath a ledge. It swelled, held, and fell.

The next droplet did not gather in quite the same place.
