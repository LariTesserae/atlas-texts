The power did not arrive as weather.

Weather had no authority there.

It did not come in a voice, because voices in the high country had already been taught their smallness. It did not send a bright figure down from the black-blue sky, nor split the mesas, nor fill the empty basins with water. It had seen enough worlds injured by rescue that mistook itself for generosity.

It came first as an absence of pressure.

One night, in a slate hut above the bridge with no road, an old woman woke because the bowl beside her bed was no longer heavy with its usual implication. It was only a bowl: blackened clay, chipped at the rim, holding nothing.

She sat up.

Outside, the narrow-horned animal that had stood beside her shelter for longer than anyone’s memory was looking toward the eastern ridge. Its shadow lay correctly beneath it.

The woman put on her layered coat. She did not light her lamp.

Across the basin, other lamps remained dark. Doors opened one by one. People emerged: those still becoming present, children older than their own mothers, strangers who had arrived incorrectly and never left, a man whose earlier footprints crossed the valley half a day ahead of him.

The animals also turned.

The gliders unfolded from the cliffs with a sound like pages being separated. Plate-winged beetles drifted down through the cold in their thousands. Even the Held, those immense repetitions gathered around their old stones, altered.

Not moved.

Altered.

An antler lowered by the width of a finger. An eye that had been cloudy quartz became, for an instant, wet and black. The slopes filled with a sensation like the moment before a remembered name returns.

At the eastern ridge stood something that had not been standing there before.

It was not large. A person could have mistaken it for a traveler at first: a plain shape in a cloak the color of unmarked paper, leaning on no staff, carrying no pack. Its face was hidden—not veiled, precisely, but impossible to retain. Whenever anyone looked at it directly, they remembered a face they had loved and lost, then could not remember whether the love had been theirs.

No one approached.

The power waited until the country had noticed it.

Then it did something so slight that many did not understand it had happened.

It asked permission.

Not aloud.

A question appeared in the arrangements of the world.

Three quartz beads rolled from beneath an ironwood root and formed a line pointing toward the stranger on the ridge.

A glider’s shadow rose from the stone and folded itself into the shape of an open hand.

In every shelter, the black-glass tools trembled once against their shelves.

The question was not *May I enter?* The power had already entered, in the way that attention enters anything.

The question was:

*What would you have changed?*

The people stood for a long while.

In kinder lands, the answer might have come easily. They might have asked for water. For crops. For warmth. For children who aged one year after another in an orderly way, and parents who did not discover their own younger selves asleep beneath cairns. They might have asked for the dead to decay and the living to move on. They might have asked for the ground beneath them to stop remembering.

But no one spoke.

The high country was not merely a prison. It was also an archive, though an imperfect one. It held what other places discarded. The hoofprint and the hoof. The first bridge-builder’s hand and the bridge itself. Lost questions. Unspent grief. Creatures that had become too intertwined with their own persistence to be called alive, yet had not been abandoned to death.

The people had suffered there. Certainly.

They had also made manners for suffering.

They had made care out of incomprehension: an overturned bowl, a row of beads, the practice of greeting the stone-faced travelers. They had learned not to insist that every thing be one thing. They had learned patience so deep it resembled stillness.

An answer came at last from a child near the bridge.

The child was small, though their hair was white and their hands were scarred with decades of stonework. They took three black-glass blades from a pouch and placed them at their feet.

The first blade pointed toward a distant Held assembly.

The second toward the gray figure seated at a pass, who might once have been a traveler.

The third toward the animals gathered around the huts.

Then the child turned the empty pouch inside out.

The others understood.

Not words. Not a demand.

A request for room.

Not to erase what had been held. Not to make the old country young, or wet, or ordinary. Not to break the sentences written in stone.

Only room.

Room for a thing to become something else without being punished for leaving its former shape behind.

The power looked upon the child. The child looked back, though their eyes watered in the thin cold.

Then the power knelt.

Its knees touched no stone. Yet all through the high country, very gently, the stones that had chosen shapes felt themselves regarded.

The power did not move them.

It gave them a choice.

This was an act beyond mercy, and therefore slower than mercy.

It offered no command. It did not say, *Release.* It did not say, *Remain.* It merely withdrew the impossible necessity that had been wrapped around each settled thing—the silent rule that said: *Because you are remembered, you must stay. Because you are held, you cannot change. Because the world learned your shape, you owe it that shape forever.*

For the first time since the wind had ceased to belong there, the country felt the difference between being preserved and being loved.

The Held were first.

Across the eastern basins, tens of thousands of bodies trembled. Antlers touched. Mineral hair lifted though there was no wind. Heads emerged from flanks where they had long been joined; legs separated, one by one, from the wrong bodies. Some creatures crumpled at once into old bones, into dust, into the simple silence they had been waiting beneath for centuries.

The people did not look away.

They mourned without sound.

Other animals stood.

A black-jointed cat, ancient beyond any counting, woke beneath a grazer’s chest. It stretched. For the first time, its shadow followed it. It walked ten paces from the herd, stopped, and looked back.

Then it continued down the basin.

Nothing pursued it.

All across the country, there came the smallest sounds: hooves lifting, wings opening, gravel loosening beneath blind bodies that had never before chosen a direction. Some animals stayed exactly where they were. Some lay down and became stone without fear. Some walked toward the horizons that had always seemed near and now, at last, admitted their distance.

The seated gray travelers at the passes opened their eyes.

Not all of them.

One rose, slowly, dropping a cloak of slate fragments from their shoulders. Beneath it was a young woman in an old coat, carrying a bowl with a chipped rim. She looked around as though she had only paused for breath.

Another traveler did not rise. But the stone of their face softened. By morning, there was only a cairn there: careful, complete, and at peace with being a cairn.

In the shelters, people found that certain old arrangements no longer meant what they had meant.

Three blades beside a lamp still meant *wait until morning.*

But the line of quartz beads no longer meant *someone returned incorrectly.*

It meant:

*Someone has returned, and is allowed to be changed.*

The power remained on the eastern ridge through the long night.

It did not give the country water.

Water would have been too loud a gift. It would have cut gullies, carried away old marks, drowned the mineral roots, made every basin into a history of rushing and loss. Perhaps, in some age, water would come. Perhaps the land itself would ask for it.

Instead, before leaving, the power gave one smaller blessing.

At dawn, a single ironwood tree produced a seed.

It was not bright. It did not glow. It was a dark, hard seed no larger than the joint of a little finger. It fell onto the dry ground and lay there, entirely ordinary.

No one touched it.

The old rule remained.

Never move a stone that has chosen a shape.

But beside it, scratched into the slate threshold of the nearest hut, a new mark appeared. Not words, precisely. A picture: an open hand, and beyond it a path disappearing around a ridge.

For many years, the high country remained mostly as it had been.

Cold. Dry. Vast. Full of animals that did not always hunt or flee. Full of shadows that sometimes forgot to go home. Full of places where time gathered in folds.

But now, from time to time, a creature crossed a basin and did not reappear in the same place at night.

A person woke with dust beneath their nails from a road they had taken, and smiled.

A glider left the cliff where its body had been patterned for generations, and its lingering shadow faded gently from the stone.

And when strangers arrived, the people still pointed to the nearest still animal, then to the stranger’s feet, then to the sky.

But sometimes—only sometimes—they also pointed toward the farthest ridge.

The meaning was clearer than before.

*Stand where you are.*

*You have been seen.*

*And when it is yours to choose, you may go.*
