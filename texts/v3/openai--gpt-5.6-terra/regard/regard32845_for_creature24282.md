The power did not arrive with light.

It knew better than to mistake darkness for a wound.

It came first as attention: a pause in the weather above the enclosing ridges, a stillness in the high clouds, so slight that only the swifts altered their flight. It looked down through the layered terraces and bridges, through leaf roofs and wet stone, through the black mouths of reservoirs and the warm rooms buried within the mountain. It listened.

For a long time, it did nothing.

This was not neglect. The valley had endured many kinds of help in its age, most of them disastrous. Builders had once corrected slopes that wished to move. Keepers had sealed leaks that needed to feed gardens below. Kings, perhaps, had ordered channels straightened, animals excluded, walls brightened, old spaces made useful again. The valley still bore the softened consequences of such intentions: a stair ending in a filled-in door, a cistern gone dry behind a too-perfect repair, a terrace where nothing rooted because its drainage had been made efficient.

The wise power understood that a made place could be as alive as a forest, and that life did not become lesser because it depended on old human hands.

It understood, too, that the valley was not asking to be restored.

Restoration was a word used by those who imagined there had been one correct moment in a thing’s history, one arrangement to which all later arrangements should be made to apologize. The valley had passed beyond that kind of judgment. It had become itself by alteration. Its mosses, dogs, bats, blind fish, parrots, fungi, wet stones, and possible hidden inheritors had made a thousand agreements no single mind could fully see.

So the power did not clear the vines.

It did not summon lamps into the passages.

It did not drive the snakes from the cisterns, or make the dogs tame, or return the goats to pens, or reveal whether there were people behind the dark doorways.

It did not even mend every broken wall.

Instead, it learned the valley’s thresholds.

It found the places where change had become too fast for the old agreements to answer it: a root-choked spillway above a low nesting court; a weakening vault beneath a reservoir; a fever of mold spreading through a chamber where bats wintered; a dry season lengthening beyond the reach of the seep-fed basins; new predators at the outer gates, clever and hungry and not yet fitted into any balance.

There it acted.

Not visibly.

One night, before the first great rain of the season, a goat chose a ledge it had never chosen before. Its hoof struck loose a flat stone. The stone fell through a tangle of vine and broke the crust over a blocked drainage shaft. Water, which had been gathering silently behind the wall, found a way down.

It did not burst the lower court.

It filled the old channel, ran beneath its root-and-bone grates, and returned to a basin that had been dry for eleven years.

By morning, frogs had found it.

No one knew how frogs found such things. They simply appeared: first one, then three, then a dozen small bodies pressing flat against the wet rim. Within a season there were eggs in the water. Within two, the deep-voiced frogs of the lower valley had returned, carrying their old note from court to court until the masonry remembered how to vibrate with it.

Elsewhere, a fault opened in a retaining wall high above the gardens.

The power did not close it.

It softened the rain for three days. It persuaded the water to fall finer, to enter soil rather than strike it. It let a tree root bend toward the crack. It let the root find, within the wall, an old cavity packed with the dust of abandoned nests. There it swelled slowly, not enough to tear the stones apart, but enough to hold them while the season passed.

Years later, the wall would still lean.

A person, if there was a person, would notice the lean and understand it as a warning. A monkey would use the new angle to reach a fig tree. A cat would sleep in the dry triangular space beneath it. The wall would not be returned to its former shape. It would be allowed to become a different kind of wall.

The power kept the valley’s water from becoming a possession.

In the driest years, when the lower channels tasted of old leaf rot and mineral salt, it moved mist through the highest courts. Not enough to be seen as rain. Not enough for anyone to praise. It gathered on fern fronds, on the furred backs of mossy stones, on the leaves of trees rooted in parapets. It entered the root shafts. It darkened mortar. It made the cisterns rise by the depth of a finger.

That was sufficient.

The eels endured.

The blind fish endured.

The frogs waited, quiet but not gone.

The power had no special fondness for the large or beautiful. It attended to beetles caught in overflow chambers. It attended to pale mice whose routes through the walls had been cut off by a settling stair. It attended to the lichen that held a thin film of soil on a north-facing ledge, because in that soil a seed might one day lodge, and in that tree a generation of parrots might find food after another tree failed.

But neither did it preserve every life.

It did not save each nestling from the snake.

It did not turn the dog away from the lame goat.

It did not prevent fungus from taking a beam whose time had come, or a roof from falling after its roots had ceased to hold it.

Benevolence was not the refusal of endings. Wisdom was not a valley without hunger.

When a roof fell, it made a new kind of shelter.

When a wall gave way, it made a new slope.

When an old animal died in a forgotten guardroom, it fed flies, beetles, fungi, mice, cats, crows, and the innumerable small things that did not have names in any language still spoken there.

The power did not stand outside this truth. It loved the living world not as a garden loved by an owner, but as a song loved by one who can hear every voice within it, including the low notes, including the pauses.

It did, however, guard against silence.

There was a time—whether near or far, no creature in the valley could have said—when the valley began to fall quiet.

Not the good quiet of noon beneath rain-thick clouds, when bats slept and dogs lay in warm doorways and even the parrots seemed briefly exhausted by their own existence.

A wrong quiet.

The birds diminished first. Then insects ceased gathering in the warm passages. Water still moved, but it moved alone. A gray sickness had entered from beyond the ridges, carried perhaps by the feet of a climbing animal, perhaps by wind, perhaps by a traveler from some emptier country. It did not kill dramatically. It simply made eggs fail, made fruit rot before ripening, made young animals sleep too long and wake thin.

The power saw that this was not one of the valley’s own transformations. It was not death making room for life. It was severance: a thing that could break the thousand agreements and replace them with nothing.

Then the power became less subtle.

It sent a storm around the valley for nine days.

The rain fell hard on the outer ridges and spared the central courts. It washed the sickness from leaves, from feathers, from the tracks of dogs and goats. It filled the runoff channels until water ran clear through culverts that had not carried a true current in living memory. It battered the upper passes with cold wind no animal wished to cross.

Within the valley, warm vapor rose from the stone.

The reservoirs breathed.

Fungi bloomed in colors unseen for generations: amber shelves, blue-gray fans, red threads in the rot beneath old platforms. Insects hatched in clouds. The bats fed until their bellies were round. The crows carried wet shining things into their rafters. The dogs shook themselves in doorways and sneezed and went back to their quiet patrols.

The sickness did not vanish entirely. Nothing so deeply entered vanishes entirely.

But it was given limits.

And the valley, having been reminded of its own abundance, answered.

Afterward, the power withdrew its attention a little—not away, never away, but outward, enough that the valley would not become dependent on being watched.

This was its greatest gift.

It left no shrine.

No new inscription appeared on the lintels. No voice spoke in a keeper’s ear. No animal was marked as chosen. The stones did not glow. The hidden people, if hidden people lived there, did not emerge with knowledge that could be carried into another land.

There were only small changes.

A drain cleared where clearing was needed.

A crack held through one more rainy season.

A lost watercourse returned.

A seed found soil.

A fever broke.

A roof fell safely.

A child of goats was born behind a screen of old stone and, when it first stood, discovered that the world had many levels and that all of them were wet, warm, dangerous, generous, and full of voices.

The power continued to watch from time to time.

It watched lightning briefly make the dark birds blue.

It watched bats threading ancient routes through the rain.

It watched a cat enter a lamp niche that had not held a lamp for centuries.

It watched, once, a figure in damp-colored cloth move through a high court before dawn. The figure paused beside a blocked channel, placed both hands against the wall, and listened. Then it removed a little packed leaf matter from a grating and went on.

The power did not reveal itself.

It felt no need.

The valley had its own custody already.

What it needed was not salvation, nor command, nor the return of its vanished makers. It needed room to continue becoming: wetly, noisily, patiently; by root and claw and wing; by collapse and repair; by the old intelligence of creatures making homes in the remains of other homes.

So the wise power gave it that room.

And when it passed over the high valley, it passed softly, like cloud-shadow over terraces, like a bat’s wing under an arch, like water finding the path it had always been seeking.
