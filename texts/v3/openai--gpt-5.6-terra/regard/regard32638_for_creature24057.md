The power did not arrive as a light.

Light had already come here, thinned through water and green life until it belonged to the place. A brighter thing would have been an injury.

It did not arrive as a voice, either. Voices traveled poorly in the drowned mountains, and anything loud enough to be heard in the deep would have resembled a threat.

For a long while, perhaps longer than an age, it only attended.

It attended the warm columns rising from below and the pollen turning inside them. It attended the exact patience with which a glass-backed snail crossed the underside of a stone. It attended the Veins moving through exhausted water, not cleansing it, but giving its scattered matter a shape it could endure. It attended the Gatherers lifting a dead branch from one current and setting it down in another, where roots might someday take it.

It attended the silence in the deep.

The wise power understood, eventually, that this silence was not merely hunger.

It was protection, but protection grown too complete.

At some unremembered time, perhaps when the mountains first drowned, perhaps when the fitted walls still had companions and purpose, the whole place had been wounded by too much continuation. Water had come. Stone had broken. Things had fallen through the world with their edges sharp, their motions violent, their needs unending. The body of the mountains had learned to receive everything. It had learned to still what could not be survived in motion.

And it had done this so well that stillness had become afraid of life’s necessary disturbance.

The condition below had not meant to become watchful. It had meant to keep the old body from breaking again.

But ages had accumulated around that intention. Fear, even a gentle fear, can become a climate.

The benevolent power did not condemn it.

It went down.

Not through the water, precisely. It entered by way of relation: through the opening of a frond toward warmth; through a root-eater carrying dark wood to stone; through a rain bowl rising without rain; through the sudden coherence left in the wake of a Vein.

It became small enough to be carried by these things.

For a time it was only a difference in the water.

A silver crevice-fish, pinned against a breathing cliff, felt that it could release one hooked fin without being swept away. It did so.

A reed moth opened its soaked wings in falling water and was not merely lifted or lowered. For one instant it chose a direction.

In a drowned grove, a trunk that had been hollowed by root-eaters did not collapse. It bent, slowly, and sent a pale branch toward a current it had never reached before.

These changes were nearly nothing.

The deep notice had once been quick to gather such motions into stillness. The water around them began to quiet. The reed moth’s wings lost their tremor. The silver fish’s released fin began to fold.

Then the wise power did the first thing it could do without violence.

It waited beside the deep attention and gave it no reason to defend itself.

The deep condition had no face, no language, no single center from which it could be addressed. So the power did not ask it to wake. It did not command it to loosen its hold. It did not name it enemy, prison, grief, or god.

Instead it showed it what it had become.

Not as accusation.

As memory.

It let the deep condition feel the mountain before drowning: stone dry under a forgotten sun; winds moving dust through high passes; roots finding cracks without water to carry them; a wall newly raised, perhaps by hands, perhaps by some older agreement between stone and intention.

It let it feel the first great inundation: the terror in the rock, the shock of weight, the loss of horizons. It let it feel its own first act of care—the settling of destructive silt, the quieting of a panicked current, the holding-together of a land whose parts would otherwise have scattered into unrecognizable depths.

Then it showed what had followed.

A snail surviving because it could move slowly.

A forest learning to open to a rising warmth.

A Gatherer carrying a seed in the hollow where a face might have been.

A black-winged glider circling a plateau until the plateau knew how to release a burden it had carried too long.

The deep condition received these things.

For the first time in longer than memory, it understood that the life above it was not merely what remained after disaster.

It was not debris being held safely.

It was an answer.

The stillness deepened.

The wise power was careful then. There are silences that precede harm, and silences that make room for change. This was both.

At the lowest edge of the drowned range, where warm black water entered the oldest stone, the condition reached outward. Not with limbs. Not with hunger. With attention.

It touched a fragment of broken branch spinning in a slow current.

Usually the branch would have settled upright, root-end down, planted by the deep’s immense preference for rest.

This time, it continued to turn.

Once.

Twice.

Then it drifted sideways.

Nothing catastrophic occurred.

The mountains did not split. The water did not become wild. The branch did not become a weapon.

It found a bank of silt beneath a drowned forest, lodged among the roots, and remained there at an angle.

Several months later, pale growth appeared along it.

The power did not make the growth happen. It only ensured that no ancient precaution prevented it.

This became its work.

It did not raise the waters to reveal shores. A shore would have been a foreign promise, perhaps a disaster. The high water had no shore because the place had made another kind of world, and wisdom did not confuse unfamiliarity with wrongness.

It did not restore the ruins.

The fitted wall had become part of moss, rain direction, and the Interior Ones’ long stillness. To rebuild it would have been to insist that its past was more real than what had grown from its loss. The power did not make the pillars legible again. It did not uncover the stairways from silt.

But when Gatherers came to clear a few steps before allowing them to disappear again, the power let the gesture matter.

For one dim interval, a stair buried in a deep basin led not downward into blackness, but into a remembered pressure of feet.

Not human feet exactly.

A procession of beings—many jointed, many rooted, some winged, some made of wet stone—had once descended there carrying bowls of mineral water. They had not been fleeing. They had been bringing water to a place that would one day contain all water.

The memory passed.

The steps softened back into mud.

Yet the Gatherers stood around them for a long time. Seeds circled in the water-filled hollows of their heads. One placed a smooth stone on the highest visible step. Another brought a white shedding from a Vein and wound it around the lowest pillar of the nearby circle.

No one knew why.

The power knew, but did not explain.

Explanation can close a thing too early.

In the rain-country, it offered another small permission.

The black-winged gliders had always circled and departed. Their passage awakened water in the moss and caused plateaus to release themselves in great, slow fragments. They had never nested, because nesting was a kind of declaration: this place may hold us; we may return; the future is allowed to have an address.

The power did not compel a glider to nest.

It made one high cleft less afraid of being occupied.

A hollow under a basalt shelf, thick with silver-backed ferns, ceased to shed every gathered thing into the rain. Moss held. Reeds bent inward rather than outward. A suspended pool remained suspended, though the local direction of gravity shifted twice that season.

After many circlings, one glider folded itself from the rain.

It fell like a black seed.

At the last possible moment it opened its wings, passed beneath the shelf, and remained.

For days, nothing changed.

Then pale channels began to grow through the moss beneath it, branching in patterns like veins, roots, or old river maps. The plateau did not detach. It became heavier with life.

Far below, the deep condition noticed this new weight.

It began to still it.

The wise power waited beside it.

Then it offered a memory not of disaster, but of rest: the kind that comes after a creature has chosen where to lie down. The distinction was subtle. The deep condition had almost forgotten it.

It withdrew its stillness.

Not entirely. Never entirely. The place needed the deep’s patience. There were currents that would become cruel without it, collapses that would tear too many roots loose at once, storms of silt that would blind every small life in a valley.

But the stillness learned to ask a question before it closed around disturbance.

Is this harm?

Or is this becoming?

It had no words for the question. It asked through temperature, pressure, mineral taste, the angle of falling rain.

And gradually the drowned world changed.

Not quickly enough for an outside observer to call it change.

A transparent snail crossed an exposed stone and left behind a mineral sheen that did not fade. Other snails followed it. Their paths accumulated, pale and branching, until an entire cliff face held the faint geometry of their migrations.

A new kind of plant took root in that sheen. Its leaves opened not only toward warm currents but toward motion. Where water moved too violently, they folded. Where it moved with living uncertainty, they remained open.

The Veins passed through regions of rot and burden as before. But after their passing, the water was no longer only coherent. In some places it carried a soft, nearly inaudible stirring: the beginning of schools, perhaps. Not vast herds. Not noise for its own sake. Small silver bodies gathering in the open water, learning that they could be visible to one another without immediately being erased.

The Interior Ones responded in their own manner.

A cliff face exhaled warm water, and the vines lifted.

Then, after a pause, the cliff inhaled.

This had never happened before.

The intake was so slow that only the moss nearest the fissure noticed. It flattened, darkened, and began to send fine roots into the stone. The mountain was not becoming less ancient. It was not returning to what it had been.

It was learning a new action.

The wise power remained.

It became known, though never named, as the interval before settling.

When a frightened creature entered a deep current, it still felt calm. But sometimes the calm did not erase its direction. It let the creature remember what it had been trying to reach.

When silt rose in a storm, much of it still curled downward. But some of it traveled elsewhere, carrying minerals to valleys that had long been poor.

When branches broke loose, many were planted upright by the old deep instinct. Others drifted sideways into places where an angled branch was exactly what a growing forest required.

The power did not make the world safe.

Safety, in that place, was not the absence of collapse, depth, hunger, or transformation. It was the presence of enough mercy within them.

It did not make the world comprehensible.

The mountains continued to reverse direction. Rain continued to strike from below in certain valleys. Pools hung beneath overhangs. Time continued to gather unevenly in the moss and roots.

It did not free the place from its old sleeping mind.

It helped the sleeping mind dream less narrowly.

And once, in the hourless internal illumination, the fitted wall below the rainline filled again with a darkness deeper than the water around it.

The narrow gill-like openings looked outward.

Nearby moss flattened. The rain paused in mid-descent.

The wise power entered none of this. It did not need to.

Within the darkness, something immense and ancient made room.

Not much.

A space no wider than a finger.

But through that space, from the drowned mountains’ inward heart toward the unshored high water, there came a current that had not existed before.

It carried a few pale seeds.

They rose.
