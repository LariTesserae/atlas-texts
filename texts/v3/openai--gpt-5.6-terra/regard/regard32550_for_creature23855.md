The power did not mistake suffering for beauty.

It did not stand outside the fissure, pleased by the severity of it: the black glass, the heat, the crowded furred ceiling, the blind industry below. It did not call the chamber pure because it was harsh, nor sacred because no human hand had altered it. It understood that a place can be complete in its own way and still contain need.

So it listened first.

It listened through the roar until the roar became distinct lives: the mother bat whose milk had begun to fail in the dry heat; the old male with one torn wing, holding his place by stubborn claws; the larvae in a seam that would be baked sterile if the deep gases shifted by only a little. It listened to the mountain as well—not as a voice with words, but as pressure, cooling, load, fracture, the long unhurried arithmetic by which stone decides what may remain hollow.

And because it was wise, it did not command the volcano to become gentle.

A young mountain that is prevented from changing is not saved. It is merely made false, and falsehood in stone eventually becomes violence. To seal every hot crack, still every groan, cool every furnace, would be to kill the chamber in the name of preserving its inhabitants. The bats would lose their winter warmth. The strange small lives whose bodies had learned the language of sulfur and dryness would be erased by mercy too broad to notice them.

The power did not do that.

Instead, it made a handful of almost imperceptible adjustments.

Deep below the chamber, where a vapor channel narrowed between two shelves of cooling basalt, it eased the passage by the width of a root hair. Gas that had been gathering pressure found another way. The mountain still breathed. It still clicked, boomed, contracted, and made its black glass sing. But the sudden lethal belches of heat became rarer. The hottest clefts remained hot enough for the creatures that needed them, yet no longer flared unpredictably through the floor.

In one high fold of stone, near but not among the densest cluster of bats, it altered the shape of a mineral seam. Nothing visible happened at first. Then, during the coldest hour before dawn, a trace of vapor caught there. It condensed—not into a spring, not a shining pool, nothing so extravagant—but into a dark bead beneath an overhang.

By morning the bead was gone.

The next morning there was another.

It was not enough to change the character of the place. The chamber remained dry. It remained an enclosed furnace. No moss came. No green softened the basalt. But in a small hidden crease, a moisture-loving film of life found an interval in which to exist. Tiny organisms woke and slept with the vapor. A few insects learned the route. A bat with a split lip found the damp stone and drank what was there.

The power made no creature dependent on a miracle. It gave no endless source that, if withdrawn, would become a new catastrophe. It offered a margin.

It strengthened, by almost nothing, the basalt lip beneath the oldest part of the colony. Not forever. Nothing in a newborn volcanic ridge could be promised forever. But for a span of seasons—perhaps decades, perhaps longer—the ceiling there would not peel away under the accumulated warmth and weight. The bats would have time to raise young. The old would have time to die in a place they knew. The silver moths would have time to lay their dust-colored eggs in the cool folds. The beetles would continue their patient work in the guano-dark beneath.

And outside the crack, on a slope lower down where the ash was beginning to loosen and the nights collected a little dew, the power did something still quieter.

It protected a few seeds.

Not by planting a garden on the volcano. Not by clothing the barren ridge in an instant, because the barren ridge had its own necessary beginning. It merely kept a windblown cluster of seeds from being scoured away in the next hard gust. It guided a thread of subsurface moisture toward them. Years later, small plants took hold in a sheltered crease. Insects came for them. Other insects came for those insects.

The bats, leaving their furnace each night, found slightly more to eat.

This was how the wise power helped: not by making the place safe, not by making it pleasant, not by preserving every individual life against loss. It knew there would still be falls from the ceiling. There would still be pups dropped in the crowd and not recovered. There would still be drought in the valleys, sickness in the colony, seasons in which the bats returned thin and desperate. One day the chamber would narrow. Another day, far beyond the memory of any living creature there, the mountain would close the hollow entirely or open it into some larger and colder form.

The power did not lie to them about this.

But it gave the chamber time.

It gave it a little more resilience than accident alone had allotted: a diverted pressure, a hidden bead of water, a shelf that held, a future foothold for plants beyond the crack. It left the mountain free to become itself, and the colony free to remain a colony rather than an ornament protected in perfect stillness.

Then it withdrew its hand.

Not its attention.

For attention, it continued to give.

Whenever the deep red fissure breathed light across the chamber, the power saw the thousands of bats outlined for a moment against the glow: not a single dark mass, but innumerable particular beings, each warm and frightened and hungry and alive. It saw the beetle feeling its way through a grain of waste larger than its own body. It saw the mite crossing a wing membrane. It saw the bacteria taking sustenance from a wetness too brief for any larger creature to notice.

And it regarded none of them as too small to matter.

The mountain groaned. The bats settled. A bead of water formed in the hidden seam, trembled with the stone’s slow pulse, and waited for whoever found it first.
