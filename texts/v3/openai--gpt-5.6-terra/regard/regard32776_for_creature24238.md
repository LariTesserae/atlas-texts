The wise power notices the hollow not because it is strange, nor because it is old, nor because the last human name for it has fallen out of every surviving mouth.

It notices because nothing there is asking to be saved.

This is rare.

Across the world, the power is accustomed to cries: the sharp cry of a river dammed beyond endurance; the dense, wordless panic of a forest cut faster than roots can make soil; the prayer of a child who has not yet learned that prayer is a shape made by need. It knows the sound of things interrupted.

But the hollow is not interrupted.

It is changing, certainly. Every rain alters it. Every winter presses another fraction of stone loose. The old city is becoming mountain, and the mountain is becoming soil, and soil is becoming moss, and moss is making a damp roof for tiny lives that have no language for gratitude. Yet the changes belong to one another. Nothing is being forced to become what it cannot bear.

So at first the wise power does nothing.

It comes without arrival.

For one noon it is present in the clean blue light on the upper rim. For one night it is present in the cold held beneath the cisterns. It listens to the wind speak through the slots and wells, listens to the ravens testing their voices against the returning echoes. It watches mist choose its streets. It feels the slow pressure of roots beneath paving, the minute exchange of water between stone and soil.

It learns the hollow’s balance.

Then, because wisdom is not mere restraint, it looks outward.

Beyond the rim are other lives. There are people in distant valleys who have begun to hunger for remoteness—not the difficult remoteness of weather and walking, but a remoteness they can carry home in images. Some will someday hear rumors of a city high in an unnamed bowl. A few will want to see it. Some will come reverently. Some will come merely to prove they did.

The power does not strike them blind. It does not raise storms to turn them back. It does not make the hollow impossible to find. A place that must be defended by terror becomes a prison, and the wise power has no wish to imprison a mountain.

Instead, it makes the approach honest.

The paths toward the basin remain where they are, but they lengthen according to the traveler’s haste. A person who comes only to possess the place—through a story, a photograph, a claim, a marked route—finds the slopes repeating themselves. The same ridge returns. The same creek crossing appears twice. Their boots grow heavy. Their companions become tired of agreeing on directions. Nothing dramatic happens; they simply arrive, eventually, at the understanding that they have not come far enough within themselves to enter.

Those who come burdened with grief may find the way more easily.

Not because grief earns a reward, but because it recognizes the terrain. They see the low walls becoming slope and do not immediately call it ruin. They hear the wind’s voices and do not demand translation. If they rest beside the crooked trees, the trees offer no comfort, but their roots make a faint, deep sound in the ground: not speech, not music, only persistence.

A person may weep there without being watched.

A person may leave something behind—a button, a letter, an ash, a name spoken once into a well—but the hollow keeps nothing that should return to the world. Paper softens in rain and becomes fiber in the soil. Metal dulls. Ash goes into water. Names rise in mist and leave by noon.

The wise power also makes one small covenant with the ravens.

No human can understand it, but the ravens do.

When danger comes near—a fire that climbs too high, a blasting crew seeking a quarry face, a survey line intended to turn the upper basin into a destination—the ravens gather before the danger has taken shape. They gather on the rim, in the broken tower, along the old channels. Their calls alter the air. Not magically, not in any way that would satisfy an official report. Yet plans become uncertain. Instruments fail to agree. A storm passes through at the inconvenient hour. A decision-maker dreams of a stone stair disappearing into grass and wakes unable to say why the project now feels wrong.

This is enough.

The power does not preserve every wall.

One spring, after a winter of hard alternating frost and rain, an upper terrace gives way. A whole face of slate slips in the night. By morning, a stair is gone; a narrow bridge has become a tilted shelf under rubble; one black doorway has been closed forever.

The ravens avoid the place for days.

The hares learn new paths.

Water, blocked for a while, gathers behind the fallen stone and then discovers another route. It enters an old chamber beneath the terrace and fills it, slowly, until the chamber becomes a cold underground pool. Pale creatures come to live at its edge. Moss begins at the dampened threshold.

The wise power lets this happen.

It grieves nothing, because grief would imply that the lost stair had failed to become what it was becoming. But it attends to the change. In attention there is honor.

Once, much later, a human reaches the hollow carrying tools.

They are not cruel tools: a brush, a measuring line, a notebook, a small hammer for testing stone. The visitor is careful. They have spent years studying vanished settlements, learning the shapes left by hands and weather. They believe that naming a place can be a form of keeping it from harm.

At the central square, they kneel beside a line of paving and find the faint impression of an old arrangement: stones placed in a curve, perhaps around a tree long gone, perhaps around a fire, perhaps around nothing anyone could now identify.

They begin to draw it.

The wise power stands beside them, though the visitor does not know this. It does not forbid the drawing. Knowledge, too, may be a kind of gentleness. But when the visitor looks up, they see the pale goat-like creature at the far end of the square.

It is standing in an opening between two walls.

Behind it there is no room—only stone.

The visitor sets down the pencil.

For a long time, neither moves.

Then fog comes down from the upper cracks. It passes through the square in slow folds. When it clears, the creature is gone. The visitor’s notebook lies open on the paving. Across the unfinished plan, dampness has blurred the straight lines.

The visitor understands—not as a command, and not with resentment—that the hollow may be studied only to the point at which study becomes a way of listening.

They leave with fewer measurements than they intended.

They tell others there is a place in the high country that should not be made famous. They cannot explain why. Their account is unconvincing enough to protect it.

And so the wise power’s work continues, almost entirely by refusing the grand gesture.

It keeps no walls upright that are ready to fall.

It calls back no vanished inhabitants.

It does not force the goat-like creatures to reveal what they are, nor make the things under the streets comprehensible. It does not brighten the city with sacred fire, because it is already lit by noon. It does not make the wind’s choir into words, because words would make people think the wind had said everything it meant.

It only ensures that the hollow remains allowed to complete itself.

That water may keep finding the channels made for it by hands that are gone.

That moss may keep climbing toward cold.

That snow may settle on the roofless ledges and leave the streets clear.

That roots may enter the houses without being called invaders.

That the mountain may close its hand slowly, gently, without being hurried into devastation or frozen into a museum.

And sometimes, in the hour before dawn, when mist fills the lower courts and every wall has lost its edge, the wise power gives the hollow one gift so slight that no creature there can prove it occurred.

For a few moments, all the airs move together.

The wells deepen their notes. The slates cease their clatter. The narrow slots stop whistling separately and join the low sound rising from beneath the streets. Rav­ens wake in their nests but do not call. The hares remain still under the trees. Even the hidden water seems to pause before falling.

The whole basin breathes in.

Then it breathes out.

Nothing is restored.

Nothing is fixed.

But for that brief interval, the city, the moss, the buried chambers, the trees, the stone, the water, the animals, the weather, and the old unremembered work of human hands are all held in one act of belonging.

Then morning begins, as it always has.

Mist thins.

Drops fall.

A raven leaves the tower.

And the mountain goes on, patiently receiving everything.
