The attention of the benevolent power did not arrive as light.

Light would have been a violence in the Rootward.

It would have entered the shafts in clean, descending blades, made the Kept shield their eyes, caused the pale folds and furred plates to draw tight against themselves. It would have named every chamber at once and ruined the useful mercy of darkness: that one only needed to know the next handhold, the next breath, the next sound.

So the power did not send light.

Nor did it send water.

Water was the first thing lesser mercies would have thought of. Looking upon the dry plain, the thirsty cisterns, the bone-pale soil below the city, they would have opened a spring beneath the central void. They would have made fountains leap in every square. They would have filled jars and ditches, softened the hard fields, washed the dust from children’s hair.

But the wise power looked more deeply than thirst.

It saw that the Rootward had not merely endured dryness. It had become dry in the way a bell becomes bronze: not lacking something, but made by what had been taken away. Water in its chambers would have swollen the old fibers, drowned the small useful hollows, loosened the mineral shelves on which the city rested. It would have brought worms and rot, then roots that reached greedily upward and broke foundations in their search for sun.

And so the power did not send water.

It did not command Below to speak.

This was hardest for those who prayed.

For centuries people had stood at the central bridge and offered their questions to the breathing dark.

*Will the eastern wall hold?*  
*Which child will survive the fever?*  
*Where have the missing workers gone?*  
*Was my mother noticed?*  
*Do you know me?*

Below answered in its old manner: through pressure, closure, room made or withheld, a path that remained open, a path that became impossible. It had never spoken in words.

The power could have changed that.

It could have put a voice into the inhalation. It could have made the root walls say: *Yes. No. Wait. Go left. Your mother was held. The workers are dead. You are loved.*

But such answers, true even, would have made the Kept smaller.

They would have stopped listening with their hands. They would have stopped teaching children the difference between a settling wall and a warning wall, between ordinary fear and the fear that must be obeyed. They would have carried every uncertainty to the bridge and waited for a sentence.

The power had seen this happen in other places. It had seen a people given an oracle so clear that, within three generations, they forgot how to deliberate. It had seen a mountain given a voice and become surrounded by people who no longer heard the weather.

So it did not make Below speak.

It listened first.

For a long time, no one knew it was there.

The Kept noticed only small things.

A listener named Uren, old enough that the skin on his palms had become almost without lines, sat beside a thick cord near the east folds. For two hours he listened to the pressure inside it. Then he lifted his hands and said, “There is another rhythm.”

The others touched the cord after him.

They felt nothing unusual.

“It is not in the cord,” Uren said. “It is in the space between its beats.”

This was considered an old man’s saying. It was repeated with respect and no action was taken.

But over the following days, the Rootward’s sounds grew strangely orderly.

Not quieter. Never quieter.

The dry pods still cracked in the dark. Fibrous veils still scraped. The great ropes beneath the bridge still tightened and loosened with Below’s breath. Yet the noises no longer collided as they had. A sudden sharp split in the western chambers would be followed, after exactly seven slow knocks, by a long low shiver in the north walls. The tremble of the bells above passed down through the foundations and returned, faintly changed, from places where no bell’s vibration should have reached.

Children began to play at finding the pattern.

They tapped it out on the braided bridges.

Three knocks. Pause. One. Long pause. Two.

Their mothers told them not to make a game of deep sounds.

Their grandparents did not stop them.

Then a child was lost.

Her name was Meret. She was six years old, noticed at the Grandmother Post before her name had fully left her elder’s mouth. She had a habit of walking with one hand held out before her, not touching anything, as though she expected the dark to take her hand first.

During the loud splitting season, while everyone was gathered near the grinding floor, she followed a scatter of seed-capsules through a side passage. She went beyond the last lamp, beyond the furred wall that marked the safe way home, and into a section of old growth none of the adults had entered since before her grandmother’s birth.

Her absence was found quickly.

Her mother made one sharp sound.

Every person in the chamber froze.

The sound was not a name. It was not even a cry. It was the shape of Meret’s absence, forced suddenly into the air.

The walls answered.

A thousand fine rootlets tightened at once beneath the mat. Lamps bent toward the west. From the central void came an inhalation so deep that dust rose in small columns from the bridge planks.

Then there was a new sound.

Not from Below.

Not from the walls.

A soft sequence of impacts moved through the floor: close, then farther; close, then farther.

Like bare feet walking where there was no path.

The Kept followed.

They followed slowly, because the floor rose in narrow ridges ahead of them and the first law of the Rootward was not to move when the floor rose beneath one’s feet. They stood still until each ridge settled. Then another formed, always a little farther west.

It led them through an old meal chamber filled with hardened woody stone. Through a collapsed dwelling whose woven panels had been preserved in a root wall for perhaps a hundred years. Along the edge of a cleft that breathed out air colder than any chamber had a right to be.

At last they found Meret asleep.

She lay curled inside a hollow no wider than a cradle, her head resting on a fold of pale root-flesh. Her hands were full of seed-capsules.

Around her, the old growth had opened.

Not cut. Not broken. Opened.

The chamber beyond was vast. It had no lamps, yet it was not entirely dark. The roots there bore a dim internal gleam, not a color but a presence, as if the mineral warmth they drank from the earth had been gathered in their deepest fibers and was slowly, patiently remembering how to be seen.

No one entered.

The Kept knew a threshold when they felt one.

Meret woke as they called to her. She did not seem frightened. She handed her mother the seed-capsules and said, in the small offended voice of a child interrupted in important work:

“It wanted me not to step there.”

“What wanted you?” her mother asked.

Meret pointed—not into the chamber, but upward.

“There,” she said.

There was only stone, roots, and the immense layered weight of the city.

The adults looked at one another.

No one spoke against a breathing wall.

After that, the wise power’s attention was understood differently by different people.

The upper city said the Rootward had become more dangerous. They heard that a child had wandered into forbidden growth and returned with a strange tale. They sent a magistrate with two masons to inspect the western passages.

The magistrate was a practical woman named Salet. She had authorized repairs to three collapsing cisterns after receiving warnings from the listeners, and so she did not mock them. But neither did she kneel to a root.

She descended carrying a bronze measuring rod, a tablet, and an escort with iron-tipped staffs.

At the entrance, an old lamp-tender stopped the escort.

“No iron beyond this point,” he said.

“They are guard staffs,” Salet replied.

“No iron in the flesh of it.”

“I have the right to inspect—”

“You have the right to inspect,” said the lamp-tender. “You do not have the right to injure what you inspect.”

Salet looked into the dark beyond the lamps.

Then she took the iron tips from the staffs herself. She placed them on an entrance shelf beside the oil jars. The guards went on carrying bare wood.

This was the first thing the wise power did for the city.

Not a miracle.

It gave one person, at one useful moment, the ability to recognize a boundary without feeling diminished by it.

Salet measured the new opening. Her rod could not reach across it. She counted footsteps along its edge, then counted again, and got two different numbers. She asked the Kept why they had not recorded the chamber.

“Because it was not there,” said Uren.

“Everything is somewhere before we record it,” said Salet.

Uren placed his hand on the wall.

“Not this,” he said.

Salet might have dismissed him. Instead she stood beside him in the dark for a long time.

At last she felt it: the rhythm between the pulses.

Not an extra beat.

A pause that seemed to have weight.

The power was not in the roots. It was not nestled in the central void, nor looking down from a height, nor hiding in the city’s deepest stone. It was present as a kind of care given to relation. It attended to the spaces in which things touched without consuming one another: city and Rootward, Kept and Below, breath and silence, need and restraint.

Salet lowered her measuring rod.

“What does it require?” she asked.

Uren gave a dry little laugh.

“Nothing,” he said. “That is why it is difficult.”

The city had been taking from the Rootward according to the harvest law. Not perfectly. There were bribes, hidden sacks, desperate winters, overseers who looked away. But the law existed, and its violations were recognizable.

The city had also been taking something else.

Pressure.

Over many generations it had built downward: deeper refuse vaults, heavier storehouses, cisterns widened without proper footings, wealthy homes whose additions spread over old foundations like stone fat. Each builder assumed the earth below was merely there to bear what was put upon it.

The Rootward bore it too.

Below made room.

It shifted, tightened, absorbed, hardened dead portions of itself into pillars. It held up entire streets without any person above knowing where their weight went.

The wise power did not punish the city for this ignorance.

It let the listeners understand it.

One by one they began to report the same unease. Not a warning of immediate collapse. A fatigue.

The phrase passed through the Rootward and was carried up in knotted cords:

**Below is tired.**

At first, the city council objected to the wording.

Stone could crack. Mortar could fail. A cistern could weaken. But how could an underground growth be tired?

The Kept did not argue.

They took the councilors down.

They made them leave their armed guards at the lamp shelves. They led them in single file, hands on guide cords, through passages too narrow for robes of office. They made them stand on the central bridge in darkness while the void inhaled beneath them.

The councilors felt the old planks tremble.

They felt, through the soles of their shoes, the unfathomable slow work of something that had been carrying them all their lives.

One councilor began to pray.

Another asked for more lamps.

Salet, who had come without her tablet, said only, “What must be moved?”

The Kept did not answer immediately.

They listened.

For nine days the listeners sat by the thickest cords. They took turns sleeping near the outer lamps, so their dreams would not press too hard upon the deep walls. They ate little besides seed meal and salt broth. They traced the city’s foundations above in their memories: kiln yards, cisterns, markets, barracks, the old governor’s sunken house, the granaries by the eastern gate.

At the end of the ninth day, Uren gave the city a list.

Not of sacrifices.

Not of people to be expelled.

Of weights.

Two storehouses must be emptied before the next hot season. The eastern cistern could no longer be widened. A planned palace addition must be moved three streets west. Refuse was not to be packed into the old shafts. The dead of the prison district were to be carried farther out beyond the walls, not because Below rejected the dead, but because the city had begun treating burial as disposal.

And the bell tower at the southern gate, whose foundations drove a hard repeating strain into the oldest root cords, was to be rebuilt on a broader footing.

The council protested.

Of course it did.

The granary owners protested most. The southern gate wardens said their tower had stood for four hundred years. The palace architects produced drawings and calculations. Priests accused the Kept of using superstition to gain exemptions. One speaker demanded that the Rootward be cleared, burned, and replaced with proper stone substructure.

That night the bells at the southern gate rang the hour.

Once.

Twice.

On the third stroke, the tower did not fall. That would have been too plain.

Instead, every lamp in the Rootward leaned toward the south.

Every flame, even those shielded in closed niches.

The deep breath of Below stopped.

For the first time anyone remembered, it stopped.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a hand that has been carrying a great weight for too long and has begun to lose feeling in its fingers.

Above, in houses throughout the city, people woke.

Not because they heard anything.

Because they no longer felt the old wooden knock beneath their dreams.

By morning, the council had voted.

The storehouses were emptied. The cistern was reinforced rather than expanded. The palace addition was moved. The southern bell tower was dismantled stone by stone, and its bronze bells were hung temporarily from a timber frame beyond the gate.

The tower’s foundation, when opened, proved split through its lowest course. Beneath it the ground had sagged into a dry, widening seam.

The masons who saw it did not become believers.

They became careful.

This was the second thing the wise power did.

It made care practical before disaster had to make it sacred.

Years passed.

The Rootward changed as it always had. New walls rose. Old hollows closed. A bride’s copper hair pin disappeared from a sleeping chamber and emerged thirty years later in the wall of the salt presses. Children continued to be noticed at the Grandmother Post, or not noticed, and to leave or remain according to natures no one condemned.

But the city changed too.

Not entirely. No city does.

There were still greedy people, foolish people, officials who called caution a burden and patience a tax. There were droughts. There were fevers. There were years when the seed meal harvest was poor and years when too many surface folk came down seeking coolness and had to be turned away.

The wise power did not prevent suffering.

It did not make every ruler just, every child safe, every structure sound.

It did something quieter.

It caused the city to remember, with increasing difficulty to ignore, that it stood upon a living agreement.

The new builders learned to consult the listeners before digging. Their plans included not only walls, drains, and load-bearing columns, but places where no construction would go. Empty spaces appeared in architectural drawings: protected voids, breathing margins, corridors left unbuilt because the earth below had its own work.

Surface children were taught the entrance rules in school, though most never descended.

**Do not cut a cord thicker than your wrist.**  
**Do not sleep against a breathing wall.**  
**Do not answer sounds that call from beyond the lamps.**  
**Carry your dust home.**  
**If the floor rises beneath you, stand still.**

Teachers explained them as old safety instructions.

Some children understood them as more than that.

Carry your dust home: do not leave the cost of your living in a place you do not intend to tend.

Do not answer every call from darkness: not every unknown thing is yours to master.

If the floor rises beneath you, stand still: when the world tells you that you have reached a limit, do not mistake motion for courage.

The Kept did not object to these interpretations.

They did not praise them much, either.

They knew rules became weak when spoken too often away from the place that taught them.

Uren died in a winter of hard winds aboveground. He had asked to be carried to the outer lamps, as was proper, and there he sat wrapped in woven cloth while relatives hummed his family rhythm into their hands.

Before he died, Salet came down to see him.

She was old herself by then, her official seals passed on, her fingers bent with age. She sat beside him without speaking for a while.

At last she asked, “Was it truly a god?”

Uren’s mouth moved in something that might have been a smile.

“The Rootward?” he said.

“The attention. The thing that came.”

He considered.

The great body beneath the city breathed in.

The lamps leaned.

For an instant, Salet thought she felt that weighted pause between pulses: a presence neither claiming nor withdrawing, neither solving nor abandoning.

Then Below exhaled.

Uren put his hand flat upon the living floor.

“It was wise enough,” he said, “not to need us to call it anything.”

After his death, the root-mass near the outer lamps tightened for a season. A crack in the wall closed. Dust vanished from the corners of his alcove. His palm marks remained for years on one thick cord beside the east folds, though no one had carved them there.

And far beneath the city, in the chambers no person entered, the benevolent power continued to do almost nothing.

It kept no throne.

It demanded no shrine.

It received no sacrifices except those already due from everyone who lives among other lives: restraint, attention, repair, the willingness to move when one’s weight has become too much for the thing beneath.

It watched the Kept place their palms against the floor.

It watched the city learn, slowly and imperfectly, to place its weight more gently.

And when Below inhaled, the power made room for that breath.

When Below exhaled, it let the breath pass through stone and sleep and bone, touching each person without possession.

Not a promise that the city would endure forever.

Not even a promise that Below would.

Only the older, sterner mercy:

that for as long as they remained together, neither would be asked to survive by forgetting the other.
