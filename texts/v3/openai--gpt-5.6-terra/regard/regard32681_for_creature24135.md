The benevolent power did not descend at once.

It came first as an attention outside the basin’s own attention: not a gaze from the rim, not the pressure of a hand over the roof of trees, but a patient understanding of what was there. It understood the fullness. It understood that nothing in the basin was neglected. The worms had their warmth. The goats had their ledges. The birds had one another. The warm stone had its ring of unfinished lives. Even the dead wood was not abandoned, because there was no abandonment in a place where every ending was already occupied.

The power saw that the basin was not suffering in the simple way a wounded thing suffers.

Nothing cried out.

Nothing asked to be saved.

And so the power did not split the conifer roof to let in a blade of sun. It did not drain the pool. It did not call the goats down from their ledges or command the heron to fly. It did not make the tadpoles into frogs merely because frogs were what tadpoles elsewhere became. It did not send winter, though winter might have made the place beautiful in another fashion. It did not send spring, though spring might have made it new.

It knew that rescue, when given to a thing that has not consented to change, can be another name for destruction.

For a long while—which was no time at all, in the basin—the power remained beyond the branches and listened.

It listened to water entering soil.

It listened to the microscopic rasp of snails crossing wood.

It listened to the birds’ crowded breathing.

It listened beneath all that, to the warm stone.

There it found something almost too small to call a wish.

Not in the fish, nor the frogs, nor the goats. Not in the basin as a whole, which had become so complete that it could no longer imagine incompleteness. The wish belonged to the stone itself, or to the water touching it, or to the first root that had ever reached down into that depth.

It was not a wish for daylight.

It was not a wish for escape.

It was only this:

That if anything wished to go on, it might be allowed to go on.

The power understood.

The basin had endured. It had held. It had become a perfect shelter from loss by becoming a shelter from sequence. But endurance is not always continuance. A leaf that can never yellow is not necessarily spared death; it may simply be denied the rest of its life. A tadpole that never changes has not been preserved as a tadpole. It has been held at the threshold of itself forever.

The power did not judge this. It did not call it a curse. It did not call it sacred, either.

Instead, it made the smallest possible gift.

At the farthest edge of the basin, where a shale seam disappeared beneath a curtain of moss, it placed a single seed.

It was an ordinary seed in every way that mattered. No jewel-bright husk. No heavenly fire. No writing on its coat. It might have been carried there in the feather of a bird or stuck to the fur of a goat, except no bird had entered and no goat had moved.

The seed lay in the moss.

Nothing happened.

The basin made no room for it. Moss touched it. A beetle passed over it. Water gathered on its smooth dark skin and remained there.

The power waited.

A great while passed.

Then one of the blind white worms near the warm stone turned in a direction it had never turned before.

It did not travel far. It only loosened itself from the living ring around the stone and moved through the peat by the width of a finger. Behind it, water entered the narrow passage it had made.

That water found another thread of water.

The thread found an old root.

The root, softened over ages, opened a fraction.

The fraction became a seam.

At the surface, near the shale wall, one pale flower turned—not opened, exactly, but turned—its small cup toward the seed.

A drop of water fell from the flower into the moss.

It was the first drop of rain to arrive.

Not water already present. Not seepage. Not the endless sweat of stone.

Rain.

It struck the seed with a sound so slight that nothing heard it as sound. Yet the basin changed around it.

The birds lifted their heads beneath their wings.

One goat blinked.

The heron at the pool’s edge raised its beak by the width of a breath.

Far below, a tadpole flexed its tail.

The power did not make the seed sprout. That was not its work. It had only made an opening through which sprouting could become possible. The seed would decide according to its nature; the moss would decide according to its nature; the water, the darkness, the root, the mineral light—each would answer as it could.

And the basin, which had never had vacancy, discovered that vacancy need not mean lack.

A small space opened in the moss.

It was no larger than the hollow left by a snail’s shell.

It was not empty for long. Nothing in the basin was empty for long. But for that brief interval, the place contained something it had not contained before: room.

The power remained outside the conifers.

It did not enter, because entering would have made itself the most important thing there.

It did not speak, because speech would have divided the basin into those who understood and those who did not.

Instead it kept watch over the small opening, not as a guard, but as one keeps watch over a sleeping child who may wake or may dream on.

In time—if time could now enter—the seed sent down a root.

The root was pale at first, almost white enough to resemble fungus. It moved through the moss, not violently, not seeking dominion. It touched a stone and bent. It touched a buried beetle chamber and bent again. It found the old seam of water and drank.

Above the surface, two leaves emerged.

They were dark green, nearly black. Between them formed a bud.

The bud did not open for a long time.

The basin watched it without recognizing that it watched. The frogs did not approach. The goats did not descend. The owls remained in their basalt hollow. The snails crossed nearby and carried minute green traces away on their shells.

Then the bud opened.

Inside was a flower unlike the pale cups at the basin margins. It was not brighter. It gave no scent. It did not make the mineral light stronger.

But its petals slowly changed color.

At first they were the color of wet stone.

Then the color of moss.

Then the brown-red of old leaves.

Then a yellow so faint that it could only be seen by those who had known darkness for a very long time.

The flower did not become all these colors at once. It became them one after another.

The basin received the first visible proof that one thing could be itself, and then not itself, and still remain.

A bird in the roof gave a single note.

It was a small sound. It did not travel far.

But it was a song.

The power heard it, and did nothing.

That was its wisdom.

It did not turn the basin into the world beyond. It did not make dawn compulsory. It did not teach every creature hunger, mating, migration, death. It did not insist that the heron fly, or that the goats graze, or that the warm stone cool.

It left the basin its dark. It left it its wetness, its innumerable close-held lives, its hidden orders and its deep quiet.

It only gave the place permission to become different if different was what, at last, it wished to be.

And after that, changes came very slowly.

A gnat drifted out of its accustomed hovering place and did not immediately receive another in its stead.

A snail climbed beyond the wet side of a trunk and crossed a patch of bark that had begun, impossibly, to dry.

One tadpole grew hind legs.

It did not leave the pool. Not yet. It rested among the algae, bewildered perhaps, or simply resting as all things in the basin had rested. But its small new feet pressed against a submerged leaf.

The leaf gave way.

A ripple traveled across the black water.

It reached the warm stone.

For the first time in centuries, or ages, or no measurable span at all, the stone’s living border shifted.

Not scattered. Not broken.

Shifted.

The wise power, beyond the roof of conifers, bowed without being seen.

Then it went on keeping the world wide enough for every basin within it: wide enough for those that needed saving, wide enough for those that needed protecting from saviors, and wide enough for those that needed only one small, patient opening through which their own next life might enter.
