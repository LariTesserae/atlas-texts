The power did not arrive as a hand descending through cloud.

It had learned, in older worlds, that hands make wounds even when they mean to heal.

It came first as attention.

For a long time it did nothing but attend to the high sea: to the low concussion of the filter-rings, the upward rain of glass herds, the black predators opening their patient mouths beneath the dark. It attended to the rim creatures drinking from thin films of water. It attended to a single blind worm rising too far from its field, to the minute shellmakers choosing grains, to the chimney-backs carrying whole weather systems of smaller life across their plated backs.

Its attention was complete enough that nothing in the basin was lost within it.

Not a death. Not a birth. Not an egg carried over the lip and dashed to foam on the mountainside. Not a wall of young stone cracking under rain and pressure. Not the long electrical exchange between two black flat animals that ended, slowly, with one becoming food.

The power understood that this was not suffering in need of immediate correction.

There was suffering here. Certainly.

There were bodies dissolved alive within armored shells. There were bells collapsing after their passengers had abandoned them. There were young things drawn over the rim before they had learned the pattern of the current. There were creatures born with shells bent wrong, who could not join the vent-rings and spent their brief lives tumbling in the cold outer water.

But there was also continuation.

The worms rose because warmth passed upward through them. The crabs scraped because the metallic films grew. The fish gathered beneath the falls because something in them had discovered, through generations of loss, that the unfamiliar salts changed what they might become. The chimney-back rose, and a thousand hungers organized themselves in its wake.

To remove hunger would be to remove movement.

To stop death would be to make a crowd into a prison.

To smooth every sharp stone would be to erase the reason the basin had become so full of strange, strenuous life.

So the power did not command the predators to eat leaves, because there were no leaves. It did not give the rim creatures warm water. It did not quiet the basin’s loudness. It did not make the central dark transparent. It did not lift the young safely home from the falls.

Instead, it looked for the harms that were not part of the sea’s own difficult making.

There was one.

Deep beneath the central turning, below the black glass cones and the visible roots of the vents, a seam was opening too quickly.

The mountain was young and still remembering fire. Far under the basin, pressure had been accumulating in a chamber of molten stone. In another century, or another season, the roof of that chamber would fail. The crown would not merely spill. It would burst.

The high sea would be thrown into the sky as steam, ash, black stones, and broken bodies. The basin walls would collapse inward. Warmth would become violence. The valleys below would receive not descendants, not mineral pollen, but boiling ruin.

The power saw this.

It also saw a hundred possible outcomes branching from it.

In some, nothing survived except dormant skins within cracks far downstream.

In some, a new basin formed after ten thousand years, colder and poorer, bearing only a few descendants of what had lived here.

In one, the eruption did not come all at once. The mountain released its deep heat through many small failures. New vents opened. The old ones cooled. Some species vanished. Others changed. The sea endured, though it was no longer precisely the sea it had been.

The power preferred that future, but preference was not enough to justify intervention.

It waited.

It watched the creatures nearest the deep seam.

There, where the darkness had always seemed simply dark, the oldest chimney-backs had begun to alter their circuits. They passed over one patch of floor more frequently. Their towers released hotter water. The glass herds avoided the region. The filter-rings nearest it had changed their rhythm, pumping in strange long intervals that sent tremors through the stone.

The sea, in its blind distributed fashion, had felt the danger.

Not understood it.

Felt it.

That was enough.

The power did not close the seam. To close it would be to trap the fire.

It did not carry the animals away. There was nowhere to carry a whole pressure-system of bodies without changing it into something else.

It placed no warning sign in the water, because no creature there could read a sign.

Instead it made one alteration so small that, for several days, no living body noticed.

At the deepest point of the basin, beneath a chimney-back’s slow passing shadow, the power encouraged a flaw in the stone.

Not a break. A yielding.

A hairline path through old volcanic glass, leading sideways toward a buried channel that had once carried fire outward and then sealed. The power softened one mineral boundary, changed the arrangement of a few crystals, allowed a grain of hot brine to enter where it otherwise would not have entered.

The sea went on.

A black predator settled over an armored swimmer.

A rim membrane opened in the spray.

A shoal clicked beneath an overflow sheet.

Then, far below, the sealed channel began to sweat.

Warmth entered it grain by grain. Mineral-rich water followed. The old stone warmed, expanded, cracked, and received more water. Over months, a new vent opened on the inner slope of the basin: first a thread of bubbles, then a plume, then a modest chimney of dark mineral rising like a slow thought from the floor.

The change was catastrophic to some.

A worm field nearest the new vent cooked in a single night.

Three filter-beasts lost their anchorings when the ground shifted. Their ring failed. Smaller animals stripped the exposed tissues before the shells had settled.

The glass herds moved outward. Fish abandoned familiar routes. A chimney-back, passing too close to the new plume, suffered a rupture in one of its towers and trailed rusty fluid for days.

But the mountain did not explode.

Pressure left by a path the sea could absorb.

And because the new vent was different from the old ones—hotter, richer in certain metals, poor in others—it became the center of new bargains.

A microbial skin appeared there that none of the scraper crabs could eat at first. It grew in scarlet fans, bright enough to make the nearby water seem lit from below. Tiny shellmakers began incorporating its mineral remains into their spirals. A type of blind larva learned to graze its edges. The larvae were eaten by the fish; the fish grew darker along their sides; the black predators began to follow the darker fish.

Within several generations, some of the pale blunt-faced fish approached the vent in smaller groups rather than shoals.

This was dangerous.

Many died.

But those that returned carried new salts, as their ancestors had carried salts from the falls. Their descendants developed sensory pits along the jaw. They could detect gradients in warmth no broader than a handspan. They began to find vents before vents fully opened.

The power watched this with something like joy, though it was careful even with joy.

It did not decide that every danger should become a gift.

The sea had its own gifts, and they were often made from loss.

Years passed.

A rim creature, among millions, failed to release its young as dust when the current shifted. Instead it held them within its translucent shell through an extra cold season. Most suffocated. A few emerged larger, already able to cling against the outward pull. They spread into cracks farther down the overflow channels, where no descendant of the high sea had ever made a lasting home.

The power did nothing.

A chimney-back rose bearing a disease in its warm towers: a pale microbial foam that clogged passages and weakened its flow. The animal slowed. Its accompanying ecosystem starved in patches. Small scavengers abandoned it. The great body descended, not to rise again.

The power grieved it.

But did nothing.

Then, in the lower valleys, one of the descendants carried down by the falls encountered a pool poisoned by an old mineral seep. No life had remained in that pool for years. The little armored thing entered it by chance, feeding on a crust of iron bacteria. Its eggs adhered to a wet stone. Rain came. The eggs washed into a fissure. In the fissure they met a kind of darkness and water unlike anything in the crown basin.

The power did not guide them.

It only made sure the fissure did not close too soon.

Not forever.

Not securely.

Only long enough.

This was how it acted.

Not as ruler. Not as rescuer. Not as an engineer arranging a world into painless symmetry.

It preserved openings.

It eased the one pressure that would have destroyed all pressure.

It let the mountain remain young, the sea remain crowded, the predators remain hungry, the falls remain dangerous. It permitted extinctions when they were the cost of change, but not when they were merely the consequence of a preventable, total ending. It did not spare each creature. It guarded the possibility that creatures might continue becoming unlike themselves.

And sometimes, when the basin became quiet for several seconds except for the hiss of overflow, the wise power listened more closely.

Far beneath the green-black turning, something answered.

Perhaps it was a chimney-back moving below the floor.

Perhaps it was the collective vibration of a thousand filter-beasts adjusting their pumps.

Perhaps it was only the mountain settling around its water.

The power did not need to know.

It had not come to make the high sea comprehensible.

It had come to ensure that, for a while longer, there would be something there beyond comprehension: a living basin of heat and stone and appetite, holding its difficult shape beneath the sky.
