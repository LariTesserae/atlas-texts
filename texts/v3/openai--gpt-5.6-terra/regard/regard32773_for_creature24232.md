The power did not descend at once.

It watched first.

It watched through three storms and the long, charged quiet between them. It watched the bell-trees lose their crowns and make new ones. It watched the Still-Walkers brace a cracked shelter against the wind with their own bodies while children slept inside. It watched a root listener turn pale-side-up beneath a grove, and watched the gardeners understand.

It watched an old woman die.

She had been marked years before by a glass moth: black lightning branches across both hands, reaching into her sleeves. In the last days she could no longer walk safely in moving wind. Her family carried her between them to the outer slope after the storm, wrapped in leaf-scales and orange-dish skin. They laid her where the wiregrass had begun to gather.

No one prayed in words.

One person struck a bowl-kite bone against a stone. Another held up a polished leaf-scale to catch what little light moved through the copper haze. The old woman’s eldest child pressed their forehead to the ancestor-stone they had brought from the shelter floor.

Then they left.

By morning the wiregrass had covered her.

The benevolent power understood, then, that this was not a place asking to be saved.

It was a place already saved many times, by those who lived within it.

The Garden had drought without thirst. It had danger without malice. It had hunger, but no waste. Its storms broke what had become brittle. Its winds carried ruin and nourishment in the same breath. The people suffered, certainly. They mourned. They feared. They lost children to a wrong step, hunters to a hidden vent, homes to lightning, whole histories to the blowing dust.

But they were not abandoned.

Their world had rules, even where the rules were fierce. It gave warning, if one learned to hear. It gave food, if one learned not to take more than the storm had offered. It gave death, but did not make death meaningless.

The power knew that an easy hand could unmake such a place.

It could bring rain.

That would be simplest.

One soft rainfall in the High Dry Garden: clear water falling from a blue opening in the haze. The Still-Walkers would emerge in wonder. Children would lift their faces. The bell-trees would gleam. The wiregrass would darken and lie flat. The crater floor would smell of wet stone for the first time in its remembered existence.

Then the orange dishes would rot.

The mineral mouths of the plants would seal or drown. The root-stone would soften. The static lichen would fail. The grazers’ translucent organs, made for dust and charge, would cloud. The bowl-kites would lose the hot currents under the rim. The garden would become a different garden, and all its old treaties would end.

The power could still the storms.

But then the blue bushes would starve. The copper crown would become a dead ornament. The bell-trees, never struck, would grow heavier and more fragile until a merely ordinary wind shattered them all. The insects would cease their vibration. The spores would settle. The Garden’s great music would fade into the silence of a thing that had survived too long without change.

The power could close the old vents.

But the vents had roots below the roots. To seal them might trap pressures that had slept under the mountain since fire still had a tongue. It might turn the crater into a lid.

The power could reveal itself to the Still-Walkers.

It could stand beside the lightning tower in a body of clean light and say: *I have seen you. I am good. Tell me what you need.*

But wisdom knew that even kindness can become a command when it speaks with the voice of the sky. The Still-Walkers might kneel. They might surrender their listening to a greater listener. They might ask the power to decide which storm was safe, which child should climb the tower, which ancestor-stone should be kept, which vent should be fed to lightning.

And then, slowly, their skill would leave them.

Not because the power wished it, but because dependence is a kind of forgetting.

So it did not give rain.

It did not hush the wind.

It did not make the Garden gentle.

Instead, on the fourth storm, it did something almost too small to call a miracle.

The storm came early from the west.

The roof-chimes said east.

This had happened before, rarely: a crossing current high above the crater could make a false report of the dangerous wind. It was one of the oldest terrors in the Garden, because it made wisdom look like error. The people had begun sealing their shelters for a storm that would not come from the direction they expected. They were moving the young bowl-kites’ fallen nesting grass away from the wrong wall. The ash-gray rollers had already started inflating, rolling in confused clusters toward the center.

The power saw what was coming.

A pressure shear above the broken northern rim.

A green discharge would enter low. It would strike the tower sideways, not from above. The copper crown would take part of it, but the hidden rods beneath the stone had been shifted over many seasons by settling root-stone. The charge would travel outward into the blue bushes.

Then it would run along the old buried line of ancestor-stones beneath the shelters.

It would not destroy the Garden.

It would destroy the people.

The power did not shout.

It did not stop the lightning.

It sent one glass moth down from the cliff face.

Only one.

It was not even a living glass moth, though no one there could have known that. It was a shape made from the memory of moths: four transparent wings, a small charged body, a blue-white glimmer in the dust.

It drifted through the haze and settled on the copper crown.

The tower hummed.

A child saw it.

The child was perhaps seven storms old, old enough to know not to touch a blue bush, young enough not to know which warnings mattered most. They were helping their aunt pull resin cloth over a shelter entrance. When the false glass moth landed, it opened and closed its wings three times.

Not fluttering.

Tapping.

The child froze.

They had heard that rhythm once before, in a story told by an ancestor-stone keeper: three taps, then a pause, then three more. The rhythm of a root listener warning that the danger was not where the sound was coming from.

The child looked at the tower.

Then at the wiregrass.

The grass was lifting in thin red threads, but not toward the western shelters. It was drawing itself eastward, away from the tower’s buried rods.

The child made the warning call.

It was not loud. Nothing in the High Dry Garden needed to be loud.

A shard of bell-tree petal struck against a stone: high, low, high.

The aunt heard. She looked. She saw the grass.

Then the whole crater changed its posture.

The Still-Walkers did not panic. They did not ask why. They did not wait for certainty. Certainty was for places where the air stayed still.

They moved.

Shelter mats came down. Children were lifted and carried. The old and injured were brought out into the wind. The blue bushes nearest the tower were covered in ground-sheets of powdered resin and orange-dish skin. Two adults climbed the tower with hooked bone tools and tore loose the copper strips that led into the old earth.

One of them was struck by a small preliminary discharge.

Their cloak flashed white.

They fell, got up, and kept working.

Below, a root listener emerged beside the tower base, then another, then dozens. Their pale undersides all faced north.

The green light came over the crater rim.

It did not look like lightning at first. It looked like the sky remembering that it had once been a sea.

Then it came down.

The discharge struck the tower sideways exactly as the power had seen it would.

Copper burst from the crown in a fan of white and green. The tower cracked from top to bottom. Bell-trees along the northern grove rang so hard that their petals shattered, filling the air with glittering blades. Bowl-kites slammed into the cliff wall. The wiregrass rose in a red column taller than a person and lashed itself flat again.

The charge went down the tower.

It found the severed copper.

It struck outward into the resin-covered bushes, across the bare volcanic stone, and into the old northern vent.

For a moment the crater floor opened its eyes.

Blue fire ran through every salt vein. The black stones glowed from within. The vents gave out their low, felt voices: not one voice, but many, layered so deeply that the people’s ribs seemed to answer.

Then the storm moved on.

When morning came, nothing was wet.

The tower still stood, though split open. Half the copper crown had melted into green-black ropes. The northern blue bushes were dead, their branches reduced to delicate skeletons of glass. Six bell-trees had lost their crowns. The wiregrass lay in tangled drifts against the eastern wall.

Three people had been burned.

One would die before the next dimming.

But no shelter had taken the full charge. No ancestor-stones had burst beneath sleeping bodies. The children were alive.

The Still-Walkers gathered after the storm, as they always did, to assess what had been broken and what had changed.

They found the glass moth at the tower’s base.

Its wings had become ordinary mica.

Its body was a small black bead of fused mineral, warm despite the cold air.

No one claimed to know what it meant.

The child who had seen it first wanted to keep the bead. The elders considered this. Then they placed it beneath the tower, beside the strange red eggs from the vents and the black translucent shed-skins that sometimes appeared after storms.

Not as an idol.

Not as proof.

As a question.

That was enough for the power.

Afterward, it remained near the High Dry Garden, but not above it.

It entered the place in the forms the place could accept.

Sometimes it became an improbable stillness in one chime among a hundred, telling a careful person to look twice.

Sometimes it became a flash on the far side of a polished leaf-scale, leading a lost child away from a vent.

Sometimes it made a root listener turn a moment earlier than it otherwise would have.

Once, in a famine season, it guided a dead bowl-kite’s body into the crater rather than over the outer cliffs. The people took only what they needed. The rest fed the rollers, the listeners, the wiregrass, the soil-that-was-not-soil.

Once, when a young gardener climbed the tower during a charged dusk despite the black marks of a glass moth on their hands, the power did not save them from their choice.

But it made the lightning strike the copper crown before it struck the person.

The gardener came down blind in one eye, with their hair burned away and their palms marked in green-black lines. They lived. They became a reader of damaged chimes, able to hear fractures in ringing things. In later years, they taught children that luck was not the same as permission.

The power did not erase grief.

It did not turn danger into spectacle.

It did not demand worship, because the Garden already had enough things that required attention.

And in time, the Still-Walkers developed a story.

They said that after certain storms, when the tower sang in a note no tool had struck, a small guest might be walking in the Garden.

Not a god.

Gods, as they understood them, would want the wind to obey.

This guest did not.

It loved the Garden enough to let it remain difficult.

It loved the people enough to help them remain themselves.

So when a child heard an unexpected note in the chimes, they were taught not to say, *We are protected.*

They were taught to say:

*Something has noticed. Listen better.*
