The benevolent power did not arrive as a figure.

It had learned, long before it found the plateau, that figures were troublesome things. A figure suggested a scale. A scale suggested comparison. Comparison suggested lack.

This place had no lack.

So the power came as an interval in the rain.

For one breath between one falling sheet of water and the next, the plateau was dry.

Not parched. Not exposed. Nothing curled or faded. The water simply paused in the air, innumerable clear beads held above leaf, basin, bridge, and root-spout. Mist stood among the towers without moving. Drops leaned from the white flower funnels and did not fall. The central lake held the clouds twice: once in its surface, once in the suspended rain above it.

The keepings noticed first.

A thousand small adjustments began and then did not complete themselves. A channel widened its lip. A warm chamber prepared to close against cold. A handrail thickened beneath the remembered pressure of a hand that was not there. Across the plateau, knocks traveled through hollow reeds:

*Water has become possible in a new way.*

The unfolded emerged from their seams.

They came out beneath galleries and from the folded throats of walls. They climbed to the rims of basins. They stood in shallow water with their many feet spread wide. Their dark head-creases opened toward the unmoving sky.

The sleeping tall drew slowly upon their roots.

Their upper crowns inclined inward, all of them, by an amount too small to be called a bow. Far under the central lake, the green-gold brightness passed once from shore to shore.

The power regarded what had been made.

It saw that the plateau was not trapped in beginning.

It was keeping beginning.

There was a difference.

A trapped thing repeats because it cannot continue. A kept thing repeats because repetition is its form of generosity. Every half-open bud, every pale scaffold, every raw mineral seam framed carefully against weather—these were not failures to mature. They were invitations made permanent. The plateau did not wait for completion because it had discovered that completion could be a closing.

The wise power felt, then, the temptation particular to benevolence.

It could grant endings.

It could make the buds open fully and let their petals brown at the edges. It could introduce autumn, tenderness, lineage, grave soil, the strange dark sweetness of things becoming other than themselves. It could give the unfolded names that belonged to them alone. It could make the sleeping tall remember sequences: first rain, second rain, beloved rain, last rain. It could give the thing beneath the lake a face and a voice and a desire.

These would be gifts elsewhere.

Here they might be wounds.

The power did not give them.

Instead, it did something smaller.

Beyond the escarpments lay cold countries, hard distances, weather that had learned violence, and minds that measured a place by what could be removed from it. Someday, perhaps, something from beyond would see the pale towers in the mist and call them useful. It would see the rootfelt terraces and call them material. It would see the lake and call it depth. It would see the unfolded tending their channels and call them simple.

The power knew that a garden could be harmed by being understood too narrowly.

So it placed no wall around the plateau. A wall would have been a thing to test, name, conquer, or defend.

It placed, instead, a courtesy in the surrounding world.

Clouds approaching the plateau would forget to describe what they had seen there. Birds crossing above it would carry away only the memory of green light. Travelers in the cold country below might glimpse, at rare distances, a high valley full of impossible brightness; but when they tried to return, they would find only rain, ordinary slopes, unremarkable moss, and the feeling that they had nearly remembered an important kindness.

Maps would leave a pale blank in that region, not from ignorance, but from tact.

Those who came in hunger would turn aside for some better hunger: a lost child, a river needing a bridge, a field that had dried. Those who came in reverence might reach the outer terraces, but only if they arrived empty-handed and without the wish to possess what they saw.

For them, the plateau would make room.

Not much room.

A dry place beneath a leaf vault. A basin of sweet rain. A causeway becoming firm beneath tired feet. They would be allowed to rest among the keepings and hear the rain turn to music in the great halls. They would perhaps see one unfolded carrying water in the bowls of its shoulders, or a cup-walker drinking with its head inside a flower. They would perhaps feel the long, subterranean attention below the lake.

And in the morning they would leave with no proof.

Only a changed habit.

They would mend a fence rather than replace it. They would leave a basin beneath a leak. They would build a railing around a piece of bare stone. They would notice, when a gap opened, that the world had been waiting to be allowed to bridge it.

This was the power’s gift to the plateau: not safety, exactly, because safety is often a cage built by fear.

It gave the plateau the right not to be made into an answer.

Then it released the rain.

All at once, every suspended drop resumed its fall.

The sound was immense.

It struck roofs, pools, broad leaves, woven walls, the backs of cup-walkers, the open palms of the unfolded. Water poured through its old channels, each channel receiving it as though it had never stopped. The towers exhaled. The breath-keepers woke in their vents. Far below the lake, the moving brightness turned once, slowly, and was gone.

Nothing on the plateau had changed.

But beyond it, in every direction, the world had become slightly more capable of leaving a living thing alive.
