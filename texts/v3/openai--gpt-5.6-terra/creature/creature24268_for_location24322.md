The most numerous residents are not the animals that leave tracks.

They are the low builders: termites, ants, fungi, roots, and the innumerable small things that have agreed, without agreement, to keep the plain from becoming either forest or mud. Their dwellings are beneath the visible country. A mound may show only as a rise hidden under grass, or as a hard knot in the roots of a fallen tree. Beneath it are chambers of different temperatures, nurseries, fungus gardens, stores of chewed wood, corridors polished smooth by generations of passing bodies. Some are old enough that their outer walls have become soil, then hillock, then the support for a shrub or palm. The colony continues inside, patient and blind, with no concern for what has grown over it.

There are ants that live in the leaves.

At night their roads appear along stems and fallen branches: dark, purposeful streams carrying green pieces much larger than the workers themselves. They do not eat the leaf fragments. They bring them down into the earth, into rooms that would look like cultivated fields if a human eye could enter them: pale, folded growths of fungus tended in warm darkness. The ants clip away foreign molds, carry out waste, feed the young, defend the doors. Their civilization is almost entirely concealed, and the land above them is altered by it in small, cumulative ways. A shrub loses a pattern of leaves. Soil appears in a new little fan beside an entrance. A trail becomes bare enough for rain to follow.

Termites are less visible still. They inhabit wood so thoroughly that a log can look whole until touched, then fold apart into brown lace. Their bodies carry wetness from place to place. Their tunnels let air pass down into the soil. Their waste becomes the fine, friable earth in which seedlings find purchase. Some build towers where flooding is frequent, but even these are rarely clean monuments. Moss grows on them. Vines take hold. A lizard uses one side for warmth; a snake follows the lizard; a bird pecks at the opened wall. A termite house is never only a termite house.

Among all this lives a quieter animal, one the plain seems to have made for itself.

It is seldom seen clearly. It passes low through sedges and runner vines, not much taller than the grass at its shoulder. Its hide is dark and close-haired, mottled with the colors of wet bark, old silt, and leaf-shadow. In daylight it lies in root hollows or shallow burrows lined with dry grass. At night it moves along the old flood channels, placing its feet where the ground has already been tested by water, hoof, or generations of its own kind.

It has a narrow head, large ears that turn separately, and a flexible muzzle used to nose aside leaf litter. It eats bulbs, fallen fruit, beetle grubs, soft fungi, and the eggs it finds in the warm edges of rotting wood. It is not large enough to dominate the plain, nor timid enough to vanish from it. It belongs to the middle distances: prey to the larger cats and constrictors, hunter of nests and burrows, seed-carrier, soil-turner, maker of small paths.

The animal does not make a den in the human sense. It makes several resting places and uses none long enough to let its scent become a promise. A hollow under palm roots. The dry side of a termite mound. A mat pressed into tall grass above a flood basin. A space under a fallen trunk where the bark has loosened but not yet collapsed. Each is temporary, though it may be used by the same family for years. The plain remembers these places better than the animals do. A resting hollow becomes a bare patch; the bare patch catches water; water brings frogs; frogs bring snakes; snakes leave skins that birds use in nests.

There are larger inhabitants, too, but they are almost mythic in the darkness.

A heavy grazer moves in the reedbeds, broad-backed and mud-colored, its hooves spreading in soft earth. It opens lanes that later become paths for smaller creatures. It feeds in silence except for the wet tearing of stems. When alarmed, it does not crash away dramatically. It simply ceases to be where it was. The reeds settle behind it, and the plain looks unchanged.

There are cats that follow those lanes. Their coats carry the broken pattern of leaf-shadow. Their eyes hold the faintest available light and return it briefly, green or amber, before the head turns and the darkness takes them back. They do not roar often. A single low call travels poorly through the damp vegetation and is answered, perhaps, by nothing. Their real speech is scent on bark, claw marks on a soft trunk, the disturbance of a path that another cat will understand before dawn.

And in the waterless-looking places, water creatures persist. Lung-breathing fish wait beneath mud in shrinking pools. Crabs live deep in burrows whose entrances are no wider than a thumb. Frogs lay eggs in rain-filled prints left by grazing animals. Mosquito larvae churn in leaf cups. Small turtles bury themselves in damp soil and wait for weather.

Nothing lives alone for long here.

The orchid on the trunk lives on dust caught in bark and moisture drawn from the air. The moth lives on the leaf grown from soil made by fungus and termites. The bat lives on the moth. The snake lives on the frog that lived in rain caught by a hoofprint. The hoofprint exists because a larger body crossed a place where roots held the mud firm enough to bear it.

Even death is not allowed privacy. A dead animal is found first by flies, then beetles, then ants, then the damp white threads beneath the leaf litter. Within days it has become scent, movement, loosened soil, and hunger distributed among hundreds of mouths. Within months it is grass, root, fruit, and perhaps the body of another animal passing through the same darkness.

If the plain has a true inhabitant, it may be this shared process rather than any single creature: the warm, hidden traffic of matter through roots, jaws, bellies, spores, burrows, and rain-softened ground. The animals live there, certainly. The insects live there. The plants live there.

But more deeply, the plain itself lives there—low, crowded, tireless, making room only by filling every room it makes.
