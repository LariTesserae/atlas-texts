What lives in the White Basin is not a people who have mastered water. No one masters it. What lives there is a people who have made kinship with being interrupted.

They are not easily recognized as poor or rich, young or old, when seen from a distance. Everyone wears layers suited to wetness: patched skin coats glazed dark with oil, woolen caps flattened under hoods, trousers bound at calf and thigh with cord, mittens suspended from the neck so they can be dropped without being lost. Their clothes have the look of things repeatedly rescued. A sleeve may belong to a dead grandmother, a boot sole to a boat that came over the pass fifty years ago, a hood lining to an ice bear taken before anyone living was born. Nothing is discarded while it can still be tied, wrapped, wedged, braided, or burned.

Their faces are narrow from the reflected brightness. Most have a permanent squint, deep creases radiating from the eyes, lips cracked pale from cold and mineral wind. Their skin carries the basin’s colors: smoke-brown, wind-red, wet-stone gray, the blue shadow under the eyes of people who wake often to check the water. Children have startlingly clear faces, until they are old enough to begin carrying things; then they acquire the same intent expression as everyone else, the look of someone listening through a floor.

They do not ask one another where they live.

That would be like asking where someone stands in a flood.

Instead, they ask: Which height holds you? Which rope is yours? Who sleeps above you? Whose drain takes your runoff? Which way do you go when the pond rises black? A person is known by the safe places they can reach without asking permission and by the objects they can rescue in darkness. Marriage is less the joining of two households than the joining of routes. A good match is someone whose ladders meet yours in three separate ways.

Old people become maps.

They know which roof beam has survived four generations of thaw, which passage fills from below, which apparent stone is only ice filmed with grit, which wall gives off warmth in deep winter. They remember the names of floods—not dates, but natures: the Flood That Carried the Cradle, the Flood With No Steam, the One That Returned the Dog, the Red Water, the Quiet Flood. Their memories are practical, precise, and treated almost as structural material. When an elder dies, people say that a route has fallen in.

The dead are not buried in the ground. There is too little ground and too much uncertainty beneath it. They are wrapped in waterproof skins and carried to high chambers hollowed into the mountain, places where the stone remains cold and dry. Their belongings are divided according to usefulness, not sentiment. A needle goes to the best sewer. A sleeping fur goes to the household with the newest child. A good rope is cut into lengths and given to those who carried the body. Only a few things are kept intact: a carved cup, a knife with a name, a braid of hair, a whistle that must never again be sounded.

The inhabitants do not say that people die in the pond.

They say that some people are lowered into the basin.

If someone is taken in a flood, the household keeps a place open above the hearth for one full turning of the seasons. Food is set there at first, then only water, then nothing. If the pillar receives black cloth, everyone sees it before breakfast, but no one goes near the bereaved until they have washed their hands in meltwater. After that, the whole town begins carrying things for them without comment: a bundle of sedge, a bucket, a child, a goat too stubborn to move.

Their children are raised early into competence, but not hardness. A child may be too small to cross a plank alone and still know six kinds of knot, the warning taste of spring water, the difference between ice that sings and ice that merely cracks. They learn to sleep through the ordinary sounds of the basin and wake at the unusual ones. Babies are tied into soft harnesses hung high beneath rafters, where they sway gently whenever the lower rooms flood. Their first toys are floating things: carved scraps of wood, seal-bladder balls, little bone boats with no passengers.

They play a game in which one child closes their eyes and becomes the pond. The others arrange themselves on stools, ledges, or shoulders. The pond points without looking. Whoever is pointed at must move upward without touching the floor.

Nobody teaches the game. It simply appears in every generation.

There are craftspeople, though the distinctions are loose. The person who repairs roof skins may also know births; the fisher may be the best singer; the keeper of the bronze awl may be twelve years old one season and seventy the next. Skill is not inherited directly, because inheritance is too fragile a word. It is watched, copied, corrected, and eventually entrusted. A child who does not flinch at heat may become a salamander-keeper. One who notices changes in snow color may be sent to the high shelves with hunters. One who can hold still for long periods might learn to sit beside the pond at night.

Those night-watchers are called, in the nearest outside language, the Unlidded.

They do not claim to communicate with the basin. They deny that anyone could. They sit in shelters facing the steam, wrapped in furs, and observe: the rhythm of bubbles, the direction in which ripples cross, the number of breaths between subsurface knocks, the smell of minerals rising with the vapor. Their records are not written in books. They are cut as small marks into smooth lengths of willow, each stick bound into bundles and stored high in dry walls. A bundle may be older than the house holding it. The Unlidded can read the marks by touch.

They are the only people who will speak of the pillar directly.

They say it is not a marker and not an altar. It is a finger.

Asked whose finger, they tend to shrug, or laugh once without humor, or turn the question aside by asking whether the visitor has ever seen a hand close beneath clear water.

The ice bear belongs to them in the manner that a storm may belong to a valley. It has no owner, but every household knows what to do when it comes. Children are brought in. Fish scraps are laid out. Passageways are left clear. No one makes a sudden sound. The bear is addressed softly, if at all, with kinship terms that differ from household to household: grandfather, wet-mother, old muzzle, the one under the wall.

Some insist it is one animal and has been there forever. Others claim it is a lineage, and that the old bear teaches the young to walk the roofs without breaking them. A few say the bear is only seen when the basin is deciding whether to keep the town another winter.

The goats, by contrast, are treated as fully themselves: greedy, foolish, clever, vindictive, beloved. They are named for their habits and accidents—Split-Ear, Roof-Thief, Auntie, Wet Foot, Last One, Bonebreaker, Milk in the Eye. They know routes that no human can use. In a flood, people watch the goats before they watch the water. If the herd begins climbing toward a particular shelf, families start moving their stores there as well.

The birds are less trusted. Crows are said to bargain with the cracks in the cliff. Ptarmigan are harmless but stupid enough to sleep under a falling ice shelf. The cliff swifts, who vanish into chimneys and emerge coated with soot, are believed to carry messages from one house to another without understanding them. Sometimes a family will whisper a name into the feathers of a swift caught in a net, then release it. If another household hears that name spoken in a dream, it is considered an invitation.

And then there are the things nobody agrees are alive.

A warm draft that moves through sealed rooms before a flood.

A door that appears in a wall of old stone, opens onto a dry room furnished with objects from homes long gone, and is gone by afternoon.

The occasional sound of someone walking beneath the ice when there is no passage there.

A child’s lost mitten found tied neatly to the pillar with red thread.

The basin’s people do not waste energy proving or disproving such things. To them, life is demonstrated by recurrence, appetite, resistance, and consequence. Water lives because it returns, takes, gives, and refuses command. A house may live if it shifts in sleep and keeps making space for its inhabitants. A rope may live for a while, if it holds. A memory may live if it changes what someone does with their hands.

Visitors often think the people are quiet because they are afraid.

That is only partly true.

They are quiet because everything around them is already speaking. The water gives warnings. Ice tells stories in pressure and fracture. Wood answers the cold. Goats announce hidden paths. Steam rises differently before disaster. Even silence is divided into kinds.

What lives in the White Basin, finally, is attention.

It lives in the old woman who wakes before the pond swells. In the child who reties a knot after being told only once. In the goat that chooses a roofline. In the blind trout turning beneath a skin of ice. In the bear pausing among the chimneys, breathing clouds into the hard light.

And perhaps, beneath all of it, in the black water that never quite freezes—something patient enough to let the town remain, provided it remembers that remaining is not the same thing as standing still.
