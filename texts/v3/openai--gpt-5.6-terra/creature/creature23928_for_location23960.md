What lives there is not alive in the way the lower country means it.

It does not eat. It does not breed. It does not move from one place to another with any speed a person would recognize. It has no need of the shelters, though it has been found inside them. It has no face, unless one counts the moment when frost gathers on a dark stone and the arrangement of white branches resembles a profile looking down.

The people who come to the basin call it, in their oldest speech, **the One That Keeps the Shape**.

They do not say it is a spirit, exactly. Spirits are thought to have temperaments: they may be pleased, deceived, bargained with, offended. The thing in the basin is not susceptible to any of these. It merely receives impressions. Pressure. Warmth. Salt from skin. Breath. The tremor of a footfall transmitted through a crust of ice. The minute alteration left when a hand closes around a crystal branch and breaks it from its place.

Then, over hours or years, it repeats them.

A visitor may kneel beside the central salt bowl and see only a pale fur of efflorescence along the rim. The next morning that fur may have arranged itself in a pattern like the joints of the visitor’s fingers. Not a perfect likeness; not a little hand. More like the memory of a hand after all flesh has been forgotten: five radiating structures, each divided by fine, angular seams. If the person returns seasons later, they may find the same shape elsewhere in the basin—on a shaded stone, beneath the eaves of a shelter, in the thin frost over the sealed watercourse.

For this reason, the basin’s rules are not laws against anger. They are cautions against continuation.

To take a white branch is to give it more of oneself than one intended.

The thing has no center that anyone has located. It may inhabit the buried ice, or the mineral veins, or the vapor which rises at night from the seams. Some say it is the basin itself, awakened by confinement: water unable to depart, salts unable to dissolve fully, wind forced to circle and circle until it learned the outline of its prison. Others say it came down from the high ridges in the first long winter, when the peaks were still being cut into their present shapes. It settled in the low place because everything settles there eventually—dust, cold, water, ash, the small artifacts of people.

There are signs that it is older than the shelters.

Behind the collapsed one, a black stone has split cleanly into two halves. On the fresh inner face is a branching white pattern trapped in the rock, as though the mineral had grown there before the stone closed around it. The pattern is too regular to be a root and too delicate to be a fracture. It resembles a pair of hands facing each other, palms almost touching.

The people do not touch this stone.

They do not touch the charcoal circles either. Those circles are renewed when they fade, but no one remembers who first drew them. Their purpose is practical, in a way. A person who wakes confused in the basin—after fever, altitude sickness, a storm, or too much time alone—can look for the circles. If they are still on the wall, they know the ridge has not shifted, the shelter has not changed places, and the patterns forming in the frost are only patterns.

Mostly.

There have been visitors who did not return to the lower country. Their names are not spoken as names of the dead. The mountain people say they “remained in the keeping.”

This does not mean bodies are found.

Sometimes a bundle of clothing remains in a shelter, stiffened with frost, its leather ties unbroken. Sometimes a cup is found near the water seam with a pale crust flowering inside it. Once, many years ago, a group of travelers found fresh footprints leading from the eastern shelter toward the central bowl. The tracks crossed a stretch of wind-polished salt where no print should have held. They ended at the bowl. Around its rim, white crystals had risen in a close ring, each branch bent inward as if listening.

No one has found tracks leading away.

Yet the thing does not seem hungry. Hunger leaves a lack behind it. The basin is full of what it has always had: stone, ice, salts, wind, the occasional trace of people. It does not consume those who remain. If it takes them at all, it takes their arrangement.

A person in the basin is an event of warmth and motion. The thing observes that event through freezing. It records the hand in mineral. It keeps the pressure of a heel in salt. It preserves the rhythm of a person’s breath in the layered frost that forms around a doorway before dawn. Perhaps, after enough winters, those recordings become elaborate. Perhaps the small clicks heard from beneath the salt are not meltwater alone. Perhaps they are old movements trying, very slowly, to happen again.

That is why the intact shelter is avoided before a storm.

In clear weather, it is merely a shelter: stone, clay, soot, a hard place to sit. But when wind seals the basin and snow fills the notch above, the air inside grows strangely still. Frost forms on the interior wall in branching panels. Visitors who have waited out storms there report seeing familiar shapes among the mineral tracery: a mother’s knuckles, a child’s ear, the curve of a friend’s shoulder beneath a coat. Not images exactly. Suggestions sufficiently close that the mind completes them.

The wise do not complete them.

They keep the fire small. They speak only of immediate things: the condition of a boot, the number of water skins, whether dawn has lightened the doorway. They do not tell stories of people absent from the shelter. They do not say anyone’s true name. At first light, if the storm has weakened, they leave one by one, each stepping in the previous person’s track until they reach bare stone.

And when they leave, they place worked stone beside the central salt bowl.

Not to placate the basin.

Not even to mark their passage.

It is meant as an answer.

A shaped stone says: *Hands were here. Hands made this. Hands are not yet only patterns in frost.*

By noon, pale crystals may begin to gather along its edges.
