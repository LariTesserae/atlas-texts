What lives in the valley is not one species, nor even one kind of life. It is a population held together by dampness, labor, and a long familiarity with things that should have ended.

The people of the embankments are called **the low-keepers** by those few who have come down from higher country and left again. They do not call themselves that. Most names are inconvenient in a place where a spoken word can bring water crawling over the floorboards. They identify one another by lamp-patterns, by the rhythm of a hand on a pipe, by the color of a rain cape hung outside a door.

They are narrow people, generally, brown and salt-stained, with the careful gait of waders. Their bodies have adapted to the valley without becoming entirely part of it. Their feet are broad from generations of soft ground. The skin on their shins is pale and tough, marked with old cuts from reeds and metal. Many wear small plugs of wax in their ears, not to block sound but to prevent the low pressure from entering too deeply on the nights when the opening below the excavation stirs.

No low-keeper sleeps facing downward. No low-keeper allows a mirror in the house. Children are taught to look at water only through the gaps of their fingers, and never to count the stars beneath it.

They are born in houses on stilts, but they are named on the mud.

On the first morning after a child can stand, relatives carry them to a preserved footprint near the dwelling: an ancestor’s boot mark, a dog’s paw, the deep oval left by a crane stabilizer. The child is set in it and made to balance while the family shines lamps toward them from the walkway. It is believed that the valley must be shown the child’s weight before it can decide whether to keep them. Those who step out cleanly are likely to live. Those whose foot fills with black water are watched carefully for the rest of their lives.

Some become **sound-watchers**. They work in pairs beside the canals and settling ponds, carrying padded hammers and hooded lanterns. Their task is to listen to pipes, to translate the valley’s pressures into messages. One tap on a main line means the water has found a weakness. Two means a pump is choking. Three slow taps, followed by silence, means that something beneath the mud has shifted position.

The sound-watchers are never old. Either the work preserves them in some strange way, or it chooses younger bodies to wear out. Their faces remain smooth and untroubled while their hands become dense with scar tissue. When they retire, they often move into the drowned sheds and live alone with submerged lights.

There are also **net-families**, who fish the flooded ruts and shallows. They eat the blue-glowing fish only after salting them for three days and holding them beneath a covered lamp for one night. Fish that do not dim in the lamp’s presence are returned to the water immediately. The net-families bring up useful things from the mud: copper wire, intact bearings, rusted tool heads, coins from cities that have not yet been built. They bring up less useful things too.

Small hands, sometimes, made of clay.

Mouthless masks.

Lengths of hair braided around machine bolts.

Once every few years a net will rise full of pale, thin creatures with human fingernails along the edges of their fins. These are not eaten. The family cuts the net loose and lets it drift away, however valuable it may have been.

The dogs live with the low-keepers but do not belong to them. They sleep beneath walkways and in the lee of pump houses. They are hairless or nearly so, except for a ridge of gray fur along the spine. Their faces are elongated, their ears folded shut against the mist. In daylight—which no one in the valley has seen, but which some insist exists elsewhere—they might look merely diseased. At night they seem made from wet ash and old leather.

They never bark.

When they gather on a spoil bank and face the same direction, the people leave lamps burning in their windows and do not cross open water. The dogs are listening for the thing below. Sometimes they dig shallow holes with frantic precision, uncovering old footprints that were buried years before. Sometimes they carry these footprints away in their mouths, as if they were bones.

The birds are older than the settlement and may be older than the machinery. They are called **level birds**, because their bodies refuse all angles. They fly flat over the reeds, their wings seeming to pass through the mist rather than displace it. Their legs are too long, their necks too thin, their beaks black and blunt. Seen up close, their feathers are not feathers but layered strips of something like wet paper.

A level bird will stand beside a worker for hours without moving. It is waiting for the worker’s reflection to arrive.

When it does, the bird may peck at the water. If the reflection pecks back, the worker must go home by a route they have never used before. If the reflection does not respond, the worker is safe for another week.

The insects are not truly insects. They hatch from electrical cabinets, fuel drums, and the warm seams in the ground. Their blinking bodies are made of tiny paired lights separated by a dark middle. When enough gather, they form shapes: a hand pointing toward a channel, a face looking down from a conveyor belt, a slow procession of workers carrying invisible loads. They do not bite. They do not land. But people cover their eyes when passing through a swarm, because the lights can arrange themselves into memories.

The machinery has its own inhabitants.

Inside the pumps live colonies of grease-black eels, each no thicker than a finger and fitted with little mineral teeth. They feed on vibration. When a pump stops, they become restless and begin chewing wires, seals, and the soft parts of anyone unlucky enough to put an arm into the housing. The low-keepers know this, which is why the pumps are never truly turned off. Even an abandoned station will be kept ticking once every few minutes, enough to keep its eels dreaming.

The conveyors host iron moths. Their wings are dull as oxidized tin, and they lay eggs in the creases of moving belts. The larvae consume rust but leave rubber untouched. In places where the moths are plentiful, whole buildings have been eaten down to their clean internal shapes: ladders, bolts, cables, bare skeletons of steel standing in the reeds.

Beneath the dredger lives a single great turtle, or something that has learned the form of one.

Its shell is the size of a barge and layered with slag, chain, old signs, and dead roots. Its head has never been seen. The dredger’s bucket has struck the shell more than once and returned with its teeth flattened. The turtle moves only when the water is still. Each time it shifts, a new island appears somewhere in the marsh, black and steaming, threaded with old cables and bones.

The people leave offerings on these islands: tins of condensed milk, broken lamps, children’s shoes, bits of polished metal. Not to appease the turtle. To convince it that the valley is already occupied.

And below all this is the sleeper.

It is not worshiped. Worship would imply hope of being noticed, and no one wants that.

The sleeper is the reason the water does not know where to stop. It is the reason the horizon stays indistinct and the ground keeps yielding up objects from other times. Its turning makes the pumps hesitate because the pumps are built from ore that once belonged to it. Its pressure passes through mud, water, foundation piles, human ribs, dog skulls, fish bladders, and the round glass bulbs of lamps.

Some say it is a buried animal, vast enough that the excavation was only a scratch in the mud above its eye.

Some say it is the valley itself, trying to wake from the shape imposed on it by engineers.

The low-keepers say nothing. When the lights dim together, they close their shutters, stand barefoot on the floors of their houses, and wait to feel whether the pilings beneath them are still pressing downward—or whether, for the first time in generations, something below has begun to press back.
