They are not, at first glance, a people shaped by deprivation.

They are too green for that.

Not in color, exactly—though many dye their veils, belts, and work-cloths with the black-green resin of the wall gardens—but in manner. They move with the economy of vines seeking a trellis: slowly until there is reason to be swift, then with a sudden purposeful certainty. They conserve nothing that does not matter. They waste almost no breath, no moisture, no gesture, no space that can be made useful. Yet they are not visibly pinched or starved. Their city feeds them with dense crystals, bitter leaf flesh, mineral meal, root-eel castings, and the swollen insides of vapor pods. They are lean, strong in the hands and feet, broad in the ribs, and dry-looking in the way of polished wood.

Their skin tends toward pale brown, ash-gold, or the chalky umber of the chamber walls, often dusted deliberately with mineral powders. The powder is practical: it reflects harsh light, discourages certain insects, closes the pores somewhat during the hottest hours. But it is also identity. A household may wear a particular dust mixed with crushed leaf pigment, shell, pollen, or soot. From a distance, the inhabitants seem to carry fragments of their gardens and masonry on their bodies: one family blue-gray as copper oxidation, another veined in red ochre, another nearly white beneath the sunshaft.

They have large eyes, or seem to, because the dark around them is emphasized with grease and charcoal. Their gaze is trained upward and sideways. In a city where falling heat, moving shadows, changing pipe-notes, and the tilt of a leaf may all be warnings, looking directly ahead is almost a kind of carelessness.

Their mouths are often covered.

The mouth-veils are not the same as masks. A mask conceals; these regulate. They are layered meshes of reed fiber, pollen cloth, hair-fine vine thread, sometimes fitted with small clay beads or shell valves at the lips. In the fierce middle hours, when the sunshaft is most merciless and every exhalation is a loss, conversation becomes nearly silent. The people use the language of hands then: quick signs close to the chest, broad signs in open plazas, small finger movements passed under tables or behind curtains. A palm turned out means *air is wrong here.* Two fingers pressed to the wrist means *wait until the tower changes its note.* A hand held lightly over another’s mouth-veil is not an insult, but tenderness: *save yourself; I understand.*

Their spoken language is mostly for dusk, for the cool interval before the throat-palms open, and for the private chambers where a household has chosen to spend some of its gathered moisture.

When they do speak, they speak oddly softly.

Not because the city is quiet—it is never quiet—but because shouting has no useful place in their history. The wind organs swallow voices. A human cry does not travel far. A name called across an avenue becomes merely another thin thread in the roar. Their speech has therefore become intimate, close-range, rich in breathy consonants that can be felt through a veil or against an ear. Lovers speak with their foreheads touching. Parents teach children by placing a small hand against the throat, so that the child learns a word as vibration before learning its shape in the air.

They call themselves, depending on translation, the Kept Breath, the Inner People, or the Ones Between Drying.

None of these names is quite right.

Their oldest stories say that the first ancestors did not enter the stone to escape the desert. They emerged from it. They were formed in the sheltered hollows where heat met mineral and the breath of many living things accumulated without becoming rain. They do not think of the city as a building in the ordinary sense. It is a body that has agreed to contain them.

The shaft is its eye.

The wind organs are its lungs.

The root terraces are its dense green digestion.

The inaccessible hot cracks are its deeper organs, which must not be disturbed.

This makes the Bellows-Walker difficult to classify. It is not quite an animal in their thought. It may be an intruder, a messenger, a damaged part of the stone trying to heal itself, or an ancestor who entered the hot channels and was altered by them. Different districts preserve different understandings. The workers who maintain the largest organs—called throat-keepers, pipe-masons, or simply the deafened—tend not to speculate. They know that, when the unfamiliar wet boom comes from behind the walls, one does not open panels. One checks the fans, lowers one’s eyes, and lets the deeper body have its movement.

Some children are told that the Bellows-Walker carries all the water that ever existed, hidden in a sealed black skin. This is why it makes a wet sound. Others are told that it is made of old voices, compressed by pressure until they became a creature.

The children believe both versions for a while.

There are few children, and they are conspicuous. A child in the hollow is not merely a new member of a household. It is an event in the city’s balance. More lungs mean more breath, which means more vapor, which can mean more gardens—but also more mouths, more heat, more need. Births are planned through long conversations between households, garden-tenders, root-minders, and the people who listen to the organs. No council rules this absolutely, though the old council bowl remains swept and ready. Instead, agreement gathers slowly across the chamber through cords, signs, and visits.

When a child is born, the household does not celebrate with noise. They hang fresh mica in the nearest shadeway, so that the changing light announces it. Neighbors see the flash and bring gifts: a polished shell scraper, a packet of mineral dust, a braid of ladder-stalk, a single root-eel shed skin for luck. The infant is held beneath the throat-palm leaves at first cooling, where the gathered crystals make the air briefly thick and sweet.

A child’s first lesson is not speech. It is listening with the body.

They are laid against the warm clay bases of the organs. They learn which vibration means rising heat, which means a fan has caught, which means an upper seam is opening, which means danger. By the time they can walk, they can identify their household tower by its pitch. By the time they are old enough to tend leaves, they understand that a wrong sound is never just a sound.

This has made them a people with a peculiar relationship to silence.

They seek it, but do not trust it.

A silent organ is sick, blocked, or dead. A quiet stretch of garden may mean a fan has stopped moving air through the roots. Silence behind a wall may mean a chamber has sealed, taking someone’s breath with it. In the rare protected rooms where the roar is softened by layers of fiber and clay, people sleep uneasily at first. They say that true silence is the sound of the outer land waiting.

Their dead do not vanish from civic life.

The drying galleries are high and bright, arranged where the rising warmth can take what must be returned. Bodies are wrapped not as mummies are wrapped, to preserve them, but as seed bundles are wrapped: in perforated leaf cloth, root fiber, woven bands marked with the person’s labor signs. A pipe-mason may be given a braid of copper wire. A throat-palm keeper may wear a crown of old leaf ribs. A child may be tied with a cord bearing the colors of every household that helped raise them.

For a time, the body remains visible in the gallery.

Visitors climb to it. They touch the wrappings with their foreheads. They speak, if the hour permits speaking. Then, gradually, the gardens take what can be taken: vapor, salts, fibers, dust. Bones are cleaned and used sparingly. Some become tools. Some are ground into plaster for family walls. Some are fitted into the frames of wind organs, where the dead continue to make sound.

This is not considered grim.

To them, the cruel thing would be to seal a body away where its remaining breath could not become leaf, food, shade, or song.

They preserve the empty districts for much the same reason. An unoccupied house is not a failure; it is stored possibility. A swept room waits for a future family, a returning branch of kin, a child not yet born, or a stranger found in some impossible second hollow beneath the desert. Their population has contracted and expanded before. They know this from the maintenance marks: old rows of sleeping shelves crowded with handprints, abandoned kitchens with many small grinding stones, civic halls whose floor tiles have been worn thin by feet no living person remembers.

They do not say the city is dying.

They say it is holding its shape.

There are lineages among them, but not in the usual sense. Households inherit tasks more often than blood. A person born among the throat-palm climbers may be apprenticed to an organ-tuner if their hearing is keen; a child of pipe-masons may become a keeper of the drying galleries if they show patience with the dead. Kinship itself is made visible by cords. Everyone wears cords knotted, dyed, braided, or threaded with small tokens. One cord means those who fed you as a child. Another means those whose breath shares your sleeping chamber. Another marks the gardens to which you are obligated. Another may name a person chosen as beloved, rival, teacher, or inherited responsibility.

In the loudest parts of the city, cords are easier to read than faces.

Their artists are often mistaken for engineers, because no art can afford to be useless for long. They carve the mouths of wind organs so that certain temperatures produce intervals and harmonies rather than mere alarm. They weave awnings that cast patterns useful for telling the sun’s angle. They set fragments of shell, mica, translucent pod-skin, and polished copper into walls, creating slow constellations that move across rooms as the shaft-light changes. Some old plazas are covered in mosaics visible only at noon, when the sun is directly overhead; others are meant for the brief colorless light before night.

Their greatest art may be the tuning of absence.

A skilled organ-maker can shape a tower so that its low note leaves room around it: a kind of hollow in the roar where a human voice can briefly be heard. Such places become courts, confession chambers, marriage alcoves, teaching rooms. People travel far to stand inside them and say aloud what they cannot risk saying elsewhere.

There are also singers, though their singing is not loud enough to oppose the organs. They sing into vessels, into walls, into the broad undersides of leaves. They press their mouths to clay resonators and make the city carry their notes in altered form. A song may emerge from a pipe several streets away as a rhythm under the constant howl. The best singers do not try to make themselves heard. They learn to make the wind speak with them.

Most have never seen open water.

The word for it changes from district to district because no one is sure how it ought to sound. Some pronounce it like a hiss. Some like a swallowed chime. Some make a sign: both hands cupped and then opened, fingers spilling downward. The sign is used for impossible generosity, uncontrolled grief, or a dangerous person who cannot keep anything.

A few elders claim the sunshaft sometimes shows it.

They say that when dust moths cross the light at a certain angle, their wings scatter brightness against the white dome in trembling patterns. For a heartbeat, the ceiling seems to ripple. The children stare upward. The old people fall silent beneath their veils. No one agrees whether this resemblance is proof that water existed or proof that the city has invented a ghost to explain its own longing.

At the yearly opening, the inhabitants become something different from what they are in ordinary days.

They do not hide their mouths then. The city is already spending air beyond all caution. The pipes are screaming, the vines are lifting, heat is rushing up through every street. People climb the terraces and roofs in their oldest work-cloths, their faces striped with white mineral paste. They widen the fracture together. They let the desert light enter like a blade.

And when the seed-pods split, they raise their hands.

Not in prayer exactly. Not farewell either.

They make signs into the unbearable brightness: signs of kinship, signs of direction, signs used for newborns and for the dead. They send no written message with the silver down. They do not know who, if anyone, might find it. But they behave as if another hollow might already exist somewhere beneath another stone, listening through its own roaring lungs.

For those few hours, the Kept Breath become extravagant.

They exhale.

Then the fracture is closed. The chamber settles back into its managed weather. The city resumes its long work: keeping vapor moving, roots fed, pipes tuned, empty rooms swept, old structures mended for those who may never come.

And in the center of it all, under the hard descending sun, they live not as survivors waiting for rescue, but as caretakers of a difficult abundance—people who have learned that life can persist without water, provided it is never allowed to become still.
