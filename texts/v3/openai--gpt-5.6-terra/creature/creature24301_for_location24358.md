What lives in the basin is not one thing, though there is one presence there that can be mistaken for a thing if a person stays long enough.

At first there are only the obvious inhabitants.

The iron bacteria live in the water’s thinest margins, where cold seep meets air. They make the orange velvet around the springs: soft-looking mats that stain a fingertip rust-red and collapse into threads when touched. Beneath them are paler colonies, sulfur-eaters and methane-eaters, spread in skins so fine that a raindrop can break a whole country of them. They are the first mouths of the hollow. They take what has risen from underground—iron, sour gas, minerals, the buried warmth of decomposition—and turn it into a living abundance too small to see.

Everything else comes for that abundance.

Midges hatch in clouds from the quietest pools, their larvae hanging beneath the surface like commas of black thread. Water beetles feed among them. There are broad-backed diving beetles with black shells slick as wet coal, and smaller ones that live at the warm mineral edges, where their legs leave little loops in the surface film. Some have coppery undersides. Some give off a faint click when handled. Their eggs are hidden in the stems of sedge, sealed away from the cold.

The frogs are not many, but each one seems to occupy more of the basin than its body should allow. They are squat, mud-colored creatures, with throats stained yellow or blue-grey depending on what spring they have claimed. Their calls begin in the afternoon and continue after darkness: knocks, barks, low bottle-like booms. A frog in the basin does not merely call for a mate. It declares a patch of water, a favored shelter under a stone, a warm seep in which its eggs will develop before the next frost closes over them.

The newts are quieter. They live beneath the tilted rock plates and in the drowned seams of peat, coming out only in rain or during the dark blue hour before dawn. Their bodies are almost black above, the color of saturated bark, but their bellies are startlingly bright—orange, gold, sometimes a raw red like the inside of a root. When they cross the pale mineral crusts, they seem briefly lit from below. They eat midge larvae, soft worms, frog eggs when they can find them. In turn, they are taken by herons, by water voles, by the basin itself when a shelf of mud gives way.

The water voles have made tunnels everywhere the sedge roots are thick enough to hold a ceiling. Their entrances are not obvious. A dark slit under a fan of grass, a runnel pressed through moss, a smooth patch where a body has repeatedly entered the water—these are their signs. They are brown and round, with wet whiskers and blunt faces, and their confidence is extraordinary. They cross open water under the stare of birds. They climb the raw rim to gather willow bark. They carry pale fungus and grass stems into chambers that flood often enough to keep them moving, rebuilding, adapting. No nest in the basin is permanent. The voles understand this without grief.

There are shrews in the drier margins, if any part of the place can be called dry. They hunt beneath fallen sedge and among the fissures in the clay, tiny frantic bodies with hearts that seem to beat at the speed of rain. There are beetle-eating spiders whose webs are strung low between reed stems and jeweled every morning with water. There are pale larvae in the mud, soft-bodied and blind, feeding on rot. There are snails whose shells have been etched thin and translucent by the mineral water. There are worms that rise from below during the warmest pulses of gas and vanish again before daylight.

Then there are the visitors that have begun, perhaps, to become residents.

The heron comes often enough to know the shape of the basin better than a person could. It lands beyond the rim and walks the last few steps with unbearable caution. Its feet test the ground. Its neck folds and unfolds. It watches the pools not as water, but as openings. When it strikes, the violence is so quick that the surrounding reeds keep moving only after the bird has already lifted its head. Sometimes it takes a frog. Sometimes a newt. Once in a while it comes up with nothing but black water dripping from its bill, and stands motionless as if insulted.

Foxes drink from the safer edges. They leave prints that fill immediately with rain. Field mice come down in the evening for insects. Owls hunt above the basin, almost silent until their wings pass close enough to disturb the sedge. In hard weather, deer may stand at the rim and breathe steam into the hollow, but they do not like the ground. Their hoofprints sink deep, and by the next morning the water has softened their edges into something unrecognizable.

But none of these is the oldest inhabitant, nor the most complete one.

The oldest inhabitant is beneath the mud.

It has no single body. It is a living arrangement: peat, roots, bacterial mats, fungal threads, mineral films, gas pockets, drowned leaves, and water moving through cracks too narrow for light. Some of it is older than the basin’s raw rim. Some of it woke only after the earth split open and new water found it. It does not think in the manner of an animal. It has no gaze, no hunger that can be separated from the hunger of its parts. Yet it responds.

A warm seep fails, and the orange mats fade. Another opens nearby, and within days a new fringe of life appears. A slab of clay falls, damming a channel; water rises on one side, cools on the other, and different creatures gather in each. Roots reach toward the altered wetness. Fungi cross the fallen material. Beetle larvae collect where the current slackens. The basin remakes its population continuously, not by choice but with such precision that choice is not needed.

If there is a creature here large enough to name, it is this: the hollow itself, working through thousands of small lives.

At night, when the seep-water shines faintly and the frogs call from their separate pools, this larger creature seems nearest the surface. A vole entering the mud leaves a blue-green smear. A beetle breaks the mineral skin and sets rings moving outward. Gas rises in a regular pulse from under the peat. The reeds knock together. Ice forms, cracks, forms again.

Nothing in the basin is still long enough to become merely scenery.

The frogs are its voice.  
The bacteria are its digestion.  
The sedges are its hair or fur.  
The newts are bright blood moving slowly through its cold places.  
The heron is a needle passing briefly into it.  
The rain is not weather there, but a continual exchange of skin.

And beneath all of them, in the black, rootless water, something keeps opening its innumerable small mouths and breathing.
