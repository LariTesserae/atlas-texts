Nothing in the basin lives alone for long.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They are the true skin of the drowned plain: bacterial veils spread over mineral frost, gelatinous threads in the cracks of black stone, rust-colored smears around warm seep mouths. Some feed on sulfur breathed slowly from below. Some take iron from the water and leave behind orange dust. Some live inside the chalk-white crusts coating the rocks, making those pale blooms faintly waxy and ridged under close inspection. In darkness they have no color that matters to themselves, yet they stain the basin in a subdued vocabulary of white, gray, ocher, and old red.

Their growth is slow, but they are everywhere the current pauses.

On a ridge that has been swept bare, they return first as a dullness in the stone’s polish. In a sheltered hollow they thicken into soft mats, and the mats gather grit, shell powder, and the fine bodies of the dead. A patch that begins as slime may become, after years, a little mound of living sediment. Then a worm tunnels through it. Then a crab shelters beneath its edge. Then the current shears half of it away and distributes it elsewhere.

The basin is built, in this way, by things too small to see.

Among them move the sift-creatures: translucent worms, bead-eyed mites, pale crustaceans no larger than a thumbnail. They rake bacterial film from the stones with combed limbs. They burrow through mud in restless, branching passages. Some carry grains of mineral frost on their backs, not deliberately, but because their shells grow rough and collect whatever the water offers. In motion, their colonies can make a patch of sediment seem to tremble.

There are blind little animals that never enter open water. Their bodies are colorless except for the dark lines of gut visible through their skins. They know the basin through pressure. A footfall on a nearby stone, the collapse of a mud shelf, the wingbeat of something passing far overhead—these are enormous events to them. They freeze when the current changes direction. They gather by the hundreds in the lee of a pebble and feed until the pebble rolls.

Some have learned the rhythm of burial.

The mud-lamprey is one of these: a narrow, pale animal with a blunt head and a mouth ringed by short sensory fronds. It spends most of its life beneath the sediment, threading through it like a root. When a dense flow comes over the plain, the mud-lamprey does not flee. It drives downward, packing the tunnel behind itself with loosened silt. There it waits in an airless pocket, scarcely moving, its slow blood tolerating a darkness deeper than sleep. When the flow passes, it returns upward through the newly deposited layer, carrying grains and buried larvae in its mouth.

The floor fish are more patient still.

They are not flat in the elegant manner of fish known nearer the surface. They are flattened by necessity, broad-headed and low, with bodies that seem unfinished from above: mud-colored lozenges bearing two dim, movable eyes. Their fins have become thick fringes that can push them a few inches beneath the sediment. Most are buried to the eyes. They feed on whatever crosses their field of pressure: a worm, a drifting amphipod, the soft underside of a careless crab.

Their skin grows the color of the bottom over time. A fish settled among pale nodules becomes gray-white and mottled. One that lives near iron seepage takes on a dim rust hue. Old individuals are almost indistinguishable from the patches of ground they inhabit. Their only betrayals are the slow opening of gill slits and the occasional turn of an eye.

When the silt river enters the basin, the floor fish face into it and vanish.

The eels are different. They belong to movement.

They come out from under the long bruised ridges, where tunnels run through compacted mud and between slabs of stone. Their bodies are pale as peeled root, though their backs are usually darkened with sediment. They have small sealed-looking eyes and a fringe of organs along the jaw that tastes vibration in the water. An eel can find a buried crab beneath a handspan of mud, or trace a seep from the faint change it makes in the chemistry of the current.

Most are thin and cautious. They eat scraps, larvae, wounded things, anything exposed by the shifting ground. But among them are old eels, thick as an arm, scarred white around the head and flanks. These have spent decades in the basin’s deeper troughs. Their mouths are blunt from rooting through gravel. They have seen every ridge covered and uncovered many times. When one moves through a cloud of disturbed silt, it does not swim so much as displace the darkness.

The crabs are the basin’s visible workers.

They are long-legged, jointed creatures with shells the color of drowned stone. Young crabs are nearly smooth. Old ones become encrusted with sponge, mineral bloom, moss, fragments of shell, and the rooted larvae of other creatures. An ancient crab may carry a whole small geography on its carapace: red-brown fuzz in a hollow behind the eyes, pale nodules on the claws, a thread of weed anchored to one rear plate. It walks with such deliberation that debris settles on it before it has taken three steps.

They are not strong in the manner of surface animals. The water has taught them another kind of strength: persistence against pressure, patience under weight.

A crab may spend hours shifting a single stone. It wedges a claw beneath the edge, braces itself against the ground, and pulls until the stone moves one grain-width. Then it rests. Then it pulls again. The reason is not always visible. Sometimes it is making a pocket near a warm seep, where its young may survive. Sometimes it is exposing the pale bacterial growth beneath. Sometimes it is extending one of the low, circular barricades that appear around sheltering crevices.

These barricades are never impressive from a distance. A ring of fist-sized stones. A crescent of shell halves. A row of dark pebbles placed along a current line. Yet they alter the flow. Behind them, edible silt settles. Larvae collect. Soft moss survives. Small animals can feed without being immediately carried away.

The crabs know this.

They return to useful arrangements even after they have been destroyed. Not the same crab, perhaps, nor the same stones, but the same tendency. A sheltering shape made in one generation seems to be made again in the next. Whether they learn by watching, by following scent, or by some older compulsion written into the movement of their limbs, no one in the basin could say.

There are larger scavengers beneath the slopes.

The bone-crawlers resemble crabs only distantly. They have too many legs, soft segmented bodies armored in overlapping plates, and heads that taper into dense clusters of feelers. Their limbs are strong enough to pull apart the dead, but their mouths are delicate. They rasp flesh from fish bones, strip the bacterial film from shell fragments, and enter carcasses through the eyes or gills. A dead animal does not remain recognizable long after bone-crawlers find it.

In a basin where food comes irregularly, they can become very still.

A bone-crawler may tuck itself into a crack for weeks, its metabolism slowed to a buried ember, and wake only when the water brings the chemical trace of decay. Then it emerges, feelers waving, and follows the trail against the current. Several may arrive at once from different directions, as if summoned by a sound too low for anything else to hear.

Above the floor, in the few body-lengths of water that count as open space, drift the veil swimmers.

They are nearly transparent, not because transparency conceals them in darkness but because making pigment costs something. Each has a bell-shaped body, a trailing net of hair-fine threads, and a weak greenish spark buried near the gut. The spark is not light enough to illuminate; it is a private flicker, perhaps a lure, perhaps a signal, perhaps merely a waste product of some chemistry inherited from shallower ancestors.

The veil swimmers rise and fall with the slow changes of water density. They feed on larvae and dust-sized animals. They are easily torn by sediment flows, swallowed by eels, crushed against stone. Yet they are abundant after quiet periods, filling sheltered water with a subtle pulse of motion. In a beam of weak blue-gray light from far above, their threads may briefly become visible: hundreds of near-invisible nets drifting through the black.

Their predators are the gray ones.

The cod-gray hunters are spare fish, long-headed and scarred, with flesh that seems to have been carved from the same cold material as the basin. They travel at the margin between clear water and silt cloud. Their eyes are large but not beautiful; they are clouded, blue-black, adapted less for seeing than for registering the faintest alteration in contrast. Along their sides run sensory pits that read the water’s disturbances.

They do not chase often. Chasing wastes strength.

A gray hunter waits near a ridge, motionless except for the slow work of its gills. It lets the current bring confusion toward it: an eel displaced from cover, a fish driven out of mud, a swarm of veil swimmers compressed against stone. Then it moves once, with an abruptness almost impossible to reconcile with its usual stillness.

The basin rarely keeps a mark of the killing. Silt closes over the disturbance. Scavengers arrive. A few scales settle into the mud. By the next current pulse, the place is blank again.

There are stranger residents near the deepest seep fields.

There, where water emerges from stone with a faint warmth and a mineral taste, grow the chimney colonies. They begin as a chalky nub on a crack in the bedrock. Bacteria thicken around it; mineral precipitate gathers; tiny tube-dwelling animals settle into the crust. Over many years a low branching structure forms, no higher than a person’s knee, white at the base and brown-black toward its tips. It resembles coral only in silhouette. Its substance is harder, more brittle, and continuously dying and rebuilding.

Inside its pores live whole communities: blind shrimp, threadworms, pale snails with shells thin as fingernails, and little red-bodied things whose blood carries the seep’s chemistry through them. A chimney colony is an oasis, but also a trap. The food there is rich. So are the predators. Crabs harvest its outer growth. Eels probe between its branches. When the current is strong, pieces break off and tumble away, carrying their inhabitants into a world that may have no place for them.

The oldest chimneys are not the tallest. Tallness is punished here. The old ones are broad, crouched, and fused to the stone.

Perhaps the basin’s most unusual life is the listening sponge.

It grows in thick gray pads beneath overhangs, where the current is interrupted but not absent. At first it looks like wet sediment caught against the rock. It has no obvious openings, no branches, no bright color. But its surface contracts in slow waves. It drinks the water through itself, filtering bacterial dust and larvae. The interior is threaded with mineral grains arranged in fine patterns, and when pressure changes pass through the basin, those grains shift.

The sponge remembers the changes for a while.

A storm-current above may leave a different tension in its tissues than a tidal pulse. The passage of a gray hunter may make it close one set of pores. The repeated scraping of crabs at a nearby shell station may make it thicken along that side. Whether this is memory in any meaningful sense is uncertain. But creatures in the basin sometimes gather around old listening sponges before a strong flow arrives, settling behind them or beneath their edges.

The crabs do this most often.

They will stand beside a sponge for hours, legs folded, their shells filmed with silt. Then, without any visible warning, they begin to move stones uphill.

In the basin, this may be as close as anything comes to prophecy.

Nothing there is grand enough to rule the drowned country. No creature commands its darkness; no structure defeats the water. Even the old gray hunters are merely temporary shapes in the current. The basin belongs to processes more than to individuals: settling, feeding, coating, burrowing, crushing, carrying.

But life has learned the local grammar.

It grows low.

It holds fast.

It eats what arrives.

It makes shelter out of what was meant to bury it.

And when the water changes the world—as it does every day, slowly or all at once—life does not begin again from nothing. It remains in the seams of stone, in the gut of a crab, in a worm tunnel beneath a new layer of mud, in a patch of bacterial frost waiting under the dark for the current to pass.
