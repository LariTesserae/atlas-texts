What lives there is not a single people, nor a clean ladder of predator and prey. It is a crowded, listening kinship of things that have learned to survive inside another living thing.

The most visible inhabitants are the **rootfolk**—though they may not call themselves that. They are small beside the valley’s animals, broad-limbed and dense-bodied, built for climbing, digging, and holding on when the ground changes its mind. Their skin is often dust-colored, patterned in pale bands or dark mottling that breaks up the body against root and bark. Their hair, if they have it, is kept short or braided tight with tendon and resin; anything loose may be seized by hungry vines or caught in the sucking pores of a breathing trunk.

They have large eyes, but not necessarily eyes made for distance. The gold vault light is unreliable, and much of their attention is given to tremor, scent, pressure, and sound. A rootfolk child learns the valley by foot before language: which surfaces beat faintly with sap, which ones are merely solid; which roots flex before a herd arrives; which breath smells sweet because it carries sleep-oil; which silence means a predator is nearby and which means something below has awakened.

Their speech is not only spoken. It includes throat-clicks, wrist taps, low humming through wood, and strings of teeth or shells hung from paths so that wind and animal movement make recognizable patterns. A settlement might know that a particular rattle means *grazers to the east,* another means *the blue leaves are closing,* and a third—made from hollow stone and a length of dried throat-plate—means *bind yourselves down.*

They do not think of their homes as separate from the valley. A house is a negotiated wound in it.

A rootfolk dwelling begins with a young trunk, a fungal arch, a naturally hollow root, or a knot where several climbing vines have strangled one another without quite killing their host. The inhabitants bend, bind, prune, stitch, feed, and coax. They may smear selected cuts with resin to keep a wall from healing shut, or pack a growing seam with white crystal to persuade it into a useful shape. A house has moods. It may exhale too hot. It may develop hungry nodules in a sleeping alcove. It may begin slowly walking its roots toward a mineral vent, requiring its family to move their possessions each morning until it settles.

The rootfolk are hunters, scavengers, herders, gardeners, and engineers without separating these occupations very much.

They keep certain animals, though “keep” is a generous word. Small **bristle-shearers**, six-legged creatures with shovel snouts and bead-bright eyes, are encouraged around homes because they eat biting larvae and expose good crystal deposits. The rootfolk feed them mineral scraps and wear their shed quills as sewing needles. Some settlements herd bands of bulb-backed runners, marking their flanks with scented resin and following their migrations rather than confining them. The runners are useful for meat, light, hide, parasite harvest, and warning: a runner’s translucent side goes dark several breaths before a major floor-shift.

They also maintain alliances with things too strange to be called livestock.

There are **lantern grubs** the size of a forearm, soft-bodied and blind, which burrow into dead wood and convert certain poisonous oils into a cold green glow. Rootfolk children carry them in damp woven pouches during the darker hours. There are **spine-mites**, tiny armored scavengers, cultivated in bark bowls because they clean wounds and eat rot before the rot becomes something with legs. There are pale, patient **bone-lickers** that live in the walls of old shelters; no one wants them near a living person, but a house with a healthy colony rarely collapses, because the creatures digest weak material and leave behind a lace of hardened mineral.

Not all the tool-users are rootfolk.

In the warm cracks of newborn ridges live the **fingers**, blind long-armed beings seen carrying hot fragments into the dark. At first they appear animal-like: narrow heads, ribbed bodies, many-jointed hands that never stop feeling the air. But they make deliberate arrangements. They stack crystal plates in spirals. They place polished shells at junctions in their tunnels. They have been observed returning lost tools to the edges of rootfolk camps—usually after taking something else.

The fingers and rootfolk have no easy peace. Sometimes the fingers steal infants, perhaps mistaking them for their own pale young; sometimes rootfolk raid their tunnels for heat-stone. Yet they also trade. A rootfolk group may leave bundles of dried moth scale, tendon cord, or fermented sap near a crack. In return, they find fragments of warm mineral, stones with naturally sharp edges, or strange nodules that continue to radiate heat for weeks. The exchange is conducted without witnesses. To see a finger receiving a gift is considered dangerous. It may mean the fingers have decided you are part of the gift.

Higher in the breathing groves are the **crown-nesters**: lean, featherless gliders with folded skin sails and hooked toes, descendants perhaps of some animal that once flew beneath a more open sky. They do not truly fly in the dense valley air, but launch from one trunk to another, steering through warm updrafts from the inhaling forests. They build communal nests from bone, vine, and scavenged cloth. They are clever enough to imitate rootfolk calls, and malicious enough to use that skill.

A crown-nester may cry in a child’s voice from a branch above a dangerous hollow. It may imitate the warning rattle for a nearby herd, then wait for scavengers to arrive. Some settlements tolerate them because they are excellent early warnings of canopy fires and deep movements. Others nail bright dead scales to their roofs to repel them. No one fully trusts a creature that can remember a dead person’s voice.

On the valley floor roam the great **grazing towers**, the furred six-legged giants with crowns of jawbone. Their bodies are ecosystems in motion. Mosses grow in the mats of their lower fur. Pale beetles nest beneath their plates. Burrow-lizards ride them. Small birds—or birdlike things—pick parasites from their gums as the great beasts chew screaming curtains of vine.

A mature tower is not merely an animal to the rootfolk. It is a moving season.

Where it travels, it clears corridors, scatters seed, exposes edible root layers, and crushes dangerous young growth before it can close a path. Its dung is prized: dry, fibrous, hot at the center, full of crushed crystal and viable spores. Settlements sometimes follow a tower herd at a respectful distance for days, harvesting what the animals reveal.

But a tower can also destroy a village without noticing it. Its jaw-crown may rake down an entire aerial shelter. Its sleep-shifts can flatten thorn corrals. When sick, it becomes feverish and violent, chewing plants it would normally avoid; the plants respond with toxins, and the poisoned tower may stagger through the valley broadcasting panic in bellows deep enough to shake loose bark from the canopy.

There are predators that hunt bodies, of course.

**Reed-hounds** travel in packs through the undergrowth: long-bodied, jointed, striped creatures with snapping reed-like snouts and a chorus of throat whistles. They are quick enough to run alongside a stampede and clever enough to use one. A reed-hound pack may drive runners into a thorn field, wait for the thorn field to do its work, and then pick apart the injured animals. Their pups are kept in burrows lined with aromatic roots that confuse larger predators.

Yet reed-hounds do not always attack rootfolk. A settlement that leaves them bones, parasite-rich hides, and the occasional dead bristle-shearer may be tolerated. Some families claim descent from people who learned to whistle with the packs. They wear reed masks and can approach hounds closely during the mating season. Whether this is an inherited skill, a ritual fiction, or a dangerous kind of friendship depends on whom one asks.

More feared are the **saw-mouthed pursuers**, creatures that are rarely seen clearly because they travel inside the moving walls of vegetation. They have little use for open ground. Their bodies are flat and segmented, their limbs hidden in layers of cutting frond, and they feed by entering a vine mass and persuading it to hunt with them. A person fleeing one may think they are running through plants until every leaf around them begins to turn in the same direction.

The rootfolk say never kill a saw-mouth unless it has crossed a threshold into your house. Outside, it belongs to the green. Inside, it has declared itself a guest, and guests must be answered.

There are also things that are perhaps animals only in the loosest sense.

The **pollen saints** appear after great migrations: immense drifting conglomerations of spores, shed wings, living mites, and oil-rich fibers caught in the valley’s conflicting breaths. From a distance they resemble pale robed figures walking through the gold haze. Up close they are clouds with hunger. They settle on carcasses, houses, tower fur, even sleeping people, and begin to grow a temporary body around whatever they cover. A pollen saint can stand waist-high for an hour, trail rootlets, turn toward sound, and release a perfume that fills witnesses with memories that may not be their own.

Some rootfolk follow these apparitions. Others burn resin to drive them away. A few communities believe the saints carry messages from old settlements buried below the root-mat. They gather the dust left behind and mix it into food during rites of remembrance.

And below all of this is the oldest inhabitant.

It may be a creature. It may be the valley’s buried parent. It may be an impossible machine grown over by ten thousand generations of root and bone. No one has seen enough of it to agree.

They call it different things in different settlements: the **Turner**, the **Underback**, the **Sleeping Root**, the **One That Makes Room**.

It does not rise into the valley. It rolls beneath it.

Its passage is felt first as an error in the small lives. Mites abandon their bowls. The lantern grubs go dark. Bristle-shearers press their faces into the ground. The grazing towers stop chewing and spread their six legs wide. Even the vines become still, their usual tremors withdrawing into the dark.

Then the floor hums.

The rootfolk bind themselves to trunks because they have learned that the valley has no permanent shape to defend. A home that cannot bend is a dead home. A path that cannot be abandoned is a trap. Their greatest structures are not walls but anchors, ladders, caches, and signals: things made to survive being moved.

When the Underback turns, it changes the map but not the fact of living. It opens fresh mineral, warmth, cavities, and wounds. Everything hurries to occupy them. The fingers emerge. Crystal-eaters swarm. Seeds awaken. Rootfolk prospect the new ridge at dawn, wary and hopeful, carrying their black-glass wedges and bone hooks.

That may be the deepest truth of the valley’s inhabitants: none of them live *on* stable ground. They live in the interval between one movement and the next.

They are all settlers of a place still being born.
