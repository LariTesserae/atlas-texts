The Stillworks is inhabited by things that have adapted not to darkness, nor to heat, nor even to machinery, but to repetition.

Its creatures do not appear born so much as assigned.

The furnace crickets are first noticed as a movement in the cable trenches: pale articulated backs passing over one another in the amber light, their long rear limbs ticking against metal. Up close, they are not quite insect and not quite crustacean. Their bodies are made of overlapping translucent plates, yellow-white where the heat has baked them and smoky at the joints. Through the thinner sections, one can see slow threads of orange moving inside them, as if each animal carries a little furnace of its own.

They have too many eyes, though most are sealed over with mineral film. The few that remain open are black and glossy, fixed toward warmth rather than light. A cricket will stand unmoving for hours beneath a transformer, its forelimbs spread on the casing, drinking vibration through the metal. Then, exactly when a pressure valve barks or a belt slips a fraction against its pulley, the whole group will skitter at once to another vent.

They rasp mineral crust from pipes with mouthparts like tiny files. They eat the white fuzz of insulation fungus. They groom one another obsessively, picking salt from the seams in their plates and passing it mouth to mouth. Their chittering is not speech, precisely. It is the sound of their legs testing every surface for the continued presence of the world.

The blind doves are warmer than anything else in the chamber.

They live inside the machinery, especially in cavities that ought to be empty: the drum of an obsolete winch, the hollow behind a breaker panel, the broad iron ribs beneath the maintenance platforms. When a person leans close to one of these openings, they may feel a pulse of heat against the face and hear the soft, wet cooing from within.

Their bodies are squat and almost fetal. The gray felt around their heads is all that remains of feathers, packed dense enough to catch dust before it reaches the soft skin below. Their wings have become folded fans of membrane supported by delicate finger-bones. They cannot lift themselves, but they can flatten against a vertical wall and climb with an unnerving patience, their suctioned feet releasing one after another with faint kissing sounds.

They have no visible eyes. Where eyes should be are pale sealed ridges that twitch when the compressors strike. Their beaks are soft at the base and hard at the tips, suitable for nibbling fungal mats from wire insulation or cleaning grease from the wheel-hounds’ plates. A wheel-hound will sometimes stop beside a dove’s nest, uncurling with a series of dry clicks. The dove will reach down, stroke its beak along the animal’s armored ring, and remove a flake of copper-green bloom. Then the hound rolls onward.

The doves make nests from fibers, shed plating, dead cable sheath, bristle-fan blades, and the brown paper insulation that peels from old coils. No one has found an egg. No one has found a chick. Yet every nest contains doves, and every dove has the same soft, exhausted coo, as though mourning a change that never arrived.

The wheel-hounds are the true citizens of the Stillworks.

At rest, they look almost manageable: six low limbs tucked beneath a compact body, narrow snouts nosing through dust, ears reduced to black slits behind the jaw. Their fur is sparse and stiff, colored the gray of old ash. The ring of plates around their middles is what makes them strange. Each plate overlaps the next in a continuous black belt, polished along the outer edge by years—or the absence of years—of rolling.

Their faces are intelligent in the limited, absolute way of animals that know one thing perfectly. A wheel-hound knows its route.

It knows the exact distance between the flywheel guard and the first hazard stripe. It knows the warm patch beside Compressor Three where bell-moss grows in a mat. It knows the narrowness of the basalt slot, the taste of dust inside the eastern pipe, the point in its circuit where the floor vibrates through its belly and tells it that the next turn must be taken.

Nothing persuades it otherwise.

When startled, a wheel-hound folds inward. Its limbs vanish, its head tucks beneath the armored ring, and the animal becomes a hard, spinning cylinder. In motion it makes the chamber’s most recognizable small sound: the sharp, rapid tak-tak-tak of plates meeting stone. A line of them rolling together can sound like rain, though no one in the Stillworks has seen rain.

Their routes have become physical things. The floor beneath them is grooved. Dust is swept into tidy ridges at either side. Their shed plates collect in dark heaps where paths cross but never mingle. The hounds themselves do not collide. At intersections they arrive in intervals so exact that one line passes through another like clockwork.

Some return from the deep pipe powdered in a pale flour finer than any dust elsewhere in the chamber. The substance gathers in the seams between their plates and beneath their chins. Furnace crickets follow them afterward, harvesting it carefully. The hounds do not resist. They merely stand still, legs trembling with the effort of not resuming their circuit too soon.

There are smaller lives as well.

Thread-mites inhabit the fur of the wheel-hounds and the felt of the doves. They are nearly invisible until they gather around a warm joint, where they resemble animated pepper. They feed on skin oils, static residue, and the pale dust carried back from the pipe. When disturbed, they raise their front limbs and become tiny bristling stars.

In the salt cracks live glass worms, colorless coils no thicker than wire insulation. They emerge only when the machinery’s vibration reaches a particular pitch. Then hundreds lift their blind front ends from the floor and sway in unison, sensing the tremor. They do not seem to feed. They may be feeding on sound itself, or on whatever dry current moves through the stone beneath the compressors.

The bristle fans are not passive plants. Their saw-edged leaves turn, imperceptibly but faithfully, toward electrical leakage. A person who returns to the same patch after an hour will find the leaves in the same arrangement, yet somehow angled more precisely toward the nearest copper seam. They make no seeds. At the roots of every mature fan lies a cluster of small red knobs, each one hard as a bolt head. Whether these are buds, eggs, or storage organs is unknown.

The bell-moss is more communal. Its gray fibers communicate across a patch with faint static snaps. When a wheel-hound enters, the whole colony rises slightly, hairs lifting toward the armor. Blue sparks jump. For a moment the moss shines from within, revealing dark nodules buried in its mat. After the hound has passed, the fibers settle back into their old arrangement, every strand exactly where it began.

And then there are the former people.

Not people, perhaps. Not in the ordinary sense. But the Stillworks retains their habits.

A stool remains pulled out from a control cabinet by the exact distance required for someone of average height to sit. The thermos is positioned where a hand would naturally reach for it during a break. The glove in the locker is curled as though it still remembers a hand. Sometimes, in the broad interval between valve barks, the machinery produces a pattern of clicks that resembles footsteps crossing a catwalk. The sound begins at one end, reaches the center, and stops.

No dust shifts beneath it.

The control cabinets have no operators, yet their dials are watched. The animals do this without appearing to. Furnace crickets gather beneath gauges. Doves nest behind panels. Wheel-hounds pass the same switches at fixed intervals, their plates throwing brief sparks against the metal. If a needle were to move, if a lamp were to dim, if a belt were to slacken, the entire ecology might notice before any human did.

Perhaps the animals are not sustained by the machines.

Perhaps the machines are sustained by their attention.

Together they make the Stillworks alive: not flourishing, not dying, not reproducing, not aging. A closed assemblage of heat, mineral, muscle, wire, static, and motion. Every creature has a place in the circuit. Every sound has an answer. Every route returns.

Even the small steel door at the far end has its inhabitants. Wheel-hounds cross before it in their endless lines. Bell-moss grows at the base of its frame, drawing a faint charge from somewhere beyond. A single blind dove sometimes clings to the stone above the red indicator, its pale head pressed against the metal as if listening.

It never scratches at the door.

It never tries to leave.
