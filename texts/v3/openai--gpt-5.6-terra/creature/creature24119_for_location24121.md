What lives there is not a community in the ordinary sense. It is a concentration of thirst.

The first inhabitants are the ones that remember water before it arrives.

They are the grazers: tall, narrow-bodied animals the color of dry grass and old leather, with dark knees and high, listening ears. From a distance they resemble a herd of antelope, though their faces are too blunt and their gait too measured. They do not wander. Each file follows routes older than the rail spur and more reliable than any surveyor’s map: from the thorn slopes to the wetland, from the wetland to the mineral pools, from the pools to the shade beneath the conveyor or the black basalt shelf.

They know which pools are safe after rain and which bloom with bitter salts. They know that a wet-looking channel can become a wall of mud before a cloud has crossed the ridge. When the first pressure change comes in the afternoon—the invisible lowering of the sky before a storm—they turn their heads toward the escarpment. The youngest gather close. The older ones continue feeding, but their tails become still.

Their hoofs are divided and soft-edged, made not for speed over open plains but for the broken shelves and slick mineral crusts of the basin. In the dry channels they leave prints that hold shadow until sunset. Beetles enter those prints. Insects drink from the faint damp beneath them. A whole temporary ecology gathers in the mark of one passing foot.

They are not tame, though they have learned the works.

The pumping house is part of their landscape. The cableway towers are part of their landscape. They pass beneath the moving ore buckets without looking up, except when an empty bucket hits its neighbor with a crack sharp enough to sound like stone splitting. Then the herd tightens briefly, as if remembering some old predator with iron teeth.

At the wetland they stand knee-deep in reeds, drinking with their nostrils flared wide. The water stains their muzzles pale. They do not fight much. In a place with so little water, aggression is expensive. The hierarchy is conducted by position: who drinks first, who waits in the reed shadow, who is permitted to stand nearest the one seep where the water comes cold from under the salt.

The valley also holds the smaller lives that make use of everyone else’s patience.

Dark beetles come out after noon, when the stone is hot enough to hurt bare skin but still holds little shadows beneath it. Their shells are not merely black; they are oil-black, blue-black, black with a dusting of copper. They move in and out of cracks with the deliberate traffic of workers. At dawn some climb the weather mast, the railings, the backs of abandoned tools, and hold themselves against the metal where condensation gathers. A bead forms on a leg, a wing case, a feeler. They drink it before it falls.

There are pale foxes, too, nearly invisible in the dust unless they move. Their coats are the color of salt mixed with ash, with a dark stripe down the spine and ears too large for their heads. During the day they sleep in culverts, under sheets of corrugated iron, in burrows dug beneath discarded concrete slabs where the soil remains cool. At night they follow the edges of the settlement. They inspect every leak. They know the smell of fuel, coolant, carrion, wet rope, human food, and the particular mineral scent of a pipe about to begin sweating at dawn.

They eat insects, eggs, lizards, scraps from lunch tins, the occasional exhausted bird that has struck a lamp. They are clever enough to avoid the striped scavenger, though sometimes a fox will trail it at a distance, waiting for it to open something difficult.

The striped scavenger is the valley’s most disliked and most necessary resident. It has the hunched shoulders and heavy jaw of a hyena, though it is leaner, built for climbing steep ground and enduring long hunger. Its coat is pale gray in daylight, banded in charcoal along the flanks; at night it appears and disappears by sections, first a stripe, then a shoulder, then the low shine of an eye.

It comes down from fissures in the upper escarpment after the machinery quiets. It drinks from the bad valve beside the processing shed, where a dark crescent of permanent damp has gathered beneath the rusted coupling. It gnaws old bones until they become powder. It tears open dead animals left too long in the heat. It raids nests when it can. Its calls are rarely heard, but when they are, they travel through the basin with unsettling clarity: not a roar, not a bark, but a series of broken cries that sound at first like metal being dragged across stone.

The workers dislike it because it takes what they leave outside. The grazers fear it. The carrion birds follow it. The valley would be fouler without it.

Above them, the birds make their own settlement in the cliffs and machinery.

Some are little cliff swifts that nest in the ventilation slots of the pump house, their mud cups hidden behind wire mesh. They sweep through the basin in quick dark bands, drinking on the wing from the wetland and gathering insects driven up by the herd. Some are ground birds with barred throats and long legs, walking the rail spur as though it had been laid down for them alone. At the loading platform they peck at grain spilled years ago and at the pale larval things that live under the dust.

Then there are the great carrion birds.

They perch on cableway towers, on the black uprights of transmission poles, on the volcanic necks above the western gorge. Their wings are broad enough to cast moving shade over a person. They have bare, folded necks patterned in red and blue-gray, and they use the rising evening air with an economy that makes the machines seem clumsy. A tower is merely a new cliff to them. An ore bucket stalled on the line is merely a temporary branch.

They watch everything.

When a grazer calves, they know before the workers do. When floodwater exposes some long-buried carcass in the reed sink, they are already descending. When the striped scavenger comes down, one or two will settle at a respectful distance, pretending indifference.

The people live there, but they do not own it.

There are fewer of them than the buildings suggest. Perhaps twelve at a time, perhaps twenty when a rail consignment is due or a pump fails. They occupy the shaded rooms attached to the works, the trailers beyond the slag windbreak, the narrow dormitory whose exterior has been painted with a faded mural of mountains no one in the valley has seen. They are operators, mechanics, water technicians, drivers, watchkeepers, cooks, and the one person who understands the weather instruments well enough to distrust them.

They are highland people or have become highland people: sun-browned, dry-eyed, careful with speech. Their clothes are practical and repeatedly mended. Dust enters every cuff and seam. Their voices carry across the basin, so they have learned not to waste them. A worker can call from the pump house, and someone at the loading platform will answer without raising their head from a wrench.

They have rituals that are not called rituals.

Every morning, someone checks the drip pans under the pipes. Someone empties moths from lamp housings. Someone walks the wetland edge to see whether the salt has advanced or withdrawn. Someone taps the fuel drums, listens to the generator, watches the cableway line for a bucket running crooked.

The newest workers are told never to park in a dry channel, no matter how clear the sky is. They are told not to leave boots outside after dark. They are told that the foxes will take gloves, that the birds will take meat, and that the goats can open almost anything with a latch.

The goats are not exactly owned, though several bear old paint marks on their flanks. They came first with herders, then with labor crews, then simply remained. They sleep under conveyors and in the shade of ore hoppers. They chew labels off drums, insulation from cables, thorn branches dragged down from the slopes, and any shirt left on a line too low to the ground. Their horns are long and swept back, their coats coarse and dust-colored, their pupils horizontal and unreadable.

They are perfectly at home among the works. A goat can stand on a narrow steel beam above a six-meter drop and appear to be considering a philosophical problem. They climb slag heaps for warmth in the morning. They shelter under the loading platform during rain. In flood season they gather on the high embankment beside the rail line and wait, silently, for the brown water to finish speaking.

There are animals no one sees often enough to name confidently.

A cat-shaped thing moves among the basalt shelves at dusk, marked like broken cloud-shadow. It leaves round prints beside the mineral pools and takes young grazers only in the far upper gullies. Its presence is usually detected from silence: the birds stop calling, the foxes disappear, and even the goats lift their heads.

There are burrowing reptiles beneath the salt margins, pale as old bone, that emerge only in the first warm hour after dawn. There are frogs in one reed-choked sink that may spend years sealed in mud, awakening only after the right rain. There are moths whose larvae live in the warm dust under electrical cabinets. There are tiny translucent flies around the wetland, so numerous in certain evenings that the air above the reeds seems to tremble.

And in the machinery itself live the things machinery always invites: spiders in junction boxes, geckos behind control panels, wasps inside hollow handrails, mice in insulation, ants colonizing the folds of tarpaulin. The workers curse them, then work around them. The place is too dry and too difficult for absolute exclusion. Anything that finds shade, warmth, or a drop of water earns a provisional right to remain.

The oldest inhabitant may be the wetland.

Not the visible water—the surface that shrinks, clouds, clears, turns copper under dust, and sometimes vanishes beneath a skin of salt—but the wetness beneath it, held in black mud and reed roots and seams in the rock. It receives everything: rain from distant slopes, leakage from old pipes, the hoof-churned dirt of the herds, feathers, dung, oil sheen, dissolved minerals, ash from cook fires, the bodies of insects.

It is not pure. Nothing here is pure. Yet it is alive with endurance.

At night, when the last generator stops, the wetland becomes the center of the valley’s attention. Birds settle along the reed margins. Grazers arrive in careful groups. Foxes drink and retreat. The striped scavenger waits on the edge of the lamp’s reach. Workers fill their tanks in low voices, filtering what can be filtered, accepting what cannot.

Above them, the cableway continues for a while, carrying its small black burdens through the blue dark. A bucket clicks. A goat knocks loose a stone. Somewhere high on the cliff, the unseen cat moves from one shelf to another.

Then the valley resumes its great, specific quiet: the quiet of many lives listening for water.
