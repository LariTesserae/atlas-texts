What lives in the service valley is not exactly a workforce.

The people assigned there call themselves keepers when they must use a word. Elsewhere, on manifests and rotation boards, they are listed as cold-core maintenance personnel, high-altitude industrial technicians, structural equilibrium observers. Their names appear beside inspection intervals. Their signatures appear beneath declarations that nothing has changed.

They do not live there continuously. No human body could quite do that. The cold enters through seals. The noise gets behind the eyes. After enough shifts, a person begins to hear the fundamental tone in silence, in sleep, in the bones of their teeth. So the keepers rotate through the rooms beyond the bulkheads: narrow insulated quarters, a warmed galley, a medical bay where everyone’s hearing is tested, a locker room filled with respirators drying in careful rows.

But some of them stay too long.

It is not officially called staying. A keeper may request extended observation duty. They may fail to board the lift at the end of their cycle. They may explain, reasonably, that the Core’s values have become clearer to them than to anyone else; that the vibration pattern has acquired a minor irregularity; that the eastern fan has a sound “like an extra syllable.” The request is sometimes granted. After that, their personal belongings begin to diminish. A mug. A photograph. A spare pair of boots. Eventually there is only a bunk, a headset, a white maintenance suit hung with its sleeves folded inward.

The oldest keepers are said to become tuned.

They walk without visibly reacting to the noise. Their hands find handrails at exactly the right moment. They can tell which bank of compressors is under service by feeling the cold through the floor grating. They do not read the gauges so much as glance at them, as one glances at the face of a sleeping person to make sure the person is still asleep. Their mouths move behind respirator masks, but they are not speaking to one another. They are counting beats.

There may be three such people in the valley at a time. There may be one. There may be none, and the fresh labels may be printed by a machine in a sealed room.

The workers do not fear the Cold Core in a dramatic way. No one tells stories about things reaching from the blue windows. No one claims to have heard voices in the ducts. The Core’s terror is more exact than that. It is the terror of a system that has no detectable fault and therefore no obvious mercy.

A keeper can place a gloved hand against the white segmented casing and feel nothing through the insulation. No heat. No vibration distinct from the rest of the valley. No pulse.

Yet nobody touches it twice.

There are also the plants.

They are alive in the strict old sense: they convert light, retain water in microscopic tissues, extend themselves into favorable places, withdraw from unfavorable ones. But their lives have been pressed flat by the valley’s conditions until they resemble maintenance phenomena.

The grey lichen around the bolt collars remembers every tremor. Its surface has grown in rings so fine that, under magnification, they form a record of the machinery’s unchanging frequency. If a bolt is replaced, the lichen will creep back around its new edge in a pattern that precisely reproduces the old one. The keepers sometimes lift it carefully with spatulas before maintenance, preserving it in small labeled trays. They say the growth is protected because it is rare.

What they mean is that it is useful.

The black-green threads near the heat exchanger lean always toward the same leak of warmth. Their root-filaments enter cable sheathing, grease deposits, microscopic seams in insulation. They have learned to drink from condensation that does not quite occur. When the machinery was once shut down for seven minutes—this was before the current safety regime—the threads all bent at once toward the Cold Core.

No one saw them bend. The security cameras were off during the event.

The pale shrub in the ceramic tray is older than any currently employed keeper. It has no known name. Its leaves are furred with white crystals, and its stem is dark, almost metallic, with a jointed texture like stacked washers. At intervals of several years, it produces a single flower the color of oxidized copper.

The flower does not open.

It forms, hardens, and remains closed until it falls away.

Keepers collect these fallen flowers. They keep them in lockers or carry them down when they leave the valley. They are not supposed to. A flower from the shrub is classified as contaminated material, though no contaminant has ever been identified. In ordinary warmth, the petals unfold after several days into precise radial structures, each one filled with a dust so fine it seems to disappear on the fingers.

Those who have carried the dust away say that machinery near it runs more quietly.

No one has demonstrated this under controlled conditions.

There are smaller inhabitants, too, though no one finds them unless they are looking for something else.

In the seams behind the warmest control cabinets, there are films of dark microbial life, so thin they look like staining. They live on plasticizers, trace hydrocarbons, skin flakes caught in vent filters, molecules stripped from the dry process gases. They grow slowly enough that a century might be a single season to them. When sampled, they prove difficult to kill. Freezing preserves them. Heat improves them. Radiation does nothing obvious. They are not native to the place; their closest relatives are ordinary organisms found in human lungs and soil.

But after generations in the valley, they have developed a strange preference.

They congregate around surfaces that are mathematically constant.

Not warm surfaces. Not nutrient-rich surfaces. Constant surfaces.

The rear casing of Gauge Bank Seven, whose pressure indicators have not moved in eighty-one years, bears a faint velvet bloom on its inaccessible underside. The lock mechanisms of doors that have not been opened are dark with them. They avoid the moving belts, the cycling relays, the rooms where people breathe. They inhabit certainty.

The keepers call this maintenance mold, and remove it whenever procedure requires.

It always returns in the same places.

Then there are the things that may not be alive, but have become too entangled with life to dismiss.

A hanging chain in the western access bay has swung through the same narrow arc for so long that the chain’s links are polished on one side and dusted on the other. Keepers passing it will sometimes touch the lowest link for luck, despite the posted prohibition against interfering with dynamic indicators. The chain has been given names by successive crews. Pendulum. Sister. Old Hand. It has outlasted the people who named it.

The loose washer on the central handrail is another such resident. It vibrates on its edge, always aligned toward the shaft. No one remembers placing it there. It has been removed several times. Each time, after a period ranging from hours to weeks, a washer appears in the same position: perhaps the same washer, perhaps not. The inventory systems do not record an anomaly. New keepers are told not to mention it in reports.

The warning beacons by the exterior platform are inhabitants in their own way. They flash red into the clear, useless brilliance beyond the aperture. Nothing approaches. Nothing departs. Their lamps are old enough that replacement records should exist, but do not. Their timing is fractionally different from one another, except on the anniversary of the Core’s first activation, when they synchronize for exactly one minute.

Nobody knows this because the anniversary is not in the official calendar.

The Core itself lives there, if living is allowed to mean persistence with appetite.

It does not consume fuel in any measurable quantity. It does not exhaust waste. It does not warm its surroundings. It receives flow from below and sends something upward, or receives flow from above and sends something downward; the instruments are clear on the values, but not on the direction. All readings are correct. They simply fail to answer the question a human body asks when standing near a machine: what is it doing?

The blue behind its armored windows is not light in the usual sense. It does not brighten the surrounding metal. It does not cast a shadow from a passing hand. A keeper who looks at it for too long begins to see blue afterimages even with their eyes closed, not as spots but as narrow vertical rooms extending behind the eyelids.

Some keepers report dreams after long observation shifts.

In these dreams, they are not inside the service valley. They are inside the shaft, descending beside white metal segments taller than buildings. The blue radiance shines through cracks. Below them, impossibly far down, they can hear the turbines.

Or perhaps they are above the shaft, climbing toward the roof glass, while the whole valley shrinks beneath them: the rails, the frost, the tiny shrub, the platform open to the white world. In the dream, the valley is revealed not as a place built around the Core, but as a narrow organ grown from it. A cooling fold. A throat. A maintenance scar.

They wake with their headset still on.

By morning, they have usually forgotten the dream.

What remains is a feeling that the valley has been waiting—not for repair, not for rescue, not for an emergency that will justify its existence—but for recognition. It has been operating long enough to accumulate habits. Its machines have learned one another’s frequencies. Its frost has settled into preferred shapes. Its plants have made a small ecology from leakage and vibration. Its human keepers have adapted themselves into instruments of verification.

Nothing in the valley asks to be changed.

That is why the people who live nearest to it become so careful.

They renew grease without adding too much. They replace a cracked panel with one manufactured from the same mold. They remove dust only where the dust might interfere, leaving the scalloped drifts around old bolts untouched. They walk along marked paths, not because the floor is dangerous, but because unmarked footsteps would be new.

And at the end of each inspection, they stand before the white shaft and read the stenciled words that have replaced the older name.

**DO NOT ALTER FLOW.**  
**EQUILIBRIUM IS LOAD-BEARING.**  
**ALL READINGS ARE CORRECT.**

Then they sign the logbook.

Then, if they are still themselves enough to leave, they go back through the bulkhead doors into warmer rooms, carrying the valley’s chord in their chests.

Behind them, the Cold Core continues to live in its narrow artificial ravine: blue, soundless at its center, and perfectly awake.
