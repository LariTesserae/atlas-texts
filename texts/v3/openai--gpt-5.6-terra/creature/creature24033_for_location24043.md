What lives there is not a people, exactly, though from a distance it resembles one.

The oldest inhabitants are the fixed ones: animals that solved the problem of darkness by refusing to go anywhere. Tube worms rooted in mineral seams extend crowns of red, white, and smoke-grey filaments into the passing water. When the chamber trembles, they vanish at once, each becoming a sealed chalk cylinder indistinguishable from the architecture around it. Some grow alone at the edges of hot seepage, but most live packed together, their tubes fused into walls that are always quietly lengthening.

There are clams buried in the black basin mud, their shells pale as knuckles. They do not open toward light. They open toward chemistry. Inside them live bacterial gardens, nourished by sulfur and iron brought upward from the earth. Their bodies are small engines converting poison and mineral heat into flesh. Around their beds, minute white crabs sift the sediment grain by grain, stealing strings of bacterial slime, eggs, molted skin, and whatever soft thing has died recently enough to still matter.

Sponges occupy the high vaults and shaded ceilings. Some are broad and shelf-like, making roofs beneath which fish rest motionless. Some rise as chimneys, their pores drinking constantly. Some have the soft, furred appearance of drowned moss, though they are full of needles and channels and patient internal currents. A sponge may look inert for decades while its cells rearrange themselves, digesting what arrives, healing holes, incorporating stray grains of stone. Tiny brittle stars cling to them in clusters, arms braided into their skin. Their limbs comb the water with tireless, delicate greed.

The shrimp are the city’s crowds.

They gather where warm water meets cold, where the mineral haze is thickest, where bacterial films grow on stone and shell. Seen together, they make a trembling weather: thousands of nearly transparent bodies, each marked with a faint ember of red or blue. They do not swim so much as negotiate. They rise, settle, turn sideways, disappear through holes too narrow to notice. When something large disturbs the chamber, their cloud contracts instantly into cracks and underhangs. When safety returns, the crowd loosens again, spilling into the lanes as if the streets themselves have exhaled.

Among them move larger shrimp with dark, polished claws. These are not merely scavengers. They are gardeners, though no one would call them gentle. They scrape bacterial lawns from the warm stones. They prune feathery colonies. They pick parasites from the folds of fish and worm crowns. A dead animal becomes theirs first, but not for long. In this city, death is never private. Within minutes, amphipods arrive in pale hopping swarms; bristle worms emerge from seams; small crabs gather beneath the body; snails climb it; a fish takes the eyes. By the time the water has carried the last loosened tissues away, the bones have acquired residents.

The fish are mostly narrow things.

There are blind eelpouts with soft white heads and bodies the color of old glass. They live in the drains under the terraces, entering headfirst into spaces that appear too small to contain a spine. They sense the city through pressure: the opening of a clam, the scrape of a crab, the minute turbulence made by a shrimp’s tail. Their mouths are modest, but their patience is enormous.

There are flat fish that hang beneath sponge shelves, almost vertical, scarcely more substantial than drifting leaves. Their eyes are reduced to dark stains beneath skin. Some have sensory whiskers along their jaws and taste the water as it passes. Others emit tiny pulses of greenish light when startled—not enough to expose an enemy, only enough to confuse the boundaries of their own bodies. In the lower streets, where even the grey-blue ice-light cannot reach, a fish may be known only by the brief illumination of its gills as it turns.

There are hunters too.

A black, broad-headed fish lives beneath the central basin’s rim, where the warmer water blurs every outline. It is seen rarely and never all at once. First a pale edge of jaw; then an eye reflecting nothing; then the slow collapse of the shrimp cloud above it. It does not chase. It waits while the city conducts itself around its absence. When a wounded animal enters its water, it opens its mouth, and the current does the rest.

The crustaceans know every boundary.

Crabs live in the lowest corridors, where the sediment is deepest and the flow is slow enough to read. They are white, translucent, rust-red around the joints, or black with mineral dust. Some wear gardens on their backs: bacterial fuzz, tiny worms, hitchhiking larvae. Others are clean-shelled and ceremonial in appearance, with long limbs that tap continuously on stone. Their tapping is not speech in any human sense, but it establishes something like possession: this ledge has food; this crack is occupied; this carcass is claimed until the next stronger claw arrives.

Small octopuses inhabit the older districts, where tube walls have thickened into labyrinths. They are the closest thing the city has to solitary residents, though solitude never lasts. Their skins take on the texture of shell, sponge, sediment, or mineral crust. A resting octopus may be mistaken for a ruined corner until one eye opens. They collect nothing and build nothing, yet their dens become marked by debris: empty clam shells, crab joints, the translucent spines of fish, a few bright scraps carried in from the outer sea. They are thieves of shelter rather than makers of it.

The central warm basin has its own inhabitants, stranger and more temporary.

There, bacteria bloom in films of orange, black, and milk-white. They coat grains of iron and gather in drifts that fold and unfold with the rising water. Tiny grazers move over them—mites of the sea, copepods, thread-bodied worms, larval forms too unfinished to name. Many arrive from elsewhere as specks, settle briefly, feed, molt, mate, and vanish. Some become part of the city. Most become food before they can.

The larvae are perhaps the true citizens.

They enter through the narrow mouths of the chamber from the enormous sea beyond: shells no wider than dust, worms without tubes, fish without fins, creatures that have not yet become their own species’ recognizable shape. The current carries them into streets already full. Some find bare stone. Some settle on a dead shell. Some fasten to the side of a living animal and begin to build. Others drift too close to a feeding crown, a sponge pore, a shrimp’s grasping limbs. The city receives them all without distinction.

A bare surface does not remain bare because of them.

A fallen tube becomes a reef. A reef becomes a wall. A wall shades a crevice. The crevice shelters eggs. The eggs hatch into grazers. The grazers draw fish. The fish leave waste. The waste feeds mats. The mats bind sediment. The sediment gives new larvae somewhere to settle.

Even the algae high near the ice has residents. Minute snails move beneath its green veil, grazing paths so fine they resemble scratches in glass. Flea-sized crustaceans shelter among its threads. Once in a while, a pale fish ascends into that thin twilight and hangs there, where the chamber is closest to the world above. It cannot see the ice, perhaps, but its body turns toward the dimness as though remembering a brightness none of its kind has known.

Nothing living there is fully separate from its dwelling.

The worms are walls. The clams are furnaces. The sponges are roofs and reservoirs. The shrimp are traffic. The crabs are cleanup crews, thieves, masons of overturned shell fragments. The fish are shadows crossing districts. The bacteria are the hidden food supply, the black and white script written on every warm stone. The dead become foundations. The young become mortar. The current distributes everyone’s labor.

And beneath all this living density, in the narrowest seams of basalt, there may be organisms smaller than sight: cells feeding on rock, on hydrogen, on sulfur, on the faint chemical difference between one cold water and another. They do not rise into the city. They do not need its streets. But their slow transformations alter the seep, and the seep feeds the basin, and the basin begins the whole crowded cycle again.

So what lives in that hollow is a multitude with no single center.

A city of mouths, pores, claws, filaments, gills, eggs, hides, and hungry membranes. A city whose inhabitants are also its bridges, towers, gutters, markets, ruins, foundations, and weather.

When the chamber is briefly disturbed and everything folds shut, withdraws, hides, or goes still, it can look empty.

But it is never empty.

It is only holding its breath in water.
