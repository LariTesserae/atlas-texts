The people of the Underwork are not, as those above sometimes imagine, a hidden people in the grand sense. They have no single costume, no underground pallor, no secret mark pressed into the forehead. If brought suddenly into daylight, washed, fed, and given dry clothes, many might pass for farmers, riverfolk, laborers, traders, fugitives, widows, apprentices, old soldiers, children who have not yet decided where they belong.

But they would give themselves away soon enough.

They look upward before they stand. They test a floor with the outer edge of the foot. They do not lean against walls without feeling for dampness, and they know from a wall’s temperature whether there is water, fire, open air, or trouble behind it. They carry things close to their bodies. They can pass one another in passages scarcely wider than shoulders without touching, except perhaps for the brief, habitual palm against a back: *go on; I have you; mind the beam.*

Their hands are the first thing one notices. Nearly everyone’s hands are repaired hands: lime-whitened, scarred by fishhooks and broken crockery, ridged with rope burn, stained black or orange or green according to their work. The nails are short. The knuckles are broad. Children learn to wrap cloth around a leaking pipe before they learn to write their names, if they ever write them at all. An old person may not remember the year a certain flood came, but will remember that afterward the west drain was lined with three lengths of cracked blue tile and that the second tile should never be stepped on.

Their skin tends toward the colors of the place: lamp-brown, flour-pale, soot-smudged, damp-shining. Some spend their days near the upper shafts and carry a narrow band of sun across the bridge of the nose or one cheek, a strange bright inheritance from the world above. Others, especially those who work the deep channels, grow accustomed to dimness so completely that open noon makes their eyes stream. The people say the surface is always shouting, even when it is quiet.

They wear clothes made for catching on things and being caught.

Nothing hangs loose unless it is meant to be useful. Sleeves are tied back with cord. Skirts are hitched through belts. Trouser knees are patched in overlapping colors. Most wear soft-soled sandals made from layered cloth, old leather, and flattened rope, though some go barefoot on familiar routes. A person’s clothing is full of pockets, loops, hooks, and tucked tools: a scraper, a bit of chalk, a wax plug, a bundle of thread, a small knife, a fish bone needle, a twist of fever mint, a key that opens some gate nobody else can remember needing.

The wealthier households possess dry things. A dry blanket. A dry box of papers. A dry store of grain untouched by rats. A room high enough that one can sleep without worrying about floodwater touching the feet. The truly wealthy own knowledge: which surface family can be trusted, which old tunnel still leads somewhere, where the cleanest seep begins during a long summer, how to settle a dispute before it reaches the basin hall.

No one in the Underwork is entirely one kind of worker. A woman who tends the fungus trays may also repair duck pens, trade herbal paste, and be the only person trusted to set a child’s fractured finger. A man who keeps goats may have inherited the songs used to time the basin gates. A child sent to fetch a spoon may return with the spoon, two eggs, news of a blocked drain, and a baby swallow that has fallen from its tube. The settlement makes specialists, but it does not permit them the luxury of being only specialists.

There are repairers, certainly.

Some repairers work in public, surrounded by baskets of brokenness: pot-menders with their drills and wire staples; plaster workers carrying wet lime in shallow pans; rope-workers who can look at a frayed line and name the season it will fail. Others work in places most people avoid: the narrow galleries behind bulging walls, the low crawlways beyond old kilns, the half-flooded joints where timber rests against brick and both have been slowly persuaded to endure another year.

These people are respected, but not admired in the clean, ceremonial way of surface craftsmen. The Underwork’s respect is practical. People make room for them. People stop asking questions when they say, “Don’t put anything on that shelf,” or “Nobody sleep in the blue-tile passage tonight.” If a repairer marks a wall with red clay, even the most stubborn household begins moving jars.

There are also listeners.

They are not appointed as such, though some become known for it. They listen to the water’s different voices in the channels. They listen to roof beams complain. They listen to the swallows, the ducks, the rats, the old people muttering in their sleep. A listener can tell the difference between a leak that can wait until morning and a leak that has already begun deciding which room it wants to destroy.

Children tend to become listeners before adults do, because children are low to the floor and have not learned which sounds to ignore.

The children of the Underwork are not quiet children, despite the low ceilings and the warnings constantly given them. They are fast, ducking, muddy, and difficult to contain. They know routes through storage shelves that adults cannot use. They name the rats. They use old drain covers as sleds on the sloping tiles. They dare one another to touch the blind fish. They keep collections of things that would be rubbish elsewhere: interesting hooks, shells, bottle necks, broken teeth from combs, rusted nails, beetle wings, smooth pieces of tile bearing the fragment of a painted face.

They are given chores early, not because the adults are cruel, but because every small hand can carry something, and the Underwork survives by the accumulation of small carrying. A child may be responsible for checking the clay cups under a particular leak, feeding a hen that bites, turning the fungus trays, keeping a string of onions out of goat reach, or remembering which elderly neighbor needs hot water at dusk. A child who has been trusted with one real task walks differently afterward.

They play at emergencies.

They strike imaginary bells. They build tiny walls from tile shards and then flood them with poured water. They pretend to be surface patrols and hide among baskets. They hold solemn funerals for dead beetles, then forget the beetles entirely when someone discovers a frog in the south drain. Their games are noisy rehearsals for the world they have inherited.

The old people are everywhere and rarely alone. In a place where unused corners fill quickly, old age is not removed from the household and tucked behind closed doors. An old person sits near an oven, or on a platform beside a busy passage, or under a shaft where daylight reaches for an hour each day. They sort beans. They mend strings. They watch children incorrectly doing things and call out advice. They know the past not as a clean story but as a map of alterations.

“That arch was lower before the shaking.”
“Those fish used to be darker.”
“Your mother was born during a western rain.”
“No, do not scrape that mold. We tried it once.”

Some are deep-bent from a lifetime of ducking beneath beams. Some have lungs made rough by lamp smoke. Some have one good eye and can nevertheless thread a needle better than anyone. Their memories are a form of infrastructure. When one dies, people do not only mourn a person. They mourn an access route, a recipe for waterproofing paste, a warning about a particular family aboveground, a dozen names for the same kind of rain.

The Underwork takes in those who have nowhere else to go, though it never says so plainly.

A person may arrive through an upper passage with a bundle, a wound, a debt, a child, or a story that does not bear much examination. They are given work before they are given questions. First they carry water, sweep a drain lip, gut fish, turn compost, haul charcoal, hold a lamp. The settlement watches how they behave around common things. Do they leave a gate unlatched? Do they take more dry space than they need? Do they notice when a child is too close to a brazier? Do they return a tool clean enough to use again?

Trust grows there like moss: slowly, in seams, and only where dampness remains.

There are people who go above every day, and they are slightly different from the rest. They bring the surface down with them in their hair and clothes: dust, leaf smell, cold air, horse stink, market spices, rain that has actually fallen. They are gatherers, traders, scavengers, messengers, sometimes thieves, sometimes merely people with relatives on both sides of the hill. The Underwork depends on them and envies them a little.

When they come back, everyone asks the same questions in different order.

Is it raining?
Were there patrols?
Did the apple trees fruit?
Has the road washed out?
Who died?
What did the sky look like?

The last question is asked casually, almost never directly. The answer matters more than anyone admits.

Some of the above-goers become unable to settle below again. They begin lingering at shafts. They complain that the air is too thick. They dream of distance. Others return from a season outside and find daylight intolerably wasteful: too much space with nothing stored in it, too much weather falling without anyone catching it in a cup.

Animals belong to the population as fully as people do, though no one would say this in a formal meeting.

The goats know which households spill the best peelings and which doors are poorly tied. They recognize certain voices and pretend not to hear others. A goat can halt an entire passage with the simple decision to stand sideways. Hens are blamed for thefts they did not commit because they are available, noisy, and morally unconvincing. Ducks are treated as fools until they reveal that a drain has changed direction, at which point everyone speaks of their wisdom.

Cats have their own territories, their own grudges, and their own informal economy. A good ratting cat is fed first after a flood. An old cat that no longer hunts is allowed to sleep on warm bricks because it has earned that warmth in earlier years. The swallows are never handled unless they have fallen. Children are taught this with unusual sternness. To disturb the swallows is to interfere with weather, air, insects, and luck all at once.

The blind fish are perhaps the nearest thing the Underwork has to elders who cannot speak.

People touch the channel water before releasing them. A cook may mutter an apology while dropping the smallest ones back after a meal. Someone newly arrived is sometimes told, as a joke or a test, that the fish know every secret said over the basin. It is unclear whether anyone believes it. Still, few people speak their worst thoughts near open water.

There are arguments, of course. The Underwork is too crowded for the idea of universal harmony to survive a single afternoon.

People quarrel over smoke, noise, wet laundry, goat damage, borrowed knives, unswept steps, and whether a certain channel belongs to a certain household or to everybody. They quarrel over the correct use of scarce dry timber. They quarrel over children. They quarrel over whose turn it is to take the dangerous upper watch when word comes that patrols have been seen.

But even anger has boundaries. One does not damage another person’s water route. One does not let a feud leave a basin gate unchecked. One does not bring fire into an argument near the grain rooms. And when the cracked bronze bell sounds, every private grievance is expected to become smaller than the next bucket.

Those who cannot live by that expectation usually leave, are made to leave, or become stories told with a particular silence afterward.

The people have ceremonies, though they might call them habits.

When a child takes their first trip alone to the surface, someone presses a little lump of softened wax into their palm, to plug a leak or a crack or, if needed, their own ears against too much wind. When a new wall is finished, the household nearest it leaves a thumbprint in the final wet mortar—not a name, not exactly a blessing, but a record that someone had been there when the wall was still new enough to need hope.

The dead are not easily removed, and so death has been made practical. Bodies are washed with basin water drawn before dawn, wrapped in cloth that has been boiled and mended many times, and carried upward when it can be managed. Not all can be. Some of the oldest dead are said to remain within the Underwork in other forms: in the mortar, in a carved peg, in the bone needle passed down through a family, in the familiar curve of a stair worn by hands that no longer possess hands.

No one finds this entirely frightening. In a place built from its own remnants, it would be stranger to vanish cleanly.

And beneath all these lives—human, feathered, furred, whiskered, winged, pale and blind in the channels—there may be another inhabitant.

Not an animal in any clear sense. Not the hill, exactly, nor the pressure of earth, nor the old works that were there before the present Underwork assembled itself from brick and beam. People do not agree. Some say the shudders are the buried foundations settling into sleep. Some say there is water far below, turning slowly in chambers no one has found. Some say the place itself has grown attentive after so many generations of being patched, fed, cursed, warmed, and relied upon.

When the ground shifts gently and the clay pots hanging from the old wooden wheel begin their thin ringing, everyone stops for a moment.

The goats raise their heads.  
The swallows go still in their tubes.  
A hand closes around a child’s shoulder.  
Somebody looks at the nearest wall.

Then the tremor passes. A pot is set back upright. A duck resumes complaining. Someone says, as someone always says, “It’s only settling.”

And the Underwork, full of those who know how to settle without ever truly being still, continues living.
