What lives in the basin is not quite a town in the ordinary sense. It is a custody.

The people call themselves, when they need a word that will survive being written down, the *level-keepers*. Not pumpmen, though they maintain pumps. Not miners, though they inherit shafts and extraction ledgers. Not divers, though much of their work is done beneath the waterline. A level-keeper is anyone trusted to know what height the water should be, and what it means when it is not.

There are perhaps eighty of them at any one time, though numbers are difficult in a place where rooms are often inaccessible for weeks and people may work alone across several shifts without seeing daylight or one another’s faces. They live in the upper dry chambers, in narrow dwellings built from former stores, breaker rooms, lift stations, and offices. Their homes have raised thresholds and drain channels through the floors. Most have a warm pipe running visibly along one wall, clothed in its gray mineral fur. People sleep near these pipes in hammocks or shallow berth-boxes, wrapped in felted waterproof cloth. The wealthiest families possess old doors that close properly. The poorest have curtains of oilskin weighted with chain.

They are pale, though not with the translucent pallor of sickness. Their skin has the color of limestone dust, old wax, watered milk. The younger ones often have broad pupils and a habit of looking downward, not because they cannot bear light but because the ground is more reliable than distance. Their hair is cropped close or braided tightly under caps; loose hair is disliked, remembered as a thing that clogs intakes, catches on valves, tangles in mask fittings. Their hands are marked by the work: white crescents beneath the nails, rust stains in the creases, flattened fingertips, narrow scars where pressure cuffs or cable sheaths have bitten.

Children learn the basin before they learn any history beyond it. They learn the routes by touch. They are taken along the dry galleries with their lamps shuttered, one hand on a rail and one hand on the shoulder ahead. They are taught which pipes are safe to lean against, which hums indicate flow and which indicate strain, which patches of wall remain warm even in the coldest season. Their first games are games of listening: identifying a pump by rhythm, counting the interval between valve-thumps, finding a familiar person in darkness by the shape of their gloves.

At twelve or thirteen, depending on the family and the demands of the works, a child is given a collar suit. The occasion is neither celebrated nor mourned openly. The suit is tested in a shallow flooded corridor while relatives watch through a window. The child must descend, find a tagged valve, turn it exactly a quarter, and return without stirring the silt. If they panic, the water makes no accusation. It simply closes around them. The level-keepers consider this mercy. Fear, they say, must be taught to move carefully.

Their speech is low and economical. The basin punishes unnecessary noise with confusion; a shouted instruction becomes a vibration in pipes, a mutter in ducts, an apparent voice from another room. So they use taps on railings, lamp shutters, gloved signals, chalk marks, bits of colored wire tied around handles. When they do speak, they often speak in measurements.

“Two fingers above the seam.”

“Warm at the elbow.”

“Brown coming in.”

“Hold the third.”

These phrases carry more feeling than they first appear to. “Brown coming in” may mean bad weather somewhere beyond the ice. It may mean a family will have to abandon its rooms. It may mean everyone will work until their knuckles split, turning valves to keep the water from crossing a familiar threshold.

There are elders, though age is not calculated cleanly. Old people are those who remember a previous arrangement of the works: a stairwell before it flooded, a boiler room before its ceiling came down, a gallery when it still had windows instead of green-black pressure on the other side. They are valued less for authority than for their internal maps. An elder might sit wrapped in blankets beside a gauge panel, barely able to walk, and tell a repair crew that behind the fourth plate in the west wall there is an unused drain, or that the lower east lock has a second latch that can only be reached from beneath.

The dead remain nearby. Cold preserves them longer than the level-keepers would prefer. Bodies are not sent upward; there is nowhere upward to send them, and in any case the ice is not thought to be a door. The dead are sealed into old maintenance lockers, wrapped in absorbent cloth and weighted with limestone chips. Their names are scratched into copper tags. When the water rises into a burial gallery, the lockers are moved if possible. If not, the room is marked on every surviving map, and no one enters without speaking the names softly through their collar.

Some people say the dead assist with maintenance. Not in the way of ghosts walking corridors, or hands turning valves in the dark. The level-keepers are not sentimental enough for that. But an old worker’s habits remain in the town: a certain wrench always found hanging where they left it; a door that has never jammed since their death; a pressure gauge that seems to read true only when its cracked glass is tapped twice. These are not miracles. They are continuities. In the basin, continuity is close enough.

The level-keepers do not worship the shaft, though outsiders—if there have ever been outsiders—would call their attention to it religious. They keep lamps around its gallery. They record its sounds. They lower weighted lines and bring them back wet, measuring the depth of the wetness on the rope. They maintain books of pressure readings going back generations, written in cramped waterproof ink. The oldest books contain graphs whose axes no one entirely understands. Still, every new reading is added.

They believe, or act as if they believe, that the shaft is under pressure from below and must be answered with pressure from above. Water is the answer. The correct water level is a sentence spoken continuously, one that must not falter.

What lies below is never named the same way twice.

Among the practical workers it is called the Upward Force.

Among children, before adults correct them, it is called the Sleeper.

In the oldest maintenance logs it is merely marked by a blank space beneath the words: **DO NOT REDUCE HEAD**.

Sometimes, when the pumps alter their rhythm, a deep current rises through the shaft. It does not splash. It does not visibly move the water at first. Instead, the lamps around the iron gallery dim at their edges, and everyone nearby feels a fullness in the jaw and behind the eyes. Tools tremble on their hooks. Bubbles emerge from cracks in the shaft lining, but rise slowly, as if reluctant to leave. During these events the level-keepers close the surrounding doors, take their hands off the rails, and wait.

No one claims to have seen anything in the shaft.

A few claim it has seen them.

There are other residents, less ambiguous.

The blind shrimp are called seam-lice by the workers, though they are treated with a degree of respect. They live in the warm channels and clogged filter beds, where they comb the mineral film with their fine pale limbs. Children once collected them in jars, until a generation of keepers noticed that filters with shrimp stayed clear longer. Since then, no one removes them. When shrimp gather densely at an intake, a maintenance crew is sent to inspect the flow. The creatures are not understood, but their preference is trusted.

There are colonies of thread-worms in the insulated conduits, almost invisible against the black cable bundles. They feed on algae, mineral slime, and perhaps the softened outer layers of old rubber. They are harmless until a conduit warms too much, at which point they mass around it in pale knots. This is useful. A knot of worms means heat is escaping somewhere it should not. The workers call them little inspectors.

The lichen on the exterior walls is not merely vegetation. It has entered the local accounting. Its black islands expand or retreat according to mineral seepage, pressure shifts, and the slow chemistry of the water. Certain families keep lichen charts as carefully as others keep pump logs. A change in one of the larger patches can announce trouble months before a gauge registers it. The black lichen has no known use, but it is watched with the intense politeness reserved for a neighbor who never speaks and always knows when someone has been ill.

The occasional fish from the upper world becomes, briefly, a citizen.

No one touches it. No one tries to feed it. Lamps are dimmed in the galleries where it circles. People make quiet notes of its route, its duration, whether it favors warm water or cold. A fish that survives more than three days is considered remarkable. A fish that finds its way back into the northern inflow is remembered for decades. There is an old song with no melody, only a sequence of valve-taps, said to record the path of a trout that lived for nineteen days in the upper workings and disappeared through a grate no person had believed open.

The most numerous inhabitants may be the things that do not know they are alive: the valves with their preferences, the cables that sing under load, the mineral felt that grows in distinct family patterns from pipe to pipe. The level-keepers speak to these things because speech is part of inspection.

“Easy,” they tell a reluctant wheel.

“Stay with us,” they say to a pump whose vibration has changed.

“Not tonight,” they tell the northern gate when rainwater begins forcing itself through.

This is not superstition, exactly. In a place where every object is old, burdened, and under pressure, courtesy has practical value.

And perhaps something lives in the water itself—not a creature large enough to strike a wall or rise visibly from the shaft, but an attention distributed through current and pressure. The level-keepers do not discuss it directly. They only observe that the basin behaves differently when neglected. A valve left ungreased may seize at the worst possible moment; a flooded passage may clear just before it is needed; a lamp may pulse near a hidden leak, though its wiring has been dead for years.

Their explanation is simple: the town has learned its own survival.

It has been held together for so long by hands, rituals, improvised braces, recorded measurements, and the disciplined refusal to demand more space than it can safely give that it has become a kind of organism. The people are its nerves. The pumps are its lungs. The shaft is its buried throat. The water is not outside it, not entirely; the water is the medium in which it thinks.

So the level-keepers live there with their children, their old maps, their shrimp, their remembered dead, and their low lamps. They do not dream of draining the basin. To drain it would be to expose foundations that have only remained sound because they are held under water; to alter a balance nobody fully understands; to discover what the original shaft was made for before it became necessary to keep it filled.

They maintain the narrow margin because the margin is home.

Above them, ice closes and opens its opaque eye.

Below them, something presses upward without haste.

Between those two immensities, the people keep the water level exact.
