What lives there is not one thing, though the hollow sometimes feels as if it has gathered itself into a single breathing animal.

At its center, beneath the fig-root column and beside the black pool, lives an old monitor lizard the color of wet stone.

It is not large by the standards of open country. Aboveground, among thorn trees and sun-blasted termite mounds, it would look thin, almost diminished: a narrow-bodied creature with a scarred tail and a head too angular for its neck. In the sink it is immense. It knows every passage at ground level, every gap beneath a fallen slab, every root tunnel that opens near the surface and every ledge from which nestlings sometimes tumble. Its claws have worn shallow white scratches into the stone steps near the entrance.

The lizard does not own the hollow. Nothing owns it. But many smaller lives arrange themselves around its habits.

The frogs know where it sleeps, under the shelf of a collapsed wall where the earth stays warm even during rain. They do not call there. The rats use the high roots when it is moving. Ant trails split around the places its belly has polished smooth. When it takes a snake, the beetles arrive afterward; when beetles gather, the tiny pale geckos come; when geckos come, the snakes return. Its body is part of the hollow’s weather.

It drinks from the pool without disturbing the blind fish if it can help it. Its tongue enters the water and returns, enters and returns. The fish, white as peeled root, knows the tremor of its feet and remains beneath the leaf-dark surface. Sometimes, in the season when the pool shrinks to a bowl of thick mud, the monitor lies beside it for hours, mouth open, taking heat and dampness from the same air.

There are younger monitors too, but they do not remain. The hollow raises them in its cracks and leaf heaps, feeds them grubs, eggs, drowned insects, and the soft bodies of rain-swollen snails. Then, once their scales harden and their tails grow long, they find the broken roof or one of the root chimneys and leave for the bright, cruel country above. Most do not return. A few come back in drought, thinner and more careful than before, and descend with the smell of dust on them.

The oldest one never leaves.

Perhaps it cannot. One hind foot is bent from some old injury, and its tail ends in a blunt healed knob. Or perhaps the world above has simply ceased to interest it. The chamber gives it water, shade, carrion, eggs, small prey, and the steady knowledge of walls. It has lived long enough beneath the limestone to become moss-colored along its flanks. A pale fungus sometimes grows on the old scar at its shoulder during the wet season, and it sheds this skin slowly, against roots and rock, leaving translucent scraps that the ants dismantle.

But the monitor is only the most visible resident.

In the upper cracks live bats with faces like folded leaves. They sleep pressed into dark clusters, their bodies warm enough that the stone around them seems to breathe. They leave each evening and return carrying the night in their fur: pollen, dust, moth scales, the faint metallic smell of insects crushed between teeth. Their droppings feed a black, soft community beneath their roosts—mites, beetles, blind threadlike worms, fungi that bloom in pale fans when the air is wet enough.

In the pool lives the blind fish, though perhaps there is more than one. No one would easily know. It has no need of eyes in water that reflects only darkness. Its body is almost colorless, except for the small red shadow of blood near its gills. It feeds on what falls: ant bodies, drowned midges, softened leaf flesh, the eggs of mosquitoes. During flood rain, when the pool joins the temporary channels on the floor, it moves farther than anyone expects. Then, when the waters retreat, it is found again in the central basin, as though the hollow has poured it back into place.

In the root galleries live ants that tend fungus as people once tended fires. Their chambers are warmer than the surrounding soil. Their pale gardens swell after rain, then contract in dry weeks. The workers carry leaves down from the green world in disciplined lines, their burdens trembling over their heads. They are not merely collecting food. They are carrying sunlight underground, piece by piece.

There is a snake in the lower stones, a small thick-bodied one with a head shaped like a leaf bud. It is seen so rarely that some of the chamber’s inhabitants may not understand it as a creature at all. To the frogs, it is a change in the arrangement of shadows. To the geckos, a dry coldness on the wall. To the monitor, it is sometimes food and sometimes not worth the effort. The snake is old, perhaps older than the lizard, and its scales have become dull with the minerals of the place. It waits in flooded crevices and takes frogs with almost no movement. When it sheds, its skin remains caught in the root curtains: a ghost of a snake, slowly furred over by mold.

There are also the descendants of whatever people brought down.

Not people themselves, not now. But near the mossed steps, in the cracks around the old platform, grow little plants that do not appear elsewhere in the sink: bitter-leaved herbs with gray undersides, and a thin red flower that opens only after rain. Perhaps their seeds came in clay bowls. Perhaps they fell from hair, clothing, food bundles, offerings. The plants have made themselves at home in the shrine’s broken geometry. Small bees visit them when enough light reaches the entrance. Their roots move through the fitted stones, patiently taking apart what hands once assembled.

And there are memories in the form of habit.

Animals pause at the platform.

Not because they know it was sacred, if it was sacred, but because the stone remains dry longer than the ground around it. Because insects shelter in its seams. Because the red-painted wall retains a little warmth. The monitor rests there sometimes after feeding. Frogs hide beneath its lip. A bat, fallen young and still alive, may crawl there instinctively toward the faintest remaining heat. The old human place has become another kind of den.

If there is a true inhabitant of the hollow, it may be the moisture itself.

It lives in the limestone, in the roots, in the swollen joints of ferns and the eyes of frogs. It carries minerals from the ceiling into the pool. It softens dead things until they can be eaten. It keeps the monitor’s skin from cracking, the fish’s small world from vanishing, the fungus gardens alive beneath the roots. In the dry months it retreats but does not go. In the rains it speaks from every wall at once.

Everything down there depends on its refusal to leave.

So the sink is inhabited by a lizard, fish, bats, ants, frogs, geckos, snakes, snails, beetles, roots, molds, and the small surviving consequences of vanished people.

But it is also inhabited by continuance.

By lives that have learned not to need much distance. By creatures that survive in shade, in seepage, in the little pause between one rain and the next. By things that do not announce themselves until a hand is placed on stone, a foot shifts near the pool, a leaf trembles where no wind has entered.
