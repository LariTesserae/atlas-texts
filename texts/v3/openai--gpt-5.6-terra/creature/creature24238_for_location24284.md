What lives in the hollow is not a people exactly, though it has the habits of one.

The first inhabitants are the airs.

They come over the basin rim in separate bodies: a high, dry wind carrying the cold mineral scent of the upper slopes; a lower wet breath that rises from the hidden chambers and drains; the afternoon current that follows the sun across the terraces, touching each exposed wall in turn. They know the city’s passages better than any map could. They enter through windows too small for a human head, descend stairs that lead nowhere, gather beneath bridges, and leave by cracks between foundation stones.

In the old wells, one wind lives as a low note. In narrow drainage slots another becomes a whistle. There is a gust that always catches at the same row of loose slates near the western square and makes them chatter briefly, irritably, like old people remembering an argument. In winter, the winds take on more rooms. They pass through roofless houses and do not leave until spring.

The mist is their quieter kin.

Each morning it is born in the low places: from a sunken court, from the mouth of a buried stair, from seams in the basin wall where cold water has been waiting through the night. It does not drift aimlessly. It follows routes. It fills certain lanes before others, pauses at particular thresholds, coils around the old crooked trees, and rises in pale folds through the broken colonnades. By noon it has usually withdrawn, but not entirely. Some remains in cisterns, under arches, in rooms whose floors lie below the level of the streets. Those places are never wholly empty.

Moss lives there in colonies so ancient that individual patches have become indistinguishable from the walls they cover.

It is not decorative moss. It is structural, deliberate in its slowness. It works upward against the expected direction of growth, following cold, following shade, finding the high stones that hold moisture longest. It fills old mortar lines, then the spaces where mortar was never used. In places it has put a soft green lip around doorways; in others it has swallowed whole sections of paving and turned former rooms into damp, dark gardens.

Beneath the moss are finer lives: pale threads of fungus, small blind beetles, water insects with long jointed legs, things that live in the thin black soil accumulating in cracks. They move slowly because there is no need to hurry here. A day lasts a long time in the basin. So does a death.

The crooked trees are the nearest thing the city has to elders.

They stand in courts that may once have been designed around them, though it is just as possible that the courts were made because the trees were already there. Their roots go beneath paving, through old drains, along chambers no living creature enters. Sometimes a root rises in a room with no roof and becomes a low wall of its own. Sometimes it slips through a doorway and holds a fallen lintel above the ground, not quite supporting it, merely refusing to let it settle.

The trees have survived frost, thin soil, snow blown sideways through their branches, and the strange cleanliness of the streets around them. They give little shelter. Their leaves are too dark and sparse. But hares come beneath them at dusk, and ravens sometimes wait in them when rain is heavy enough to flatten their feathers.

The ravens are citizens in the obvious sense. They possess height, observation, and custom.

Their nests are in the upper ruins, where stone still rises above the basin floor in towers or broken vertical walls. They know which ledges remain sound and which ones have become brittle. They gather loose fibers, grass, old wool caught on thorn bushes, and occasionally things brought in from beyond the rim: a strip of blue cloth, a bit of polished metal, a white bone too large to belong to anything in the hollow.

They do not speak in words, but their calls travel through the city and return altered. A raven cries from the tower, and the sound comes back from a square below, thinner; from a well, deeper; from an unseen chamber, as if another bird has answered from underground. They listen to these returns. Perhaps they know which echoes belong to which places.

The mountain hares live closer to the ground. Their paths are almost invisible: a bent grass stem, a dark pellet beneath a wall, a repeated passage through a gap where two stones lean together. They use the city as weather uses it. A stair is a slope. A doorway is a burrow mouth. A collapsed house is a safe hollow, unless an owl has found it first. They do not care whether the old streets were meant for feet, wheels, ceremony, or grief.

The pale goat-like creatures are something else.

They are not common enough to be called a herd, nor regular enough to be called residents, but they belong to the hollow more completely than anything else that visits it. One may stand on the upper ledges for several days, always in sight and never quite approachable. Its coat collects rain and mist until it seems to have been made from the same grey fibers as the clouds. Its feet find holds where no holds appear. It eats little that can be seen.

Sometimes it descends into the streets at night.

In the morning there may be a trace of narrow cloven prints on a wet terrace, ending at a wall. Or a low branch will have been stripped of leaves far above any ordinary animal’s reach. Once in a while, a visitor finds a single dark hair caught on a stone corner, coarse as wire and smelling faintly of wet earth. Nothing has ever seen the creature enter one of the buildings. Nothing has seen it leave the basin.

There are also the ones that live below.

Not monsters, not necessarily. The city has too many underplaces for total emptiness: cisterns whose water remains black even at noon; old stairwells filled with soil and cold roots; chambers reached through openings no wider than a shoulder; drains that go beneath the streets and continue toward the mountain. In these places live insects without color, soft-bodied things that feed on mineral films, small eels in the deepest pools, perhaps salamanders that have forgotten the sky.

There may be larger life in the buried parts. Something occasionally dislodges a stone from beneath a pavement. Something causes a pool to ripple when no drop has fallen and no wind has reached it. But the basin does not offer evidence freely. Its hidden inhabitants are not secretive so much as irrelevant to being known.

And then there is the city itself.

It lives by receiving.

It receives water through cracks in the cliff and gives it channels. It receives seeds, but only permits them to settle where its old courses still lead. It receives snow on broken roofs and keeps its streets open, as though maintaining the last outlines of circulation. It receives roots, birds, loose stones, the bodies of insects, the annual fall of dark leaves, and it makes places for all of them without changing quickly enough for the change to be seen.

Its walls lean toward one another in certain lanes. Its stairs preserve the memory of climbing. Its blank doorways continue to face the places where rooms ought to be. There is no intention in this, perhaps. Yet it can feel like patience.

If there were people here once, they are no longer the inhabitants most suited to it. The hollow has passed beyond the scale of firewood, meals, arguments, ownership, and sleep. It is occupied now by continuities: dampness, root pressure, slow erosion, repeating nests, the seasonal routes of small bodies, the work of wind on every available opening.

The city is not abandoned.

It is simply inhabited by things that do not need to announce themselves, and by one vast old resident—the mountain—which has begun, very gently, to close its hand around all the rest.
