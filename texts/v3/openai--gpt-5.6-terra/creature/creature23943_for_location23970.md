What lives there is not large.

At first it seems to be only an alteration in the water near the seam: a clarity moving inside clarity, no more substantial than the cold pulse that comes through the clay. The weeds nearest the crack incline away from it, though there is no current strong enough to bend them. Silt lifts from the floor in a small, careful cloud and remains suspended, each grain held at a different height.

Then the shape occurs.

It is usually seen between one slow blink and the next, in the narrow interval when the eye stops expecting a thing to be an object. A pale joint appears among the roots. Not bone exactly. Not root, either. It is smooth, faintly translucent, with a branching structure visible inside it like the veins in a leaf. It bends once, and the mud gives way around it without rising.

There are many such joints.

They do not belong together in any arrangement that a body on land would recognize. One may emerge from the crack beneath the bench while another rests against the old doorway several feet away. A third may be folded in the darkness of the glass jar, its tip turning with the ball of unmixed water. Yet when one moves, the others seem to understand. The movement is passed between them through the masonry, through the weed-roots, through the thin bright trails of the snails.

It may have begun as something that fell in during the first flood.

There are hints of a hand in it. A suggestion of fingers, though too numerous and too delicately jointed; a pale wrist narrowing into threads; a palm that opens on both sides. Sometimes it presses against the underside of the broken ceiling. Where it touches, the stone becomes momentarily transparent. Beyond it there is not floodwater or sky but a dim green distance full of descending roots.

At other times it resembles a plant learning the gesture of an animal.

Its limbs divide at the ends into fine tendrils, each one tipped with a colorless, lidless eye. The eyes do not look outward. They look into surfaces: mud, iron, brick, drowned wood, the milky composite shells of the snails. They linger over the old objects with a tenderness that is almost investigative. The brass handle turns a fraction after one has touched it. The collapsed shelf exhales a little black sediment. The ladder rung trembles, though nothing climbs it.

The blind fish knows it.

When the fish enters the room, the thing gathers itself closer to the floor. Not hiding—making space. The fish passes through curtains of weed and comes to rest above the crack, where it holds perfectly still. Its red spine brightens. Below it, the pale branches unfold from the mud in a slow circle, and for a time fish and thing occupy the same place without touching.

Afterward the fish may leave by a route that does not exist.

The snails also attend it. Their shining trails appear ahead of them because they are not trails at all, but preparations: thin diagrams of where the creature will be. In the mornings—if the pallid pressure above can be called morning—one may find several shells arranged around the seam, facing inward. Their bodies are withdrawn. Their milk-glass grains have become faintly warm.

The creature does not eat in any visible way. It gathers differences.

A leaf newly washed down from the flooded world will settle near it, and the leaf will slowly lose the brown violence of its journey. A torn edge becomes green again. The outline of an insect’s bite closes. Sometimes the leaf is then drawn sideways into the wall, just as seedpods are. Later, a leaf of the same kind may be found growing from a crack where no soil lies.

Clay flakes that fall into its reach do not sink. They remain around the pale branches until their orange color has faded. Then they are released as soft gray mud. Rusted nails acquire a dark shine. Moss spreads across places that had been bare for years. The fungus under the shelf produces its silver dust in greater abundance when the creature is near, and the motes ascend through the ceiling as though carrying messages from the room to whatever lies above it.

It is not clear whether it is making the place alive, or whether the place has made it as a way of noticing itself.

There are old human features within it, but they are incomplete and never remain long. A curve like a shoulder beneath a drift of roots. The brief white oval of a cheek, turned toward the wall. Once, near the doorway, something like a mouth opened in the pale material and released a cluster of clear bubbles. The bubbles traveled upward. Each contained, for an instant, the tiny reflected shape of a room with dry walls and a window bright with summer.

They reached the broken ceiling and passed through.

The mouth was gone when the bubbles were gone.

It does not seem lonely. Loneliness would require a boundary between itself and the basin, and it has almost none. The cold water comes through the tear in the earth; the creature opens around it. The roots enter the wall; pale filaments enter the roots. Rain thickens the world overhead; somewhere in its dispersed body a pulse changes pace. It is present in the pressure of the water, in the slight angle of the weeds, in the inexplicable dark sphere turning in the jar.

Still, there are moments when it gathers.

This happens rarely, perhaps once in many seasons, when the basin receives a great weight of new floodwater and the old room becomes darker even than usual. The branches withdraw from the masonry. The pale threads unweave themselves from the roots. The many distant joints fold inward, one after another, until something nearly whole is kneeling at the mouth of the crack.

It is then about the size of a child.

Its body is made of wet translucence, mud-filaments, the pale underside of fungus, and the soft mineral glow of things long buried. It has no fixed face. Where a face should be is a depth of moving water in which the doorway, bench, ladder, jar, and fish all appear at once, small and far away.

It lowers itself toward the new water rising from below.

Not drinking. Listening.

The hum that has waited beneath all things becomes almost audible then—not a sound, but an immense patience. The creature answers by placing one many-fingered hand against the sharp clay edge.

By the next day, the edge is still sharp.

But a new root has appeared in it, white as a vein.

And somewhere beyond the wall, where no room was ever built, something begins very slowly to remember the shape of a door.
