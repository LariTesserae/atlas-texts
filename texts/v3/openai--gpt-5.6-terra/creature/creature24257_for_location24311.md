The Lattice is inhabited first by people who have learned that abundance is not the same thing as wealth.

They do not speak of water as a utility. They speak of it by condition, by history, by destination. There is wash-water, green-water, reagent water, medical water, condenser return, memorial water. A child may be given a spoonful of real liquid water on a birthday, not necessarily to drink, but to touch: to feel it gather itself against the skin, to see how it reflects a face. Most citizens have seen rain only in archived footage or in the sealed climatology rooms beneath the central libraries, where a few cubic meters of weather are maintained for study.

Those rooms are popular with old people.

They go in, sit under artificial clouds, and listen to water strike stone.

The inhabitants call themselves Latticers when speaking to outsiders, but among themselves the word is too broad. A person is more likely to identify by district, shift, line, clan, reservoir, or machinery. They are a Ninth-Reservoir woman. A child of the Kestrel Conveyor. A south-stack man. A person from the old platforms beyond Furnace Seven. Someone who grew up under the blue lamps of the Glassworks Transit Spur may carry that fact in their speech for life: a habit of pausing before a promise, a preference for dim rooms, a tendency to count time in arriving freight.

The city is sparse, and that sparseness has shaped them. They are accustomed to distance. They do not expect to encounter strangers by accident. To visit someone may require an hour in a rail capsule, a walk through a pressurized corridor, another lift, another door. Invitations therefore have weight. Meals are planned. Arguments are postponed until they can be afforded. Lovers may live ten kilometers apart and consider themselves near one another.

Their houses are not built to impress the plain. Nothing built here can impress the plain for long. Habitation towers are deep-walled, narrow-windowed, and full of internal courtyards where heat is managed like a dangerous animal. The richest apartments are not necessarily the largest but the coolest, the quietest, the closest to a reliable water loop. People decorate their rooms with things that resist dust: woven metal cloth, fired tiles, polished ceramic, old machine plates etched with family histories.

Many households keep a little sealed terrarium of native film ecology: a black-gloss patch growing across a warmed mineral tile, folding itself flat when someone stomps nearby. Children are taught to recognize its moods. They tap the glass and watch blue veining travel across it. Some families cultivate particularly responsive colonies and give them names. Others find the practice unsettling. To them, the films are not pets but weather in miniature.

A Lattice child learns early how to listen through the soles of their feet.

The crust has voices. Some are harmless: rail vibration, crawler traffic, settling plates, the distant rhythmic compression of refinery pumps. Some are warnings. A repeated hollow tremor under a corridor may mean a seam is widening. A soft, glassy crackle beneath a tower can mean the crust is lifting, slowly and invisibly, against its pilings. People pause in conversation when the floor gives a particular sound. They look toward the walls, not from panic but from attention.

The oldest local saying is: *The ground is not still. It is only slow.*

There are surveyors whose whole profession consists of making that slowness legible. They walk the open plain in reflective suits, carrying resonant hammers and antenna frames. They kneel beside black seams and lower threads of sensor glass into them. They map shifts that may not matter for fifty years. Their work is regarded with a peculiar reverence, somewhere between engineering and divination. A good seam-reader can tell a district council not merely that a rail line will fail, but how it will fail: buckling upward, dipping sideways, opening beneath the third support, carrying a warm breath of subterranean dust.

They are often wrong in the details.

They are almost never wrong in the long term.

There are also people who live outside the Lattice’s neat official geometry: seam camps, crawler families, scavenger collectives, the cliff crews who move with the excavators and sleep in modular shelters bolted into fresh-cut shelves. They are less likely to trust the city’s reservoir authorities or its maps. They know abandoned transfer points, undocumented maintenance tunnels, old hydrogen lines whose valves no longer answer to any central system. Their children grow up with the horizon visible from their beds.

Some are born in moving freight convoys and take years to acquire a permanent address.

The city calls them peripheral workers when it wants their labor and unregistered residents when it wants to deny them water allotments. They call the city, collectively, the Still Place.

It is not entirely an insult.

Among the nonhuman inhabitants, the sievebacks are the most visible, but perhaps not the most consequential. They are not merely animals in the ordinary sense. Their bodies are walking chemistry. A herd’s passage alters the ground behind it. The soft membranes beneath their shells lift and redistribute trace metals; their waste hardens into granular fans where mineral films take root; their shed shell plates become favored nesting surfaces for smaller organisms. Wherever sievebacks travel repeatedly, the plain acquires faint routes of life: darkened seams, clusters of static-fed film, glass fleas gathering in pale clouds at twilight.

The city has tried to farm them.

It has never worked well.

Sievebacks tolerate containment for a time, provided the enclosures are large, warm, and threaded with the correct mineral substrate. They will graze industrial floors, accept heat from buried coils, and even breed. But after several generations their shells become brittle, their parasite nodules cease to flash, and their young fail to anchor properly beneath the adults. Eventually the herd begins to move in a single direction, pressing itself against any wall that blocks it.

No one understands what they are seeking.

Old route records suggest that their migrations predate the Lattice by thousands of years. In some places a herd will pause at a black seam and remain there for days, arrayed in a broad silent ring. Their shells angle inward. Their feet stop tapping. Then, without visible signal, they continue.

The train schedulers call these events gatherings.

The seam-readers call them listening.

There is a belief, not officially endorsed, that the sievebacks are following changes far below the crust: movements of mineral pressure, shifts in buried charge, the slow rearrangement of some immense conductive body in the deep plain. The belief is popular among children and freight workers, among people who spend long hours outdoors and have watched a herd turn away from a route shortly before the ground broke open.

The wind kites are more difficult to love.

They cling to towers in the night, translucent sails folded around them like broken glass. They feed on electrical leakage with such delicacy that a whole flock can make a power line sing. Maintenance crews find their shed rib-crystals tangled in insulators and their young clustered in the warm mouths of exhaust vents. Their bodies are so thin that they can be overlooked until they move. Then the industrial skyline appears to blink.

At dawn, they leave.

They rise not in flocks but in drifting assemblies, each individual finding a current, unfolding one plane after another until it becomes almost invisible. The oldest kites are vast enough to cast shadows. They may remain aloft for months. There are persistent stories of specimens whose mineral sails bear scars from obsolete antenna arrays, proof that they have circled above the Lattice since before the present city was built.

No one has seen one die in the open air.

When a wind kite reaches the end of its life, it is said to fold itself into the upper currents and become part of the weather.

The glass fleas are less romantic. They infest insulation stores, warm cable trenches, and the fur lining of old protective gear. They are tiny, faceted, nearly transparent creatures that feed on heat differentials and static charge. In the right light they gather like spilled sugar. Their bites are painless, but their presence means a system is leaking energy somewhere it should not. Every maintenance apprentice learns this before learning to read a pressure gauge: if the fleas are gathering, follow them.

The quarry eels are treated with professional courtesy.

They are blind, boneless-looking things, though their bodies contain rings of dense mineral cartilage. In the deep fissures they move through gas pockets as fish might move through black water. No one knows what they eat. Some drilling crews insist they feed on vibration; others say they live on metallic dust precipitating out of warm subterranean currents. Their emergence during boring operations is interpreted as an inconvenience, a hazard, and an omen in roughly equal measure.

The ceramic bells lowered over them are made in only three workshops. Each is fired with a minute percentage of material taken from old eel casts, because someone long ago discovered that ordinary ceramic made the creatures panic.

No one knows why.

There are smaller inhabitants of the city that few people notice: the heat mites living in the undersides of exchangers; the red-thread colonies that form in uncleaned gas filters; the pale dust moths whose wings are so mineral-heavy they can only fly in the hot updrafts above smelter berms. There are bacteria and film-organisms engineered to digest industrial residue, but after generations in the wild they have hybridized with native chemistries. They bloom in drainage sumps and along the feet of forgotten blast walls, making patterns no one designed.

Some of these patterns recur across great distances.

A refinery technician may find a blue-black spiral in a coolant trench, photograph it, and discover that a survey team has found the same spiral two hundred kilometers away beneath an abandoned rail spur. The official explanation is convergent growth.

People accept this explanation because they have learned to accept many things.

But there are others in the Lattice who make a practice of watching the abandoned structures. They are called rindkeepers, though the term began as an insult. They study the mineral skin that grows over old steel and concrete. They record where salt sheets advance, where girders are consumed, where obsolete walls become incorporated into the crust. Their work has practical value: the new mineral formations sometimes contain recoverable elements, and the settling patterns can warn of changes in subsurface pressure.

Yet the rindkeepers care for more than extraction.

They regard an abandoned station, a sunken warehouse, a bowed rail line, as a kind of ancestor. Not an ancestor of blood, but of continuity: a human-made thing that has outlived its purpose and entered another system. They leave small plaques on structures about to be surrendered to the plain. Not legal markers. Not memorials exactly. They record what passed through there, who worked there, what failed, what was repaired, what could not be saved.

The plaques are ceramic, so that they may remain readable for a long time.

Then the salts take them too.

And beneath all of these lives there is the thing in the black seams.

No one agrees that it is one thing. The geological authority says the seams are fractures, void networks, pressure channels, ancient fault systems filled with conductive residue. The mining companies call them risk zones. The seam-readers call them mouths only when they are drunk, tired, or speaking to children.

But there are records.

There are old drilling logs in which a borehole opened into unexpected warmth and the instruments detected a pulse at intervals too regular for settling rock. There are survey photographs of mineral growths that appear in one season along a fissure, rise in branching lattices taller than a person, and collapse to powder before anyone can sample them. There are reports from freight crews of lights moving beneath translucent crust, miles from the nearest power line. There are entire herds of sievebacks that have vanished into regions where no herd ought to be able to pass.

The Lattice does not build near certain seams.

It says this is because the ground is unstable.

That is true.

It is not the whole truth.

Whatever shifts below is not quick. It does not hunt. It does not announce itself. It may not know the city exists in any form that people would recognize as knowledge. Perhaps it is only the old world continuing its mineral thought beneath the new one: pressure becoming structure, structure becoming current, current gathering in immense dark networks under the crust.

Perhaps the city is a brief warmth on its back.

Perhaps the sievebacks have always known this and are not migrating at all. Perhaps they are keeping pace with something too deep and slow to see.

At night, when the transit halls have emptied and the fountain’s ribbon of water turns in its glass column, the Lattice can seem almost gentle. People stand beside the water. Wind kites cling to the outer towers. Glass fleas bloom around a faulty rail insulator. Somewhere beyond the lighted districts, a crawler stops for a herd and waits without complaint.

The city breathes through pipes.

The plain breathes through cracks.

And all the things that live there—human, animal, mechanical, mineral, remembered—go on adapting to one another beneath the sharp, merciless sun.
