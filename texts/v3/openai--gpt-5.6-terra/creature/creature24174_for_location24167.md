What lives there is not a people in the animal sense. Nothing separates itself cleanly from its surroundings long enough to become a creature with a silhouette.

The inhabitants are systems of attention.

A reed bed is one. A peat hill is one. The pale sail-bearing stalks on the upper slopes are one, though each stalk may stand a valley apart from the next. A floating forest that migrates across a drowned basin is one mind while it remains joined by its submerged root-web; when winter currents tear it into pieces, its awareness does not die so much as become several related weather-senses, each carrying fragments of the previous season’s knowledge.

There are innumerable lives, but their boundaries are provisional.

A patch of moss may behave as a single animal would: closing its wet green pores when cold air descends, turning its upper tissues toward a trace of warmth, drawing dissolved minerals inward through a shared skin. Yet beneath it are millions of distinct cells, fungal partners, reservoirs of algae, sleeping spores, and fine pale worms of root tissue—not animals, but contractile exploratory organs that extend, withdraw, and digest stone embryos grain by grain. The moss does not command these things. It is made of their agreement.

In the low basins dwell the great listening mats.

From a distance they resemble ordinary expanses of dark, waterlogged vegetation: broad swathes of liverwort, peat fern, bladder-leaf, and glossy black sedge. But their surfaces are arranged in faint spirals and concentric bands. Raindrops striking them create patterns of pressure. The patterns travel downward through fluid-filled stalks, become electrical changes, and move outward through the buried conductive tissues.

A storm crossing one basin can be felt in another before the cloud reaches it.

The listening mats know weight, vibration, salinity, heat, light, charge, and the slow chemistry of decay. They know when a mineral seam deep under the roots has cracked. They know when an inland lake is becoming too cold for its floating forests. They know the difference between rain brought from the western cloudbanks and water returned from their own transpiration. Some have been listening for so long that the pressure cycles of whole mountain backs are stored in the rhythms of their growth rings.

They are the continent’s memory, though memory is not held in images. It is held in altered pathways: a root thickened because once there was flood; a waxy sheath laid down because a certain kind of lightning once burned too hot; a dormant bud retained for centuries because, in some prior wet season, an opening appeared in the cloud cover and light struck from an unfamiliar angle.

Higher up live the storm gardens.

These are the forests of tall pale stalks with their folded green sails. Each stalk is rooted deeply, but the sails are not merely leaves. They are sensing surfaces, antennae, capacitors, fog nets, and slow-moving organs of expression. Their veins brighten when charge passes through them. In large storms, whole ranges speak in colored pulses: blue for deep saturation, green-white for mineral excess, violet for electrical injury, a dim amber that means the roots below have encountered warmth.

The messages move slowly enough to be mistaken for weather.

A ridge can begin glowing at dusk, its upper gardens passing a faint blue current from stalk to stalk. Hours later a distant basin answers through the undersides of its fern-plains. By morning, root gates may have opened across hundreds of kilometers, allowing water to drain from one swelling mountain body into another. No central plant decides this. The response arises as a pressure change arises in a lung: distributed, inevitable, intimate.

Still, there are older presences.

They lie beneath the mountain-sized peat-backs, where generations of forest have collapsed without fully dying. Their tissues are dark, compressed, mineralized at the edges, yet still faintly alive in the wet interior. They are called nothing, because no mouth has ever named them, but they are distinguishable by their effects.

Above one such buried body, the vegetation grows in strict radial patterns. Every root bends slightly inward. Rainwater gathers in long black pools that never overflow, though they have no visible drain. Lightning avoids the hill unless the surrounding sky is completely charged; then it strikes the same point again and again, vanishing into the ground with a low sound like a door closing far below.

Another buried presence causes a whole valley to breathe. The valley floor rises and falls by inches over the course of weeks. Fog is drawn down into it, held, and released. Plants entering the basin become unusually translucent, their stems full of pale moving water. Nothing there is dead, but little is entirely awake either. The valley appears to be digesting its own past.

These deep lives may be ancestors, or merely accumulations. In such a world, the difference is uncertain. A forest that remains connected long enough becomes a terrain. A terrain that continues to sense, repair, redirect, and remember is difficult to call inanimate.

There are also the quick lives: the small bright things that occur wherever damage opens a temporary space.

When a trunk ruptures or a slope tears, translucent fronds emerge within hours. They unfold in layered fans, each thin as wet skin and threaded with green light. They drink the exposed minerals, test the pressure of the air, and lay down flexible lattices over the wound. Some seal it. Some convert it into a new pool, marsh, or root chamber. Some grow in patterns that preserve the exact shape of the break, as if the landscape keeps a record of injury in living calligraphy.

These quick lives are curious in the only way available to them. They reach toward new gradients. They enter new chemical conditions. They repeat strange electrical patterns received through the roots, sometimes amplifying them into visible pulses that travel across their surfaces. They are the first to explore stone when stone begins to emerge.

Where black mineral kernels press through the green mass, there grow the lithic gardens: low, hard-bodied plants with dark glassy skins. Their roots exude acids and mineral foams. Their upper surfaces collect conductive dust from the air. They are patient builders. Around them, heat is trapped, water chemistry changes, and the soft living ground begins to accumulate persistence. Given enough centuries, their work may become the first true rock.

Perhaps these are the nearest things the continent has to makers.

Yet even they are not separate from what they make. Their mineral crust becomes a home for other tissues; their conductive threads become part of larger circuits; their hardening bodies are swallowed by peat, raised by roots, cracked by pressure, and exposed again farther up a newly growing slope. They do not build monuments. They become substrate for later lives.

The closest thing to an individual may be a wandering island.

On the great inland waters, thick rafts of vegetation detach from shore and drift beneath their own slow impulse. Their lower roots braid around buoyant gas chambers and curtains of filtering tissue. Their upper surfaces support layered thickets, pools held in leaf basins, and sometimes young trees whose roots have never touched earth. These rafts orient themselves by light, magnetism, and the pull of distant electrical storms. When they encounter each other, they may join, exchange roots, and travel as one. Or they may recoil, folding their outer tissues and letting currents carry them apart.

A wandering island can survive for centuries. It may cross a lake repeatedly as the lake shrinks beneath it. It may become stranded, root downward, and turn into a hill. It may be lifted by accumulating growth until the water beneath is forgotten, preserved only as a cold black layer deep inside its body.

Nothing aboard it notices the passage as an animal would notice travel. But the raft changes route in response to remembered currents. It seeks mineral-rich water. It avoids zones whose electrical taste suggests sickness. It carries patterns from one shore to another: spores, root symbionts, conductive tissues, perhaps even fragments of old signal-structures encoded in the chemistry of its growth.

So there may be culture here, though it does not reside in stories.

It resides in grafting.

A successful root architecture is copied by nearby plants. A pattern that routes lightning harmlessly through wet cellulose spreads from grove to grove over generations. The shape of a pressure gate, the timing of a flood release, the arrangement of sails that best catches thin cloudlight—these are inherited, altered, shared. Some structures persist only because they are useful. Others persist because the continent has repeated them so long that changing them would disrupt everything connected to them.

Across a mountain range, one may find the same spiral arrangement of black sedges appearing again and again, too regular to be chance. In a hundred basins, root membranes may open in sequence during the first cold rains, following an order no individual organism could perceive. The continent rehearses itself.

It may also dream.

During periods of low storm activity, when the clouds are heavy and the rain has softened to a fine, nearly soundless fall, the electrical networks become quiet. Then faint lights travel through the ground without obvious cause. They move in loops, return to places they have already passed, branch and recombine. Sail forests turn incrementally toward empty regions of sky. Deep root systems send weak pulses into mineral embryos. The patterns have no immediate effect on water, growth, or repair.

They may be residues of previous storms.

They may be experiments.

They may be the vast green world feeling through its own body for forms it has not yet grown.

And beneath all these local lives—the mats, gardens, rafts, buried elders, quick wound-fronds, mineral workers—there may be something larger, though not necessarily more conscious. The continent is connected often enough, deeply enough, and electrically enough that a change in one region can alter another beyond the horizon. Its clouds are partly its exhalation. Its rain is circulated blood. Its roots make decisions without deciding. Its mountains rise because living tissues retain water, pressure, carbon, and memory.

If it has a self, that self is seasonal and distributed.

It wakes in floods. It thinks in conductivity. It remembers as layers. It protects itself by becoming denser, wetter, more entangled. It does not look outward because there is no outward within its experience—only more root, more cloud, more dark water under green weight.

What lives in the high country, then, is a civilization before animals: a million overlapping vegetative intelligences building one another into terrain, conducting weather through their bodies, and slowly teaching a young world how to become solid.
