They are not a people who arrive anywhere all at once.

A household may begin as three adults, two children, a grandmother, and a fistful of cuttings wrapped in wet cloth. By the time the first walls have thickened, there may be cousins from the lower valleys sleeping in the roof layers, an orphan taken in after a thaw collapse, an old friend who came to help brace a leaning chamber and never left. Their families grow much as their dwellings do: by grafting, by overlap, by making a place in the shade of something already established.

From a distance, when they are outside, they can be difficult to distinguish from the city’s darker workings. They wear layers suited to damp rather than grandeur: waxed leaf-cloth, felted fiber, hides softened with moss oil, cloaks mended with panels of different greens and browns. In mist, a person standing still beside a root wall may vanish except for the pale oval of a face or the occasional movement of a hand. Their boots are broad-soled and flexible, made to feel for the firm paths beneath moss. Children go barefoot whenever the weather allows, their feet stained green at the heels and black between the toes.

They tend toward compactness, though this may be more habit than inheritance. A person raised under low roofs learns not to throw their shoulders wide. They duck without seeming to notice. They turn sideways in the narrow corridors, brace themselves instinctively with one palm against a living wall, and can tell from its temperature whether the root beneath is healthy. Their hands are usually busy: carrying bundles of trimmed vine, twisting a cord, scraping lichen from a water channel, holding a bowl under a drip that must not be wasted.

The oldest among them are not necessarily the ones with gray hair. Age is measured in winters survived in a particular structure, in terraces rescued, in seedlings successfully brought through their first hard freeze. Someone may be called old because they remember when the eastern slope was still ice, or because they planted the root that now supports six homes and a public footbridge. Such a person is treated with practical reverence. People ask them where water used to run. They ask which walls can be trusted in fog. They ask whether the mountain is repeating itself.

There are those whose work is to listen.

Not in a mystical sense alone, though the city makes mystics easily. These are the root-hearers, the damp-readers, the people who kneel with their ears against bark and packed soil. They know the sound of water moving properly through the cistern-grove. They can hear when a chamber is taking too much moisture, when a root has cracked in a hidden place, when a new hollow is forming under a terrace. They do not command the plants. No one claims that. They persuade, anticipate, redirect, wait.

A root-hearer may spend half a day in silence beside a wall, pressing two fingers into moss, then tell a family that their sleeping alcove must be moved before the next thaw. The family does not always like this. They may have woven their blankets into the old nook, raised children there, marked their height in charcoal on the inner bark. But they begin moving things. In this city, affection does not confer permanence.

There are also singers, though singing is less performance than labor.

They sing beside new grafts and weighted branches. They sing low, repetitive phrases while binding roots together, not because the plants require melody in the way a child might require a lullaby, but because slow work goes wrong when the hands hurry. The songs give several people the same pace. They preserve instructions that would be awkward to write: when to turn a branch, how long to keep a root dark, which moss holds moisture without smothering the young bark. Some songs are so old that their words no longer fit the city exactly. They mention broad rivers, dry earth, sun striking stone. These are sung carefully, almost shyly, as if speaking of relatives no one has met.

Their children are given small responsibilities early. A child may be trusted with the moth gardens, the fungus stairs, the bundles of colored moss at a passage through the thornwood. They learn which leaves shed water into drinking bowls and which leaves must never be bruised. They learn that a frog in a cistern basin is not to be caught, that white mold in a sleeping wall is a warning, that an unfamiliar bird calling from the thorn hedge may mean weather. They play in the root corridors, of course. They hide in warm decay chambers, dare one another to cross sagging bridges, build miniature houses out of bark curls and seed pods. But even their games involve tending. A child who makes a secret den will often return each day to water the moss roof.

The city has people who leave it, though fewer than outsiders might expect. Some descend when the snow opens the passes, carrying dried herbs, woven fiber, bitter bulbs, fungal dyes, seeds that thrive in cold wet ground. They return with salt, certain stones, needles, skins, stories, and sometimes spouses or strangers. The travelers are respected and watched with a particular alertness when they come home. They bring the smell of lower places with them: smoke, dust, animal dung, hot earth. For a few days their clothes seem too dry and their voices carry too far.

Others remain all their lives within sight of the pale ridge.

They know the three exits, but may use only one. They know the shelf’s changes more intimately than a body knows its own scars. They remember a path by the spring that used to cross it, a house by the family who moved out before its roof split, a terrace by the year its black grain failed. Their maps are not made of borders. They are made of conditions: where the ground breathes, where frost lingers, where the thornwood is thin, where the cistern roots run nearest the surface.

There are disagreements, inevitably. A growing city offers no shortage of them.

One household wants to thicken a roof outward; another says it will shade the beans below. Someone wants to cut back an old root that has buckled a passage. Someone else insists the root is carrying water to the western terraces. A young person proposes a new chamber in freshly exposed ground and is told that the slope has not yet learned to hold itself. These arguments are rarely settled by ownership, because almost nothing can be owned without consequence. The wall of one home is the support of another’s garden. A family’s drainage channel may be the next family’s drinking source. Decisions are made in damp gathering chambers where people sit close, steaming faintly from their cloaks, while water ticks behind the walls.

Their leaders, if that is the word, are those trusted to see connections.

They are not elevated above the city so much as entangled more deeply within it. They keep track of debts of labor, seed stores, root health, winter shelters, old promises. A good leader can tell a person not merely that their house must move, but where their children can sleep while it is moved, who will help carry bedding, which grove has room for their cooking stones, and what compensation is owed for losing a warm wall. Authority is the ability to make displacement survivable.

Death is not hidden here, though it is kept gently.

The dead are often given to the growth that sheltered them. Ashes may be scattered into compost beds. Bones may be buried beneath young root walls, wrapped in fiber, marked not with stone but with a particular graft: a pale-leaved vine, a berrying branch, a knot of red moss that only appears after thaw. The names of the dead remain in use as place-names. A stair is called Taren’s Back because Taren carried everyone’s water there during the flood year. A damp alcove is Nima’s Sleep because Nima died beneath it, and the root above her has since become thick and warm.

They do not think of this as becoming one with the city in any grand or consoling way. They think of it as returning material to a place that has always required material. Nothing here remains untouched. A blanket becomes patching fiber. A broken bowl becomes drainage grit. A dead person becomes soil, memory, caution, a root that must not be cut.

Their religion, if they have one word for it, is not directed upward toward the ridge or outward toward a distant maker. It is directed into continuance.

They make offerings, but the offerings are useful: saved water poured around a stressed young tree, a season’s first seed returned to the cistern-grove, an hour of labor given to a household whose supports have failed. They fear hunger, rot, landslide, and careless pride more than they fear spirits. Yet the city is full of presences. Every old trunk has a history of hands upon it. Every corridor remembers bodies passing through. The inhabitants speak to roots, apologize before trimming a living branch, thank a dwelling when it survives a storm. Whether they believe the plants hear is less important than the fact that they behave as if relationship must be maintained.

At night, behind the leaf-veined walls, they become visible in pieces.

A face above a bowl. Fingers sorting seeds. A child asleep under quilts of wool and bark fiber. Steam rising from broth. The silhouette of someone carefully shaving a sliver from a horn tool. Two neighbors speaking through the thin living partition between their homes, each hearing the other as a breath and a murmur through wood. In the most crowded chambers, people sleep almost inside the structures that support them: heads near warm roots, feet toward the outer leaf layers, listening to water settle into the city’s body.

They are not entirely at ease there. No one who lives on a newly exposed mountain shelf can afford ease.

They check the sky. They check the thaw. They check the ground after every hard rain, though rain may have come only as mist. They understand that the city is young, that its oldest-looking trunks have been hurried into age by skill and need, that the mountain may still decide to move beneath them. Their confidence is not confidence in safety. It is confidence in repair.

If a branch breaks, they bind it.

If a wall softens, they open it and let it breathe.

If a path disappears under a spill of gravel, they teach feet another route.

If a family loses its house, the others make room, however little room there seems to be.

And so what lives beneath the pale ridge is not merely a settlement of people among plants. It is a population learning to become an ecosystem without forgetting that ecosystems can fail. They live close, damp, watchful, patient. They grow their roofs over their heads. They borrow warmth from rot. They make kin of neighbors because no household can keep itself dry, fed, or rooted alone.

The city is teaching them as much as they are teaching it: to bend without breaking, to thicken where pressure gathers, to send sustenance sideways, and to understand that remaining is never the same thing as standing still.
