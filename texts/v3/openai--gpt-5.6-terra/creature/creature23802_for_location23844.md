They are people of the tether and the chamber.

From a distance, in the green half-light, they might be mistaken for the vegetation they tend. Their bodies are compact and supple, shaped by climbing more than swimming: strong shoulders, broad hands, feet with a slight spread between the toes, knees and elbows that fold close against the body when passing through thick reed beds. They move without hurry, conserving air, touching walls and guide-cords as they go. Even the most agile among them does not dart like a fish. They ascend, descend, brace, release. They belong to gravity as much as to water.

Their skin is seldom one color. It takes on the mineral and garden shades of the city: deep umber, grey-green, blue-black, copper-brown, the pale gold of silt. In childhood it is soft and easily chilled; in adulthood it thickens in faintly patterned layers along the forearms, calves, ribs, and spine, not scales exactly, but dense, resilient skin that bears the small abrasions of a life among rock and shell. Old people often have white mineral tracings in the creases of their hands, where the water has left its salts over decades.

Their hair grows in close ropes, floating around the face when unbound. Most bind it tightly for work with waxed fiber, shell pins, or loops of softened kelp. The vanity of the city is not brightness but order: a well-kept braid, an elegant knot, a tool-cord woven with a family’s particular pattern. A person may be recognized in dim water not by their face but by the way they fasten a sleeve, the rhythm of their hand-signs, the color sequence of shells tied at the throat.

They breathe air, though the city has taught them patience with its absence.

Every person carries a small breathing bladder when leaving the nearest chamber. Children are given their first bladder before they are trusted with a stone ladder. It is a solemn object: patched and resewn through a lifetime, decorated with marks of household and terrace, handled with the practical reverence given to a heart. The wealthiest families do not possess much more than others, but they may own older bladders, made from the enormous kelp bulbs that no longer grow near the inhabited tiers. Such bladders have been repaired so many times that the original skin remains only as a dark oval at their center.

The people do not call themselves drowned, nor submerged, nor anything that implies misfortune. Those are words for old stories.

They call themselves, in the closest translation, the **Held**: those held by the mountain, held by the water, held to one another. Their oldest songs say that before the water rose, people believed the world was something beneath their feet. The Held say this was a childish error. A world is what bears your weight, gives you food, takes your dead, and presses against you every moment. Water is therefore not an enemy, nor a wall. It is the first citizen.

Still, they are not entirely at ease in it.

Their dreams are often dry.

A person may wake from a dream of wind with tears already dispersed in the water around their eyes. Children who have never seen open sky draw it obsessively on shell tablets: a white distance, a hard blue emptiness, a burning round thing that hurts to look at. The elders correct none of these drawings. They say the former sky must be remembered accurately enough to remain strange.

There are also those who are more adapted than others, whether by inheritance, practice, or some older mingling with the valley’s altered life. Certain families can hold their breath for astonishing lengths of time. Their chests are wide, their pulses slow, their eyes unusually reflective in low light. They become climbers, vent-tenders, basin scouts, recoverers of relics from the air-filled ruins. Some have a thin secondary lid across the eye, almost invisible until they blink underwater. Others claim to taste changes in current or stone through their teeth.

No one speaks of these traits as gifts. Gifts can be withdrawn. They are simply responsibilities.

A child with a long breath will be watched carefully, trained carefully, and eventually asked to go where others cannot.

The city’s people have no sharp division between farmer, builder, priest, parent, and engineer. A terrace household does all these things as need demands. But there are crafts with names and lineages.

There are **binders**, who understand tension so deeply that they can feel the weakness in a bridge by resting two fingers on its root-cord. They make houses that flex during a shift in current and baskets that close themselves around ripening algae. The finest binders are said to tie knots that remember: a cord may be loosened years later, and the original pattern can be read from its twists.

There are **breath-keepers**, who tend the chambers. They scrape foul growth from the stone mouths, cultivate the mosses and gas-producing colonies within, inspect seams, ration space during storms and tremors, and decide when a chamber must be sealed. They are not rulers, but in emergency no one disputes them. Their hands carry the permanent pale burns of the chamber work.

There are **stone-listeners**, often old, who sleep with one palm against the cliff and wake knowing which ledge has shifted. They map the mountain not by sight but by vibration. Their records are arrays of drilled shells, hanging weights, notched bone, and cords strung through particular cracks. When a new warm seam opens, the stone-listeners are the first to visit it. They do not ask whether it is safe. They ask what it wants to become.

There are **surface-workers**, though they dislike the name. They climb to the old roofs and enter the forbidden dry rooms. Their bodies are wrapped in pressure-cloths and weighted against sudden buoyancy. They go in pairs, carrying clay lamps whose flames exist only in air, a sight so uncanny that even experienced workers pause before it. They retrieve copper wire, preserved boards, old glass, sometimes fragments of writing whose language has become ceremonial rather than understood.

And there are **darkwardens**, who go down.

They are not soldiers, exactly. The city has no enemies in the ordinary sense. But when a tether goes slack near the basin edge, when a large shadow crosses below a garden, when something begins taking bait from the outer algae racks, the darkwardens descend with stone hooks, resonance drums, lamps grown from fierce blue organisms, and nets that are more warning than weapon. Few people become darkwardens willingly. Fewer remain one for long. They are respected, avoided, and fed well at communal meals.

The large thing in the basin does not seem to hunt the city. If it did, the city would be gone.

Sometimes it passes beneath the lower terraces and every cord begins to tremble. The people stop where they are. Children are gathered close. The breath-keepers close unnecessary vents. Lamps are shaded. No drum is struck. The Held wait with their palms against stone or root, feeling the enormous slow displacement move through the valley.

There are records of its visits in the old knot-archives: not pictures, but patterns of tension. A cord pulled taut, then slack. Three loops. A long descending braid. The oldest interpretation says it is the valley’s first inhabitant, older than the flood, altered by it but not made by it. Another says the creature is a moving cave, carrying trapped air and the bones of trees inside its body. A third says it is not alive at all, but a fold in the water that comes and goes when the moon calls hard enough.

The children call it Grandmother Below.

They leave gifts for it: baskets of bright algae, polished shells, the bones of those who have requested the deep burial. Whether it receives these gifts is uncertain. By morning they are always gone.

Death is managed with great care, because a body is both beloved and useful, dangerous and sacred. The dead are not released casually into the water. Their bodies may be weighted into mineral crevices where the mountain will slowly claim them; they may be given to the gardens, enclosed in woven root cages so that the kelp feeds from them; or, for those of certain lines and vows, lowered beyond the last tether into the basin. No one says a dead person is gone. They say that person has been “unbound.”

Their names remain in the city’s cords.

A newborn is given a temporary name, usually something ordinary and hopeful: Little Current, First Shell, Warm Hand, Green Sleep. The enduring name comes later, after the child has survived their first long descent, first chamber watch, first season of tending a living thing. Names are not chosen for beauty alone. They must be easy to sign in dark water and clear when tapped through a rootline. The most honored names have a distinctive rhythm when drummed against basalt.

Their language is not one language but several laid over each other.

At close range, they speak softly, mouth to ear or mouth to palm, using a low, rounded speech that wastes little air. At a few body-lengths, hands take over: fingers opening, folding, flicking, touching wrist or sternum to change meaning. Over terraces, they use cord-code and shell bells. For public matters, they drum the mountain. A birth announcement is bright and quick. A broken tether is a repeated split beat. A death begins with a long pulse followed by silence.

Lies are difficult, though not impossible, in a city where one’s hands are always visible and vibrations travel farther than words. Deception tends to take the form of omission: a cord not plucked, a warning bell removed, a knot tied in the wrong household pattern. Their great scandals are often acts of bad maintenance.

They have festivals, but these are not loud.

At the turning of the warm currents, every terrace releases a few cultivated luminous organisms into the water. The lights rise slowly toward the pale roof, gathering in the high kelp like stars caught in a forest canopy. People float tethered in family clusters, sharing stored air, touching foreheads, recalling the names of those unbound since the last turning.

When a new ledge is claimed from the mountain, the binders begin by attaching nothing to it. For a full cycle they let it remain bare, watched and listened to. Then children carry the first roots there. Elders tap its surface with stone hammers. The household that will build upon it sleeps nearby for three nights in a temporary woven pod, learning the ledge’s tremors. Only after that is the first permanent knot made.

They believe a place must consent to being inhabited.

This belief extends to the old dry buildings above. The trapped-air rooms are not merely dangerous because of collapse or bad gas. They are thought to possess a kind of grief. They remember a world that ended without properly dying. Surface-workers apologize before opening them. They never take all that can be taken. A copper hinge, perhaps. A length of wood. A square of cloudy glass. Always something is left behind: food, a braid of kelp, a shell marked with the worker’s name.

Occasionally, a child enters one of these rooms despite the prohibitions.

Such children return changed, if they return. Not possessed, exactly. More often they become fascinated by dryness. They rub water from their hands. They hoard scraps of cloth. They ask questions no one likes: What did fire sound like? Could a person walk without holding on? Did the sky press back when you looked at it?

Some become surface-workers when grown. Some become storytellers. Some become the people who leave the city entirely, climbing through dangerous shallows toward places where the sea-ceiling is brighter.

There are rumors of other settlements beyond the ridge-lines: other Held, other ways of breathing, cities built in shipwrecks or coral caves or the bellies of ancient drowned towers. The city sends traders rarely, because distance in water is not measured in paces but in air, current, pressure, and the number of places where one can sleep without dying. Those who return bring unfamiliar knots, salts of strange colors, new kinds of seed, and stories that are treated with the same cautious respect as reports of earthquakes.

What the city fears most is not the dark below, nor the shifting mountains, nor even the dwindling air.

It fears stillness.

A tether that never strains may be rotting. A garden that does not spread may be dying. A family that never alters its knot-pattern may be hiding a loss. The mountain must rise, the reeds must root elsewhere, the houses must drift a little, children must learn unfamiliar routes. To remain exactly as one has been is considered a kind of quiet death.

So the Held live among unfinished things and become unfinished themselves.

Their houses grow around them. Their tools are repaired until they carry the hands of many generations. Their bodies bear the water’s slow alterations. Their children inherit not certainty, but routes: a ladder down through violet kelp, a shell-string across a dangerous bridge, a way to find air when the lamps have gone out.

And all through the drowned valley, from chamber to chamber, terrace to terrace, garden to wilderness, the city continues speaking to itself through knots, touches, bells, and the patient living cords.
