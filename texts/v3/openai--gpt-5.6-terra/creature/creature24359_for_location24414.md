What lives on the plain is not a people who conquered dryness. They are a people who learned that dryness is not emptiness.

They are called, in their own oldest speech, something that means *those who listen below*. Other settlements call them the Lowlanders, or the Dust-Cloaked, or simply the Plain People. They do not much object to any of these names. A name is a thing used from a particular direction, they say. From above, they are low. From the storm, they are dust-colored. From the ground, they are listeners.

They are human in the broad, recognizable sense: two-legged, tool-making, social, expressive faces beneath hair wrapped or braided against grit. But the plain has shaped them over many generations into a particular kind of humanity. They tend toward compact frames and broad chests, with strong feet made for long walking on shifting crust. Their skin is commonly darkened by the sun and wind, though its shades vary as much as the soil—brown, red-brown, deep umber, ocher, ash-gold. Fine pale scars are common across hands, forearms, lips, and eyelids: mineral burns, thorn scratches, healed dust abrasions. They wear loose layered clothing of woven dryfiber, softened hide, shed reed-skin, and the glossy membranes harvested from certain broad-leaf plants. Their faces are often veiled outdoors, not ceremonially but practically. A person’s veil may carry the colors of their household, their trade, their district, or simply whatever red dust was cheapest that season.

Their eyes are prized, spoken of, painted around, and protected. Dust is always trying to enter them. Children learn to squint before they learn to run. The oldest people often have pale, weathered eyes from a lifetime of glare off salt flats and mineral roads. They are not frail, however. Old age is visible here but not hidden. Elders sit in shaded thresholds and know more of the living map than any surveyor could: which road was once a vapor grove, which wall rests on a warming seam, which family moved because the ground beneath their courtyard began to breathe sulfur.

Their cities are crowded because crowding is a survival skill. A lone house on the open plain is a foolish thing: too exposed to dust, too distant from hands, too easily swallowed when the ground changes. But a block of houses can brace itself. Roots can be trained under foundations. Clay can be shared. Shade can overlap. A storm can be waited out among many walls.

Thus their smallest important unit is not the individual household but the **yard**: a cluster of related dwellings facing a common shaded court. Several families may sleep in separate rooms, cook at separate hearths, keep separate stores, and still belong to one yard. The yard has its own animals, its own root plots, its own dust screens, its own old disputes, and its own responsibility for the stretch of lane outside its gate. Children belong partly to their parents and partly to the yard. If a child is found climbing a roof, stealing preserved tubers, or poking a finger into a fresh ground-crack, any adult of the yard may correct them.

The people do not speak loudly unless something has gone wrong. Their ordinary speech is low and close, carried along lanes rather than cast into the sky. Even their markets are subdued. Buyers tap goods with a knuckle, hold up fingers beneath a shawl, murmur prices through cloth. Noise is considered a kind of waste—not forbidden, not shameful, but careless if there is no need for it. Their songs are therefore built differently from songs of wetter, louder lands. They favor layered hums, breathy choruses, hand percussion against hollow stems, the clicking of polished mineral pieces, and melodies that can be sung inside a courtyard without crossing a district.

Yet they are not solemn people.

They laugh with their whole bodies, bending over, covering their faces with their hands. They tease relentlessly. They are suspicious of anyone who speaks too grandly about endurance, because everyone endures; it is no particular achievement to have been born where the air strips the mouth dry. Their humor is often about the failures of certainty: the builder whose proud straight wall had to become a curved wall after the ground rose beneath it; the trader who called a patch of crust worthless and then watched it become a valuable salt bloom; the young lover who gave a stone token only for the stone to split open and release warm vapor at the recipient’s feet.

They cultivate many plants but claim ownership of few. A garden here is not a rectangle subdued by irrigation. It is an agreement with a rooted community. A family may tend a grove of vapor reeds, prune thorn-vines away from a lane, protect a patch of groundleaf from grazers, or spread mineral dust around the roots of a valuable pale-trunk tree. But the plants are understood to have chosen the place first. If a grove begins to fail because the underground breath has shifted, people do not insist upon it. They harvest what can be harvested, gather seed, and move their work elsewhere.

This has made them patient observers and reluctant planners. They build for generations, but not for permanence. Their best architecture anticipates change. Walls are thick but segmented, so one section can be taken down and rebuilt. Courtyards have deliberately empty margins where a mineral ridge may rise. Main avenues are broad not only for traffic but because their edges will inevitably move. Storehouses are raised on many small supports, not a few deep foundations, so they can be adjusted when the surface buckles. A wealthy household is not one with an enormous house. It is one with enough stored material, enough kin, and enough practiced hands to rebuild quickly.

Among them live many other creatures that are neither fully wild nor truly owned.

The small horned grazers are perhaps closest to livestock, though they wander freely through much of the city by day. They are sturdy, shoulder-high animals with backward-curving horns and dry, velvet-like muzzles. Their coats range from pale dust-gray to glossy charcoal. They feed selectively on the tender mineral tips of waxleaf mats, preventing the thick vegetation from choking paths and courtyards. Their milk is scant but rich, salted almost before it leaves the animal, and turned into sharp cheeses that can last through long bad seasons. Their horns, shed in layers, are carved into needles, spoons, whistles, fasteners, and small protective charms.

The long-legged browsers are more troublesome. They are tall as doorways, soft-footed, with narrow heads and large, patient eyes. They reach over walls to strip leaves from roof gardens and will stand outside a kitchen for hours if they smell dried fruiting bodies. No one claims them entirely. Some bear painted flank marks indicating that a particular yard has long fed and sheltered them, but the animals ignore these marks whenever it suits them. They are tolerated because their droppings are precious: warm, fibrous, mineral-rich, and alive with the microorganisms that help root gardens thrive. Their dung is gathered daily, dried, fermented, mixed with crushed crust and plant pulp, and used in soil beds, mortar, and fuel cakes.

The burrowers are less loved. They are broad-bodied, pale-snouted creatures with powerful digging forelimbs and teeth that never stop growing. They can undermine a wall in a night. They also bring up young material from below: warm dust, soft stone, glittering mineral nodules, sometimes poisonous salts. Children are taught to read the little fans of excavated powder outside a burrow. Gray powder means leave it alone. Pink powder may be useful. Black glossy grains mean call a ground-reader immediately. Burrowers are trapped for meat in lean times, though their flesh has a bitter mineral flavor and must be smoked with aromatic leaves for days.

The birds are everywhere and are treated as neighbors with opinions. Roof birds line eaves in dense rows, their feathers dull green, cream, and rust. They make low throat-calls that people can distinguish as weather signs. A sudden silence among them often means a dust front is coming. Other birds follow the warm ground vents and nest in the walls of fresh mineral ridges. Their eggs are small, hard-shelled, and highly valued. It is considered rude to take all eggs from a nest, not because the birds are sacred exactly, but because a person who takes everything has demonstrated that they cannot be trusted with anything.

Insects provide an immense hidden labor. There are dust-bees that nest in brittle clay chimneys and make a dark, sharp honey from waxleaf flowers. There are soft-bodied vapor mites that gather around warm cracks, harvested in some districts as feed for birds and fishless pond cultures. There are lacquer beetles whose secretions seal baskets and roofs. There are tiny white gnawers that can ruin a storehouse in a week, and whose arrival is one of the few things that will make a whole neighborhood shout.

The predators remain at the city’s vegetated edge: long, low hunters whose hide resembles the shadowed underside of leaves; lean pack-creatures with large ears that can hear burrowers underground; winged ambushers nesting in thorn thickets. The people do not hunt them often. Predators keep the margins from becoming overrun, and they take the sick grazing animals before sickness can enter the pens. But when one begins crossing regularly into settled lanes, it is dealt with quickly and collectively. A predator that learns the city is not merely an animal problem. It is a failure in the boundary between tended life and untended life.

There are specialists among the people, though they do not divide themselves cleanly into professions.

There are **ground-readers**, who kneel with palms against the earth at dawn and dusk, feeling for warmth, vibration, and the faint pressure of gas beneath a crust. Some use thin rods of resonant mineral; others trust their bare hands. They advise builders, gardeners, caravan leaders, and marriage negotiators. A proposed union between two yards may be delayed because one yard is likely to relocate within the decade. This is understood as sensible, not romantic tragedy.

There are **root-speakers**, who know the temperaments of plants: which reeds can be cut without killing a stand, which vines should never be pruned in the hot season, which leaf sap will bind salt mortar, which tubers store heat, which flowers open only above a useful mineral seam. Their knowledge is partly botanical, partly inherited story, partly local superstition that no one is eager to test.

There are **dustwives** and **dustkeepers**, who maintain lanes, sweep roofs, inspect air screens, and oversee the great communal clearing after storms. Their work is humble and politically important. A blocked lane can isolate a yard. A poorly cleared roof can collapse. A dustbank left against a wall can invite burrowers, insects, roots, or worse, conceal a new crack. In some districts, the dustkeeper is more respected than the nominal district speaker.

There are **saltwrights**, **sap-brewers**, **animal-callers**, **roofers**, **memory-carriers**, **market weighers**, and people who specialize in moving whole houses. There are no engineers by that word, but there are families who can look at a lifting shelf of ground and tell you how many walls must be dismantled before it becomes dangerous.

Their religion, if it is called religion, begins beneath the feet. The plain is not worshiped as a mother or a god in any simple way. It is too indifferent for that. It kills children with bad air. It splits foundations. It raises bitter plants where sweet ones used to grow. But it is also the source of every wall, road, root, animal trail, and stored mineral. They speak of the ground as **the Making Place**—not a person, not a force that listens, but an unfinished act in which they happen to live.

Their dead are not buried deeply. Deep ground is too active and too valuable to disturb without reason. Bodies are instead placed in elevated mineral chambers beyond the busiest districts, wrapped in dryfiber and layered with salt, aromatic leaves, and resin. In time, the bones are cleaned by heat and insects. Families may bring certain bones home: a wrist bone set into a doorway, a tooth threaded on a cord, a polished knuckle joint carried by a traveler. The dead are not thought to reside in these remains exactly. But the remains are proof that a person once had weight on the plain, and that the plain has not erased them entirely.

Children are taught the city as a living thing and the plain as its larger body. They learn routes by texture beneath their soles: crushed white mineral for a market lane, black grit for an old volcanic shelf, smooth packed clay for an animal corridor. They learn to smell a dangerous vent before they can read. They play games that look like racing but are really training in evacuation: who can reach shade fastest, who can tie a face-wrap in the dark, who can identify a safe wall by tapping it. They collect colored dust in folded leaves and trade it like treasure. They dare one another to touch the warmest ground cracks, though every generation is told not to.

Visitors find the people of the plain hard to understand at first. They seem poor in the ways of lands with rivers: no broad fields, no canals, no wet orchards, no boats, no polished abundance. But they are rich in stored possibility. Their homes contain dried foods, seeds, mineral salts, resins, fibers, skins, tools, roof materials, repair stones, family knowledge, animal relationships, and obligations that stretch across districts. A traveler may arrive with coin and still be helpless. Someone born in a poor yard may own almost nothing and yet know three safe routes across a moving shelf, the nesting habits of roof birds, and the name of every person who owes their grandmother a favor. Such a person is not poor by local measure.

And beneath the people, beneath the animals, beneath the packed streets and rooted courtyards, other lives continue their slow work.

Microbial mats bloom in warmed cracks, green-black and metallic, breathing gases that feed the deep roots. Blind pale worms move through mineral seams where no water ever runs. Fungal threads, dry as wire, connect plant roots under entire districts. In the hotter pockets, translucent crust-creatures form delicate colonies around vents, growing mineral shells in branching fans. Most people never see these things clearly. They know them by consequence: a sweet smell in the dust, a sudden flush of growth, a wall whose mortar hardens too fast, a patch of ground that hums faintly after sunset.

The city lives because all these lives overlap without entirely agreeing.

People prune what they cannot destroy. Animals eat what people cannot use. Roots split the stone that builders quarry. Dust covers the paths that dustkeepers clear. New ground rises beneath old houses. The plain makes room by refusing to stay still.

And the people, who have never known a finished world, make their homes in that refusal.
