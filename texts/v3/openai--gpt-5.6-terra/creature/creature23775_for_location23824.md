They are called, among themselves, the Underroot People only when speaking to strangers, and there have been no strangers for a very long time.

More often they use no collective name. A person is from a particular drip, a particular bridge, a particular old sheltering root. They say, *I sleep beneath the root that leans east,* or, *My mother kept the lamp at Blackwater Bend.* To belong to the city is not to belong to a boundary. It is to know which sounds are ordinary where you live, and which silence means that something has arrived.

They are small-looking people in the vastness of the hollow, but not frail. Their bodies tend toward compactness: broad feet for mud, strong hands, bent backs from working beneath low leaf ceilings. Their skin is dark with weather, though always filmed with damp, and their hair is kept close-cut, braided, or rubbed with pungent oils to discourage lice and moss-gnats. Older people often wear their hair in long ropes stiffened with root resin, decorated with fish vertebrae, carved shell, or the iridescent wing cases of insects gathered after storms.

Their clothing is layered, because cold is more dangerous than rain. They weave marsh fiber into rough tunics, wrap themselves in softened browse-beast hair, and wear capes made of overlapping water-shedding leaves sealed with wax. The leaf capes make them difficult to see in the dark lanes. From a distance, one can mistake a person standing still for a curtain of vegetation, especially when their cloak is hung with bits of moss deliberately encouraged to grow there.

Their sandals are broad and flat, made from rootwood slats strapped to the feet with braided cord. They make a peculiar soft sucking noise in wet ground. Children wear smaller versions, often tied with bright cords dyed from fungus or crushed beetles—not because brightness is decorative, exactly, but because in the green-black gloom it helps adults find them. A child can disappear behind a hanging fern with alarming ease.

They do not usually speak loudly. Their language is full of clicks, breath sounds, and low throat-notes that carry well through reed walls without traveling too far down a lane. They have many words for water in motion: water that is falling, water that is rising, water that has remembered a channel, water that has stopped where it should not stop. They have fewer words for distance. Far away is simply “beyond the last familiar drip.”

Their names change.

A child is given a temporary name soon after birth, usually connected to the circumstances of their arrival: Reed-Split, Rain-Quiet, Small Lantern, Root-Sleeping. At the age when they can cross three bridges alone and return without being called, they choose or are given another. An adult may take a new name after surviving an illness, losing a home to flood, finding a missing person, or encountering one of the unnamed predators and living. The oldest residents sometimes have names too long to say in full. Their close kin use only the first sound.

The people are not farmers in the sense that a dry-country people might recognize. They tend possibilities.

They encourage edible tubers where the soil remains warm. They transplant medicinal molds between shaded bowls. They cultivate pale, soft-bodied beetles for their oil, though “cultivate” may mean leaving them the correct kind of rot and asking children not to disturb their breeding walls. They gather fish from the channels using woven traps, but never take the first fish caught in a new trap. That fish is returned with a strip of boiled root tied around its tail, so the water knows the trap is not greedy.

They keep no strict distinction between food, medicine, and omen. A fungus can be all three depending on where it grew, who found it, and whether it pulsed before being touched.

The elderly are especially valued because they remember the shape of things that no longer exist. Not merely old paths or houses, but the behavior of places. An elder may say that a particular patch of ground once held water even though it has been dry for twenty years, and people will listen. Someone may recall that a root wall made a hollow knocking sound before it opened one spring and swallowed a work platform. Someone may remember the smell that came before the last visit of a long-limbed predator.

Memory is practical architecture.

There are people whose work is remembering. They tend the bark maps in the communal chamber, adding fresh stains, knots, and carved markings. They are sometimes called wet-readers. They do not predict the future exactly. They study the rearrangements of dampness, fungi, animal paths, and root pressure. They advise where a family should move before a floor turns soft. They mark corridors that have become too quiet. They can look at a trail of displaced moss and tell whether it was made by a child, a hound, a six-legged browse-beast, or something that should not be followed.

Their maps contain no permanent center, because the city has never had one. The communal chamber is important, but it is not the heart. Hearts are too fixed. The city’s life travels through it in shifting currents: a new fungus garden, a safe dry platform, a cluster of births, a season in which the channels are rich with fish. The town’s center is wherever people have begun to leave their lamps burning longer.

Children belong partly to households and partly to the lanes. They are watched, but not continuously. They learn early to distinguish safe sounds from dangerous ones. The slow scrape of a browse-beast against a root is safe. The chittering under bridges is safe unless it stops all at once. The flap of the pale upside-down birds is safe if it comes in groups; one bird flying alone at night is not.

They are taught to carry three things when they first begin wandering: a twist of bitter leaf, a polished shell that catches lamp-light, and a reed whistle that makes a sound unpleasant to certain small animals. They are taught never to use the whistle merely for play. They are also taught that if they become lost, they should not run. Running makes the fungi flare, and the hollow notices light moving quickly.

Their games imitate the city’s dangers, though gently. They play Floodhouse, in which children pile reeds around a chosen sleeper and then decide whether the water is friendly or hungry. They play Root-Crossing, balancing along low ridges while blindfolded, guided only by the dripping patterns. They play Naming, in which one child points to shadows and the others invent names until someone says a name that feels too exact. Then everyone must be silent for a count of one hundred heartbeats.

No one agrees on whether the game began as a warning or a joke.

Their dead are not buried deeply. The ground is too wet and too alive, and burial implies that a body can be placed somewhere and left unchanged. Instead, the dead are washed in rainwater collected from particular leaves, wrapped in woven fiber, and placed in shallow root hollows above the floodline. A person’s tools, if useful, are distributed among kin. Their name is not used for some time. Their bones are eventually recovered if the roots permit it; the smallest bones may be polished and hung in doorways, not as trophies, but so that the dead continue to announce arrivals.

They do not regard this as haunting. They regard it as courtesy.

The city also contains those who are not human but are treated as citizens of a kind.

The marsh hounds have their own sleeping bridges and are fed before long rains. No one commands them. A family may be accompanied by the same hound for years, but the animal may leave at any time and return with scars, pups, or a different temper. When a marsh hound refuses to cross a bridge, people do not cross it either.

The six-legged browse-beasts wander through courtyards and sleeping spaces with the indifference of visiting elders. Their wool is combed only after they have eaten. Their young are never touched. A browse-beast that enters a house uninvited is considered inconvenient but fortunate; it means the house still smells enough like a living place.

The little black foragers are fed in cracks near kitchens. Children sometimes make them tiny shelters from shells and reed scraps. Adults pretend not to notice. The foragers understand more than they should. They steal food, carry scraps away, discover corpses, expose bad stores, and occasionally gather in large silent crowds outside a dwelling before its inhabitants realize that mold has entered the walls.

There are also solitary residents: an old pale bird that returns every year to the same roof vine; a blind channel-swimmer that surfaces beneath one particular footbridge; a scarred grazer that accepts food only from widows. These beings are not named publicly. Families may carry private names for them, but saying such names outside the household is thought to make the relationship brittle.

Some people leave the hollow, though not often.

They are called ridge-walkers, and they travel light: a stone blade, a bundle of dried bulbs, a lamp protected in a clay shell, enough cord to haul themselves from mud, and strips of bright woven cloth to mark a return route. They go out after storms to examine new ground, gather unfamiliar seeds, or seek relatives whose houses have migrated too close to an outer edge.

Many ridge-walkers come back changed. They bring stories of open sky that no one wholly believes. They speak of wind that moves without touching leaves first. They describe a light that comes from above and remains for hours. Some return with sun-burned skin, dry cracked lips, and a restlessness that makes them unable to sleep beneath the canopy. Most recover. A few do not. They move their mats to the outermost roots and spend their remaining years listening upward.

The people do not consider themselves trapped. Trapped things struggle against their enclosure. The Underroot People know that the hollow feeds them, hides them, drowns them, shelters them, confuses them, and occasionally takes one of them without explanation. This is not kindness. It is not cruelty. It is the condition of being alive in the same place as something larger.

Their deepest fear is not death in the hollow.

It is becoming legible to it.

To be fully known by the roots, the water, the patient molds, the hunting things beyond the lane entrances—to become predictable enough that the place can anticipate your steps—this is worse than being lost. So they vary their routes. They change the position of lamps. They do not tell the same story in exactly the same words twice. They move a sleeping mat now and then, even in a safe house. They teach children that habit is a trail, and trails are invitations.

Yet they remain tender with one another.

In the long rains, when the chamber fills with amber light and the city outside becomes a vast breathing darkness, they sit close. They share boiled roots, fish paste, hot sap, and the sharp medicinal broth made from bitter leaves. They mend sandals. They comb damp hair. They count who has returned. They listen to the water knock softly against the living walls.

And when a new path appears in the morning, they do not call it a miracle.

They send someone to walk it carefully.
