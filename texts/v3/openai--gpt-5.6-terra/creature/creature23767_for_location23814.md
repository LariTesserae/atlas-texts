What lives there first is movement.

Not individual animals, not workers, not even the water itself, but the habit of things being carried. Everything in the flooded works has learned to yield to current or make use of it. A loose glove circles a pillar for three months. A wrench falls from a service platform and is found two districts away in the mouth of a drainage culvert, polished at the edges. Fish hatch in a reservoir, spend their first weeks among the ribs of a submerged freight elevator, and are taken as adults through an intake channel into halls whose names were printed on plans before their species arrived.

The sea has residents in the ordinary sense. It has populations. It has neighborhoods.

The silver fish are everywhere, but they do not belong everywhere equally. In the long rail cuts, where the walls run straight for kilometers and the lamps are arranged in receding files, they travel in broad, disciplined shoals. They hold themselves at a particular depth, always a little above the old tracks, where the current is cleanest. From a distance they resemble a second system of lighting: a moving constellation passing beneath the fixed lights overhead.

In the old assembly bays they gather around the pillars. The concrete columns rise from the floor in grids so large that a person swimming among them may lose sight of the next row. Fish turn the grid into territory. One school occupies the shadowed side of a pillar, another the bright side. When the pumps alter, the boundaries loosen. Hundreds of thousands of bodies tilt at once, showing their pale flanks, and the entire bay seems to flash and extinguish.

Carp live lower. They are creatures of sediment, of drowned paperwork and collapsed ceiling tile, of the black fur that gathers beneath loading doors. They have made the former receiving docks their riverbanks. Their mouths work patiently through the deposits left by decades of circulation: softened wood, insect husks, algae, pellets from broken hoppers, whatever comes down from the upper dry levels in the rainwater and the drains. In some basins their backs breach the silt so often that workers have mistaken them for moving lengths of hose.

The catfish are known by place before they are known by sight.

There is one in the flooded locomotive shed beneath Sector Nine, enormous and old enough to have scars from a propeller or a gate mechanism across its head. It rests inside a derailed freight car whose side has rusted open. Maintenance crews call it the Foreman, though no one claims to have seen all of it. Its barbels emerge first from the doorway, feeling the current. Then its head, broad and black in the worklight. When it moves, smaller fish do not flee in panic. They simply make room.

Others keep to turbine chambers, where the water is loudest. They drift in the turbulence below the shut wheels, feeding on whatever is stunned or broken loose when the machinery cycles. Their lives are organized around impact and leftovers. They know the deep concussion of a gate opening as closely as land animals know thunder.

The eels have become the most intimate inhabitants of the complex. Fish pass through it; seals visit it; birds come and go. Eels occupy its nerves.

They live inside cable runs, behind control panels, in lengths of conduit sealed at both ends except for a crack no wider than a finger. A diver opening a junction box may find one folded inside around the old wiring, its skin reflecting the colored indicator lamps. They slip through the machinery with an ease that makes the place seem built for them. In dry upper rooms, workers sometimes find them in floor channels beneath the grates, breathing in shallow water among bolts and dead moths.

Some are marked with bright bands where their skins have been burned or stained by old insulation compounds. Under red emergency lighting, they seem to carry the warning colors with them: black, yellow, black, yellow, disappearing under a panel door.

The birds live closest to weather.

Gulls claim the high trusses, especially in halls where vapor collects against the ceiling and the lamps burn warm enough to attract insects. Their nests are untidy constructions of wire insulation, reeds, packaging foam, rags, and feathers plastered flat by condensation. The oldest nests become platforms, then mounds. In a few upper bays, generations of birds have built enough material into the angle between beams that small dry islands exist overhead: white-streaked, noisy, smelling of fish and hot metal.

The swallows are more precise. They nest in the mouths of abandoned ventilation ducts and enter the flooded halls through openings no larger than a handspan. Their flight is complicated by the machinery. They know where steam will vent before it does. They skim low over the water when the intake fans are quiet and climb rapidly when the pressure alarms begin. They drink from the surfaces of sheltered pools between floating pallets. In the artificial dawn, when the timed lamps brighten district by district, they are often the first things to move.

Then there are rats, whose empire is almost entirely inferred.

They live in the dry ribs of the place: ceiling voids, insulation jackets, electrical chases, the hollow spaces inside walls meant to contain pipes. Their paths cross the flooded world by way of cable bundles and upper catwalks. They leave droppings in panel rooms and chewed foam in tool lockers. They steal from workers’ boats when boats are moored overnight. They carry seeds into places no seed ought to reach.

A reed bed in a floor drain may begin with a rat. So may a patch of fungus behind a warm transformer. So may the sudden appearance of a tomato plant, thin and yellow, growing from a crack in the service deck beside a vending machine that has not worked in twenty years.

The seals are not residents exactly, though some stay long enough to acquire habits. They arrive by way of the discharge works, following tidal water into the system, and at first they are confused by the absence of horizon. They surface under ceilings. They lift their heads toward lamps. They sleep on buoyant barriers and maintenance pontoons, their bodies rising and falling with pump-induced tides rather than with any moon.

A seal that has spent a season inside learns the quiet routes. It learns which basins hold fish after the hopper cycles and which current corridors can pull a young animal hard against the grilles. It learns the sound of boats. Workers sometimes find them waiting at the edges of lit platforms, watching through the water as gauges are read and nets are cleared. Their eyes make the divers uneasy. Not because the animals are threatening, but because they seem to expect the place to explain itself.

The workers live there in intervals.

They do not inhabit the sea as the fish do. They arrive in shifts, with sealed food containers, charged radios, replacement filters, lamp housings, inspection tablets. They know the place through procedures: access routes, pressure tolerances, lockout sequences, the locations of emergency air pockets. Their maps are made of symbols and colors. They call a drowned freight terminal a Basin C. They call a flooded avenue between warehouses a current corridor. They call the blackness below the old cooling towers a restricted depth.

But long-serving workers begin to belong to it in other ways.

They recognize individual patterns in the sound of pumps. They know when a fan bearing has begun to fail because the hum changes from smooth to granular. They can tell which district they are in by the kind of condensation on the railings: fine and cold near the intake halls, oily near the generator galleries, mineral-heavy in the old water-treatment wings. They name certain fish, though they deny doing so. They leave a few moments of extra light on over a particular platform where seals haul out. They complain about gulls as if the gulls were coworkers from another department.

Some have spent so many years on the water that ordinary open landscapes make them uncomfortable. A real sky feels exposed. Wind without walls feels directionless. In the drowned complex, every danger has a gauge, a siren, a line painted on concrete. Every tide is scheduled somewhere, even if the schedule is wrong.

There are also machines that have become, if not alive, resident.

The crane in the far bay still travels. It does not carry cargo now; its hook hangs empty beneath it, trailing a ribbon of chain into the water. Its route is a loop programmed before the flood, perhaps intended for calibration, perhaps never meant to continue unsupervised this long. It crosses its overhead rail at the same hours each day. Fish scatter beneath its shadow. Birds shift on the beams. Workers listen for the click of its wheels and use it as a clock when their radios fail.

Certain valves cycle without intervention. Certain pumps correct themselves. A bank of feeding hoppers in the eastern reservoirs still releases pellets, though no surviving record agrees on who is responsible for stocking them. The machines are maintained by other machines, or by workers who assume someone else has done it, or by no one at all. Their continued operation is one of the sea’s facts, like coldness.

In the deepest zones, life thins but does not vanish.

There are pale things in the unfinished drilling chambers: shrimp, blind or nearly blind, feeding on films along the walls where mineral seepage meets the treated water. There are worms in the soft accumulations under the basins. There are bacterial veils on old steel, faintly iridescent, growing where heat leaks from buried conduits. Divers report seeing these films pulse when a distant generator starts, though this may be an effect of current, light, or fatigue.

No one spends long in those lower places. They are too far from air, too dense with pressure and machinery, too difficult to leave if a gate changes state. Yet even there, the complex is occupied. A snail clings to a pressure gauge. An eel passes through a crack in a wall. A cloud of tiny fish holds itself in the warm outflow of a pipe, each body trembling but not being carried away.

What lives there, finally, is an ecology that has accepted being engineered without becoming obedient.

It does not mistake the halls for nature. The fish do not see forests in the columns or cliffs in the retaining walls. The seals do not imagine the lamps are stars. The workers do not truly believe they control the water.

But all of them use what is available: light that comes on at six, warmth leaking from a transformer, current sweeping food toward a grille, a dry beam high above the flood line, a maintenance boat that leaves scraps in its wake. The sea provides no shore, but it offers edges everywhere—bolt heads, ladder rungs, flooded windows, the lips of pipes—and life has fastened itself to every one.
