What lives there is not a collection of animals, though it can be counted that way.

There are fish in the trenches, birds in the duct hollows, crabs in the grates, amphibians along the warm mineral seams, and black-winged things hanging from the ceiling. Each has a shape distinct enough to name. Each breeds in a different portion of the chamber. Each dies differently. But none of them, alone, seems entirely to belong.

The fish are the oldest-looking, though perhaps not the oldest. Their skins are white in the manner of old porcelain, faintly translucent around the gills. They have lost their eyes without becoming blind in the ordinary sense. Along their flanks run rows of pores that tremble when a pump cycles or a valve turns three rooms away. They feel pressure the way surface creatures feel light. A change in the facility passes through the water and gathers in their bodies. When the system is healthy, they move slowly and densely, nose to tail, combing the trenches with their mouths. When something upstream begins to fail, they turn all at once toward the same drain.

The workers say that is how the chamber tells you where to look.

The crabs are less animal than mechanism until they move. Their shells have the dull, powder-coated gray of unfinished equipment. In the joints of their legs, red mineral staining collects like grease. They live under the grates, where the water has more current, and they hold themselves against it with claws that lock onto steel. Their young are almost invisible: tiny square motes that drift through the drain channels and settle into corners where hair, oil, grit, and flakes of insulation have gathered. There they make little nests of debris. In time, the nests harden. A person inspecting the drains might mistake one for corrosion.

They eat what the chamber sheds.

The birds came later, or seem to have. They have the shabby, resigned expression of things that remember open air only as an inherited discomfort. Their feet are broad and gripping, suited to pipe insulation and cable bundles. Their eggs are laid in the shallow cups of abandoned junction boxes, among dust, feathers, and the warm skins of sleeping bodies. They feed on insects no one else notices: pale flies that hatch in overflow pans, threadlike moths that consume fibrous lagging, minute beetles that live inside the growing mineral crusts.

Their calls do not carry as calls. The machinery has taken them in.

A bird cries, and the sound is immediately stretched flat by the ducts, sharpened by the steam lines, buried under the vibration of the pumps. What comes back is not a cry but a tiny notch in the larger chord. The birds appear to listen for these notches. Sometimes, all at once, they fall quiet. That is worse than their noise. It means some frequency in the machinery has changed enough for them to hear it.

The amphibians are the nearest thing the chamber has to a true native species.

No one agrees where they originated. Some reports claim they began as larvae carried in cooling water from a lowland reservoir. Others say they were bred deliberately, years ago, from several useful organisms and released into the drains as a maintenance experiment. There are older workers who insist the chamber made them from what it had available: bacterial films, antifreeze compounds, blind fish eggs, skin cells washed from the workers’ gloves, the thin residue of life that accumulates wherever warm water is trapped inside a machine.

Whatever they were, they no longer resemble a thing from outside.

They are soft-bodied and almost transparent, their organs suspended within them like pale tools in clear gel. They have too many legs, but not quite enough for their movement to seem quick. They progress by adhesion, unfolding and refolding along wet surfaces. Around a pipe collar they will gather in heaps, dozens deep, their lamprey mouths pressed to the mineral foam. The uppermost animals glow blue-white through their skin. The ones beneath glow green. Deeper still, where the pile is pressed against steel, there is a slow red pulse.

They are warm to the touch.

Not warmly alive, exactly. More like a battery kept beneath a blanket.

When a new mineral ridge begins forming, the amphibians arrive before it is visible. They surround the leak, feed on its first chemical bloom, and leave behind a fine, pale waste that gives the deposit structure. The ridge grows around them. In places, old formations contain their shed skins in translucent layers, one molt after another, like leaves caught in ice. They have become part of the stone-making process. The stone, in turn, gives them edges and crevices in which to cluster.

Nothing in the room is merely habitat. Everything is also an organ.

The hanging creatures are the hardest to describe because nobody sees them properly. They are called bats because they fold themselves into dark bundles and because, under certain conditions, they release a fine clicking through the air. But their wings have no clean outline. In the blue light they look like torn strips of rubber, or burnt insulation, or strips of old rain gear caught on a duct. Their faces remain hidden even when they wake. They have no visible eyes, only a series of delicate pits along the front of the head that open and close with the vibration of the fans.

They do not hunt in the conventional way.

They feed on turbulence.

Where air moves evenly, they hang still. Where a duct begins to flutter, where a bearing produces a faint irregularity, where steam escapes through a crack too narrow for an instrument to notice, they gather. Their wings open. They turn their pale undersides toward the disturbance. In the course of hours, or days, a black cluster forms around the fault.

The maintenance crew has learned not to disturb such clusters. If the bats are roosting over a seam, that seam is watched. If they hang in a ring around a motor housing, the motor is scheduled for inspection. If they descend into the room and begin looping through the blue light, beating their wings against the ventilator current, someone puts on a suit and starts checking the pressure systems.

No one knows what they consume. Some say oil vapor. Some say airborne microbes. Some say the microscopic flakes shed by insulation and paint. The oldest version is that they eat bad sound: the clicks, rattles, and unstable frequencies that would otherwise spread through the machinery until something comes apart.

Perhaps this is why the chamber’s chord remains so stable.

There are smaller lives as well.

White worms inhabit the wet insulation, appearing only when a panel is lifted. Their bodies are made of repeated beads and shine with a wet metallic luster. They retreat from light but gather around a bare human wrist, sensing heat.

Mites live in the birds’ feathers and in the felted fur of condensation on the railings. They are blue-gray and nearly flat, with legs so fine they resemble scratches in the frost.

A black fungus grows behind certain warning placards, feeding on adhesive, paint, and the softened paper of old maintenance tags. It blooms only when the air pressure drops. Then it sends out a smell like wet coins.

There are red thread colonies inside the overflow pipes, soft masses of bacteria or something like bacteria, which contract when exposed to the wrong chemical balance. Their contractions move through the pipe walls as little knocks. The workers call them the knockers, though nobody jokes about them.

And among the mineral formations, too small to see until the light strikes at an angle, live things like dust motes with shells. They graze the white terraces in slow, shining swarms. They build their shells from the chamber’s salts. A whole generation may live and die between two droplets.

All of them are fed, in one way or another, by the facility’s inability to be perfect.

Every leak sustains something. Every vibration becomes a signal. Every trace of warmth, every residue of human passage, every failed seal, every microscopic crack in a welded plate is received by the room and translated into life.

The chamber does not forgive waste. It keeps it.

It receives hair, skin, oil, detergent, boot mud, blood from cut knuckles, breath caught in filters, the dead spores of distant forests brought in on freight crates. It breaks them down, passes them through mouths and gills and fungal threads, folds them into mineral crust and fish flesh and the powdery sediment in the drains. What leaves the chamber is cleaner than what enters, though never entirely clean. It leaves carrying a faint biological signature: salts arranged by crabs, bacteria shaped by amphibian guts, a trace of the black-winged things’ dry, bitter dust.

There may be one other resident.

It has no body anyone can point to.

It is the arrangement itself: the fixed chord, the unbroken dampness, the balance of heat and cold, the way a mineral ridge seals a leak just enough to preserve the leak that feeds it. It lives in the mutual adjustment of every creature and every machine. It is present when the fish turn together, when the birds stop calling, when the bats gather over a fault, when the amphibians climb a newly sweating flange.

The workers who have spent too long in the upper works sometimes speak to it without admitting that they do.

They stand at the threshold and say, “All right. What is it?”

Then they wait.

A flock resettles in the ducts.

A wet clicking begins along a pipe collar.

The fish gather in one darkening trench.

And somewhere inside the walls, beneath the constant fall of water, the chamber answers in the only language it has: by continuing.
