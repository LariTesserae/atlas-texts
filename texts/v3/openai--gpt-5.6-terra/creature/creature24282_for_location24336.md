What lives in the valley is not a people, exactly.

It is a custody.

Not one creature, not one species, not even a stable community, but a long inheritance of animals and plants that have learned the built mountain as if it were a climate. The walls are their cliffs. Gutters are their rivers. Cisterns are their lakes. A stairwell is a ravine; an abandoned storehouse is a cave; a roof garden is a forest canopy raised improbably high above another roof garden, another bridge, another dark court full of dripping leaves.

The first inhabitants are the small ones.

Moss lives there with the confidence of an old empire. It occupies the shaded seams of paving, the backs of carved figures, the mouths of drains. It drinks the warm dampness breathed out by the stone and turns every neglected surface soft and dark. Ferns root in mortar above sheer drops. Liverworts spread in the undersides of stairs. Fine white fungi lace through beams, through old baskets, through the packed soil in planters and bird nests. Some grow only in the steam-warm chambers around the reservoirs, pale shelves shining faintly in the dark; some form black velvet skins around leaking pipes and hidden channels.

The vines are not merely plants. They are roads, ladders, curtains, food stores, nesting material, and treaties between separate pieces of masonry. They begin in deliberate soil pockets—old stone troughs, niches beside doors, terraces designed for roots—and then forget the limits set for them. One reaches across an alley. Another catches it. Their stems thicken, twist, and become load-bearing in their own quiet way. Monkeys know which strands will hold them. Young parrots sleep in the dense crossings. Snakes use them to move from roof to roof without touching open ground.

The valley has snakes.

They are mostly small, dull green or brown, nearly invisible among wet leaves and mineral-stained walls. They live beneath cistern covers, in root tunnels, in the deep cracks where terraces meet retaining walls. At night, they rest on masonry still warm from the vanished day. They take frogs from basin rims, mice from grain chambers, nestlings from crowded cornices. One may lie along a stair for hours, looking like a fallen vine until it slides soundlessly beneath a door that has not opened for generations.

There are also larger things in the water.

The lower cisterns are inhabited by blind fish, pale as wax, which feed on insect larvae and the drifting debris that washes down from higher courts. In the oldest reservoirs, where the water is black and the ceilings low, there are eels. They pass through channels too narrow to inspect, moving between districts of the buried water system. Nobody in the valley needs to know where they breed. They appear after rain in unexpected basins, curled among drowned leaves, and disappear before morning.

The frogs are perhaps the valley’s true singers.

They live in every degree of wetness. Tiny ones cling to glazed tiles in the misting passages; heavy, warty ones sit in the spillway mouths and swell their throats until the stones vibrate. Their voices differ from court to court. A traveler who knew the place well could tell their height on the mountain by the frogs alone: the deep hollow knock of the lower basins, the fast metallic ticking from the rain terraces, the shrill glassy calls from gardens near the cloud line.

Birds occupy the upper surfaces of everything.

Pigeons live in the old drainage architecture, where they make use of openings too regular to be natural. Swifts vanish into cracks under cornices and emerge in sudden black swarms, turning through narrow streets at speeds that make them seem to pass through walls. The green parrots have claimed the planted levels. They strip fruit, tear leaves, scatter seeds into drains, and scream at dawn—or what passes for dawn in a valley where light arrives in fragments. Their descendants have changed the gardens. Fruit trees grow in impossible corners because a parrot once dropped a pit into a pocket of wet mortar.

Under the bridges are the dark birds: crows, grackles, perhaps creatures descended from either and now belonging to neither. Their feathers shine oil-blue when lightning enters the valley. They know the dogs by name, or by smell, or by some other durable fact. They follow the goats. They gather at dead animals without fear. They carry bright useless objects into inaccessible ledges: snail shells, white teeth, shards of colored tile, pieces of old ceramic glaze. Over centuries they have made small museums in the rafters.

The bats own the air after dusk.

They pour from cracks in the upper retaining walls in numbers large enough to alter the sound of the rain. Their flight routes are ancient and exact: out across the west terraces, under the aqueduct arches, through the hanging gardens, over the lower gate, back through a slit above a courtyard where no one would think an opening existed. Moths vanish before them. Mosquitoes vanish. Sometimes a bat blunders into a leafy passage and hangs briefly from a vine, wrapped like a dark fruit among the glossy leaves.

The mammals are more secretive, but they shape the place.

The goats are descendants of animals once kept by people. They no longer need keeping. They know every roofline, every broken parapet, every patch of hardy grass growing from the top of a wall. Their paths are vertical. They sleep in windless alcoves and give birth in forgotten shrines, their kids wedged safely behind old stone screens. In rain they gather beneath deep arches, patient and sulfur-eyed, while water falls in sheets beyond them.

The dogs are not wolves, though the valley has made them less domestic than either word can comfortably hold. They live in loose courts and familial packs. Some are thin and red-brown, built for stairs; some are broad-headed and pale, sleeping in old guardrooms; some have thick damp coats that grow moss-colored along the spine. They hunt rats, share scraps from bird kills, take fallen fruit, and occasionally bring down a goat too old or injured to keep to the heights. They do not bark often. In the echoing architecture, a bark is too much like an announcement. Instead they make low breathy calls to one another from passage to passage.

There are cats, but no one sees them until they have become used to being watched.

They live where the walls are hottest: near chimneys that no longer smoke, above buried kitchens, in the thick masonry around old baths. They are long-bodied and soot-colored, with ears nicked by generations of roof fights. Their paws make no sound on wet tile. They enter pigeon roosts through holes the size of a fist. They sleep in niches once intended for lamps.

And among all these lives something more difficult to name.

It may be a human remnant.

Not a population, perhaps. Not builders maintaining the valley in any organized sense. But there are signs that some inheritor still moves through it: a drain cleared before it becomes dangerous; a fallen stone propped back beneath a load; bundles of dried leaves tucked beneath a sheltered eave; fruit gathered from a terrace at the proper moment rather than left for the parrots. Sometimes the dogs look toward a dark doorway and wag their tails without approaching. Sometimes a monkey pauses on a gutter rail, gives a soft cough, and retreats as though an older animal has claimed the way.

If such people exist, they have become part of the valley’s low light. They do not announce themselves with flame or metal or new walls. They wear damp-colored cloth, perhaps. They know where the warm stones are. They sleep in rooms behind vines and move by memory through passages too dark for ordinary sight. The birds do not flee them. The goats do not turn their heads. They may be gardeners, or keepers of water, or simply descendants of those who stayed until staying became indistinguishable from belonging.

But perhaps there are no people at all.

Perhaps the clearing of drains is done by floods, roots, rats, and chance. Perhaps a stone settles into a useful position because the mountain is always slowly rearranging itself. Perhaps the bundles beneath the eaves are nests. Perhaps the dogs wag at smells rising from old rooms.

The valley does not explain.

It is full enough without explanation.

What lives there is the long, wet intelligence of habitation: creatures fitting themselves into spaces made for other creatures; roots taking the place of scaffolds; nests replacing cupboards; water remembering every route through the built mountain. Every arch contains sleepers. Every gutter carries a migration. Every patch of darkness has eyes in it, wings in it, breath in it.

The old makers made a valley into walls.

Everything that came afterward has made those walls alive.
