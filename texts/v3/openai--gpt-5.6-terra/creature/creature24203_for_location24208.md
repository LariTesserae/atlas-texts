They are people who have learned to live without expecting dryness, distance, or silence.

Most of them can tell, from the sound beneath a grate, whether a drain is clear. They know which stairwells take water first, which underpasses smell wrong before a flood, which walls sweat harmlessly and which are about to lose a seam. Children are taught the city’s channels before they are trusted to cross certain streets alone. They learn not to drop things through gratings, not to lean on old railings, not to walk beneath a suspended load, not to take a shortcut through a pump gallery without telling someone where they are going.

They live close together because there is nowhere else to live. Families occupy rooms grown around pipes and landings: an old warehouse office divided into apartments, three floors above a machine shop, a row of narrow brick houses with shared internal courtyards, former storage lofts reached by bridges that sway slightly in the wind. A kitchen may look out onto another kitchen six feet away across a covered alley. Bedrooms have ventilation pipes boxed into their corners. Laundry hangs in heated passageways and never quite loses the smell of damp iron and soap.

Nobody considers this especially bleak. They complain, certainly: about mold, about night freight, about the elevator in Block 19 that stops between floors when the weather changes, about repairs that create leaks elsewhere. But they also know one another by sound. A household can recognize the tread of its neighbors on the shared stairs, the cough of the old man next door, the particular rattle of a cart belonging to the woman who sells pickled greens from a former ticket booth. Privacy is made from curtains, timing, low voices, and mutual restraint rather than space.

There are workers everywhere, though many would not call themselves industrial workers. The city has blurred the distinction. A person who operates a press may also clear leaves from an intake screen on the walk home. A baker watches the pressure gauge in the basement because the oven’s steam line is shared with two upstairs flats. A schoolteacher keeps a wrench in her desk for tightening the radiator valves. Children in the upper schools spend certain afternoons with drainage crews, roof gardeners, or electrical maintenance teams—not as punishment, but as a civic education as ordinary as reading.

The people of the waterworks are respected in a manner that is almost ceremonial, though they dislike ceremony. They wear dark waterproof coats, rubber boots, gloves patched at the fingers, and badges with numbers rather than names when they are on shift. Some work the great pumps; some inspect the covered reservoirs; some descend into return mains with lamps on their helmets and radios pressed close to their mouths. Their work is dangerous, dirty, and indispensable. When a waterworks crew appears in a lane, residents move bins, bicycles, market stalls, and children out of the way before being asked.

There are old families among them who can trace their work through generations: a grandfather who maintained the intake gates, a mother who mapped the hidden channels, a daughter now studying the new pressure tunnel. Yet the city also draws in people from outside—surveyors, machinists, cooks, electricians, refugees from drier towns where factories have closed, young engineers who arrive intending to stay for a season and remain for twenty years. The valley changes newcomers. At first they look upward, unsettled by the walls and cloud. Later they begin to recognize districts by the taste of their water, the color of their brick, the rhythm of their pumps.

Their clothes favor practicality but acquire local habits. Waterproof capes are repaired rather than replaced. Boots are kept by the door in ranks, often marked with colored cord so they can be found in the dimness. People wear wool beneath waxed canvas, knitted caps under hard hats, fingerless gloves for working controls. Many carry small foldable umbrellas, though umbrellas are less useful against the dripping from above than they are against rain. A city-made person always has a rag, a length of wire, a small torch, and something for clearing a blocked drain.

Food is warm, dense, and designed for damp weather. There are canteens beneath workshops and kitchens built into old loading bays, serving bean stews, barley, cabbage, fried river fish when any can be had, bread dark with grain, onions cooked down in fat, apples from the walled yards. Roof gardens contribute greens, herbs, mushrooms grown in former boiler rooms, and fruit that ripens late in the valley’s weak light. Tea and bitter coffee are drunk from thick cups. Every district has its own preserved vegetables and its own opinion about how much vinegar is proper.

The city’s social calendar follows water and work more than seasons. There are annual days when flood barriers are tested, when roof gardens are cleared before the winter rains, when whole streets turn out to scrape moss from old steps and paint high-water marks afresh. The spring thaw is anticipated with practical dread. People stock sandbags in hallways, lift furniture onto blocks, check sump pumps, and argue over whose drain connection is causing the backflow. When the water rises, strangers become organized quickly. Ropes appear. Doors are opened. Canteens extend their hours. Someone always has a spare pump.

They have festivals, though these are not grand displays. In summer, when the cloud lifts enough for a few hours of pale evening, upper tram platforms become gathering places. Lanterns are hung beneath the railings. Music is played in machine halls after shifts: accordions, brass, battered keyboards, drums muted with cloth so they do not wake the children above. Weddings happen in covered courtyards, among wet potted trees and strings of lights. The oldest custom is to pour a cup of clean water into a public channel at the beginning of a marriage, a birth, a new tenancy, or a repaired home—not an offering, exactly, but an acknowledgement that private life depends on the common flow.

Their dead are remembered by water marks.

There are names painted beneath dates on retaining walls, under bridges, in the entrance halls of worker blocks: not all deaths, but those from floods, collapses, accidents in tunnels, illnesses brought on by years in cold wet rooms. The paint fades, and descendants repaint it. The city has no room for large cemeteries, so ashes are often placed in high garden plots or scattered from the upper maintenance walks where the wind carries them toward the slopes. Some families keep small sealed bottles of water from the year someone died, labeled and stored in cupboards beside plates and tools.

There are people who try to leave. Young people especially speak of broad horizons, dry pavements, open sky. They go to coastal cities, plateau towns, places where one can see weather approaching from miles away. A number return. They say the silence was difficult. They missed knowing that a light in the opposite window meant someone was awake; they missed the enormous shared body of the place, its pipes, stairs, lamps, and perpetual labor. They missed being needed in ways that were immediate and comprehensible.

And there are those who never leave the valley even for a day. They work in rooms without windows, under roofs layered so many times that sunlight reaches them only as a gray reflection on wet concrete. Still, they have an intimate geography: the warm route home through the laundry passages, the high walkway where crows gather, the abandoned gantry hidden under ivy, the particular landing where one can smell the fruit trees after rain. They do not possess much open land, but they possess routes, duties, borrowed rooms, and reliable doors.

What lives there, most of all, is a population of caretakers who do not always call themselves caretakers.

They keep the water moving. They replace a plate before it fails. They sweep leaves from grates. They tie branches away from wires. They mark a leak in chalk. They leave a thermos outside the pump house for the night shift. They make room, somehow, for one more household, one more garden trough, one more bridge between buildings.

The city survives because its people have made maintenance into a form of citizenship, and citizenship into a form of affection.
