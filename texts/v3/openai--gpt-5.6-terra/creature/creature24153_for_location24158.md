What lives on the plateau is not alive in the low country’s sense.

There are the green things, certainly. Mosses live there. Lichens live there. The broad-leafed vent shrubs with their black, waxed foliage live there, if a rooted thing that never knows drought, grazing, seed-flight, or season can be said to live. They spread by contact and patient pressure. A mat reaches the edge of a warm drain. One filament finds mineral water. The whole mat slowly thickens around it. Flowers sometimes open in the runoff gardens: pale, heavy flowers with no fragrance strong enough to travel and no insects to receive them. Their pollen collects in the rain, dissolves, settles into cracks. They reproduce without encounter.

Fungi live in the service tunnels.

They are white at first, almost invisible against the painted walls: a felt along a gasket, a bloom in the corner of a cable trench. Given enough warmth and the slow seepage of oil, they become elaborate. Some resemble coral growing from expansion joints. Some form broad, translucent shelves beneath leaking valves. In the oldest stations, where a pipe has sweated for longer than anyone can count, there are fungal veils hanging from the ceiling, fine as machinery insulation, trembling only when the ventilation changes.

The maintenance crews do not remove them unless they interfere with a seal.

The maintenance crews are what visitors first mistake for people.

They wear pale coats because pale fabric shows mineral residue. Their boots are black and wide-soled for wet steel grating. Their heads are hooded against the cold, their faces recessed behind dark glass or blank metal screens. At a distance they have the proportions of workers: two arms, two legs, a bowed back under a tool pack. They walk in groups when a task requires several hands, and one by one when it does not. They carry lamps, cases, hoses, inspection mirrors. They ride the rail cars. They cross gantries in rain that slicks their shoulders and never seems to soak through.

Up close, their movements are too exact to be human and too economical to be theatrical.

A crew member will stand beside a turbine bearing for six hours, head inclined at the same angle, listening through a contact probe. It will then turn, select a wrench from its belt without looking, loosen exactly three bolts, make a correction too small for the eye to recognize, and walk away. It has no need to hurry. It has no need to rest. Its work is not a sequence of decisions but a continuation of conditions already underway.

They are not quite machines, either.

Machines on the plateau have purposes visible in their shapes. A pump is built to move water. A generator is built to turn. A rail signal is built to hold distance and permission in colored light. The crew members have shapes that imply a purpose, but their purpose has expanded beyond repair. They inspect pipes that will not fail. They scrape mineral bloom from structures that will not weaken. They replace lamps before the old lamps extinguish. They open doors only to stand in the threshold for a measured interval, letting warm air meet cold rain, then close them again.

Sometimes one is found in an unused corridor, facing a wall.

It may remain there for years.

Mineral fur gathers in the joints of its coat. Moss grows around its boots. A thin line of rust-colored water runs from one sleeve and disappears into a floor drain. Then, on a day indistinguishable from all the others, it moves. Not abruptly. Its fingers close. Its head rises. It turns toward the nearest operational sound, and walks away with the slow certainty of a pendulum resuming.

No one on the plateau calls them workers. There is no one, perhaps, to call them anything.

In the oldest industrial districts there are rooms arranged for human bodies. Dormitories extend along heated galleries, each room containing a bed, a washbasin, a locker, a narrow desk facing a rain-streaked window. The beds are made. The sheets are clean and tightly folded under their blankets. Cups sit upside down on drainboards. Notices remain pinned to walls in languages whose letters have softened under condensation.

The rooms are maintained.

A pale-coated figure enters every so often. It changes linen. It wipes the window from the inside. It sets a cup exactly where a cup belongs. It checks the room’s temperature, then leaves, locking the door behind it.

There are no occupants.

Or rather: occupancy is present as a system, even after the body for which it was designed has become unavailable. The rooms hold the idea of a person with greater fidelity than a person could hold it alone.

The railways have their own inhabitants.

At intervals, a train emerges from rain: a string of dark carriages with illuminated windows, moving at the same unhurried speed across the raised causeways. Its wheels make almost no change in the larger industrial chord. It passes through stations without stopping, through loading yards without loading, beneath gantries where no cargo waits. In some cars, silhouettes sit behind the glass.

They do not shift when the train rounds a curve.

They do not turn toward the light of a passing station.

Their shapes may be seats, shadows, hanging coats, reflections from the carriage interior. But in certain angles of rain, one can see a face lifted toward the window: pale, featureless, softened by condensation, watching the black ground pass beneath the rails.

No train ever arrives from beyond the plateau. None leaves it. Yet the schedules are kept with perfect seriousness. Signals change from red to green. Switches move under their housings. Platform clocks mark their identical hours. Somewhere in a central office, a board records each passage with a small mechanical stamp.

The sound of the stamp is faint, but it is one of the sounds that keeps the place alive.

There are also things in the water.

Not fish. Not larvae. Nothing with eyes or hunger.

The black basins around the warm vents contain shifting films that gather where minerals rise through the stone. In daylight they are green-black, like oil, but at night some of them hold a dim internal color: blue along their edges, a low turquoise pulse in the deeper seams. They respond to heat. When a pipe beneath the ground warms by a fraction, the film expands toward it. When runoff cools, it contracts. The movement is so slow that it can only be known by returning after many hours and finding the shape altered.

In drainage channels these films braid with fungal threads and moss roots until it is difficult to say which substance belongs to which form of life. The plateau does not maintain clean boundaries. Metal collects mineral. Mineral collects moss. Moss drinks warm water. Warm water carries oil. Oil feeds fungus. Fungus climbs cable insulation. Cable insulation keeps a pump running. The pump sends water through a pipe, and the pipe warms the moss.

This is its food chain: not consumption, but continuation.

In the deep service levels beneath some cities, where geothermal conduits descend into the rock, there are larger presences.

Workers avoid speaking of them, if workers speak at all. The records call them accumulations, thermal assemblies, subsurface obstructions. Inspection cameras have returned images that do not settle the matter: masses of cable, mineral, root, old pipe lagging, and something darkly wet, arranged around vents in forms that resemble sleeping animals only because animals are the nearest available comparison.

They grow around heat exchangers.

They do not damage the machinery. In some cases they appear to support it, bracing a sagging conduit with layered mineral tissue, sealing a leak with a skin of fibrous growth, directing condensation away from electrical housings. They may be no more conscious than a reef. They may be the plateau’s attempt to make organs out of its infrastructure.

A technician once entered a lower chamber after an alarm that had been repeating for ninety years. The chamber was full of white vapor. At its center stood a pressure vessel, cracked open along one side. Around the crack, something had formed a thick collar, black and green and faintly luminous in places, pulsing with the rhythm of the pumping system.

The technician watched it for the required observation period.

Then it recorded: **pressure stable.**

The alarm ceased.

Above ground, the cities go on making their deep chord. The pale crews appear and vanish. The trains cross the wet distances. Roots enter warm cracks in concrete. Lamps burn behind windows prepared for absent sleepers.

And perhaps there are people somewhere in it all.

Perhaps in a sealed tower above the cloud line, where no rain reaches, someone still sits before an instrument panel and regards the plateau through thick glass. Perhaps the original inhabitants withdrew into the heated underground levels generations ago, adapting themselves so completely to the systems that their descendants can no longer be distinguished from the personnel who maintain them. Perhaps every hooded figure contains a human mind preserved in a body of valves, insulated wire, and mineral-resistant joints.

But the plateau gives no confirmation.

It does not require an audience to keep its arrangements.

Its inhabitants are the things that persist: the moss at the warm margins, the fungus in the tunnels, the water films in the basins, the blank-faced keepers of the turbines, the trains carrying their unchanging silhouettes, and the great buried assemblies growing patiently around the sources of heat.

They do not survive there.

They belong there.
