The first thing that lives in the basin is the basin itself.

Not metaphorically. Not in the loose way people call a forest alive because it grows and dies. This place has a body distributed among a thousand bodies. Its skin is the root mat. Its lungs are the hollow reeds and swollen pods. Its circulation is the black water being lifted, breathed through, cooled in leaf shade, and returned. The blue crown by the pool is perhaps its mouth, or its throat. It drinks not because it is thirsty but because drinking is what keeps the whole hidden organ awake.

Everything else lives within that larger life, and has learned its rhythms.

The frog in the root hollow is old enough that its green has gone almost black along the spine. It does not range far. Its cavity warms at particular hours when gases rise beneath the mat, and it calls then: once, twice, a blunt wooden note. Small frogs answer from other cavities, not as rivals exactly, but as parts of the same nightly measurement. When one is absent, the pattern of calls changes. The basin notices; perhaps only the insects notice; in a place like this there is little difference.

The moss crabs are the basin’s moving fragments. Their shells carry gardens: liverwort, pale threads of fungus, fragments of red-veined creeper. A crab can vanish against the floor by standing still, though stillness is never complete here. Its borrowed moss trembles with the pulses beneath it. It cleans the undersides of leaves, gathers soft rot from root seams, and takes the larvae that grow in neglected water cups. If it dies, the shell becomes a planter before the limbs have fully loosened.

There are moths that never see the open sky. Their ancestors entered through the vine curtain generations ago, or perhaps were born from eggs already clinging to a leaf carried in by wind. Their wings are white, green, and mineral-gray, powdered like the clay walls. They drink from droplets where hanging roots meet the ceiling, and they lay their eggs only in the cups of a certain fern that grows high above the pool. Their caterpillars eat the fern’s outer fronds but leave the central spiral untouched. Nobody knows why. It may be chemistry. It may be inheritance. It may be an agreement older than either species.

The translucent fish beneath the mat are not truly blind. They have eyes, but the eyes are pale and unfinished, like seeds under milk. They perceive pressure better than light. A falling beetle larva, a thread of rot, the minute shiver of a crab’s feet above: these travel through water and root fibers into the fish. They turn together in darkness, silver bones flashing green at irregular intervals.

Those green lights are messages, though not messages anyone has translated. A pulse from one fish is answered by another several root-lengths away. Sometimes the whole black underlayer twinkles for a few breaths, as if stars had been stirred into mud. Then it goes dark again.

There are spiders in the breathing spaces between broad leaves. Their webs are not the usual flat wheels. They build loose lattices of damp silk that collect mist and tiny flying insects alike. In daylight the lattices look like torn air. At night they become strings of beads. Some are tended by small ants, which harvest trapped scraps; in return the ants defend the web’s anchor points from leaf-cutters. The spiders tolerate this. Tolerance is a common form of intelligence here.

There is a lizard with a missing toe that follows the pitcher plants from rim to rim. It knows which pitchers have recently fed, because the smell changes after digestion begins. It knows which ants are intoxicated by nectar and which are merely passing through. Its body has the brown-green shine of wet bark, and when it is still, the basin’s scattered reflections move across it as though it too were a leaf holding water.

The loudest creatures are too small to see at first: leafhoppers in their vast aligned colonies. They live on the undersides of the canopy leaves, each one a fleck of shell, each one tapping, rasping, feeding. Their synchronization is not perfect. It travels in waves. One leaf begins, then another, then a whole wall of greenery hums. The sound climbs and falls through the basin like current through a machine. Predators use it. The frog times its strike to the louder intervals. The lizard waits for the quiet gaps. Even the cicadas seem to tune themselves against it.

But there are other residents that do not fit easily into the animal lists.

In the black canal formed by the old split in the ground, something long and ribbon-thin moves only when the rain is strongest. It is colored like drowned roots and carries a fringe of pale sensory filaments along its head. It may be an eel, though it has no obvious eyes and no one has seen it leave the canal. On wet afternoons, when silver cords of rain are falling through the canopy, it presses its head into the flow and remains there, receiving the cold water as if listening.

At the bottom of the blue crown’s central opening, something lives that has never been seen whole.

Small things avoid that opening not because it snaps or poisons or visibly threatens them. They avoid it because the air around it is too regular. Every few minutes the hidden gulp draws water down, and in the pause afterward there is a soft exhalation from below: warm, faintly mineral, smelling of iron, root sap, and old rain. Mosquitoes will cross above it, dragonflies will hunt over it, but neither lands. Crabs take detours. The blind fish beneath the mat turn their green lights off when they pass under its roots.

Perhaps there is only a chamber beneath the crown, a natural siphon leading into deeper water. Perhaps the sound is the work of roots compressing and releasing. Yet the blue leaves are too careful. They keep their channels clear. They shed fallen debris outward. If a dead moth lands in the center, the ribs flex almost imperceptibly with accumulated moisture until the corpse is carried away rather than allowed to sink into the dark opening.

The basin does not feed it. The basin keeps it clean.

And perhaps that is why the place has endured. The vines stop at the invisible boundary because the basin does not need more territory. The roots turn back because their work is inward. Everything that grows here grows into an arrangement already waiting for it: a particular arch, a particular pocket of shade, a familiar wet seam. New animals are absorbed into old routes. Death becomes fiber, then fungus, then water, then leaf. Even the living are not entirely separate from the machinery. They are moving valves, clean-up crews, pollinators, listeners, lights in the underlayer.

What lives in the basin is a republic of damp, patient specialists.

And beneath them all, drinking through the blue crown in the center, is something for which the entire republic may be keeping time.
