What lives there is not a single people so much as a set of adaptations.

The city’s human population is small enough that absences are noticed, but large enough that no one can belong entirely to everyone else. There are families who have been in the basin for six generations and still speak of the low valleys as though they were a foreign coast. There are seasonal workers from warmer towns, sent up for a summer contract in the growing halls or the fault tunnels, who spend their first weeks walking too quickly and waking breathless. There are surveyors who arrive intending to stay a month and remain for years because the mountain teaches a particular patience, and because departure becomes difficult once one has learned the names of all the pipes.

Most residents are workers, though the word means more here than it does elsewhere. A pump tender may also keep bees in a heated utility shaft. A crane operator may serve on the retaining-wall watch and know how to graft rowan branches. Children learn to read gauges before they learn to trust weather forecasts. Teenagers carry tool rolls, messenger satchels, seed trays, and sometimes instruments for listening to the mountain: small devices laid against stone to distinguish ordinary settling from the beginning of a problem.

There are mechanics whose hands are permanently marked by oil and mineral dust. Their workshops are crowded with salvaged fittings, obsolete valves, cracked turbine blades polished smooth at the edges, lengths of cable coiled like sleeping black snakes. They speak of the old engines with familial irritation. Turbine House Three is stubborn in wet weather. The northern lift sulks after lightning. The southern boiler has a cough that no one has cured and no one is willing to silence, because its cough has become part of the city’s night.

There are water people: cistern keepers, sluice runners, filter technicians, pipe inspectors, and the few old specialists who can locate a hidden leak by sound alone. They listen at walls with the flats of their palms. They know which alley stones stay damp after two dry days, which gutters feed the willow roots, which drains are safe to clear and which lead into systems older than the street above them. Their work gives them a kind of quiet authority. In a city where water is measured, anyone who understands its routes understands the city’s private anatomy.

The growers live in the bright halls and become pale in winter, green-smelling in summer. They raise kale, beans, herbs, bitter alpine greens, medicinal fungi, dwarf fruit trees, and experimental crops bred for thin light and cold roots. Their clothing is always damp at the cuffs. They carry warmth around them when they leave work, their coats holding the greenhouse air for a few minutes against the street fog. Some of them cultivate unofficial gardens in broken stairwells, behind transformer cages, beneath the warm breath of ventilation outlets. The city tolerates this. It has learned that a patch of leaves can stabilize more than soil.

There are miners still, though fewer every year. Some descend into fault tunnels for stone cores, rare clays, salt deposits, or the slow retrieval of old equipment left behind by earlier extraction crews. They are not romantic figures. They are cautious, trained, tired people who keep their lamps clean and do not tell stories about hearing things underground until they are old enough to no longer care whether anyone believes them. The mountain’s deep places are full of mundane sounds enlarged by distance: dripping water, shifting rock, ventilation fans, metal cooling after work. Still, there are passages nobody enters without checking the date, the pressure, and the names of the last people who went in.

The city also keeps people whose jobs would seem ceremonial elsewhere: fog readers, cable walkers, roof wardens, avalanche archivists, moss clearance crews, weather-mast climbers. The fog readers stand at certain corners at dawn and decide how much of the day can safely proceed. Cable walkers travel the high lines in harnesses, their boots ringing on metal grating above streets that vanish into cloud. Roof wardens inspect for snow load, leaf blockage, wind damage, and the subtle upward thrust of roots. The avalanche archivists maintain records no one laughs at: not because avalanches are expected, but because the mountain has taught them that old stability is not the same thing as permanence.

Children live intensely there. They know every shortcut, every pipe bridge, every stair whose middle section is dry when it rains. They play games in the old loading bays, using chalk on concrete and discarded packing foam for snow. They collect quartz fragments, shed cable insulation, rusted bolts, moth wings, blue flowers from the south slope. They are warned not to climb the old ore lift towers, not to go near the unsealed ravine gates, not to put hands beneath greenhouse vents where hot water sometimes releases without warning. They do all of these things in modified forms, cautiously and with great ingenuity.

The elders are often the city’s true map. They remember buildings before they were built over, street names before they became numbers, springs before they were routed through filters. Some carry valley accents that have thinned out among the younger generations. Some have never descended below the cloud line except for medical appointments or funerals. They sit near the public taps, in repair cafés, under the broadest greenhouse eaves, and they watch the weather enter. Their memories are practical: where the ʼ78 wall cracked, where the old north conduit freezes first, which workshop once made excellent boots, where a fox denned for three winters under the freight stairs.

Not all inhabitants are human.

The ravens are citizens by any reasonable measure. They understand shift changes, market days, and the delivery schedules of the kitchens. They have favorite chimneys, favorite cable towers, favorite people to trouble. They carry bright scraps into inaccessible nests: foil insulation, brass washers, fragments of green bottle glass. Their young learn the city’s sounds before they learn the wider mountain’s. A raven can imitate the stutter of a failing relay, the whine of a descending lift, the cough of a particular mechanic.

Moths live in the heated margins. They bloom around the greenhouse vents, generator rooms, and laundry outlets, turning the warm industrial places briefly soft. There are spiders in the cable trenches, beetles beneath wet pallets, tiny silver insects in the moss-lined drainage channels. The suspected salamanders in the ravine have become a local article of faith. Maintenance crews leave certain stones undisturbed because of them. Children make mud prints with their fingers and claim to have found the real tracks. Whether the animals are numerous or nearly gone, the city behaves as if they are watching.

The foxes come and go. They do not belong to the basin, but they use it. One may pass through at dusk, ribs visible beneath a wet grey coat, pausing under a lamp to inspect a dropped glove or a crate of wilted greens. It knows the rail bed is easier walking than scree. It knows dogs are rare, doors are mostly shut, and people here have a habit of pretending not to see a hungry thing if it remains dignified.

Plants live there more thoroughly than anyone admits. Moss lives in the mortar and under the bridge plates, storing the basin’s dampness in its dark cushion. Ferns occupy the hot edges of the machinery. Willow roots enter cracks in retaining walls and are periodically cut back, then return. Rowan trees grow from places too inconvenient to remove. Their berries feed ravens, and their roots pry at concrete with slow, unquestionable strength. In abandoned corridors, plants assemble small ecologies from leaks, rust, dust, and light reflected from windows.

And beneath everything, there are the systems that are not alive but have been lived with long enough to acquire the status of residents: the turbines, the cisterns, the ore lift, the old rail, the pumps buried in the rock. People give them nicknames, apologize to them, blame them for moods, listen for changes in their voices. A stopped pump is not merely broken. It is absent. A newly repaired cable line is said to be awake again.

Perhaps the oldest inhabitant is the mountain itself—not as a god, exactly, nor as an enemy, but as the condition in which all the others have become possible. It lives in its own immense manner: freezing, thawing, shedding grains of stone, accepting water, lifting roots, closing fractures, opening others. The people do not imagine they have conquered it. They know better.

They live in its wound, in its weather, in its slow rearrangement. And in return, they keep the pipes from bursting, the gardens from freezing, the lights hooded against the fog, and the little warm city breathing between the stone and the cloud.
