They are called, among themselves, the Kept.

Not because they have been saved. No one would use that word so high above the weather. Kept means only that they have not yet gone elsewhere.

There are perhaps a few thousand of them in the whole city, perhaps a few hundred. Their own counts disagree. Census tablets exist in the old halls, but the numbers on them change when no one is watching—not dramatically, not in a way that would make a person cry out, but enough that the city has abandoned certainty as a civic virtue. A household may be said to contain four people, then three, then four again when someone returns from an errand they do not remember leaving for.

They do not ask where anyone has been unless that person brings back something wet.

Most are born in the high quarters, in rooms built into the backs of terraces or behind the great, unused balconies. Their births are quiet affairs. The air makes labor difficult, and infants arrive blue-gray, clenched and unspeaking, with eyes that take a long time to learn the distances of a room. For the first several months they are kept near heat wells, wrapped in old cloth and laid in shallow alcoves lined with mineral fungus. The fungus is not permitted to touch their skin. It closes at a baby’s warmth, and the elders say a child who is covered by it too early will grow up knowing the inside routes of stone.

Whether this is feared or desired depends on the family.

The Kept are lean by necessity, but not fragile. Their chests are broad and slow-moving; their voices are low, economized. A visitor from lower country, if any lower country remained from which to visit, might think them elderly even when young. Their skin is bleached by dust and hard light, their hair dry enough to hold the pale grit of the city. Many wear veils over their mouths, not because the air is poisonous, exactly, but because breath is considered something one ought not to squander on the streets. The veils are sewn from generations of repaired cloth: faded ceremonial banners, sailcloth that has never been used on water, strips cut from the linings of abandoned official robes.

They dress for abrasion rather than cold. Their sleeves are wrapped at the forearms. Their ankles are bound. The wealthy, if wealth can be said to exist, possess old leather from animals nobody remembers raising. Everyone else wears layered felt made from crushed mineral fibers and fungus-thread, gray, rust-red, and the color of old teeth.

Their eyes are adapted to the city’s uncertain light. They squint less than outsiders would. Some have irises so pale they appear blank at a distance; some develop a silvery ring around the pupil after years spent working in the upper districts. It is said that people with such eyes should not be asked what they see in reflective stone. They tend to answer truthfully, and truth is not always useful.

They are not one people in the ordinary historical sense. The city has collected remnants for so long that ancestry has become architectural rather than familial. Some trace themselves to the cliff-carvers, some to the observatory guilds, some to refugees from the vanished valleys, some to the crews of the reservoir fleet. There are families whose names mean stair, gate, drain, windward, westward, and return. There are others who have no names at all, having surrendered them after an ancestor came back from one of the unreliable roads.

A person’s district matters more than their blood.

Those from the lower carved quarters tend to become stone listeners. They maintain no buildings, exactly, but they know which walls are thinking of falling inward, which stairs have begun to forget the direction they once served, which tunnels should be left alone during heat. They place their palms against masonry and wait. This is not mysticism to them. It is a trade, though a trade whose evidence cannot easily be shared. A stone listener may condemn an entire residence because it has begun to sound “too spacious” at night.

The people of the cistern wards cultivate the gray fungus. They do it in darkness or near-darkness, carrying it from chamber to chamber in sealed clay trays. They know its moods: which fans retain warmth, which will harden under a knife, which release a bitter vapor if exposed to lamplight. They use it for food, insulation, salves, binding paste, and the rare form of medicine that works on the lungs. They are often considered untrustworthy by those who live above, perhaps because they spend so much time underground, perhaps because they have learned to move through rooms without disturbing the dust.

The upper terraces belong to the watchers, though no one can agree what they watch for.

Some tend the observatories. They polish lenses clouded from the inside. They turn instruments whose dials no longer correspond to stars. They record the colors of the sky, the direction of shadows, the duration of cloud gaps, the number of black things circling the towers. Their records are exhaustive and mutually contradictory. A watcher may devote sixty years to proving that the horizon has moved three handspans to the east. Another may spend the same lifetime establishing that there is no horizon, only a habit of expectation in the eye.

They are respected, but not trusted with directions.

There are also sweepers of the Empty Reservoir.

This is among the most solemn occupations in the city. Sweepers are chosen young, usually from families that have not lost anyone to the roads for three generations. They wear white masks and carry broad brushes of bound wire and dried fungus-stalk. On mornings after the translucent fleet has appeared, they descend to the piers before anyone else is permitted into the basin. They sweep the grit left along the mooring rings, collect it in covered jars, and carry it to chambers beneath the lighthouse towers.

No one claims to know what is done with it afterward.

The sweepers themselves do not speak of it. By custom, they are not addressed by their personal names while in service. A former sweeper may return to ordinary life after twenty years, but most do not. They become quiet even by the standards of the Kept. Their hands develop a fine, glittering dryness that cannot be washed away. Sometimes, in the marketplace, one will pause beside a tray of polished glass and look through it as if expecting to see sails.

Children are less solemn.

They race where adults will not: along the high walls at noon, through empty tenements, across rooflines where the tiles shift underfoot. They play games of finding the oldest dust and games of naming things without speaking. Their toys are broken survey weights, hollow stones, bits of colored glass, shed plates from the pale wall-creatures. They dare one another to stand at the edge of shadow-thick arches and toss pebbles inside. The bravest—or least sensible—will listen at dry fountains for the sound of water.

When a child does this, the others run.

Education consists largely of prohibitions, routes, and manners of attention. A child learns the city as another might learn a language: first by being corrected, later by sensing when a sentence has become dangerous.

Do not step where the dust has settled in a perfect circle.

Do not look up when the black shapes pass directly overhead.

Do not answer a voice calling from below a bridge.

If a room has more doors when you leave than when you entered, choose none of them until you have sat down.

They are taught practical things, too. How to seal a crack against salt bloom. How to test a bridge by striking it with the heel of the hand. How to sleep during an inversion wind. How to distinguish a harmless echo from one that has come back altered. How to make oil. How to render the wall-creatures without breaking the small blue glands behind their joints. How to read the old maps, which is less a matter of geography than of recognizing the handwriting of the dead.

They have a complicated relationship with death.

Bodies do not decay easily in the high dryness. This has shaped custom. The dead are not buried in the ground; there is too much stone and too little certainty about what lies beneath it. Nor are they burned often, because fuel is dear and smoke is thought to attract the attention of whatever moves below the clouds.

Instead, the dead are given to the walls.

A body is wrapped in salt cloth and placed in one of the old uninhabited rooms whose outer face has begun to merge with the escarpment. The door is then fitted shut with dry masonry. No name is carved on it. Over time, the room collapses inward or becomes inaccessible. Eventually, the dead and the house are thought to acquire the same dignity: neither gone, exactly, nor available for questioning.

There are exceptions for those who vanish and return.

A returned person is never given to the walls. Their body, when it finally dies, is placed in a roofless observatory, beneath the open tarnished sky. This is done without anger. It is merely understood that someone who has traveled incorrectly may not wish to remain contained.

The Kept love one another, though they are careful with the word love. Grand declarations are considered unlucky because the city has a way of overhearing statements of permanence. Affection is shown through maintenance that is not called maintenance: a stair cleared before someone wakes; a lamp filled with an extra spoonful of oil; a route marked with three stones instead of two; a good piece of fungus left outside a door without explanation.

Partners often live apart, sometimes in different districts, and visit by agreed paths. They do not walk together through uncertain neighborhoods. Two people crossing a dangerous threshold can confuse its expectations. Instead one goes first, waits, and leaves a sign: a folded scrap of cloth, a line scratched into salt, a cup turned upside down. The second follows later.

Marriages, where they occur, are contracts of return. The two parties promise not that they will remain faithful—though most do—but that if either is lost in a shifting street, delayed by a repeated valley, or called away by running water, the other will keep a lamp in a particular window for a specified number of years.

Some lamps have burned for centuries.

The inhabitants do not think of themselves as trapped. That is a word for cages, and the city has too many exits, even if many of them do not lead anywhere survivable. They think of themselves as resident in an unfinished altitude. The world below has gone vague; the sky above is unreliable; the mountain continues to incorporate them by degrees. But there are lamps in windows, food in the cisterns, names still used in the right rooms, and the Empty Reservoir is swept after the ships have come.

This is enough to constitute a life.

And perhaps that is why the city has not emptied entirely. It does not demand that its people conquer it, understand it, or restore it. It asks only that they notice the changes, obey the old cautions, and leave space for those who may arrive from the void below.

During inversion winds, when the dust rises in clean sheets and the footprints appear at the eastern gate, some of the Kept go out before dawn with covered lamps.

They do not follow the prints.

They stand at a distance, in doorways and beneath archways, watching the new impressions advance across the empty plaza.

And when the footsteps stop, they wait to see whether someone has remembered how to become visible.
