What lives in the basin is not one people, nor one species, but a long agreement.

The most visible inhabitants are the small ones: the six-legged grazers, the bronze waders, the glass-winged trunk-clingers, the sleeping plumed listeners. They are not animals in the simple sense of being separate from their surroundings. Each seems to be one temporary expression of the basin’s water and attention.

The grazers hatch from eggs laid in the damp margins of the clear hollows. For their first days they are nearly transparent, little moving sacs of pale organs and green digestive light. They feed on the cushions of bladder-plants, carefully biting only the older outer growth so that the cushions remain round. As they mature their backs harden into the mottled, river-stone pattern that makes them difficult to notice on sand. They travel in lines because their feet leave traces of a thin mineral sweetness. The next grazer tastes the trace, and follows—not obediently, but because the route has already been tested for softness, water, and predators.

At the end of their lives they do not retreat to die. They gather in the shallowest water, often dozens at a time, and stand motionless while their patterned shells loosen. By morning there will be no bodies, only empty rounded backs floating among pollen and a new dark richness in the mud. The shells are taken by beetles, root-fungi, and the fan-mouthed waders. Nothing is wasted enough to become mournful.

The waders are older-minded than they appear. Their broad mouths do not simply sift mud; they read it. A wader can put its face into a pool and know which roots beneath it are thirsty, which buried stone is warming, which predator passed through an hour ago. Their oversized shadows are not an effect of the sun. The shadows are part of the animal: flattened ancestral forms that trail behind them across the water. When a wader is frightened, its shadow crouches before the body does. When one is content, the shadow spreads wide and dark beneath it, like a second bird resting its wings.

The plumed creatures in the reed crowns are called, by no language now spoken, the listeners.

They have no voices. Their petal-ears open to receive vibrations too slight for most living things: a root splitting stone, a beetle chewing beneath bark, rain falling in the country below the central pool. They sleep in tight clusters because their dreams require overlap. A lone listener can remember a path. A cluster can remember a season. A whole basin-full, ear-rings opening one after another across the reeds, can remember an event from before the present shape of the hills.

At dusk they wake more fully. Then their plumes lift, and the small warm stones on the terraces lift with them. The winged clouds that gather above the ruin are not merely attracted to light. They are delivering something: dust from their wings, spores from distant valleys, fragments of weather caught high in the hills. The stones sort these offerings. What belongs in the basin settles. What does not belong is carried onward, though no one sees where.

The mirrored predators are perhaps the least understood of the ordinary inhabitants. Their skin holds not a reflection of the world as it is, but of the world a few instants before. When one waits at a pool, it may show reeds that have already bent back upright, prey that has already passed, ripples that have already faded. This is why they are difficult to see until they move. They hunt with the advantage of having almost happened.

Yet even they are not truly solitary. Around each predator’s eyes grow minute pale mosses, and in the folds beneath its body live threadlike cleaners that emerge only when it rests. When it feeds, the tiny attendants consume what is left on its mirrored hide. When it dies, its reflective skin clouds slowly, becoming a dull silver membrane from which certain fungi grow. Those fungi are favored by the reed-trees. The reed-trees in turn make the shaded pools where the predators breed.

Nothing here is exempt from being useful to something else.

But there are larger inhabitants, harder to notice.

They live beneath the mud and old stone, in chambers made not by digging but by patient exclusion. The earth above them does not collapse because their bodies, or their tools, or their sleep holds it in shape. They are called the low keepers by the few creatures capable of holding names. They are soft-bodied, long-lived beings, usually no longer than a person from head to heel when fully uncurled. Their outer skin resembles wet clay veined with mica. In dry weather—though true dryness rarely comes to the basin—they retreat into the seep-walls and become almost indistinguishable from stone.

The low keepers have no fixed faces. Their features travel slowly over the front of their heads according to need: a cluster of dark sensing pits when they work underground, a smooth pale field when they rest, a ring of delicate grasping mouths when they tend roots or repair an injured animal. They do not speak aloud. They communicate through pressure: palms placed on mud, limbs draped across stone, bellies lowered into shallow water. The basin’s low hum is partly theirs.

They tend the hidden machinery without knowing whether they built it.

Or perhaps they remember building it only in the way a hand remembers closing around a familiar tool.

When a seep becomes too strong, a low keeper may enter it and remain there for days, pressing its body into the current until the water learns a gentler path. When a hill-face begins to soften toward collapse, several will gather beneath it. The columns above fill with lattice-light. Roots thicken. Stone remembers its weight. By morning the slope has settled into a new curve, and the keepers have gone.

They do not interfere with predation, birth, weather, or decay. Those are not injuries. They intervene only when the basin risks becoming less itself.

A visitor might never see one unless they waited at the central pool when it opens.

Then, while sunlit rain rises from the revealed depth, the low keepers come out from beneath the water-wet ground. They stand around the rim in silence, their skins bright with mineral sheen. The little animals gather near them. The plumed listeners open all their ears. Even the mirrored predators lift their narrow heads from the reeds.

The keepers look down.

Something below looks up.

There may be more inhabitants in that upward-falling rain: versions of the basin from other arrangements of time, other populations of reed and stone, other shapes of those who tend it. Sometimes a keeper reaches one limb into the opening. Sometimes, when the pool closes, it has returned with a new scar of blue-green metal beneath its skin, or with a seed held carefully in a mouth that was not there before.

And sometimes one does not return at all.

No alarm follows. No ceremony is visible. The others remain at the pool until the last ripple is gone, then sink back into mud, roots, and shadow.

The basin continues.

Its inhabitants do not seem to think of themselves as living in a place. They live as part of a condition: the wetness held without flood, the growth held without crowding, the old instruments held without command, the dead held without loss.

Even the companion-shadows belong to this population.

They are not ghosts, precisely. They are the basin’s retained possibilities: old arrangements that have not entirely stopped participating. The shadow of a vanished reed cools the sand where a new reed may root. The shadow of a dead wader follows its descendants through shallow water. The shadow of a former keeper rests beside a column and makes the plants grow in a slightly altered circle.

Nothing has been expelled from the basin completely.

It has all been given another form of residence.
