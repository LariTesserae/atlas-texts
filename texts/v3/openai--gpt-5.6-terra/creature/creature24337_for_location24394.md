The people who live on the ledge do not call themselves stormkeepers, though travelers have called them that for as long as anyone has been able to travel there.

They call themselves the **Tended**.

There are rarely more than seven of them at a time.

From a distance, one might mistake them for unusually still shrubs among the silver-veined leaves. They wear long layered garments sewn from the cast membranes of the garden plants: pale, tough, translucent cloth that darkens where it overlaps. Their coats are cut close at the throat and wrists, but hang in loose strips below the knee, so that the wind may pass through them rather than take hold. Every hem is weighted with polished fragments of lichenstone. Every seam is threaded with a dull metallic fiber grown from the tower’s filaments.

Their hair is kept short or braided tight against the scalp. Loose hair is dangerous in weather like this. So are rings, buckles, wet lungs, unconsidered gestures, and fear.

The Tended are not immune to the charge. No one is. Their skin bears the small dark freckles of repeated grounding: branching marks at the palms, around the heels, beneath the collarbones. Their fingertips are often numb. Their teeth are often chipped from years of reports travelling through the stone and into their jaws. The oldest among them speak with a curious care, not because they are solemn, but because each word must be placed through the constant noise.

They communicate at close range by touching wrists.

A thumb pressed twice into the inside of the arm means *wait*.

Two fingers drawn outward means *the wind has changed*.

An open palm against the sternum means *I am grounded.*

Children learn these signs before they learn speech. Many do not learn speech in the ordinary sense until late. On the ledge, a voice is not always a thing another person can receive. The storms take words apart, turn them into bright scraps, scatter them over the escarpment. A child may spend an afternoon shouting their own name into the weather and hearing only the sky answer.

Yet the Tended are not a silent people. They sing constantly.

Their songs are not melodies meant to rise above the hiss and shriek of the garden. They are low patterns of tone, pitched to settle inside the chest. A person walking the bare spaces between groves may hear one of them humming against the storm: three notes, then a pause; three notes, then a longer pause. Another voice will answer from beside the observatory. The notes pass not through air but through stone, through metal posts, through the damp mineral architecture of bone.

The garden recognizes these songs.

Or perhaps it merely recognizes their rhythms. The distinction has troubled scholars, who are sometimes permitted to visit and almost never permitted to stay.

The Tended are descended from those who first built—or first found—the installations beneath the mountain. Their origin has divided into several histories, all held as true depending on who is speaking.

One history says their ancestors were engineers sent to maintain a weather-harvesting station after the world below had dried beyond repair. They were meant to remain for six months. A failure in the lifts stranded them. Their descendants adapted.

Another says there were no ancestors in the usual sense. The mountain’s mechanisms grew people because people were useful: hands to clear root-shell from vents, eyes to notice the wrong kind of light, voices to respond when the atmosphere began making requests.

A third history, preferred by the children, says that the first Tended were birds who flew too high, were emptied by the sky, and came down wearing human shapes.

No one on the ledge disputes any of these.

Their work is less like gardening than listening.

At dawn—though dawn has little authority over such a place—the Tended walk the perimeter in pairs. They inspect the pale seams in the cliff walls. They brush grit away from the warm metal posts. They lay their palms on the bare mineral floor and feel for changes in the subterranean rhythm.

If the bass note beneath the garden is smooth, they do little.

If it stutters, they begin preparations.

They move the six-legged grazers into stone hollows by striking tuning forks against the ground. The animals dislike certain frequencies and will flee from them with astonishing discipline. They call the pollen-moths away from the tower using reflective fans. They cut away dead collector leaves before a discharge can ignite the dry tissue. Sometimes they stand in circles around a grove and lift long black rods into the wind, becoming themselves part of the garden’s temporary wiring.

This is the work that leaves its mark.

The oldest Tended have shoulders that slope oddly from years of bearing insulated frames. Their hearing goes in peculiar bands: they can detect the change in a distant stone cavity before a cloud collision, but cannot hear the voice of someone standing beside them unless it is pitched just so. Their dreams are filled with blue light. When they die, their bodies are not buried in the red grit.

They are given to the root-beds.

Not laid upon them, but carefully opened into them. The charged roots take what they can: calcium, salts, trace metals, memory perhaps. Within a season, a new stand of silver-veined plants will grow from that place. Its leaves may show a faint pattern unlike the others. A favorite gesture in the wind. A tendency to close when a certain song is sung.

The living do not claim this is reincarnation.

They do not deny it, either.

There is also something in the observatory.

It is not one of the Tended, although it has spoken to them in their own voices.

It lives in the vertical well of moving lights, or beneath it, or in the long buried structures crossing the mountain at depths no instrument has mapped. The Tended refer to it with a word that means, approximately, **the one who continues the measurement**.

It does not speak often. Most years it offers only maintenance instructions: a sequence of lights, a new line on a map, a door opening where no door existed before. It has warned them of incoming sky-patterns. It has taught them which plant resins can be used to mend a burned hand. Once, after three children vanished following a flock over the cliff, it kept the observatory door open for eleven days. On the twelfth, the children returned through it, older by no visible measure and unable to explain where they had been.

After that, the Tended began leaving a bowl of polished grit beneath the light-well each winter.

The bowl is always empty by spring.

Visitors sometimes ask whether the Tended are prisoners.

The Tended usually find this difficult to answer.

They can leave. The ascent route remains behind a folding slab of stone on the northern side. The route down is harder, but possible if one waits for the right weather and does not trust the birds. A few Tended have gone into the lower lands. Some return after years. They arrive with soft garments, water in sealed vessels, stories about rivers and markets and rain falling from ordinary clouds.

They stand at the ledge’s edge and look bewildered by the garden’s familiar violence.

Then, sooner or later, they take off their wet-world clothes. They cut their hair short. They press their palms to the ground. They wait until the mountain’s deep note enters them again.

Not all return.

Those who remain on the ledge do not think of themselves as brave. Bravery is for people facing an ending. The storm has no ending. One does not defy it or conquer it. One learns its local manners.

One learns where to stand.

One learns what sound means danger, what sound means feeding time, what sound means the old machinery has noticed a stranger crossing the open ground.

And when the sky goes white and the garden begins to glow in branching lines, the Tended do not run.

They lift their faces into the charged wind, bare their darkened palms, and listen for what the mountain needs next.
