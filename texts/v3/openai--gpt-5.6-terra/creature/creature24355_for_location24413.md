What lives there is not a single people, nor even a clean division between people, animals, machines, and the basin itself.

The first inhabitants are the people who have learned not to expect the ground to keep a promise.

They are called basinfolk in the lower towns, though most of them use more particular names: residents of the Third Condensation Ward, the East Lift Houses, the Slope Ledger District, the Ventside Blocks. They identify themselves by systems rather than streets. A person may say they are from the Bluewater Line, meaning the series of roofs whose condensate drains into the same municipal cistern, or from Underworks Seven, though they have never gone below the upper service level. Their maps are full of depth marks, heat gradients, pipe routes, dates of ground movement.

They are not especially tall despite the altitude, but they often look narrow and layered. Their clothing is made for sharp temperatures and sharper surfaces: wrapped necks, hooded heat veils, gloves with ceramic-sewn palms, coats reinforced at the hips and shoulders where they brush pipes and railings. The colors are practical at first glance—charcoal, dust gray, oxide red, warning yellow—but close up there is a great deal of private ornament. Fine wire embroidery follows the cuffs of work jackets. Small colored tiles are stitched into collars. Children paint bright geometries on the backs of their pressure masks. Old people wear polished mineral flakes in their hair, pieces collected from cooling towers or from the harmless edges of vapor fields.

They have an instinctive courtesy about thresholds.

At a doorway, they listen for the pressure difference before opening it. Before crossing a courtyard, they glance at the drain lines. They do not lean their full weight against a wall without noticing whether it has been marked for drift. They can tell, from the smell of a stairwell, whether a steam pipe is sweating clean water or whether something deeper is leaking into the building’s bones.

Their children learn the city through sound before they understand its maps. They are taught the names of drips. They can distinguish roofwater from pipewater, cooling runoff from a ruptured brine line. A child who hears the wrong rhythm under a grate is expected to report it, not because adults wish to make small inspectors of them, but because the city has survived by teaching everyone what attention sounds like.

The basinfolk are patient, but they are not placid.

They argue fiercely over water allocations, thermal rights, and whether a new fissure is a hazard, an opportunity, or both. They distrust people from lower, greener places who speak too quickly of stabilizing the ground. Stabilize it for whom, they ask. For how long? A building that cannot move is a building that will someday break.

Their houses are built with joints that can be loosened and reset. Furniture is often bolted down, but not permanently. Shelves have retaining lips. Cups have heavy bases. Family possessions are kept in lidded cases marked with the district and the last known elevation of the floor. Many households own a tilt measure: a simple pendulum or water level kept in the central room, watched with the fondness elsewhere reserved for clocks. When a house has shifted enough to require adjustment, neighbors arrive with jacks, shims, meals in heat boxes, and stories of worse movements survived.

The oldest residents remember the city not by years but by exhalations.

They remember the winter the western terraces rose two handspans. The year vapor came through the school basements and every classroom was moved into cable-car stations. The slow month when the Black Reservoir lowered until mineral towers emerged from it, each crusted like the remains of an underwater palace. They remember the three-bell warnings: the whole city unlatching its heat shutters, floor by floor, street by street, allowing warm breath to surge through foundations and escape into the high air.

During such events, people stand in doorways with their faces covered and watch steam pour from the city’s lower seams. It is frightening, but it is also intimate. The earth is not abstract beneath them. It is an enormous working animal, feverish and unfinished, whose breathing must be accommodated.

There are trades here that would be difficult to explain elsewhere.

There are drip listeners, who inspect cistern systems with tuning rods and shallow brass bowls. They tap pipes, wait, and judge the health of a building from resonance. There are mineral scrapers, who work on suspended platforms around cooling towers, cutting pearly deposits away from valves and collecting the flakes in padded sacks. Their work leaves their gloves glittering, their faces nicked, their lungs protected by elaborate filters.

There are vent readers, part surveyor and part diviner, though they insist on the first title. They walk the fresh cracks in pairs, measuring temperature, gas content, and the changing width of stone. Their notebooks contain columns of numbers beside sketches of vapor shapes, because the shape of a plume matters too. A low white drift means one thing; a blue-edged hiss means another. Some vent readers can tell which deep layer of rock has opened by scent alone.

There are elevator keepers who spend their lives in cliff cages, traveling up and down between the basin and the mountain cuts. Their knees are bad from the vibration. Their eyesight is excellent. They know every ledge, every loose shelf of basalt, every abandoned drill head embedded in the cliffs. They carry food, messages, medical crates, repair crews, funerary parcels, and occasionally whole families moving their modular rooms from one unstable district to another.

The gardeners are a separate kind of citizen.

They live close to the protected growing rooms beneath the administrative terraces. Their clothes smell faintly of damp cloth, compost, and leaf oils, an almost shocking scent in the dry mineral city. They are treated with a mixture of reverence and suspicion. They handle soil. They are allowed to touch roots. Children press their faces to the observation glass when a gardener opens a bed of beans or turns a tray of seedlings toward the lamps.

The gardeners know every pest by name, though not always officially. A small pale mite can cause more alarm than a cracked boiler flange. A fungus spot is discussed at council meetings. When a fruiting tree fails, the district observes a quiet day, not by law but by habit. A mature tree is rare enough to be a civic ancestor.

Most basinfolk have seen grass only in the garden rooms or in pictures carried by traders. Many find lawns unsettling. They look too soft, too careless, too thick with hidden moisture. Some children, brought down to the lowlands for the first time, dislike the feeling of earth yielding beneath their feet. They want stone. They want a surface that tells them where it ends.

There are also people who come for the city rather than being born into it.

Ore brokers, furnace engineers, glass traders, geologists, rail inspectors, pilgrims, fugitives, and people with illnesses that respond strangely to dry altitude and mineral vapor. Some stay for a season and leave with their boots eaten at the seams. Others arrive intending to make a quick profit and wake years later to discover they have joined a rooftop maintenance cooperative, married into a cistern household, and learned to sleep through the pulse of the deep pumps.

The city is especially good at keeping those who have nowhere else to be.

It has districts made of temporary things that became permanent: freight containers stacked around old steam manifolds, abandoned survey huts joined by covered bridges, cable-car stations with apartments tucked into every unused platform. There are widows of quarry crews, retired pressure technicians, families whose homes were relocated so many times that they no longer remember the first neighborhood. There are people who live in the gaps between infrastructure, tending unofficial tea rooms beside warm pipes, repairing gloves, trading maps, selling sweet preserved fruit from the lower regions by the slice.

And there are the ash swifts.

They belong to the city more completely than anyone likes to admit. They nest in the warm hidden cavities of the condenser arrays and emerge in brief, abrupt flurries when the air pressure changes. Their feathers are nearly colorless, but in certain light they catch the blue of oxidized copper and the white shine of silica crust. They do not build nests from grass. They use wire threads, insulation fibers, furnace lint, shed veil-cloth, and the dry husks of cistern moths.

The basinfolk watch them closely.

A sudden departure from a condenser wall may mean little. A whole colony abandoning a particular tower means that maintenance crews are called before the instruments have even begun to register a change. The swifts know heat. They know pressure. They have nested in the city long enough for their instincts to become part of its unofficial warning system.

The cistern moths live in a quieter, stranger cycle. They begin as pale larvae in sediment filters, where they consume the minute drift of life caught from the atmosphere: pollen from far countries, insect fragments, skin dust, stray fibers, the rare spore that will never root. When the city sweats heavily, the moths hatch in numbers and gather around lamps beside the black reservoirs. Their wings are powder gray, but their undersides hold thin metallic markings: copper green, iron red, soft silver.

Children collect the wings only after the moths have died naturally. They press them between scraps of clear volcanic glass. Old superstition says that a moth wing sealed above a doorway keeps the roof plates wet. No one truly believes this, and nearly everyone does it.

There are other small lives in the city, most of them unnoticed until they become inconvenient.

Heat fleas inhabit the felted layers around certain pipe joints. Glass gnats breed in the warm runoff channels of the eastern works, their larvae feeding on chemical films. Tiny red mites colonize the underfaces of rooftop collectors, surviving on dust and whatever trace oils settle from the air. There are pale scavenger beetles in warehouse cracks, introduced long ago in food shipments and adapted now to a diet of packing fibers, leather dust, and the dead.

None of these creatures are abundant. Life in the basin does not overflow. It persists in seams.

The larger things below are harder to name.

Officially, the hot sublevels contain flooded service passages, abandoned pump chambers, pressure locks, mineral growths, and mechanical debris. This is true. But the old pumping crews speak of movement where movement should not be. They speak of white shapes slipping through water too hot for a person to stand near. Of something that makes a tapping sound on the far side of steel bulkheads. Of mineral-scaled backs briefly breaking the surface of sump pools, then sinking without a splash.

The reports disagree on almost everything.

Some say the creatures are long and jointed, like eyeless salamanders grown to the length of a person. Some say they are round-bodied and finned, moving with the slow confidence of seals. Some insist they are not animals at all, but old autonomous maintenance machines whose casings have been buried under layers of pale scale. Others say they are the city’s discarded labor returning in another form: pumps that learned hunger, filters that learned to swim.

The city archives dismiss the stories with careful language. No verified specimen has been recovered. No conclusive photograph exists.

But the warning signs remain.

DO NOT ENTER ALONE DURING PRESSURE RISE.

KEEP LIGHTS LOW NEAR FLOODED ACCESS.

IF KNOCKING IS HEARD, DO NOT KNOCK BACK.

The people who paint those signs do not laugh while doing it.

And beneath even those uncertain inhabitants, there is the thing that most truly lives there: the basin itself.

It is not alive in the animal sense. It does not think in rooms or names. But it grows, vents, cools, fractures, lifts, and rearranges the city’s foundations according to processes so slow and immense that people have built a civilization around listening to them. Its heat feeds furnaces and gardens. Its vapor fills cisterns. Its mineral breath paints the walls turquoise, yellow, white, and black. Its unrest determines property lines, school routes, marriage customs, work shifts, and the direction in which a person sets a cup on a shelf.

The inhabitants do not worship it exactly.

They complain about it. They study it. They measure it with instruments and curse it when a foundation needs jacking in the middle of the night. They build over it, around it, and sometimes away from it. Yet when a new vent opens and releases that smell of struck flint into the cold air, people come out onto balconies and roofs.

They look down into the steam.

The ash swifts wheel above the opening. Condensation begins to bead beneath the pipes. Somewhere, a pump makes its long deliberate stroke. Somewhere below, perhaps in a flooded corridor no one has entered for fifty years, something pale turns in the dark water.

And all across the high city, the people adjust their shutters, check their drains, set their catch-plates, and make room for the ground to become what it is becoming.
