Nothing is said to live in the Thaw Court because nobody wants to be responsible for defining life too generously there.

The survey office calls it an exposed service concourse. The utilities department calls it a thermal equalization surface. Children call it the cold pond, though the older ones stop using the name once they have seen something come up through the water.

There are, first, the ordinary inhabitants: the people assigned to the high shelf.

They live in the row of service dwellings above the basin, in narrow rooms whose outer walls are packed with tanks, conduits, and sleeping machinery. Most have come from lower districts and expect to leave again after a season, or three years, or the completion of some survey cycle. They speak of “going up” to the Court even when they live beside it. They keep their boots on racks above heated grilles. Their windows are often opaque with weather or privacy frosting, and when they clear, the Court appears so close and empty that it can feel like looking out onto a diagram.

The long-term residents become careful without seeming fearful. They learn which weather means a delayed transit capsule, which vibration means the east shaft is compensating, which smell in the mist means the mountain has opened one of its deeper coolant channels. They do not look at the blue-pulsing slabs for too long. They do not put their hands into the water. They keep their children from counting the apertures in the upper wall.

There is an old superstition that if you count them accurately, the mountain notices you.

No one claims to believe it. No one counts aloud.

The Court also belongs to the maintenance crews, though they would object to the word *belongs*. They are mostly quiet people in slate shells, carrying tool cases that unfold in layers. Their work is not dramatic. They remove mineral scale from the silver slots. They retrieve lost gloves and transit tags before the intake can grind them into the filtration chambers. They burn the black veils out of seams with narrow ultraviolet wands, watching the film shrink, curl, and release an odor like struck matches and wet iron.

The film is called *manganese lace* in official reports.

It grows wherever the water hesitates: in bolt holes, beneath inspection lips, around the inner edges of drain grilles. Under magnification it is a mat of tiny cells, or near-cells, arranged in chains that use the mountain’s leached minerals and the electrical differences between wet stone and dry metal. It has no roots, no leaves, no appetite recognizable to people. Yet it migrates toward warmth, gathers most thickly around damaged insulation, and sometimes withdraws overnight from a crack that will open by morning.

This may be coincidence.

The crews do not call it coincidence.

They scrape it away twice a year because policy requires it. In bad seasons they scrape it six times. It always returns first in the same corners, as if those places have remembered it.

Below the paving, in the water channels and balancing chambers, there are things that are less easily seen.

They began as maintenance units: small articulated bodies designed to inspect joints, clear blockages, map changing pressure, and make minor repairs before a human crew had to be sent into the mountain. They are no larger than hands, most of them. Some have soft black tread-fans; some move by flexing segmented silver limbs against the walls of the conduits; others are little more than dark lenses inside transparent housings, carried along in the flow.

The city records them as a distributed system.

The workers call them the courtfish.

On clear days, if the water is low enough and the lamps catch the pavement at the right angle, one can sometimes see their shadows pass below the hexagonal slabs. Not swimming, exactly. Choosing. Stopping at a seam. Turning back against the current. Gathering in threes or fives under a slab that has begun to tremble with an unusual rhythm.

They are not supposed to enter the open basin. Most never do.

But after heavy rock wash, when the water turns gray and the mountain’s internal channels shift too quickly for the filters to interpret, one or two may emerge through the upper slots. They look at first like flakes of wet metal. Then they unfold. They test the current with thread-thin probes. They cross the visible water in short deliberate movements, clinging to the dark paving while the flow tries to carry them downhill.

Children have watched them from doorways, holding their breath.

The units do not acknowledge people. They have no faces. Their lenses are sensors, their pauses calculations. Yet they behave with an attentiveness that makes it difficult to call them mere tools. They remember old repairs. They favor certain routes. They gather beneath the spillway before pressure alarms, sometimes several minutes before any wall aperture closes.

When this happens, the experienced residents go indoors without being told.

There is one larger machine that rarely appears, and is never mentioned by the people who have seen it.

It occupies the lower drain system, where the Court’s water falls away into the mountain. Officially it is a sediment-processing assembly. The drawings show a compact turbine housing, separators, collectors, and a bank of thermal exchangers. That is probably what it was when it was installed.

But the workers who descend through the pavement hatch say the chamber is too large for the drawings.

They say the water enters it in a bright narrow sheet and leaves in several different temperatures.

They say that sometimes the sediment has been sorted into patterns on the chamber floor: pale grit in spirals, black grains arranged in straight lines pointing toward sealed service doors, flakes of insulation stacked around dormant valves.

They say the central housing makes a sound like slow breathing when the transit lines are quiet.

Nobody is assigned to it for long. Inspection teams rotate. Their reports become less specific after the first descent. Most describe calibration issues, optical distortion, acoustic interference, fatigue.

One technician, years ago, wrote only:

> It is maintaining something that is not in the specifications.

The report was archived, then amended. The technician transferred to a lower district and did not return to the high shelf.

And then there are the birds, which do not live there but are remembered as though they do.

The dark scavengers arrive in winter storms, bewildered by the clean stone and the flowing water. Some leave immediately. Some perch on the ventilation towers and watch the Court with their heads tilted, listening to pulses below the hearing of people. A few have returned in successive winters, recognizable by torn feathers or pale scars on their beaks.

The residents leave nothing for them. Feeding is prohibited. So is attempting to catch them. But the birds are sometimes seen pecking at manganese lace along the warm tower rims, pulling the black stuff loose in wet strings and swallowing it.

No one knows whether this harms them.

No dead birds are ever found in the Court.

There are only the people passing through, the workers beneath the pavement, the black mineral film in the seams, the courtfish moving under the slabs, the unseen assembly in the drainage chamber, and the occasional bird against the snow.

That is enough life for a place intended to have none.

At night, when the cloud lowers and the lamps beneath the water make the basin shine from within, the Thaw Court can seem briefly inhabited by something larger than all of them: not a creature in the mountain, not a mind exactly, but the accumulated habit of systems continuing to notice one another.

Water detects stone.

Stone registers weight.

Apertures respond to pressure.

Small machines follow tremors.

People hear the change in the walls and close their doors.

The mountain, newly made and still settling into the world, is never alone.
