What lives in the basin is not a population in any familiar sense.

The shrubs live there, certainly. They are the most visible residents: low, gray, guarded things whose leaves have learned to make almost no claim on the air. They do not reach. They do not flower extravagantly, or cast broad shade, or invite attention. Their life is conducted beneath the surface, in long lateral searches through faults too narrow to admit a fingernail. Their roots find traces: a mineral thread, a cooler layer, the ghost of vapor sealed in stone. They take what they can without changing the look of the place.

The grasslike fans live there as well, though “grass” is too soft a word for them. They are little arrangements of endurance. Their thick blades store not much water but the capacity to wait. Their brown tips are not signs of dying; they are the color of survival carried to its edge. In the warm dark, they smell faintly of resin because their bodies are closed against loss. If one were cut open, there might be pale, fibrous layers inside, tough as cordage, each one made slowly from light that never quite arrives.

The solitary swollen plant near the white floor is the basin’s most deliberate-looking inhabitant. It has the shape of something designed, though no one designed it. Its folded leaves hold themselves around a central knot. Dust settles in the silver along their edges and stays there, making the plant seem older than it is. It may be younger than the surrounding stone, or older than any seed carried there by present weather. Its seasons cannot be read from above. It may spend years changing only beneath its skin: thickening at the base, tightening its roots, shifting a fraction of an inch toward some buried seam.

There are smaller lives too, though they do not announce themselves.

Within the dull gray-green crusts, where the old vapor rose and cooled, there may be films of mineral-fed organisms: cells that do not need leaves, do not need rain, perhaps do not need sunlight in the way surface things do. They live in pores, in hairline passages, in the places where chemistry has not entirely finished speaking. They make no color vivid enough to be called green. They leave only tarnish, a dim bloom on the stone, a change in texture that could be mistaken for weathering.

Beneath the pale central patch, in the fixed polygons of white earth, there may be dormant lives held as crystals hold old water: spores, cysts, sleeping bacteria, substances that crossed long ago from living into almost-living and have remained there. They are not waiting in the hopeful sense. Nothing in the basin waits hopefully. They persist because persistence is what their structure permits. Given no change, they remain unchanged.

Even the dust has a kind of residence there. It is not merely passing through. Each grain has settled into an arrangement with the hardness beneath it. It has found a shallow depression, a lee behind a slate plate, the cupped margin of a plant’s root crown. The dust lives as deposits live: by staying. A grain fallen from the ridge may take years to move the width of a hand. A dead leaf becomes dust and joins a place that has room for it already.

The warmth in the stone lives there too, if warmth can be said to live anywhere. It is held through the long dim hours after whatever day exists beyond the ceiling has withdrawn. It travels slowly from one layer to another. It enters the roots. It leaves the roots. It rises a little from seams in the ground and is taken back. Nothing uses it all. Nothing wastes it.

And there is the stillness.

It is not empty space between possible events. It is the basin’s oldest inhabitant. The shape of the rises shelters it; the dark ceiling deepens it; the hard ground refuses to record its passing. Stillness gathers in the white patch, in the narrow shade under shrubs, in the banded hollows of the eastern outcrop. It has weight without mass. It settles around every living thing and teaches it restraint.

No animal has made a home there. No creature with eyes comes regularly to drink, because there is nothing to drink. No burrow breaks the crust. No scavenger crosses the field of dark stone. Perhaps something small, almost too small to count as an animal, once drifted in on a dry current and died without leaving a mark. Perhaps not. The basin does not require an answer.

What lives there is a sparse fellowship of roots, minerals, dormant cells, settled dust, retained heat, and the forms made by their long agreement.

They do not alter the basin enough to make it seem inhabited.

They are the basin, continuing.
