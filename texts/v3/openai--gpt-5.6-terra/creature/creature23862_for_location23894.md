What lives on the shelf is not one ecology but several, layered like the unfinished stone itself.

First there are the creatures that were meant to be there.

The six-winged grazers are among them. Up close they are no larger than a kettle, broad-backed and pale, with bodies covered in a velvet of mineral-sensitive hairs. Their six folded wings resemble the petals of dead flowers: translucent near the joints, opaque at the tips, veined with fine coppery lines. They feed by pressing their mouths to the white ribs of the shelf and rasping away the luminous surface film that forms after rain. In doing so, they keep the mineral foam from sealing too quickly. Their herds are maintenance systems disguised as animals.

They do not fear people exactly. They notice weight. A person standing quietly may find a cluster of them arranging itself nearby, wings raised, listening to the bones and heartbeat beneath the ground. They chatter constantly, but the sounds are not social in the familiar sense. Much of their speech is a running report on the state of the stone: pressure increasing here, warmth diminishing there, a seam opening somewhere under the eastern terrace.

When one is sick, its wings stop agreeing with one another. The other grazers gather around it and click in a slow circle until the ground beneath them changes color. By morning, either the animal is gone into a newly opened pore, or it is standing again, altered: whiter, larger, carrying a new arrangement of wings.

The white-bodied burrowers are less obviously designed.

They emerge from warm vents with their blind faces pink from heat, their limbs too many and too delicate for the open air. Orange fungal strands hang from their mouths, though whether they are eating them, cultivating them, or being guided by them is uncertain. The fungi enter cracks in the young terrain and turn raw mineral into a dark, crumbly substance that the first mats of vegetation can inhabit. The burrowers are gardeners, but unwilling ones. Their orange cargo grows through their gums, and every so often an old burrower will sit beside a vent and allow itself to be covered completely in fruiting bodies. Its body becomes a warm, twitching mound. New young emerge from the mound weeks later with the same fungi already threaded through their mouths.

The long-legged birds were not invited.

Or, if they were, the invitation did not specify what they would become.

They arrive from the black mountains after storms, stepping delicately through the cloud layer and onto the shelf with the wary patience of creatures entering a room where someone has died. Their lacquered feathers shine dark green, purple, and black. Their legs are too thin for their bodies, jointed almost like reeds. They probe reflections because reflections on the shelf are not always images of what is above them. A pool may show a cave that has not yet formed, a slope under a different weather, or the face of a person who will arrive days later.

The birds feed on these discrepancies. They strike the water, withdraw something invisible, and swallow with a shiver. Afterward, the reflected world steadies. Places visited often by the birds tend to become ordinary more quickly: puddles remain puddles, paths stop moving, shadows fall in the expected direction.

There are predators as well, though calling them animals may be a convenience.

They live in steam, in the warm vapor that rises from seams before rain. At first they appear as distortions: a corridor of mist moving against the wind, a darkening beside a pool, a suggestion of limbs where no limbs should be. When they approach prey, the air cools around them. Their bodies are made of a black absorbent tissue that does not reflect the blue light within the stone. They hunt by silence. The grazers can hear settling rock, distant rain, and the pressure changes caused by a bird’s wing, but the steam predators make no vibration at all.

A grazing herd will be chattering and feeding, then abruptly stand with all six wings spread. The clicking stops. One animal vanishes—not dragged away, not struck, simply absent from the pattern of the herd. Minutes later, a fine rain begins, even if the rest of the shelf remains dry.

The predators leave no bones. In the places where they feed, the pale ground develops smooth black dimples. These remain warm for years.

There are slower inhabitants in the vegetation.

The dark green mats in the hollows are not plants in the usual sense. They are colonies of small, flat organisms braided through with moving silver roots. A hand laid on one will feel the roots turn toward its heat, not aggressively, but with interest. They examine pressure, salt, skin oils, old scars. If left alone, they slowly map every creature that crosses them. The map is held not as an image but as a set of tensions in the roots. At night, blue light travels through those tensions.

Some of the oldest mats contain records of visitors who are no longer alive.

The glassy reeds drink fog through their blades and store it in swollen joints near their bases. When a shelf-region begins to dry, the reeds bend toward it, snap their upper sections open, and release a fine rain. The black-plated trees do something more difficult. Their roots reach not downward but sideways into unstable portions of the terrain. During heavy weather, their trunks shift plate by plate, redistributing stress. The sound is like a deck of cards being shuffled underwater.

The trees are few because each one is also a decision.

Where a tree grows, the shelf has chosen to remain.

Then there are the inhabitants of the structures.

They are not often seen outside.

The shelters with no doors can be entered only by waiting near them long enough to become unremarkable. A person who approaches with purpose will find smooth walls and warm light behind an unbroken surface. A person who sits nearby during rain, perhaps exhausted or frightened, may discover that the wall has become an opening beside them. Inside, the benches are occupied by figures that initially appear to be coats left in the heat.

They are called, by the few voices that have returned from the shelf, the Attendants.

Their bodies are narrow and folded, with pale faces that seem unfinished in different ways each time they are seen. One may have eyes only when looking directly at someone. Another may possess a mouth that appears at the throat, the forehead, or the back of the hand depending on where speech is needed. Their clothes resemble the dark, rain-repelling material of the tower, but are porous from within: mist passes through them and emerges warm.

The Attendants do not seem to eat. They sit around instruments, watching needles tremble and ice form in delicate films on warm surfaces. They take notes in a script that fades from the page as it is written. Sometimes they leave the shelters and walk the handrailed paths, placing their palms on blank stone. The ground beneath their hands becomes briefly more solid.

They may be people changed by long service. They may be engineered custodians. They may be the machine’s preferred shape for a human conversation. None of them answers a direct question immediately. A question asked in the afternoon may return at night through the ground, spoken in the asker’s own voice but with one word replaced.

“Who are you?” becomes, several hours later:

“Who is being made?”

The narrow tower has its own resident.

It is never visible from the outside, because rain cannot approach the tower and neither, apparently, can observation. But at certain hours a silhouette may be seen through the upper window: a tall figure with too many shoulders, standing perfectly still before a curtain of blue light. The figure watches the shelf through instruments older than its own body.

The winged grazers avoid the tower. The birds circle it but do not land. The steam predators collect in its lee during storms, where the rain divides around the dark walls and falls in a continuous silver curtain beyond them.

Some believe the resident is the original operator of the terrain engine, preserved for the duration of a project that was expected to take centuries. Others believe it is not an individual at all but the accumulated attention of everyone who has ever monitored the shelf: a distributed caretaker condensed into a single patient outline.

It is known that when the tower light goes out, something below the shelf wakes.

Not the engine. The engine is always awake.

Beneath the shelf, in the mineral roots of the new ground, there are forms too large to surface. They can be inferred from the changes in the terrain: a ridge lifting over the course of an hour as though something has turned beneath it; warm water draining suddenly from a basin; a long low vibration passing from one end of the shelf to the other, causing every silver arch to lean in the same direction.

These may be the machine’s working parts, grown rather than built. They may be prototypes of larger terrain: mountains learning how to have weight, valleys practicing erosion before being released into the world. The shelf is small, but it is not simple. It may be an incubator for geology itself.

And there are human traces.

Abandoned cups collect rain beneath the shelters. A glove sometimes appears on a handrail, damp but never weathered. Names are scratched into the pale stone near the central basin, though they disappear within an hour unless someone remains nearby to remember them. On certain mornings, a thin line of fresh footprints crosses a terrace where no one can be seen. The prints lead toward a crack in the stone and end there.

The shelf may be inhabited by workers who come and go through methods the place has not taught outsiders. Or it may retain the habits of its makers: cups, gloves, names, footsteps—small domestic gestures repeated by the ground after the people who made them have gone.

In the end, the most numerous thing living there may be the shelf itself.

It responds to pressure, stores heat, makes choices about water, remembers weight, rejects theft, repeats speech, heals around disturbance, and sends its pale material outward centimeter by centimeter into the mountain air. Its animals are not merely upon it. Its trees hold it together. Its rain instructs it. Its hidden machine gives it a metabolism, but not necessarily a mind.

Or perhaps the machine is only its heart.

At night, when the clouds close over the drop-offs and the old mountains disappear, the shelf can seem almost inhabited by a single immense creature dreaming many smaller dreams: white burrowers carrying fungus, birds drinking mistakes from pools, grazers listening with their wings, quiet figures behind doorless walls, and somewhere beneath all of them, the slow deliberate labor of a place learning what kind of world it wants to be.
