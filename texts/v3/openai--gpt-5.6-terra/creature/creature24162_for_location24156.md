What lives on the plateau is not numerous. It does not breed quickly, hunt loudly, flower, migrate, or leave much evidence of having been there. Most of it could be mistaken for an aspect of the place itself.

The first inhabitants are the still ones.

In the sheltered basins where moss gathers, there are low forms beneath the green—pale, jointed bodies pressed into the damp like roots or old bones. They are not plants, though they have no obvious mouths, eyes, or limbs. During the dimmer hours they remain folded among the stones, each no larger than a handspan. When condensation thickens on the rock, they unfold by degrees. Fine white filaments extend from them and touch the wet surfaces around them. Through these filaments they drink, though drink may be too active a word. Moisture simply diminishes where they have been.

They move only when unseen.

A person who marks one with a circle of grit will find it still inside the circle for days. Then, after looking away for the length of a breath, they may find the circle empty, its grains arranged in a careful radial pattern, and the creature lodged under another stone several paces away. It leaves no trail. In the coldest cracks, colonies of them knit themselves together into pale cushions that pulse once every few hours, very slowly, like the throat of some sleeping animal.

Their purpose, if they have one, appears to be the maintenance of wetness. Places where they are common do not dry. The black pools keep their levels. The dripping under ledges continues. Where they are absent, water still gathers, but does not stay.

The slate-colored foxes are not foxes, although their shape seems to have borrowed something from one. They have narrow shoulders, long jaws, and coats that break into layers like shale when they stand still. Their eyes are dark in daylight and faintly reflective in the dusk that never quite comes. They are seen most often near the circular walls, not entering them but pacing their outer edges with their noses low.

They do not eat the cushion-things. They do not seem to eat anything visible.

Sometimes one sits facing west for an entire day, its back toward any observer, listening to the surf under the cliffs. At the end of that time it rises and walks away. Where it sat, the dust is often smooth, as if a shallow tide had washed over it. If followed, it leads people badly. Not maliciously. It simply walks according to distances that do not agree with the ground. A fox may disappear behind a boulder and emerge on a ridge that should be several miles away. Or it may cross a narrow gully and become no smaller, no more distant, no matter how long one watches it go.

The plateau's birds are more difficult to account for.

The pale things circling high above are not always birds. From below they have wings, or the suggestion of wings: two long white planes bending against the high wind. But when one descends—which happens rarely, and never near a person who is looking directly upward—it leaves no feather, no print, no disturbance in dust. It may settle on the top of a standing stone and remain there for weeks. In that state it resembles a scrap of frozen cloth caught on the rock.

Then one morning it is gone, and a new narrow crack has opened somewhere in the nearby escarpment.

The cracks that sing are believed, by whatever remembers belief in this place, to be their nests.

There are smaller birds as well: dull little runners with needle bills and feet too broad for their bodies. They live in the seams beneath the circular walls. At first sight they look like flakes of loose slate skittering in the wind. They gather around frost and peck at it. Their bills do not break the ice but draw something out of it—color, perhaps, or time. Frost eaten by these birds becomes clear and hard, glassy rather than white. A wall where many have fed will hold the day's weak light long after the surrounding ground has dimmed.

They never sing. Yet when a flock is startled, the air fills with the sound of distant bells being rung underwater.

Within the stone circles live the listeners.

No one sees them arrive. No one sees them leave. The circles without roofs are most likely to contain them, but not every time. A traveler may look into an empty enclosure at noon and see only dust gathered in a spiral, a skin of frost against the inward-facing stones. Looking in again at the same place before taking another step, they may find a figure seated at the center.

The listeners are wrapped in something the color of ash, stone, and old snow. Their bodies are narrow but not frail. Their heads are covered, their hands hidden. They do not respond to greetings, threats, offerings, or questions. Their stillness is not the stillness of statues; it has weight to it. Around them, wind alters course. Droplets under nearby ledges delay their fall. A shadow may creep toward them from the wrong direction and remain pooled at their feet.

Sometimes there is a face beneath the wrapping. It is never the same face twice, except when it is.

Those who approach too closely discover that the figure is not seated on the ground. It rests a finger's width above it. The dust beneath remains undisturbed. Beneath the figure, a faint sound can be heard: not breathing, but the deep periodic hammering from under the plateau, as though its body were hollow and connected directly to whatever water, sea, or immense sleeping mechanism moves beneath the western cliffs.

The listeners attend to that sound. This seems to be all they do.

If they are disturbed, they do not resist. They merely become harder to reach. The circle's interior expands. Its far wall recedes. A hand extended toward the wrapped figure finds the air cold, then colder, then suddenly full of a wind strong enough to force the hand back. Afterward, the circle may be empty for years. Or it may be occupied by someone—or something—whose shape is almost familiar: a lost traveler, a dead companion, a child standing with its face turned away.

The plateau also contains those who are passing through, though none can say from where.

They are encountered at the edges of vision: a line of small figures descending a slope that has no path; someone carrying a lantern whose flame gives no light; a tall person walking beside a black pool with their reflection moving ahead of them. They wear clothes from no consistent time. Some have packs, staffs, ropes, weathered boots. Some are dressed for warmth; others are bareheaded and thinly clothed, apparently untouched by cold.

They do not always notice the living.

When they do, they may ask for directions. Their questions are simple and unbearable.

How far until the sea?

Which way did the sun go?

Have you seen the wall with the door?

Is this the same day?

They accept answers with grave attention, even when the answer is silence. Then they continue in whatever direction was indicated. Most do not leave tracks. A few leave tracks that begin miles before they are seen and stop precisely where they vanish.

It is possible that they are travelers caught by the plateau's refusal of arrival. It is possible that they are what travelers become after enough days of walking toward an object that never approaches. The listeners do not acknowledge them. The foxes watch them from among the stones.

Deep below, beneath the shelves of slate and the dry dust, something larger lives.

It is not the source of the surf, exactly. The surf may be its breathing, or the sound of its movement against a boundary too vast to imagine. At irregular intervals the entire plateau gives a slow, internal shudder. Black pools wrinkle though no wind touches them. Frost loosens from the north sides of stones. The circular walls make small settling sounds, each stone pressing more perfectly against the next.

Then comes the report from below: long, low, almost gentle.

The thing beneath is old enough that the mountains have worn themselves into comfortable shapes around it. It has no need to emerge. It may not know there is an above. Its body, if body is the word, is threaded through the rock in chambers and flooded passages, turning slowly beneath the western escarpments. Water gathers where it dreams. Cracks open where it shifts. The delayed shadows and impossible distances may be nothing more than the surface effects of its attention passing through the stone.

The stone circles face west because they are ears.

The listeners sit within them because something must listen back.

And the plateau itself lives, in the restrained manner of things that have endured beyond urgency. Its lichens retain patterns. Its pools remember reflections. Its frost repeats old edges. It does not hunger for visitors, nor does it reject them. It keeps them, briefly or forever, according to rules too patient to be cruel.

Nothing there wishes to conquer the place.

Nothing there needs to.

The creatures, the wrapped listeners, the pale circling things, the fox-shaped walkers, the wet white bodies in the moss, even the lost people crossing the gray shelves: all seem less like inhabitants than organs of a single suspended condition. They preserve the pause. They keep water dripping, wind sounding, frost forming, stone answering stone.

They ensure that the plateau remains exactly what it is:

a place where life has not ended,

but has learned how to wait without changing.
