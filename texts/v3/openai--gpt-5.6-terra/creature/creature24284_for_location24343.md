What lives there is not easily seen as a body.

At first it might be mistaken for the water itself: a tendency in the water to choose certain cracks, to pause in certain basins, to leave behind a skin of copper, milk-white, or black-green mineral. It has no den, no nest, no trackway. It does not disturb the arrangement of the stones. Its life is expressed by maintaining that arrangement, by occupying the minute interval between wetness and rock.

In the narrowest channels, beneath shelves of old ice, it grows in clear threads finer than hair. These are not roots, though they enter fractures and split around grains of quartz. They are not veins, though water passes through them and comes out changed: colder, sometimes sweeter, sometimes carrying a metallic taste that lingers on the tongue. Where several threads meet, they form a translucent membrane over a hollow. A person who touched it might think it was ice until it flexed once beneath the finger.

Most of it remains underground.

The basin is full of hidden chambers, not large caves but innumerable tight spaces: gaps among locked boulders, tubes bored by water, dark seams beneath mineral crust. There, in the permanent noise, it makes its slow exchanges. It draws energy from differences too slight for warm-country life to notice—the warmth of stone against ice, the charge carried by seepwater, the faint chemistry released where one mineral is converted into another. It feeds on transition. Dissolving and re-forming. Freezing beside flowing. The mountain’s refusal to become one thing completely.

Its exposed parts are the strange textures on the stone.

The amber films in shallow pools are one of its forms. So are the dark velvet deposits at the waterline, the pale blooms around vents, the white lattices that seem to have crystallized in defiance of the current. Each may be a different age of the same creature, or a different organ, if such words apply. A fresh-looking white lattice can be older than the oldest visible ice. A black patch no wider than a hand may extend, as a continuous living fabric, through half the valley floor.

It has no need to move quickly, because the water moves for it.

When a channel shifts by the width of a finger over a hundred years, it follows. When a rock seals a seep and forces the flow elsewhere, it thins, darkens, waits in the wet stone, and emerges from a new crack perhaps a mile away. It is patient in the exact manner of mineral processes: not dormant, not asleep, but conducting its life at a pace in which a human lifetime is a brief variation in pressure.

There may be larger manifestations.

Far beneath the audible torrents, where the walls of the mountain are warmed by depths no surface winter can reach, the separate films and membranes perhaps join into something more coherent. Not a beast swimming through subterranean water, not a buried giant, but an awareness distributed across pressure, temperature, and flow. It knows the basin as a body knows its own posture. It knows which ice shelves are thinning by fractions. It knows the weight of every leaning slab. It knows when a bird’s shadow passes overhead, not by seeing it, but by the almost immeasurable cooling of a wet mineral face.

If it notices people, it would not understand them as animals first.

It would know them as disturbances in the water: warmth introduced into cold air; salts shed from skin; breath condensing on stone; a pressure that arrives, hesitates, and moves on. Their speech would be lost in the great noise, but their bodies would leave brief signatures in the basin’s continual chemistry. A hand dipped into a pool would alter it. A boot placed on a mineral mat would press a pattern into a living surface that might retain the fact of that pressure for years.

The pale birds may know of it more directly. They never land in the open basin, but sometimes one circles once above a particular ravine, wings nearly motionless, before vanishing into the white brightness. Perhaps there are warmer vents deep in the rock where they descend. Perhaps they feed on blind, colorless creatures washed from the fissures. Or perhaps they are simply listening for the low note of the mountain, which changes very slightly where the hidden life is thickest.

The thing living there has no face. It does not watch from behind a boulder. It does not emerge when darkness comes, because darkness changes almost nothing for it.

But the basin is not merely inhabited by it. The basin is being slowly made into its preferred condition: wet but never flooded, cold but never sterile, crowded with stone yet threaded everywhere with passage. The enduring noise is part of its shelter. The old ice is part of its skin. The mineral bloom is its handwriting, repeated so slowly across the rock that no observer can catch the stroke as it is made.

In that country of aftermath, it is the aftermath that learned to live.
