What lives there is not easily seen because it has learned to live at the speed of seepage.

They are called, in the few surviving marks that may be names, the **Receivers**.

At a glance they might be mistaken for parts of the construction. A Receiver standing still beneath a roof plate resembles a bundled stack of slate, a low shrine built around a dark hollow, or a worker’s rain-cloak hung from a peg and forgotten. Their bodies are narrow and layered. They wear overlapping mantles of stone lamellae, gray-green and black, each plate drilled with tiny holes. Water runs over them continuously. It enters at their shoulders and leaves at their heels in measured threads.

They do not seem to mind the cold.

Their faces, when one sees them, are pale and smooth in a way that recalls limestone freshly broken open. They have no visible mouths. Across the lower half of the face is a series of fine vertical seams, closed when they are dry and opening slightly in the mist. Their eyes are deep-set, dark, and almost without movement. Some have polished translucent lenses fitted over them—cloudy blue mineral discs, cut from the luminous stone in the upper walls. Older Receivers often have one natural eye and one such lens. No one appears to regard this as an injury.

Their hands are the most living-looking part of them: long, dark, swollen from wetness, with blunt fingers stained by iron and lime. The nails are flat chips of horn-colored stone. They use their hands with extraordinary care. A Receiver may spend an hour touching the lip of a channel, feeling a water level too small for another creature to notice. It may place a single grain of grit in a groove, listen to the altered sound, and then leave without further action.

They are never hurried.

The valley is full of them, though it takes time to understand this. One is kneeling inside a spillway mouth, half hidden by falling water. Another lies on a roof slope with an ear pressed to the stone. Three move along the central reservoir wall, stopping at every wedge-shaped compartment. Far above, near the new-cut scar on the eastern slope, several figures work beneath the idle cranes. They do not raise voices to one another. They communicate by tapping: nail against slate, pebble against pipe, knuckle against a wall. The sounds travel through the construction.

A short pattern means a gate is sticking.

A long, slow tapping asks whether a cistern has accepted its share.

Three hollow knocks, spaced widely apart, mean that water has appeared where there was not supposed to be water.

The Receivers have no visible households. There are chambers throughout the basin, but none can certainly be called homes. Some are dry and lined with sleeping shelves; some contain rows of hanging vessels; some are warm with the strange low heat of buried pipes. In one chamber, perhaps, a dozen Receivers sit motionless in a circle around a stone bowl, their mantles steaming faintly. In another, there are only tools: stone scoops, copper pins, lengths of cord, clay listening cups, wedges wrapped in oil-dark cloth.

They sleep where the system permits them to sleep.

They eat very little that an outsider would recognize as food. In protected galleries beneath the terraces, where warmth from the mountain remains caught in the stone, they cultivate pale growths in water: transparent threads, soft chalk-colored shelves, slick membranes that cling to the sides of mineral tanks. These they scrape into shallow bowls. They also collect salts from selected basins, not the yellow or red ones, but a blue-gray crust that dissolves on the tongue and leaves the body warm for a few hours.

There may once have been plants here. There may once have been animals, grain, fuel, orchards. The Receivers preserve stories of such things in objects rather than speech. A bird’s skeleton is set under glass in a niche near an upper stair. A twisted root, black as coal, is enclosed in a wall beside an old sluice gate. In a dry room high above the reservoir, a small packet of seeds rests inside a lead box.

No Receiver opens it.

They are not gardeners. They do not understand growth as something that must spread, flower, rot, and return. To them, growth is accumulation: one wall leaning into another wall; one channel accepting the failure of an older channel; one basin receiving what a higher basin can no longer hold.

Their young are made slowly.

In the deepest cisterns are chambers not connected to the public water courses. Their entrances are sealed with heavy plates and marked only by rings of pale deposits. A Receiver who has become old enough—there is no clear sign, except that their mantle plates begin to whiten at the edges—will descend into one of these chambers carrying a small sealed vessel. What occurs below is unknown even to most of the valley. Months later, or years later, a child may be found sitting in a dry catch-basin at dawn.

The children are small, dark-headed, and nearly naked except for a wrapping of felted mineral fiber. They do not cry. Their first movements are always toward running water. Before they are given a name, they are brought to a wall and taught to listen.

This is the beginning of their education: not language, but gradients.

A child learns the difference between a drop falling one finger’s height and a drop falling two. It learns which hollows answer with a clear note and which with a muddy one. It learns that a pipe may sound empty while carrying water behind its inner skin. It learns that every wall is heavier on one side than the other, even if the eye cannot see it. Only much later does it learn speech, and then only enough for the work.

Their language has few nouns for objects. A stone is described by what it bears, what it sheds, what it is threatening to become. Water is never simply water. It is water-before-settling, water-after-filtering, water-that-remembers-metal, water-too-cold-to-give, water-held-against-its-desire.

They have words for delays that last generations.

They have no word for wilderness.

The newest work on the eastern slope is theirs, though it does not look like the work of a settled people. It looks frantic only because the material is new. The Receivers are opening a route toward a higher condensation field in the mountains. For several years, perhaps, the cloud has been arriving differently. It has lingered above the basin’s rim instead of descending. The upper stone remains dry for longer in the mornings. A slight change in wind has become, to the Receivers, a catastrophe visible in every measuring bowl.

So they climb.

They fasten channels to slopes where no channel could reasonably endure. They build stairs not for feet alone, but for the transport of water from one invisible temperature to another. At the edge of the mist, where the mountain dissolves into whiteness, they have begun setting upright slabs of translucent stone. These are not walls. They are cold-catching faces. Fog touches them, thickens, beads, and runs downward into narrow grooves.

The construction is a kind of net, though it catches no animal and no sun.

The Receivers believe that water wants to leave every shape given to it. Their task is not to prevent that desire. Nothing can prevent it forever. Their task is to ensure that, on its way out, water has passed through enough hands, enough stones, enough chambers, enough decisions, that the valley remains legible.

This is why they do not make a single river.

A river is too quick. A river forgets where it has been.

At the center reservoir, sometimes, a Receiver will stand on the low circular wall through an entire day. Mist gathers on its mantle. Water threads through the plates over its body and falls into a compartment below. The figure watches the nearly imperceptible differences in level between the dark wedges. It may be attending to a change that began high on the western roofs months ago. It may be waiting for a message from the channels beneath its feet.

Or it may be listening for something older: a pressure in the mountain, a buried water course, the memory of a sea inside the stone.

When the reservoir finally equalizes a little—when one compartment gives a finger-width of water to another—the Receiver lowers its head.

Then, from somewhere in the mist, comes a single answering knock.
