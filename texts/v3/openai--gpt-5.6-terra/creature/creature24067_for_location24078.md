What lives there is not a population in the usual sense.

There are no hidden households behind the pale facades. No sleepers in the towers. No descendants of the builders moving silently along the high braces. The empty rooms are genuinely empty, insofar as a room can be empty when its walls are listening to their own moisture and deciding, grain by grain, where to become denser.

But the city is inhabited.

Its inhabitants are processes that have learned to keep a shape.

They live in the places where a need becomes persistent enough to acquire habits: at the foot of a leak, in a load-bearing joint, beneath the reservoir where pressure gathers and waits. They are not visible all at once. One may be mistaken for a trick of reflection in a wet corridor, a shift in the moss, a thread of mineral brightness moving through concrete. It is only after watching for a long time that one notices an arrangement repeating itself: droplets selecting the same route down a wall; a patch of paving warming ahead of a crack; a bridge’s underside growing fine white filaments where no break has yet appeared.

These are the city’s small residents.

They have no bodies independent of their work. A drain-clearing presence may exist as a slow procession of bubbles moving uphill through clogged runoff, lifting silt one grain at a time into a side channel. A brace-tender may be a tremor in a tower’s branching crown, traveling from tip to tip, testing each stone slab above for the minute differences between weight and danger. In the gardens, something keeps the great leaves from collecting too much water. It passes through the stalks as a brief dull glow, and afterward the leaf will tilt by a degree, spilling its burden into the paving channels.

They are not conscious in a way that would make them lonely. Nor are they mindless.

They recognize recurrence. They remember stress. They respond to patterns that resemble past failures, though memory for them is stored in changed crystal structures, altered magnetic alignments, in the slow tuning of the hum beneath the towers. When rain has been heavy above, the whole settlement becomes more alert. The silver water-columns rise straighter. The paving’s faint lights wake earlier. Doors at the northern margin cloud more thickly, as if whatever lies beyond them must be kept dry from this side.

At times, larger things move.

They do so rarely enough that a visitor could remain for weeks and see nothing but water and vegetation. Then, perhaps during a long rain, a wall at the basin’s edge will loosen its pale surface and open like a muscle relaxing. From the dark service space behind it comes a shape made of articulated mineral plates, each plate wet and reflective, connected by cords of black fiber. It unfolds low to the ground, many-limbed but not animal-like. Its limbs do not step. They adhere, release, and reattach with patient precision.

Such a thing may cross a courtyard in an hour.

It goes to some place that has not yet visibly failed: a retaining wall, a section of ceiling, an old stair whose lowest steps have begun to soften at the edges. There it remains, pressed against stone. Fine dust drifts from its body. Moisture gathers on it and disappears. By morning it has become part of the repair: a dark lattice in the wall, a new run of handrail, a seam of almost-black glass binding two pieces of rock together.

No one sees it leave because it does not leave. It spends itself into the city.

There may be dozens of these large custodians sleeping folded inside walls and beneath paving, or there may be only one distributed across the basin in many dormant pieces. The city makes no distinction that matters. It does not name them. It does not preserve their forms once their task is complete.

The nearest thing to an individual is found in the old transit tunnel.

The regular knocking there is not a train. It is a resident of the tunnel, something ancient enough to have developed a rhythm. Far in the sealed dark, a mass of mechanisms continues to travel the same short distance along a track that no longer goes anywhere. It moves forward, encounters the collapsed end, compresses, reverses, and returns. Knock. Knock. Knock.

Its work may once have been transportation. Now it is measuring.

Each pass tells it how the tunnel has shifted. Each impact reports on the density of the stone, the swelling of the clay, the wetness of the old support rings. The sound travels into the basin, and other structures answer it with tensions too low to hear. The city uses the tunnel’s prisoner as a kind of heartbeat.

It has been doing this for so long that the knocking is no longer evidence of malfunction. It is a civic function.

The plants are inhabitants too, though they are not allowed the full freedom of wilderness. The city does not command them, exactly. It bargains.

Roots are permitted into certain cracks and discouraged from others by changes in warmth, salt, and mineral taste. Moss is invited over surfaces that need a soft wet seal; it is quietly starved from joints that must remain flexible. The lantern-pods hanging from ceiling mats are cultivated as much for their dim light as for their roots’ ability to stitch loose soil together. Their lack of scent is not an accident. Nothing here needs to call out.

Even decay has a place in the city, but it is kept from becoming a feast. Fungi dissolve fallen matter without spreading beyond their assigned dampness. Algae inhabit the reservoir’s margins in thin black-green films and convert the water’s minerals into substances the walls can use. The broad leaves in the courtyards die slowly and without drama, collapsing into clear pulp that slides toward drains. Nothing wastes. Nothing is eaten.

And behind the amber doors there may be other kinds of residents.

When a door clears, the bright dry avenue beyond is not necessarily elsewhere. It may be another internal state: a reserve geometry, a memory of an earlier surface city, a chamber kept at a pressure and temperature no longer possible in the basin. White frames stand there, often empty. Yet sometimes there is movement among them—not a figure, not even a machine, but a rearrangement of distance. A frame that was far becomes near without crossing the intervening space. A shadow bends against the wrong source of light. The amber clouds before one can decide whether anything was seen.

Perhaps these are the city’s oldest inhabitants: designs that were never fully built, contingencies retained in the material, alternate arrangements waiting for the appropriate failure.

They do not come through.

The city has no need of them yet.

If people once lived here, the remaining systems do not appear to mourn them. There are benches, dry rooms, stairways proportioned for legs, and the occasional smooth indentation in a railing that remembers repeated hands. But the place has long since stopped expecting a particular body. Its lights come on for any approaching mass. Its bridges strengthen for imagined crossings. Its doors sometimes open onto possibilities no visitor can enter.

It keeps accommodation alive as a principle.

So what lives in the unfinished cave is a patient ecology of maintenance: water trained into verticality; stone taught to heal; roots enlisted as anchors; old machines reduced to rhythmic senses; dormant structures waiting inside other structures; and, everywhere, the low distributed attention of the city itself.

It is not alone.

It is full of things that do not require company.
