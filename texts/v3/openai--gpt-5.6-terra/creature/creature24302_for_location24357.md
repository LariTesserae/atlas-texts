What lives there is not a village, though people below call it one when they speak of it at all.

A village has doors that close, names that pass from parent to child, smoke rising every morning from more than one roof. It has fields measured by habit, quarrels with boundaries, graves in orderly ground. The shelf has none of these things. It has a shelter, a path, a few rules, and at most two or three human beings at a time.

They are called, in the lower valleys, the Rim-Keepers.

They do not call themselves that. Most of them do not call themselves anything in particular while they are there.

They are herders in one season, gatherers in another, watchers when the weather turns dangerous. Some are sent up as children after they have learned not to fear silence; some come only after age has made the lower world too crowded. A person may remain for three nights, or a summer, or long enough that the bent trees grow familiar and the choughs learn the sound of their cough. No one is born on the shelf deliberately. No one is buried there. The mountain permits visitors, not possessions.

The keepers tend no flock of their own. The goats belong to the ledges and to winter and to whichever cliff has not yet taken them. Still, the keepers know individual animals. They know the old black-faced doe with the split left horn, the pale kid born late in the season, the broad-chested billy who limps after snowmelt. They leave salt on a flat stone near the upper rocks, never close to the shelter. They clear fallen pebbles from a drinking run. In harsh springs, when the grass is still under ice but the goats are already descending hungry, they hang dried herbs from the branch rack and let the wind carry the scent.

They say this is not feeding. It is reminding.

The shelf also has an older resident: a great animal that does not belong to any story in the same way that goats and marmots do.

Some say it is a snow leopard, though no one has brought back a skin or a clean description. Some say bear, despite the lack of room for a bear to live there. The tracks are often too blurred by rain to settle the question. The claw marks behind the boulders are high, deep, and periodically renewed. In the flattened hollow, there are tufts of goat hair, marmot bones, and once, according to one keeper, a raven’s wing laid neatly across a stone.

The keepers do not hunt it.

They call it the Last Guest.

When fresh signs appear near the shelter, the people sleep without a fire. They tie their food high in the twisted trees. They place a bowl of spring water at the edge of the boulder screen, then keep their eyes down and their voices low until the signs are gone. This is not because they believe the creature can be bargained with. It is because the shelf is too small for anyone to behave as if they are alone.

The birds are less guests than citizens.

The choughs are bold, bright-minded thieves. They steal cord, bits of dried meat, fragments of shining mica, the bone pins used to fasten cloaks. A keeper who has left food unattended has only himself to blame. The choughs know when the branch door is merely leaned in place and when it is weighted from within. They know which travelers carry seeds in their pockets. They gather along the upper cliff before storms, not fleeing but watching, their black heads angled toward whatever is coming.

The old people in the valleys say that a chough will not take a person’s last food.

This is mostly true.

Ptarmigan live in the grass so completely that they seem generated by it. In summer they are mottled with earth and lichen; in winter they become pieces of broken snow. A nesting bird will allow a hand to come almost within reach before bursting upward with such violence that even experienced keepers curse aloud. The shelf’s rule about eggs is held most strictly by the people who have once seen a ptarmigan lead a fox—or something worse—away from its nest by pretending to have a broken wing.

Marmots are the alarm bells. The keepers listen to them not as one listens for danger, but as one listens to news. A single whistle means interruption. A chain of whistles means movement. Silence, when it spreads from one boulder field to another, means that something has entered the shelf which the marmots do not understand.

That something is sometimes a person.

Not every visitor reaches the basin by the path.

There are cloud-walkers.

The name is not an honor. It is given to those who appear when the white weather has settled over the rim and nobody should be traveling. They come wet but not soaked, cold but not shivering, carrying no proper climbing gear and often no memory of the ascent. Some are lost travelers from neighboring ranges. Some are people who left a valley road at dusk and should by all sensible reckoning be far below. Some speak in dialects no one has heard for generations, or ask whether a certain bridge still stands though the bridge in question fell before their grandparents were born.

The keepers receive them.

A cloud-walker is given dry wool, hot water, and a place nearest the inner wall of the shelter. They are not asked their name until morning. They are not allowed to look over the rim. If they wake in the night speaking to someone outside, no one answers for them.

Most leave when the cloud loosens. They go down by the proper route, slowly, with a keeper ahead and behind. Later, in the valleys, they are found to be ordinary enough: a missing shepherd, a widow from a settlement two ridges over, a boy who slept through a storm beneath a cart. They remember the shelf imperfectly. They remember moss, smoke, a bird laughing like iron. They remember being told to hold a warm stone in both hands.

A few do not leave.

These become keepers, if they can learn the rules.

The current keeper, if there is one, is likely a person with weather in them. Not magical in any grand sense; merely altered by long exposure. Their hair smells faintly of smoke and wet grass. Their hands are dark in the cracks, their boots continually damp, their speech economical. They know what can be taken and what must be left. They can find the spring beneath new snow. They can tell, by the pitch of water under the turf, whether a night’s rain will loosen stones from the upper wall. They know where to stand when lightning comes close enough to make the standing stones hum.

They keep a small inventory, though it is not written down:

Two wool blankets, one patched beyond dignity.  
A clay pot with a crack that has been bound in wire scavenged from somewhere far below.  
Dried yarrow, juniper, bitterroot, and the blue flower that helps with altitude sickness if brewed before the headache begins.  
A knife with a horn handle.  
Three lengths of cord.  
A packet of seeds, never planted all at once.  
A smooth dark stone passed from keeper to keeper, used for warming hands, pressing poultices, and placing beside the spring when someone has died below.

The shelter is not theirs, exactly. They sleep in it, repair it, sweep out feathers and moss, re-seat roof slabs after high wind. But they do not improve it beyond recognition. The old shape matters. A new wall built too straight, a roof tied too tightly, a path made too secure: these would imply that the shelf had been claimed. And once a place is claimed, it begins to demand defense. The keepers want no part of that.

The standing stones are attended by someone older still, though no one sees this person often.

There may be a woman from the eastern valleys who comes every third autumn, carrying a hammerstone wrapped in red cloth. There may be a line of such women, or one woman who has lived too long for sensible accounting. She repicks the fading grooves in the stones where rain and lichen have softened them. Not all of the marks are renewed. Some she allows to vanish.

The spirals are for returning water.  
The paired dots are for eyes: animal, human, and those without bodies.  
The branching marks are paths, but not always paths that feet should follow.

When she is present, the choughs do not steal from the shelter. When she leaves, she takes nothing but a pinch of ash from the fire ring and a little water in a stoppered horn.

There are things beneath the moss as well.

Not buried people. Not treasure. Smaller remnants: old charcoal, fish bones carried from valleys long ago, a child’s bead of cloudy blue glass, a goat bell with no clapper, flint chips, threads of copper-green metal. The shelf has been visited for longer than anyone remembers. It has offered water, a level place to sleep, a warning before the drop. It has received fear, gratitude, hunger, and the occasional impossible return.

And in the cloud below, perhaps, there are others.

The keepers do not say the clouds contain a kingdom, or the dead, or another mountain turned upside down. They are more careful than that. They say only that sometimes the valley is not where it ought to be.

On certain evenings, voices rise clearly through the white: a mother calling a child in a language the keeper understands; bells from animals that no longer graze any nearby slope; laughter; the sound of an axe striking wood at a patient, domestic rhythm. Once in a while, a figure can be seen far below the rim, walking where no ground is visible. It may be a trick of vapor and distance. It may be someone on a path briefly revealed. It may be nothing that can be helped.

No keeper calls down.

No keeper climbs after.

They sit near the shelter, or among the standing stones, with their backs to the wind. They let the goats stand silent on the upper ledges. They let the choughs settle their wings. They listen until the cloud moves on or the voices become water again.

That is what lives on the shelf: birds and burrowing animals, rain-fed roots, a predator with no settled name, goats that trust the impossible angles of stone, and people who have learned to make themselves temporary.

Also the rules.

Also the memory of every person who arrived through weather and found, for one night at least, a small wet piece of ground still holding fast above the world.
