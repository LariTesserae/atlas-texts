The people of the basin are called, by outsiders, saltborn.

They do not usually call themselves that.

They call themselves by districts, by water rights, by the particular shelf of stone where their families have lived long enough for the damp to enter the walls. A person is from Upper Vey, or the Third Condensate, or the Blue Culverts, or from the old market roofs beside the eastern drain. Their names often include a practical second part: a pump number, a channel, a trade, a remembered storm. It is useful in a city where addresses wash away, buildings subside, and one family may move three times in a generation without leaving the same view of the sky.

They are not made for the basin in any simple biological sense. They are made by it, slowly, through habit.

Most dress for moisture rather than cold or heat. Their clothing is pale and layered: loose salt-resistant overshirts, dark mesh beneath, high boots with ribbed soles, gloves with waxed fingertips. Many wear broad hoods, not to keep off rain but to preserve a small zone of shade around the eyes. The city’s light is treated with the respect other places give open flame. Children are taught not to look into a water pan at midday. Workers keep narrow visors clipped to their collars. Elderly people sometimes wear polished mineral lenses that make the noon world amber or gray.

Their skin tends to be dry where it is exposed and soft where clothing keeps the damp close. The basin leaves marks on everyone. Fine white mineral gathers in eyebrows and hair. Rust stains the cuffs of mechanics. Those who work the condensate rails develop permanently puckered fingertips. Those who tend thermal wells carry a faint sulfur smell, even after washing. Many people have small old burns on their shins from careless contact with a hot pipe or a crust that looked solid and was not.

They are quiet in public, but not solemn.

The basin teaches economy of sound. A raised voice travels strangely through vapor and open concrete. It can seem to come from beneath a bridge, or from the far side of a cloud. People speak close when they need to be heard. They use taps on railings, hand signs, lamp shutters, colored tags tied to pipe joints. Children develop a game of identifying family members by footfall through the floors of their apartment blocks. Lovers learn one another’s rhythms on wet staircases. Workers can tell, by placing a palm on a wall, whether a pump is running well, whether a pressure line is carrying heat, whether something blind and heavy has entered the drain beneath them.

The people who keep the city functioning are not separated cleanly into professions. A pump tender may also raise siphoners in a filtered channel. A schoolteacher may inspect skimmer pans on weekends and sell the gathered mineral film to a refinery. A market cook may know more about water chemistry than a visiting engineer. Everyone is expected to understand the basic temperaments of heat, brine, salt, vapor, mold, metal, and pressure.

There are specialists, of course.

There are cloud guides, who walk the causeways when vapor thickens and travelers lose the road. They wear reflective capes and carry long forked poles that hum faintly when held near live infrastructure. They know where a cloud settles first, which bridges become slick, which culverts breathe dangerous gases before a storm. In bad weather they stand at the water houses and flash panels toward the white, calling people in with coded reflections.

There are salt surgeons, who work on buildings more often than bodies. They cut away swollen mineral from foundations, pack seep-holes with sacrificial plaster, redirect water before it can hollow a wall into collapse. Their tools are quiet: saws, heated needles, chisels, hoses, small percussion hammers wrapped in cloth. They are patient people. They speak of a building’s “thirst,” its “knees,” its “bad side.” They know that an old apartment block can survive decades if its water is given somewhere better to go.

There are drain wardens, who descend into the warmer levels where runoff channels cross beneath streets. They wear sealed coats, face screens, and lamps tuned not to attract too many insects. They count siphoner populations, trim bacterial mats, clear mineral plugs, and remove eggs from public basins when the season turns. The work is feared, but not despised. Drain wardens are considered intimate with the city’s hidden body. They are invited to meals, asked about omens, listened to when they say a district should leave its ground floors empty for a while.

And there are vapor harvesters.

They work high on gantries, water towers, upper pipe corridors, and the skeletal crowns of refineries. They maintain meshes and cooling fins that catch moisture from passing cloud. Their work makes them visible from almost anywhere in the basin: small figures moving against the white, tethered to railings, collecting water from air that seems too thin to hold it. Children are told that vapor harvesters are the reason clouds do not steal the city away.

This is not literally believed.

But it is not entirely a joke.

The city has many kinds of wealth, and almost none of them resemble coin. Clean water is one. Reliable shade is another. A room above the flood line, a functioning condenser, a stable roof, an inherited map of underground channels: these can matter more than wages. People trade maintenance favors over years. A household may owe another household two seasons of pipe work, a length of insulated cable, a place in a cistern queue, a night’s shelter during a corrosive rain.

Hospitality is serious because exposure is serious.

No one is turned from an emergency shade gallery. No one is denied a cup of processed water if they arrive visibly overheated. There are strict rules about this, old enough that no one remembers their origin. The rules are painted on cistern walls in several languages and in pictograms simple enough for a frightened child to understand: give water, give shade, report sickness, mark unsafe ground, do not hide a breach.

There are also rules about what not to give.

Untreated basin water is never offered to a stranger without saying what it is. Hot water is never poured onto salt skin. A person who has come in from cloud is not questioned until they have warmed or dried according to need. And nobody, whatever their dispute, is abandoned below a pressure line.

The people are accustomed to things that would unsettle visitors. They can sleep through the soundless arrival of cloud against their windows. They can tell by taste whether rain has come from a clean upper mass or passed through refinery vapor. They recognize the warning sheen of dangerous runoff. They do not recoil from wall-lizards in kitchens; most households leave a small damp stone near the back door for them. The lizards keep down the lamp insects, and are regarded as tenants with poor manners.

The skimmers are treated with a stranger kind of regard.

They are not pets. They are too delicate and too numerous for that. But a skimmer pan is often protected like a small shrine, especially where the creatures gather in unusually precise patterns. Some families believe the lines they make at dusk reveal changes in pressure or weather. Others insist this is superstition and then quietly check the pans before long journeys.

The heat kites are less loved. Their presence means uplift, vapor, insect bloom, and often industrial discharge. Still, they are watched. A sudden low flight of kites can mean a cloud front has entered the basin. Their falling shadows pass over market awnings, reservoirs, schoolyards, and pipe fields, and people look up without thinking.

Children have more freedom than outsiders expect. The city is open, and its dangers are visible once one has learned their shapes. They run along marked dry routes beneath overpasses. They make little boats from scale-like salt sheets and race them in drainage rills. They dare one another to stand closest to the steaming cracks in old pavement. They collect the translucent shed skins of wall-lizards and press them between pages. On cloud days they vanish into vapor in noisy groups, calling out to one another until their parents summon them home by striking spoons against the condensation rails.

Their games are often games of maintenance.

They build miniature pumping stations from broken valves and shards of plastic. They draw imaginary pipe maps in salt dust. They play stranded traveler, cloud guide, drain warden, refinery chief. The most admired child in a game is not the one who makes the loudest disaster, but the one who finds a way to route water around it.

Old people are valued for memory rather than authority alone. The basin changes slowly, but it changes constantly, and the oldest residents remember where the old ground used to be. They remember which apartment blocks stood on dry land before the shelves softened. They remember the first time a particular channel turned blue-green. They remember roads that now end at a collapsed culvert, markets that moved after a warm flood, a whole district of low workshops swallowed not by catastrophe but by decades of patient water.

Their stories are practical, but become beautiful by repetition.

A grandmother may tell a child about the year the western cloud stayed for nine days and every rail in the city dripped at once. An uncle may describe the siphoner that grew too large in the old filtration sump, though each telling makes it larger. Retired harvesters speak of climbing through cloud so dense that the world ended at their elbows. These stories are not separated from instruction. A tale about a foolish mechanic ends with the correct procedure for checking a brine valve. A ghost story ends with advice about unsafe cisterns.

Religion, where it exists, tends to be local and material.

There are households that keep a bowl of first condensate at the entrance during the cloud season. Some people make offerings of clean metal or pale stone at thermal vents, not because they imagine the earth needs gifts, but because taking heat without acknowledgment feels impolite. There are small niches beneath bridges where names are written for those lost in floods, cave-ins, and distant travel. The niches are always wet. Their candles are sealed in glass cylinders. On certain nights, people leave mineral crystals there that have formed naturally in their homes, each one a record of unseen water.

But the deepest faith of the basin may be maintenance.

That a thing can be kept.

That a wall can be patched before it fails. That a contaminated pan can be cleaned. That a child can be taught the color of danger. That water, even bad water, can be guided. That heat can be borrowed without being owned. That survival is not a triumph over the place where one lives, but an ongoing agreement with it.

Visitors often mistake the city’s spaciousness for loneliness. They see empty wet avenues, distant pylons, scattered roofs, solitary figures crossing white ground, and assume the inhabitants have been thinned out by hardship.

But the distances are full of relation.

A woman on an upper balcony is watching her brother’s pump station through a gap in the cloud. A technician on a pipe bridge is signaling a market vendor that the rain is safe to catch. A child crouched beside a water pan is waiting for skimmers to divide around a darkening patch, because that pattern will tell him whether the channel beneath has warmed. In a low apartment under a mineral-streaked window, someone is listening to the night pipes and knowing, from their small ticks and contractions, that the whole city is settling into its sleep.

And beneath it all, heat rises.

Water gathers.

The people of the basin live in that interval: not above the world, not beneath it, but inside its slow unfinished work.
