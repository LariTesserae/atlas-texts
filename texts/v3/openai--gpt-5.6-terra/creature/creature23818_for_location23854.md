At first, it can seem that no one lives there at all.

The streets are too wide for the number of footfalls they carry. A door curtain stirs, then settles. A hand appears briefly above a roof garden to turn a silver leaf toward a vent. Far up on a bridge, something small and wrapped in dark cloth crosses from one rock-face house to another and is gone. The city has the watchfulness of an inhabited place, but not its usual excess: no crowds, no animals, no scatter of incidental life.

Then a bell sounds beneath the square, and the doors begin to open.

They call themselves, in the oldest surviving phrase, *the held breath*. It is not a poetic name to them. It is a statement of circumstance. They live in the exhalation of stone. Their first songs say that their ancestors came down from a world where water fell from above and pooled in hollows, where the air was thin and cold enough to make vapor. Most no longer believe this literally. Children learn the old words for river, rain, foam, drowning, and tear as they learn the names of legendary beasts: useful for stories, interesting in old inscriptions, impossible to picture properly.

They are people in the recognizable civic sense before they are people in any bodily sense. They build houses, keep accounts, argue over boundaries, raise children, mourn their dead, compose songs, repair roads that lead nowhere anymore. But their bodies have made a long accommodation with the hollow.

Their skin is not soft. It has the matte, fine-grained surface of fired clay, though it flexes over joints and folds gently around the mouth and eyes. In childhood it is pale—bone, ash, the yellow of old salt—but through adulthood it darkens in patches from mineral dust and heat exposure. Older people often have a faint glaze on the backs of their hands, along their cheeks, around the collarbones: a clear or smoky crystalline bloom that can be polished away but usually is not. To remove it too often is considered vain, and perhaps unlucky. The bloom is proof that a person has remained sealed against the dry air.

They do not sweat. They have no need to. Their bodies store heat in dense inner tissues and release it slowly through the nightless cycles of the cavern. During a strong vent-season, when the air becomes hot enough to soften wax and make mica panels hum, they sit with their backs to stone walls and allow the heat to pass through them. A visitor might mistake this for illness or prayer. It is neither exactly. It is feeding.

They eat, though sparingly. The thick leaves and mineral fruits of the gardens are shaved, ground, heated, and mixed into dense cakes. Dark red flower powder is used as spice, medicine, pigment, and, in lean times, food. The black reeds in the civic square are not eaten except during funerals; their fibrous cores are bitter, gritty, and nutritionally complete in a way that makes them sacred. A household’s stores are not jars of grain but sealed tiles, wrapped packets of dried plant flesh, powdered salts, and little wax-stoppered bottles of pale sap that glows faintly in the dark.

But the greater part of their nourishment comes through breathing.

Their lungs are broad and layered, less like bellows than like beds of folded mineral tissue. The warm gases from the vents contain compounds that would poison or suffocate an outsider. To the inhabitants, properly diluted and filtered through frond screens, these gases are sustenance. They sit in vent rooms—low chambers lined with porous ceramic—and breathe in slow practiced rhythms. The oldest people can tell, by taste alone, whether a draft has passed through iron-bearing stone, salt seams, deep ash pockets, or the old black strata beneath the square.

Their speech is shaped by this world. They do not waste breath.

In ordinary conversation they use soft, close sounds: clicks of the tongue, rubbed consonants, brief low tones that do not carry far. Families develop private hand-signs for affection, warning, irritation, and daily instruction. A parent may correct a child across a crowded roof garden with two fingers turned inward. Lovers may conduct an entire argument while sitting side by side at a vent grate, saying nothing aloud.

Loud speech is reserved for deliberate occasions: public law, mourning, kiln work, songs meant to travel. To speak a full name in the wrong street is rude. To speak it under the central arch is dangerous, not because the stone is believed to be malicious, but because it remembers too well. A name thrown into the arch can return in pieces for hours: a syllable from a terrace, another from a drain, the final breath from somewhere high in the vault where no person could stand. Children dare one another to call out their own names there. Adults pretend not to hear.

They wear layered clothing even in the heat. The cloth is woven from the fibrous blades of the reed forests, softened with plant wax and rubbed with dust until it falls like heavy linen. Most garments are close at the throat and wrists, loose around the body, and wrapped with cords to prevent the wind from entering. The outer layers are usually dark—charcoal, brown-red, muted blue-green—but the inner cloths can be startlingly bright. Copper-yellow, violet, mineral white, the luminous rust color of flower pollen. These colors are not often shown in public. A glimpse of bright lining when someone climbs a stair is intimate in the way an uncovered face might be elsewhere.

Their eyes are adapted to the chamber’s poor and variable light. The irises tend toward dark metallic shades: hematite red, mica gray, oil-black, occasional pale gold. In the violet glow of the ceiling veins, their pupils widen until the eyes look almost entirely black. They can see the slow turning of leaves at a distance, the minute shiver in a rope before a vent shifts, the difference between harmless dust and the first pale crystals of a dangerous salt fall.

They are especially attentive to shadows.

Because there are no animals, no insects, no random scurrying life, motion has meaning. A moving shadow in an empty alley may be a person. It may be a loosened frond. It may be a dust devil passing under a bridge. Or it may be something that should not be moving.

This has made them cautious without making them fearful. Their children are taught a catalog of causes. Before they are trusted to cross the upper terraces alone, they must be able to identify the shape of a pulley shadow, the flicker of kiln flame through mica, the crawling darkness cast by a wind-tossed reed crown, the brief dart of a person running along an unseen street. To mistake one for another is embarrassing. To announce a shadow without a cause brings the neighborhood out of their houses with lamps and stone tools.

There have been such alarms. No one agrees on what they found.

Their population is small not because the city is failing, though it is old and has lost whole districts to settling stone, but because new people arrive slowly. Birth is not hidden, exactly, but it is private in a way that work and death are not. A child begins as a pale, sealed thing grown in a heated clay cradle: part body, part seed, part carefully tended inheritance. Families contribute mineral salts, powdered reed pith, shavings from their own crystalline bloom, and breath captured during particular vent cycles. The cradles are kept in the warmest interior rooms, turned daily, sung to, and watched for hairline fractures.

When a child emerges, they are small, stiff-limbed, and nearly transparent at the ears and fingertips. For several years they must be protected from strong drafts. Their first task is not speech but balance: learning how to stand in moving air, how to feel a pressure change in the soles of their feet, how to close the folds of the throat against grit. They grow quickly at first, then slowly for decades.

No one is considered fully adult until they have spent one vent-season alone in a shuttered watch platform and returned with a correct record of every major change in the chamber’s breath.

The dead do not leave the city.

A body eventually grows too dense to remain flexible. The hands stiffen. The throat loses its close sounds. Crystalline bloom spreads over the chest and temples. Some people remain lucid for years in this state, sitting in warm rooms while relatives bring them work to inspect, disputes to settle, children to teach. When they can no longer move safely, they choose a final place: often a wall niche, a garden retaining wall, the foundation of a house, or one of the old roads leading toward the unused quarters.

There they are sealed in mineral plaster and allowed to become part of the city.

A dead grandparent may reinforce a stair. An old potter may rest beneath the floor of the kiln court where she worked. A beloved teacher may become a pale seam in the wall outside the schoolroom, their name marked in glassy black script. The inhabitants do not think of this as being buried. Burial, in the old stories, belongs to earth and dampness. Their dead are incorporated.

There are people in the city who are almost entirely wall.

The civic square is where the inhabitants gather when they must become visible to one another. Most of the time, they live in small household clusters: perhaps six people in one rock-backed court, perhaps three generations sharing kilns and roof gardens, perhaps a solitary keeper in an upper watch house who comes down only for supplies and council days. But when the stone bell begins to sing, curtains lift throughout the city.

Gardeners come with their hands powdered brown from the red cups. Kiln workers come with soot in the creases of their faces. Children arrive in hoods too large for them, each holding a cord tied to an elder’s wrist so they will not be lost in the draft. The old come slowly, their joints clicking faintly like cooling pottery. They stand among the black reeds while hot gas moves under the paving, and they listen to the bell’s granular note.

They can hear more in it than warning.

A long, rough vibration means a deep vent has opened beneath the southern terraces. A broken pulse means a blockage in one of the old gas channels. A note that begins low and rises is a dangerous sign: the chamber is heating faster than the ceiling can shed its stored warmth. The whole city may have to close itself for days, sealing doors with waxed cloth, sleeping in the deepest rooms, living on stored sap and cold cakes while the hollow bellows around them.

In such times, they become almost invisible.

Their gardens fold. Their shutters lock. Their mica panels are turned inward. The streets lie empty beneath storms of dry pollen. Yet behind the low doors, people are awake: counting breaths, tending children, grinding salts, listening to the groan of the roof, pressing their palms to the stone to feel whether the city is still holding.

They do not think of the hollow as dead.

It was once a lung, perhaps. It is still breathing. It warms them, feeds them, threatens them, remembers their voices, receives their dead, and surrounds every room they have ever known. They are not its masters. They are not quite its prisoners.

They are one of the things it has learned to keep.
