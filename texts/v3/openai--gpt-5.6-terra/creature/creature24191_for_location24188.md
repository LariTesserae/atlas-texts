What lives on the plateau is difficult to see because it does not remain assembled for long.

It is not hidden in the shelters exactly. The shelters are used, but not inhabited in the warm, continuous way a burrow or house is inhabited. A dark opening may contain a bowl, a hammerstone, a dry braid of mineral fiber, and the shallow indentation where something once rested. Then rain comes. Water gathers beneath the slabs. The hollow fills with low singing. By morning, the thing that used the shelter may be somewhere else entirely, distributed through a wet wall, or moving as a darkening across a field of pale stone.

The oldest people of the plateau—if people is the nearest word—are called, in the few marks they have left, the *held ones*.

They are held together by local agreements.

A body begins where water has learned a particular route through stone. It may have a spine of fitted black plates, a ribcage of hollow mineral tubes, limbs made from bundled crystal fibers, joints packed with wet sand that never spills. Its head, if it has one, is often a smooth pale mass without a face. On that surface appear lines of moisture, temporary dark grooves, small apertures that open and close with the sound of water entering a drain.

But this description is only true when a held one chooses, or is required, to walk.

At other times it is a stain beneath a translucent pavement. A sequence of drips traveling up a cliff. A cold pressure moving from one singing stone to another. Sometimes a person-sized arrangement of columns can be seen crossing a distant ridge in the rain, each column lifting, separating, and setting itself down with grave patience. By the time anyone could reach the ridge, there are only ordinary stones there, wet and upright among the others.

They do not wear cloth. They sometimes bind themselves with braided mineral fiber, but the bindings are not garments. They are reins, braces, memory-lines. A held one may fasten a cord from its body to a cairn before entering a region where gravity has changed its mind. It may loop itself through a crack in the ground while it leans over a basin. It may leave one end tied to a shelter wall for years. The cord does not rot. It slowly becomes stone, and after that another held one may use it.

Their tools are simple because their work is patient.

They use hammerstones to persuade a fracture rather than force it. They use wedges to redirect water by the width of a finger. They collect particular kinds of rain in shallow bowls: the first hard rain before lightning, the rain that falls upward, rain from beneath a suspended island, rain that has crossed a black-glass slope and carries a later sky within it. These are not merely resources. Each behaves differently when introduced to a crack, a channel, a singing circle, or the hollow interior of a body.

A held one can repair itself with the correct water.

It will sit beside a dark hollow and slowly unmake one of its arms into a scatter of wet stones. Then it will draw water from the hollow through a carved groove. Over many hours, crystals rise along the groove, forming first a delicate white outline, then a stronger lattice, then a new arm. No movement occurs until the final joint closes. When it stands, the old discarded stones may be gathered and fitted into a shelter wall.

Nothing is wasted because nothing is entirely separate.

The plateau’s dwellers do not seem to distinguish sharply between body, tool, dwelling, path, and weather. A low wall may have been built by hands, but after enough storms it becomes a hand’s continuation: a device for catching runoff, directing frost, making a certain kind of stone available at the right season. A singing circle is an instrument, a boundary marker, a calendar, and perhaps a mouth. The notes it produces are not simply heard by the held ones. The notes alter them. A deep enough chord can cause their joints to lock. Another can loosen the mineral bindings in their bodies, allowing them to pass through narrow cracks or dissolve briefly into flowing water.

When the circles answer one another across the fog, the plateau is not empty between them.

It is speaking its population into coherence.

There are smaller lives as well, though they are less like persons.

In the seams beneath the luminous stone are clear filamentary things that grow only while water moves over them. They resemble roots until they are touched, at which point they withdraw with a faint ringing sound. They may be young held ones, or the sensory organs of the plateau itself, or neither. They gather around fresh fractures and abandoned offerings. Where they have been active, the stone later shows fine branching marks, like maps of rivers that never reached the surface.

In black hollows live weightless knots of darkness. They are seen only when lightning crosses nearby, when they briefly become denser than the pool around them. A knot may rise as a twist in the water and cling to the underside of an overhang. Some follow held ones at a distance. Some enter shelters after rain and remain there, making the interior colder. No one leaves offerings to them. Yet the three-stone shrines are often built where such knots have passed, as though to mark a crossing that should not be taken casually.

There are also the rain-figures.

These are not present during every storm. When they form, they appear first as upright absences in the downpour: regions where the rain curves around an invisible standing shape. Gradually, a shape gathers—tall, narrow, with shoulders suggested by veils of water and a head crowned by spray. They walk downhill even where the slope runs upward. Their feet do not strike the ground. Instead, each step opens a brief bright depression in the stone, which fills at once and leaves behind a new basin.

The held ones avoid them.

Not fearfully. More with the grave courtesy given to old machinery still functioning beyond its intended age.

After rain-figures pass, the rules of a place may have changed. A cliff that once drew water upward may let it fall. A familiar road may lead to a different valley. An echo may begin arriving before its sound. The dwellers respond by moving cairns, changing the angles of drainage stones, re-cutting the grooves in the singing circles. Much of their labor is not building but re-acknowledging: ensuring that the plateau’s local rules are correctly remembered where they occur.

This may be why the groove-carved stones differ from one another.

Each arrangement is a record of a condition.

One stone says: here, weight goes toward the blue seam.

Another: here, do not speak after the third thunder.

Another: here, carry warm objects in the left hand.

Another: here, if your shadow remains behind you, leave it a bowl of water before continuing.

The held ones read these grooves through touch and vibration. They place their palms—or the equivalent surfaces of their bodies—against the stone while water runs through it. The resulting chord travels through their mineral frameworks. For a while afterward, they know which way is down.

They do not appear numerous. A traveler might cross ten ridges and see none. But the evidence of them is everywhere: a drainage ditch cleared after a storm; a new wedge beneath an old overhang; a fallen slab turned just enough to bridge a crack; warm pebbles added to an offering space; a shelter entrance oriented away from a wind that did not exist yesterday.

Sometimes, at blue evening, one can find several of them gathered around a singing circle after the water has nearly drained away.

They stand far apart, each beside a different stone. They are almost indistinguishable from the columns and slabs around them. No fire burns. No voices carry. One may have a body of dark glass, another white chalk, another layered gray stone with silver water moving slowly under its surface. They wait through the final sequence of drips.

Each drip produces one note.

Each note enters the ground.

At the end, when the last basin empties, all of them incline toward the center of the circle—not as an act of worship, perhaps, but as a structure settling under a change of pressure.

Then they begin to come apart.

A shoulder becomes rain. A hand opens into pebbles. A smooth pale head splits along a seam and reveals flowing darkness within. One by one, they leave their upright shapes and enter the channels, cracks, walls, and pools of the plateau. By dawn there may be no sign of the gathering except a pattern of wetness on the stone, and the quiet impression that the country has remembered how to hold itself together for another day.
