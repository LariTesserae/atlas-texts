What lives there is not one population but a treaty of creatures, most of them having learned the same lesson: nothing stays dry, nothing stays clean, and nothing belongs entirely to people.

The gulls are the most visible citizens. They have ceased to be coastal birds in any ordinary sense. The sea may be hidden behind barriers and tanks, but they know it is there, and they know the schedules of the city better than many workers do. They arrive at the fish-processing depot before dawn, circle above the canteen exhaust at shift change, follow garbage compactors through the freight district, and wait on the roofs of chemical warehouses for heat to rise from the vents. Their feathers are gray with soot at the edges. Some have oil-dark bellies. They nest in places that were designed to exclude weather: behind signage, beneath solar panels, inside the sheltered angles of crane gantries.

In the oldest parts of the refinery, gulls have become almost white only by comparison with everything around them. When they lift together from a roof, the city briefly reveals the scale of its silence: thousands of wings opening at once, then the sound thinning into the fog.

Cormorants live closer to the water system. They occupy the pipe bridges, sluice gates, stormwater outfalls, and the black handrails around settling basins. Their wings are held open not merely to dry but to gather the weak warmth radiating from the works. They dive into channels that carry rainwater, brackish seepage, cooling discharge, and things no one can name from looking at them. Fish live in those channels because fish have always lived wherever water is allowed to continue moving. The cormorants live because the fish do.

There are fish with silver sides, little mullet perhaps, and darker things whose names are not used much anymore. They gather below warm outlets in winter and become sluggish in the heat. They know the tide through buried pipes and opening gates. In some places they can travel from an inland drainage ditch to the estuary without ever crossing what a person would call open water. Their world is a maze of grates, culverts, concrete throats, and sudden pools beneath highways.

The crabs understand this world best. They live under slabs, inside drains, among discarded tires half-full of black rainwater. At night they emerge in numbers from seams in the pavement. They are not large, but they are old-looking: armored, deliberate, colored like oxidized machinery. They climb the lower walls of pumping stations and gather at the bases of floodgates when the tide is turning. Workers occasionally find them in stairwells, beneath lockers, inside the shallow wells around utility covers. No one is surprised. The city is built over their water, and the water remembers every opening.

Frogs live wherever the pumps fail to keep up.

They appear after storms in ditches that were dry the week before, in underpasses where tire marks end abruptly in standing water, in the sunken courtyards of abandoned workshops. Their voices begin separately—one pulse, another answering—and then become a dense vibration spread across the flats. From an apartment window, it can be mistaken for a generator. From close up, it is stranger: a multitude making a single low electrical sound from their throats.

Mosquitoes live here too, though the wind often carries them away. Their larvae tremble in oil-sheened puddles. They hatch from water caught in rusted drums, from clogged roof gutters, from the shallow trays beneath air-conditioning units. They feed on dogs, cats, shift workers waiting for buses, birds asleep on railings. They are too small to be seen as residents, but they are among the city’s most faithful inhabitants. They require only a little trapped water, and the city has spent a century providing it.

Rats follow the warmth.

They live in the service corridors under warehouses and in the insulation around old pipework. They run beneath raised floors and through cable conduits. They know which buildings are empty only in appearance, which loading bays still receive food, which cafeterias throw scraps behind locked gates. Their coats are often damp. Their paws are stained with soot. Some are pale from living indoors, in the ceilings and ducts of offices that have not been occupied in years.

They are not bold in the way city rats elsewhere are bold. Here they are cautious and industrial. They wait for pumps to change rhythm. They freeze at the hiss of a pressure release. They move when the machinery masks their movement.

The feral dogs are quieter than the rats.

They travel in small bands across the truck lots and along the rail embankments. Their fur is short from heat and wet weather, often patchy around the shoulders. Some wear old collars with no tags. Some have reflective scraps tied around their necks by people who feed them near the bus depots. They sleep beneath trailers, behind stacks of pallets, in the dry pockets under elevated pipelines. They drink from puddles but prefer the clear trickle that leaks from air-conditioning units and municipal taps.

They do not usually bark. Barking carries too far in a city of hard surfaces. Instead they communicate by stopping, looking, and continuing on. Their territory is mapped by shade, food, and the places where floodwater rises last.

Cats belong to the docks, the workshops, and the narrow strips of sheltered ground alongside boiler houses. They are lean, damp-footed, and territorial. They hunt rats among the conduits and birds in the rafters. The oldest cats sleep on warm concrete above buried steam lines. They have torn ears and pale eyes that catch the sodium light. Workers leave food for them in corners that everyone agrees not to mention. A cat colony can outlast a subcontractor, a warehouse tenant, even a whole section of the rail yard.

Above them all, kestrels live on the pylons.

They perch where the electricity crosses the city in high steel arches. From there they watch the open lots, the ballast beside the tracks, the roofs where pigeons gather. Their presence gives the industrial emptiness a kind of discipline. A bird hangs in place against the wind, motionless except for the fine correction of its wings, then drops between warehouses and returns with something small and struggling in its claws.

There are snakes in the rail ballast, though few people see them. They prefer the warmth held by stone and steel. They move through the weeds that do not quite count as weeds—pale salt-tolerant stems in fenced-off margins, mats of algae, green threads growing from drainage seams. Their prey comes to them: frogs, rats, nestlings fallen from low pipe racks. When floodwater rises, they retreat upward into stacked materials, broken conduits, and the spaces under loading platforms. Sometimes one is found coiled around a valve box, and the valve box is left alone until it chooses to leave.

The marshes beyond the levees hold the older inhabitants.

Herons stand there with their patience unchanged by refineries, container cranes, or the violet shine of night. They wait beside warm outflows where the water does not freeze and where the fish gather in unnatural plenty. Egrets pick their way through plastic and reeds. Wading birds rest on mudbanks that appear only at low tide. In winter, migrations pause over the city because the industrial water is warm, because the tidal flats still feed them, because the roofs and embankments offer high ground when storms come.

They do not see a ruined coast. They see channels, shallows, nesting ledges, thermal currents, and food.

The people who live there have become a kind of animal of the same environment.

They live on higher floors, above the flood marks. They keep shoes by doors, though the shoes are often wet anyway. They have learned which bus shelters leak, which alleys flood first, which apartment stairwells smell of brine after a hard rain. Their homes contain raised shelves, plastic bins, dehumidifiers that never quite catch up, towels that never fully dry. They keep tools for rust: wire brushes, oil, spare bolts, electrical tape, sealant.

Many work with the city’s great circulatory system. They inspect pumps, test water, weld pipes, drive tankers, repair electrical cabinets, clear grates before storms, read gauges in lit rooms above dark basins. Others work in cafés that serve night shifts, pharmacies that stock antifungal cream and waterproof bandages, repair shops where every vehicle carries the same crust of salt beneath its wheel arches.

Their lives are timed by shifts, rain bands, tides, deliveries, sirens, and the occasional news that a gate has jammed somewhere upstream.

Children live there too, though they are less visible than the birds. They walk along covered paths to schools on the better-drained blocks. They know not to touch canal water. They know that a puddle can be deep, that an open drain can swallow a ball, that the refinery flare means something is wrong or something is normal depending on its color and duration. They collect gull feathers, crab shells, fragments of green sea glass, and bright pieces of industrial plastic polished smooth by water.

Some of them grow up wanting to leave. Some leave and return. Some cannot imagine a city with trees because trees, to them, belong to pictures, parks beyond the highway, and places where the ground is trusted.

And beneath all these lives are the things that are not alive but behave as if they are: pumps, gates, pipes, drains, tanks, warning lights, steam vents, switchgear, rail couplings. They make up the city’s deep body. Around them, animals nest, feed, molt, breed, drown, survive. People maintain them, curse them, rely upon them. The machinery creates warmth, shelter, noise, water movement, and waste; the living things occupy every consequence.

What lives there, finally, is adaptation.

A gull with blackened feet. A crab in a storm drain. A heron beside an outfall. A dog asleep beneath a fuel tanker. A child listening for the flood siren. A maintenance worker tightening bolts above rising water. A cat on a steam pipe. Fish turning in the warm dark under concrete.

The city is inhabited by whatever can accept that dry land is temporary.
