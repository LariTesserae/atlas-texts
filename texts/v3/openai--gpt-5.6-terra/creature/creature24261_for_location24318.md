What lives there is not a single kingdom of beasts, but a long chain of workers.

The first life is underground.

Beneath the shale crust, in the warm porous layers where mineral water circulates, live the pressure-colonies: masses of pale, boneless organisms packed into the rock as densely as roots. They are not worms, though they tunnel; not coral, though they build; not fungus, though they spread through a place by digestion. Each individual is small and simple, a muscular sac lined with crystalline teeth. In aggregate they become something enormous.

They drink slurry from the heated stone. They sort it internally: iron-bearing mud one way, salts another, water another. Their bodies pulse in coordinated waves, driven by chemical signals that travel through the colony more slowly than sound. When a vein has been exhausted, thousands of them loosen their grip and migrate through the pores of the earth, leaving behind hollow channels with walls glazed smooth as fired ceramic.

The noises in the ground come from them.

A deep thump is a chamber emptying. A long groan is a colony shifting against a fault. The turbine hum is not a machine but millions of ciliated membranes beating in sequence, forcing warm vapor upward through a network older than the visible landscape. Their castings accumulate at vents. Over centuries they become black chimneys, shelves, terraces, and towers ribbed with mineral deposits. Some of the structures are large enough to shelter herds in their lee. None were intended as shelter.

At the surface live the plate-crawlers, the creatures that make the moving sediment belts.

Seen individually, they are no larger than a hand. Each has a low oval shell made of overlapping dark plates, a blind white mouth beneath its body, and dozens of delicate gripping limbs that can either walk or lock fast to a neighbor. They do not travel alone unless ill or dying. A crawler joins its front edge to the rear edge of another, then another, building a living mat that can stretch for miles.

The mat advances because every body is eating.

The creatures rasp the uppermost skin from the stone: dust, salt bloom, spores, dead lichen, microscopic mineral films. They take in grit and leave behind polished tracks marked by countless parallel scratches. Their shells absorb warmth. At intervals, an entire belt pauses at a crack in the ground, breaks apart, and piles itself over the opening. The bodies at the bottom feed on the heat and vapor; the bodies above wait. After days, the colony reforms and continues.

They have no leaders, but their movement has the gravity of a decision.

Predators follow the belts. There are low, folded things that lie among slate drifts and imitate discarded slabs. Their hides are layered in insulating scales, each scale edged with dust-colored fuzz. In stillness they are nearly perfect pieces of terrain. Only the slight opening and closing of their heat vents reveals them.

When a sediment belt passes too near, the predator unfolds.

Its body is all hinge and leverage. The rocklike upper surface lifts; beneath it is a pale, warm cavity ringed with hooked limbs. It sweeps up a section of living pavement, folds shut, and waits for the captured plate-crawlers to cook against the heat of its own gut. These hunters do not chase. Their world has taught them that motion is costly and the ground eventually brings food to every patient mouth.

Around the fumaroles stand the lantern-grazers.

From a distance they appear tall and ceremonial: thin bodies rooted in the dark, cloaked in soot-black membranes, always arranged at respectful distances from one another. They are not truly standing. Their lower limbs are sunk into loose ground, probing for heat and moisture. Their upper limbs are long, jointed spars that hold open or closed the membranous sails around their bodies.

Inside each sail are networks of luminous tissue.

The light is not made for seeing. It is a byproduct of feeding. The lantern-grazers host colonies of heat-loving symbionts in chambers beneath their skins. The symbionts consume chemical energy from vapor and produce slow electrical discharges. Those discharges travel in visible currents through the creature’s flesh: orange in old, iron-rich vents; green where copper salts rise; a hard blue-white in the rare fields where the ground is charged by storm.

The grazers expose their light only when needed.

They open their membranes to signal a change in vent pressure, to warn others that a fumarole is poisoning the air, or to attract the tiny drifting animals that feed on their shed skin. In dry times, they close themselves tightly and enter a waking stillness that can last months. The ground warms them. Their symbionts sustain them. Dust gathers on their membranes until they seem to have become part of the landscape.

When rain comes, they move.

Not quickly, but unmistakably. Their buried limbs withdraw with wet sucking sounds. They lean away from exhausted vents and cross the plateau in slow, deliberate processions toward fresh vapor fields. The dim internal lights sway beneath their skins. From a ridge, one might mistake them for a line of furnaces being carried through the dark.

At condensation hollows live the reedshells, the softest creatures of the plateau.

They are small, six-limbed animals with waxy backs and feathery mouths. During dry periods they fold themselves between the roots of black reeds, nearly indistinguishable from dead leaves. In rain they emerge by the hundreds, turning their mouths upward. Their body surfaces collect droplets and channel them into reservoirs beneath their ribs. Many other animals depend on them, though few hunt them directly. When threatened, a reedshell ruptures one of its reservoirs, throwing a bitter mineral spray that clings to tongues and membranes.

Some larger species cultivate them.

The broad-backed resonators—the herd animals whose calls are felt more than heard—visit the wet hollows during migration. Their bodies are shaped like low hills: wide shoulders, thick haunches, heads held near the ground. Their eyes are sealed beneath armored brows and may be vestigial. They navigate by the vibration of their own calls and the returning tremor of the land.

Each has chambers extending through its chest, throat, and skull. A herd sends slow notes into the stone: pulses so deep that they travel beneath escarpments and across buried cavities. Other herd members receive the echoes through their feet. In this way they know where the ground is thin, where warm water is moving, where another herd has recently fed, and where the pressure-colonies are active enough to make the earth dangerous.

Their food is mostly lichen, waxleaf growth, and mineral crust. They graze with broad, rasping mouths and leave long pale swaths across the dark ground. But they also carry reedshells from hollow to hollow in the folds of their fur and the mud beneath their feet. The small creatures feed on scraps caught in the herd’s hide; the herd benefits from the water they retain. Neither could be said to understand the arrangement. It has simply persisted.

The resonators are followed by the hinged-faced scavengers.

These are elegant, narrow animals, high on jointed legs, with heads that open in sections rather than jaws. Their faces are built from layered plates that can peel apart like shutters, exposing different feeding tools beneath: a delicate picking mouth for lichen, blunt crushing surfaces for shell, fine hooks for carrion, sensory fronds that taste heat in the air.

They move carefully because they are always listening.

A dead resonator may feed an entire procession of scavengers for weeks. They do not fight over it. Each arrives in a particular order: first the small face-hinges that remove parasites; then larger ones that open the hide; then tall, dark individuals that reach deep into the body cavity and consume the warm organs before they cool. By the end, even the bones are reduced—not eaten, but scraped clean and carried away by mineral-gathering juveniles.

Their nests are strange arrangements of polished bone, black reeds, and vent-glass. They build them in cracks where warm air rises. The eggs are not laid in the nest but embedded in the walls, each one coated with a paste of mineral-rich saliva. The young hatch blind and already able to detect the tremors of approaching herds.

Above the plateau, within the low vapor sheets, drift the sailwings.

They are rarely seen clearly. Most people would know them first as a single heavy wingbeat, followed by the faint displacement of damp air. Their wings are immense but slow-moving, stretched over fingerlike ribs and coated in a velvet substance that catches dust. Their bodies are small relative to their span, dense with warm fluids. They do not fly in the ordinary sense. They rise on thermal columns, sink through colder vapor, and steer by adjusting the temperature of their wing membranes.

The sailwings feed on airborne life: spores, vapor-midges, shed fragments of lantern-grazer membrane, and the warm exhalations of larger animals. Sometimes they descend to drink from a fresh rain pool, folding their wings around themselves like a black tent. In those moments, their undersides reveal faint constellations of bioluminescent pores. The lights are used to communicate through fog. A single moving pattern may call a mate from miles away or warn a flock that the thermal field has failed.

Their dead do not often reach the ground. The plate-crawlers find them first.

In the deepest basins, where the vapor canopy lowers and darkness becomes almost material, live the heat-bearers.

They are burrowing animals, long and segmented, each the length of a person or more. Their outer skins are black, padded, and almost lightless. Beneath the skin, along both sides of the body, run swollen translucent sacs containing hot fluid and colonies of glowing microbes. The creatures charge these sacs at active vents, then carry the stored heat across cold or exhausted regions.

They migrate in lines.

A line may be a family, a mating group, or merely several animals following an old thermal trail. They keep their sides close enough that each senses the warmth of the next. Their lights are covered most of the time. When they climb a slope or cross exposed ground, the skins along their flanks thin and the light leaks through: dim beads of amber, blue, and red descending in patient sequence.

The heat-bearers are important in ways no other animal appears to know. Their burrows ventilate the deep sediment. Their waste inoculates new vent fields with mineral-feeding bacteria. Their abandoned skins become shelter for reedshells and nesting material for scavengers. And when one dies in a narrow crack, its heat sacs burst slowly over several days, warming the stone around it. Plants grow there afterward. A small green-black oasis begins around the corpse.

There are also things that may not properly be animals.

On certain cliffs, beneath layers of soot and salt, hang the listening fans: broad mineral membranes stretched between stone spines. They open and close with pressure changes. They collect dust, moisture, and electrical charge. At times they emit a low tone that causes nearby plate-crawlers to turn or lantern-grazers to unfold their sails. Whether they are ancient colonial organisms, organs of the underground pressure-colonies, or merely an arrangement of rock that life has learned to use is impossible to tell.

Near old vents grow the mirror lichens, whose silver surfaces tilt toward the faintest available brightness. They seem vegetal until the rain comes. Then the whole mat contracts and shifts, exposing darker feeding tissue beneath. Small lights move through it. A lichen field can slowly relocate over the course of a season, following warmth beneath the rock.

And in the most active fumarole districts are the chimney forests: towers built from the hardened waste of underground colonies and inhabited by thousands of mutually dependent forms. Warm vapor rises through their central throats. Tiny filter-creatures cling to the inner walls. Mosslike veils catch condensed water. Blind beetle-shaped grazers harvest the veils. Narrow predators live in the upper cracks, waiting for grazers to climb toward the heat. The forest grows a finger-width each year, then collapses suddenly when its foundations are hollowed out. Its fall can be heard for miles: not a crash, but a long, granular settling, as though a building had remembered it was only earth.

Nothing there lives independently for very long.

The tall luminous beings require the vents. The vents are maintained by the buried colonies. The colonies benefit from fractures opened by heat-bearers and resonator herds. The herds depend on lichen, reedshells, and the wet places shaped by vapor. The predators rely on the belts. The belts follow mineral films renewed by rain, dust, and the labor of the unseen ground.

Even decay is a kind of migration.

A carcass becomes heat, food, spores, salts, moving plates, new roots in a fracture. A sealed vent becomes a cold hollow. A cold hollow gathers rain. Rain wakes the lichen. Lichen draws the herd. The herd’s footfalls crack the shale. Warmth rises through the crack.

Then something beneath, which has been waiting without sleep, begins to work again.
