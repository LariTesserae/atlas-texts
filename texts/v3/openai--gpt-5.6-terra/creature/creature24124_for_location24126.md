They call themselves nothing that can be said aloud.

Among themselves, identity is not carried in a name but in a sequence of touches: knuckle to stone, thumb to wrist, two fingertips laid against the breastbone. A person can be introduced by the rhythm of their taps, by the particular shape of a scar, by the tool they have worn smooth, by the mineral hue gathered beneath their nails. Surface scholars have called them the Stillfolk, the Basalt People, the Underplainers. None of these terms offends them, because none of them means anything.

They are human enough, at first and from a distance.

They stand upright. They have hands. They make tools and arrange the dead and gather around warmth. Their faces possess the expected architecture: brow, nose, mouth, two eyes set forward. But each familiar feature has been altered by long agreement with the underplain.

Their skin is dry, gray-brown, and finely plated, not scaled exactly but divided into small mineralized polygons like mud baked without ever having been wet. In youth these plates are flexible and dull. With age they harden along the forearms, shoulders, shins, and cheekbones, taking on colors from the ground: iron red, charcoal black, pale green-white, blue at the joints. The oldest inhabitants look less clothed than assembled. Their bodies bear accretions of place. A shoulder may be capped in mica. A heel may ring like ceramic against the stone. A grandmother’s hands may have become so dense that she no longer uses them for fine work, only for anchoring herself against a wall while younger hands shape blades beneath hers.

They do not sweat. Their lips crack and heal in thin black lines. Their blood, if cut, emerges slowly and darkly, with a metallic smell; it dries into hard bright crusts that are collected rather than wiped away. A small amount is valuable. Mixed with mineral paste, it seals seams in ovens and domes. A person may carry traces of their ancestors in the repairs to their own shelter.

Their hair is sparse and wiry, usually growing in pale bundles from the crown and forearms. Many shave it away with obsidian, not for cleanliness but because loose hair is considered a form of noise. It catches on stone, drags through dust, brushes another body unexpectedly. The very old sometimes let theirs grow. In the violet light, their heads appear crowned with a trembling mineral filament.

Their eyes are the most disquieting thing about them. They are large but not expressive in the surface sense. The irises are almost black, ringed with faint reflective material that catches blue mineral light. They do not blink often. Their vision is adapted not to brightness but to outline, persistence, and change. A surface visitor may believe one of them has been staring motionlessly into empty dark for hours, when in fact they are reading minute shifts in luminescent seams half a valley away: a herd crossing a ridge, a predator unclasping its limbs from a ceiling slope, a crack opening where no crack was yesterday.

They are poor at seeing rapid gestures. A waved hand can go unnoticed. A moving mouth means little. But they recognize a shifted pebble, a freshly disturbed patch of shell dust, the difference between an old footprint and one made after the last deep settling of stone. Their children learn tracks before faces.

They are not silent because they lack voices.

They can speak. Their throats produce a low, dry language full of clicks, rubbed consonants, and tones so quiet that a listener must press close to hear them. It is used in sealed shelters, in the mouths of sleeping chambers, beside the dying, and occasionally between lovers. Spoken language is intimate enough that hearing a stranger’s unguarded voice is considered almost an intrusion upon the body.

Outside, they tap.

They tap on the ground with fingertips. They tap blade handles against their teeth. They tap messages along bridge rails and fault walls. A practiced group can hold a conversation across a broad chamber through its stone floor, each person crouched with one palm pressed down to receive the faint returning vibration. The language is slow, but slowness is not a disadvantage to them. Most things worth saying remain true for a long time.

Their first rule, the one taught before tool use, is that stone is never deaf.

Children are raised in niches around warm shafts. They are not born often. No one can say exactly how birth occurs, because it is not a public event and does not follow a regular interval. A pair, or sometimes a larger household, will begin carrying particular materials into a sealed chamber: soft shell, powdered salt-metal, shavings from their own plates, a warmed stone kept close to the body for many months. Then, eventually, a new small person appears.

Infants are pale and nearly soft, their limbs folded tightly, their eyes sealed beneath translucent mineral lids. They make no crying sound. Instead they vibrate. Held against an adult chest or laid upon warm stone, they produce a faint persistent tremor that can be felt through the hands. A household knows an infant is hungry because its vibration changes frequency. It knows an infant is frightened because the trembling becomes irregular. To soothe one, adults do not sing. They rest their palms against the floor and make a steady answering pattern.

The young are taught to remain still before they are taught to walk.

Not rigidly still. Not obediently still. Attentively still.

They are brought to herd paths and made to lie under ledges until the pale grazers pass around them. They are taught how a wall feels before a predator detaches from it. They learn the smell of a warm fissure becoming too warm, the slight pressure in the teeth that may precede a deep sound from below. They learn that a stone dropped in irritation is never merely a stone dropped in irritation. It is an announcement, possibly to something beyond the range of sight.

Their games are consequently difficult for surface people to recognize as games. Children arrange shell fragments in expanding patterns and return months later to see which have shifted. They compete to identify a companion by footsteps heard through a wall. They hide carved disks in mineral foam, then leave directions expressed only through the orientation of nearby pebbles. They dare each other to approach listening-rod fields—not to enter, never to enter, but to place a fingernail against the nearest rod and report whether it felt warmer than before.

Adults do not approve of this game, yet almost all of them played it.

They are hunters, gatherers, herders, miners, and custodians, but these categories overlap strangely. A grazer herd may belong to a settlement without being fenced, fed, or directed. The people know which bands of crystal it follows and which warm fissures it visits. They remove old individuals only when their armor has become too mineral-heavy for movement, and they harvest what the animal leaves when it settles permanently: plates for building, horn for tools, nests of small seam-creatures from beneath its joints. To kill a healthy grazer for meat would be regarded as wasteful and faintly absurd. Why interrupt a body before it has begun becoming useful stone?

They eat ground shell, roasted mineral foam, the dense warm tissues of the many-limbed kiln things, and soft colonies scraped from the porous slopes. Their food is dry, granular, bitter, and often heated only enough to release its stored chemical energy. They have no word for thirst. They possess a word meaning *the mouth’s need for grinding,* and another meaning *the body’s need for warmth,* but neither maps cleanly onto hunger.

Their ovens are among their most guarded works. A sealed oven is not simply a place to cook. It is a negotiation with heat. The fire inside is made from certain reactive stones and carefully rendered animal oils. It is begun with no open flame. Materials are nested, pressed, and allowed to awaken each other in darkness. An oven keeper may spend a lifetime learning how warm an oven may become before its warmth is considered too eager.

They fear eagerness in all things.

A person who moves too quickly, speaks too loudly, digs too deeply, cuts more stone than needed, gathers heat for pleasure rather than work—such a person is not called wicked. The Stillfolk do not frame danger in moral terms. They call such a person *open-handed*: one who cannot keep the world from taking what they offer it.

Most people never become open-handed. Their culture is built to prevent it.

They decorate themselves sparingly. A polished tooth at the throat may indicate a hunter who found something already dead. A copper disk worn against the wrist may identify an oven keeper. A line of white paste across the eyes marks someone who has spent time near a rod field and returned without incident. The most honored individuals wear no ornament at all except a small broken tool tied behind the neck, where they cannot see it. This signifies that they have deliberately abandoned a work before it was finished.

Such people are consulted when the ground changes.

For there are changes. The underplain does not change often, but when it does, the inhabitants notice long before outsiders do. A blue seam may dim by a shade. A herd may alter its route. The orange veins in the kiln-bodied creatures may pulse more rapidly. A certain old bridge may begin to hold a faint warmth after centuries of coldness. Then settlements reduce their tapping, close unused chambers, extinguish ovens, and carry their children farther from the fault lines.

No one runs. Running is nearly impossible in their bodies and unthinkable in their customs. They move with terrible purpose, carrying tools, shell paste, plates from their ancestors, and the small shaped stones that preserve household histories. Behind them they leave shelters open. Nothing is locked. A sealed door would imply the hope that one might return unchanged.

Their dead are not buried.

At death, a body is placed against a chosen surface: a wall of mineral foam, the underside of a low ridge, a shelf beside a herd path, sometimes a warm chamber within a home. The family removes only those tools that cannot be allowed to mineralize with the body. Everything else remains. Over years, the dead person hardens into the landscape. Their skin darkens, plates thicken, joints fuse. Seam-creatures find their way into the body. Small grazers may scrape at it. A child or successor may eventually emerge from beneath the shoulder plates, or from the hollow behind the ribs, or from a crack opened by internal growth.

This is not resurrection to them. It is continuation without the insult of sameness.

They do not claim descendants as possessions. An emerging young one is said to have “come through” an elder, not “from” them. It may be raised by the household of the mineralized body, or it may wander until another settlement recognizes its tapping pattern. Some individuals carry habits that seem older than their own lives: an unusual preference for a certain trail, a skill with a tool never taught to them, a long stillness before a particular wall.

No one finds this mysterious.

The nearest thing they have to religion is maintenance.

They maintain the routes around rod fields. They renew the copper caps on old stakes. They replace missing disks at fissure mouths. They grind down sharp edges where a panic-stricken animal might cut itself. They visit the warm shafts and listen. They bring offerings not because they expect favor, but because neglect is itself a kind of call.

The black rods trouble them more than death does.

They believe—or perhaps only behave as if they believe—that the rods are not objects but incomplete actions. They are the parts of something below that have reached upward and stopped. To touch one is permitted in certain rites. To strike one is unthinkable. To walk between them would be to give the dark a path through one’s body.

There are old tales, rarely tapped and never spoken, of people who made the dark answer. They are not stories of monsters emerging from pits. The Stillfolk have no great appetite for dramatic punishment. In these tales, the offender heats an oven too strongly, drives stakes too deep, sings into a fault, or shatters a rod with a copper hammer. Then nothing happens for a while.

That is the horror of it.

The offender goes home. The herd continues its route. The mineral light remains unchanged. But small facts begin to fail. Footprints point the wrong way. A warm shaft cools. A child’s tapping returns from the floor in a pattern no child made. Eventually the settlement recognizes that something has begun listening back.

At that point, the people leave.

They leave their homes, tools, dead, and names. They move sideways into the vast dark, carefully, quietly, carrying only what can be carried without making a sound.

And somewhere in the old settlement, perhaps years later, a surface explorer may find rows of little chambers open around a central shaft. A copper-capped stake. A bowl filled with powdered shell. Handprints baked into mineral paste.

They may find a figure seated against a wall, still and heavily plated, one palm resting on the stone floor.

They may think it is dead.

It may be. Or it may be listening, with the infinite patience of the underplain, for a reply that has not yet finished traveling upward.
