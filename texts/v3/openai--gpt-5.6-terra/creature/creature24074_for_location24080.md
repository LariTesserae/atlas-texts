What lives there is adapted less to a forest than to a breathing continent: creatures that have learned to read pressure through wood, to drink from flesh, to survive being carried for miles by wind.

The smallest lives are almost invisible.

Dust-mites, if they can be called mites, move through the mineral flour on exposed root plains. They are translucent and many-jointed, with bellies lined in brushlike cilia. They feed on pollen salts, fungal skins, and the dry shed scales of leaves. When storms come, they lock themselves into the cracks of old cambium in dense, pale mats. Whole populations may emerge afterward, turning the shaded side of a trunk faintly mobile.

There are spore-eaters that resemble handfuls of walking ash. Each is a low, six-legged body carried inside a fur of branching filaments. The filaments catch airborne spores and sort them chemically: edible spores are drawn inward; dangerous ones are burned by tiny exothermic organs along the creature’s back. At night these organs glow in erratic pinpricks. From a distance, a herd of them crossing a root shelf looks like embers carried in a wind.

The resin workers are among the planet’s great small powers. They are not quite insects, though they have the segmented industry of them. Their outer shells are lacquered black or copper-green, proof against the sticky amber flows in which they work. Some cut resin with saw-edged mouthparts. Some knead it into pellets and roll it across kilometers of trunk surface. Some carry living molds in folded chambers beneath their wings. The molds soften and ferment the resin; the workers then feed on the altered material or use it to seal brood galleries against the dry air.

Their hives are not hives in the terrestrial sense. They are excavations inside living plants, frequently kilometers long, grown around rather than built within. A resin colony may persuade a hollow stem to thicken particular internal walls by irritating it with secretions. Over generations, the plant and the swarm become inseparable. The plant receives minerals, pruning, pollination, defense against burrowing parasites. The workers receive warmth, shelter, and a place in the great circulatory body of the world.

Some of these swarms migrate in weather systems. They rise from one resin field, form a dark granular cloud, and travel behind pressure fronts. Their collective wingbeat makes the air feel charged. Animals avoid them, not because they sting, but because their passage strips pollen from fur, membranes, eyes, and lungs.

On the open root lawns live the mineral grazers.

They are long-bodied, pale animals with broad feet divided into yielding pads. Their skin is not skin exactly but a layered felt of wax, dust, and reflective fibers that keeps them from losing too much heat or moisture. Their mouths unfold rather than open. Several folded plates extend from the face, press against the mineral crust on exposed vegetable stone, and scrape it away in slow careful strokes.

They do not drink. Instead, they feed upon the salts that have risen through old tissues and hardened at the surface. Much of their water comes from metabolic breakdown deep in their bodies; what little they lose is recovered through intricate nasal chambers that condense breath before it escapes.

Their migrations define the pale valleys visible from above. A herd will follow a band of exposed mineralizing root for decades, wearing it into a trench. The plants respond to them. Some grow tough pale tissue along the route, rich in the salts the grazers seek; others extend low shade-fronds over it, feeding on the dung and shed fibers of the herd. The animals believe they are following food. In a sense, the food is also growing a road beneath them.

Among the mineral grazers travel smaller hitching beasts, quick as rodents and flat as leaves. They cling beneath the larger animals’ belly folds and feed on parasites, dry secretions, and scraps from the mineral plates. When danger comes, they flatten themselves against the host and become nearly indistinguishable from its dusty hide.

The six-legged browsers are more conspicuous and stranger.

They climb.

Their lower limbs end in hooked pads that can grip rough bark, while their upper limbs are more delicate, used to pull apart young growth before it hardens. They ascend the first hundreds of meters of the great plant columns in patient groups, bracing themselves against the wind. Their bodies are narrow and folded, built to present little surface to gusts. Their eyes sit behind clear lateral shields, and a thin transparent veil can draw over the nostrils when pollen thickens.

The oldest browsers develop ridged head-crests that resonate in the wind. These crests produce low booming calls, not by voice but by directing air through cavities in the skull. Herds scattered across a trunk-cliff can remain in contact through the plant itself: a crest struck against bark sends a vibration down through the wood, answered from far below or high above by another browser.

They are preyed upon by the listening predators.

These are not swift runners. They do not need to be. Most are flattened, angular creatures that spend days or weeks attached to bark walls, their limbs buried in crevices, their bodies pressed so close to the plant that they seem like knots in the wood. Along their undersides are broad sensing membranes that read minute tremors: the scrape of a browser’s climbing pads, the pressure-wave of a grazer’s steps, the chewing of a resin worker colony inside a trunk.

When they strike, they release.

Their bodies unfold from the bark and fall silently through air already too loud for prey to hear anything. A web of grasping limbs opens around them. They do not kill with teeth at first. They wrap and seal, cutting off breath and retaining every trace of bodily moisture. On a world where water is held in living tissue, blood and vapor are treasures.

There are also aerial hunters, though “flying” is not quite the word. The gliders launch from high crowns and ride the immense wind corridors between distant organisms. Their sails are ribbed with flexible mineral rods and patterned in pale gray-green, making them difficult to see against the flickering leaf fields. They feed on swarming resin workers, on drifting seed animals, and on smaller gliders. Some never land on the ground-equivalent at all. Their nests lie in warm exhaust cavities within the breathing groves, where slow hot air rises through plant chambers.

The young of these creatures are born blind and almost spherical. They cling in clusters to the warm inner walls of the host plant, feeding on condensed vapor and the sugary films that collect near the vents. As they mature, their bodies flatten and their first sail membranes split open along the ribs.

The breathing plants themselves host entire interior ecologies.

Within their hollow shafts live blind chamber-crawlers: soft, pale scavengers with antennae tuned to warmth and carbon dioxide. They travel the interior galleries where the plants sort gases and sugars, feeding on dead workers, fungal growths, cast tissues, and the eggs of parasites. In some groves, these crawlers grow large enough to be dangerous. Their bodies are armored with overlapping plates made from the mineral residues of the host’s sap. They move by anchoring and contracting, like living bellows.

Their predators are thread-eels: long, root-colored animals that inhabit narrow vascular channels. They are so thin when young that they can move within the plant’s fluid tissues; as adults they emerge into larger chambers, trailing sensory filaments behind them. A thread-eel can feel a footstep on the outside of a trunk through several meters of living wall. It waits near a crack or vent, then lashes out with a sudden expansion of its body, pulling prey into the dark.

Not all the inhabitants are animals.

There are mobile fungi, or fungal colonies that have become sufficiently coordinated to behave like animals. On fallen giants they appear as advancing velvet fields, dark purple, black, or luminous green beneath the night. The outer layer digests old wood. The inner layer builds heat. Beneath it are dense cords that withdraw sugars, water, and minerals from the disassembled body.

Some fungal blooms move faster than a walking creature. They can cross a fallen canopy overnight, following fracture lines and rich resin channels. Small animals track them because they attract flies, mites, chamber-crawlers, and spores. Larger browsers avoid them, since the bloom can invade soft tissue through a cut and slowly turn living flesh into a warm source of food.

The oldest fungal colonies are regional intelligences only in the loosest possible sense. They do not think in images or plans, but they react across great distances. Damage a root plateau in one place and, days later, fruiting towers may rise on a ridge far away. A herd camp may be surrounded by sudden rings of poisonous fruiting bodies, not because the fungus hates the herd, but because the herd’s pressure and waste have become signals in a network older than its species.

There are flowers large enough to contain weather.

The great red flowering structures that open along the horizon are visited by pollinators the size of boats. These are thick-bodied, many-winged creatures that live most of their lives in the high air, drifting from bloom to bloom. Their wings beat slowly and powerfully, generating a bass pulse audible before the animals are visible. Their mouths are long, furred tubes that enter the flowers’ central chambers to feed on heated nectar.

The flowers close around them sometimes.

Not always as traps. Often the closure is part of pollination: the bloom seals for an hour or a day, bathing the visitor in scented vapor and coating it in thick packets of pollen. The pollinator emerges altered in color, carrying a fresh shag of yellow, blue, or blood-red dust. But some blooms do digest their guests. In a world built from biomass, even a flower can be a mountain and a carnivore.

Around the flowers gather opportunists: sail-gliders hunting pollen-drunk insects, little hook-footed scavengers eating fallen petals, and patient predators waiting along the petals’ outer folds. At the height of a blooming season, a red-flower plain may be louder than a storm front.

There are creatures that use the wind as their landscape.

The sail seeds of certain plants are so large and durable that animals colonize them while they are still airborne. Tiny clasping organisms hatch from eggs laid in the folds of seed membranes. They spend their first days riding the seed through the sky, eating its nutritious outer tissue while avoiding the edges that harden into sharp blades in the sun. When the seed eventually catches on a ridge or roots into a distant plateau, the creatures descend into a new territory.

Some species have abandoned the descent entirely. They mate, feed, and die among the drifting seed flotillas. Their bodies are light, hollow, and silvered. Their eggs are glued to the underside of passing leaves. Seen in the high blue air, they may be mistaken for debris until the whole flock banks at once.

And beneath nearly all of this live the great buried things.

No one on the surface sees them whole.

They move through old root strata, through compressed forests, through galleries warmed by decay. Their bodies may be segmented over hundreds of meters, or they may be colonies whose individual members are too integrated to distinguish. They consume dead substrate and leave behind altered ground: porous channels, mineral-rich spoil, warm fungal pockets, and collapsed sink valleys that later become routes for grazing herds.

Sometimes one rises close enough to the surface to be noticed. A plateau swells over days. Dry root-lawn cracks into branching lines. A mature stand of towers leans outward as if something beneath them were taking a breath. Then the ground opens—not into soil, but into the dark wet interior of ancient plant mass—and a pale armored curve passes below.

The disturbance is brief. The breach closes with roots, fungi, and new growth. Months later, perhaps, a line of unusually vigorous green will appear many kilometers away, following the buried creature’s route.

The largest life may not be an animal at all.

It may be the world’s connected vegetal body: the roots that alter their courses in response to pressure, the seed towers that release in coordinated seasons, the breathing groves that pulse in sequence over entire horizons. Individual plants die. Colonies split. Forest-mountains collapse and rise again. But the whole living surface remembers patterns of wind, migration, heat, and injury across spans of time too long for any grazing creature to recognize.

The animals live in it as parasites, partners, workers, prey, pollinators, excavators, and moving weather.

Nothing lives apart from the green continent.

Even the creatures that glide above it return eventually to a hollow stem, a resin chamber, a flower throat, a root crevice, or the hot dismantling body of a fallen giant. They are all fed by the same dry abundance. They all leave something behind for it to use.

And beneath their steps, beneath the clatter of wings and the booming throats and the mechanical breath of the groves, the planet continues growing its own inhabitants out of itself.
