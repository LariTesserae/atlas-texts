What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense.

At first, one might say that the underland is inhabited by the plants themselves. Not merely occupied by them, but held in a kind of slow attention. The braided stems know the weight of a crossing body. The leaf curtains distinguish between waiting and pushing. Roots tighten beneath a careful foot and soften beneath a resting hand. A pool remembers whether something was left beside it. None of this requires thought as animals think. It is closer to a bodily memory distributed through miles of green tissue: an awareness with no center, patient enough to seem like landscape.

But this is only the first kind of inhabitant.

There are people in the bright hollows, though they are difficult to count. They are called, by those who have met them and returned, the **Unfinished**.

The Unfinished are not children, though they have the gravity and watchfulness of children who have never been hurried into adulthood. Neither are they spirits, exactly. They have weight. They leave shallow impressions in warm clay. They drink from pools, sleep in the hollows under root shelves, and sometimes leave seeds in the old clay niches. Yet no one has seen one arrive, grow old, or die.

They are usually encountered at the edges of sight: seated on a root bridge over tea-dark water; standing among the pale columns of a living thicket; crouched beside the ochre mound that never opens. If approached directly, they often appear to be only a cluster of folded leaves or a pale trunk with a human resemblance. If noticed without being sought, they may be unmistakably people.

Their bodies are made with the same gentleness as the terrain. Skin ranges from clay-ochre to pale green-white, with a faint translucent quality at the throat, wrists, and ears. In bright shafts of light, one can see branching shadows beneath it—not veins exactly, but fine rootlike tracery. Their hair grows in soft masses like moss, thread-fine vine, or the silky tassels that hang from the ceiling. Some wear it loose. Some braid it around smooth stones, dried pods, or fragments of old carvings found in the green. Their eyes are large but not uncanny; their irises carry the colors of the place: amber sap, deep leaf-green, muddy gold, the gray-white of buried stone.

They tend to wear very little that could be called clothing. Their coverings are grown, not made: broad leaves folded and softened until they hang like fabric, belts of root fiber, mantles made from the shed skins of enormous seedpods. An Unfinished may be seen wearing a pale, waxy collar that is alive and slowly opening around the shoulders, or a skirt of small overlapping leaves that close at night—if night exists for them at all.

They are warm to the touch.

They do not seem to possess names in the way travelers do. When one wishes to indicate another, they touch two fingers to a nearby root, then to their chest, and make a breathy sound that changes depending on where they are standing. The same individual may therefore be called differently in a pool basin, a root corridor, or beneath a white roof. Their identity is not separate from their place. To know one of them is partly to know the chamber in which they are most often found.

For this reason, taking one far from the underland is nearly impossible. A visitor might lead an Unfinished through three passages, over woven floors and beneath glowing soil, only to look back and find that they are no longer there. Later, in some basin that should not connect to the first, the same figure may be sitting beside a pool, calmly arranging stones in a ring.

They are not lost. They are where they belong.

The Unfinished make the old constructions, or continue making them in a manner too slow for human observation. The slab rings are theirs. So are the low platforms over water, the stairs that end in leaves, the niches containing seeds. They do not build for shelter. The underland shelters them already. Their constructions are for recognition.

A stone platform says: here is a pool that has been greeted.

A niche full of seeds says: here is a wall that has listened.

A stair ending in foliage says: here is a direction once desired.

They are fond of edges: the edge of water, the edge of a bright shaft, the place where a clay wall begins to become root. Often they sit facing these thresholds for very long stretches, still enough that moths settle on them. It can be unsettling until one realizes that they are not waiting for something to happen. In this country, waiting is itself an act of care.

Their work is similarly quiet. They lift fallen leaves from the pools, although the leaves do not rot. They untwist vines that have grown around a stone. They press their palms against swelling clay domes. They gather droplets that have hung too long from root tips and set them gently onto the ground. They carry little bowls woven from stiff grass, filled with seeds, pale pebbles, shed bark, or occasionally the small bones of animals from the world above.

No one has seen them eat in the usual way. They drink, but only in ceremony. Before drinking, they leave an object at the pool edge—not payment, but an acknowledgment that water has been received. They also stand for long periods under the brightest places in the roof, faces lifted into the green-filtered sun. Afterward, their skin seems clearer, and fine new lines appear along their hands like the veins of leaves.

Some say they feed on light. Others say they feed on duration: on the long interval before a drop falls, before a seed opens, before a mound becomes a hill or a cave. The Unfinished live upon the fact that nothing here is required to complete itself.

They are gentle with visitors, but their gentleness should not be mistaken for welcome.

A person who enters the underland with hunger for possession will find the Unfinished absent. The corridors will seem thicker. Leaf curtains will remain closed. Pools will lie beyond reach. Such a traveler may wander for hours beneath the warm bright roof and encounter only plants, only roots, only the sensation of being observed by a silence too vegetal to answer.

But someone who enters quietly may be assisted.

An Unfinished might lay a pale root across a mud seam to make it safe to cross. They might leave a fist-sized luminous fruit at the edge of a traveler’s sleeping place. They might point to a leaf curtain just before it loosens. They do not lead anyone out directly. Instead, they arrange small permissions: a bridge where there was no bridge, a familiar smell carried down a passage, a moth drifting repeatedly in the same direction.

Their warnings are less easy to understand.

If an Unfinished presses a seed into someone’s hand, it means: do not continue carrying what you brought in.

If they turn their face away while standing beside a pool, it means: leave without drinking.

If they place a hand on a wall and the wall answers with a faint tremor, it means: the chamber is remembering someone else; do not disturb it.

And if one is seen kneeling beside a path, carefully covering a pale root with clay, then the traveler should retrace their steps without speaking. Something beneath that route is trying to become more real than it ought to be.

There are other inhabitants, smaller and less human.

The pale moths are said to be thoughts shed by the root-roof. They are born where sunlight presses hardest against the thin stone, and they die only when they settle on a sleeping Unfinished. Their wings carry faint patterns visible only in the green-gold light: tiny maps of chambers that cannot be reached by walking.

The eyeless frogs are the pools’ keepers. They do not guide anyone, but they click when an offering has been accepted. A single click means a stone will do. Two means the pool prefers a seed. Silence means that whatever was left is not yet understood.

The long root-colored insects are perhaps the oldest creatures in the place. They live precisely along the division between brightness and shade, never crossing fully into either. The Unfinished watch them with unusual attention. When many of these insects gather in one corridor, it means the distances are about to become strange. A chamber may draw close. A path may decide it belongs elsewhere.

And beneath all of these—the people, the moths, the frogs, the insects, even the responsive vegetation—there is another resident.

It does not have a body.

It is the condition of beginning.

It lives in the unbroken mud hill, in the new fissures under the bright roof, in the soft stone that has risen but not hardened. It is the reason the Unfinished do not finish. It is the reason old platforms remain level while roots climb through them, why petals do not brown, why the air holds noon without letting it pass into afternoon.

The underland does not preserve life as it was. It preserves life at the moment it is about to become.

The Unfinished belong to that moment. Perhaps they were once travelers who came down through a root crack and stayed too long in the patient heat. Perhaps they are the shapes the plants make when they dream of walking. Perhaps every one of them is a future person, held in the world’s palm before being placed aboveground.

When they gather, which is rare, they gather around things that have almost changed.

They stand around the ochre mound when its vapor thickens.

They sit in circles around a seedpod that has begun to split.

They place both hands on a clay wall when a new chamber presses outward behind it.

No song accompanies these gatherings. No ritual voice. Only the crowded brush of leaves, the soft clicks below water, and the sound of many slow breaths shared between green things.

Then, eventually—perhaps after a century, perhaps after the time it takes a visitor to blink—nothing happens.

The mound remains closed.

The seedpod remains only half-open.

The wall remains warm and unbroken.

The Unfinished rise, touch the roots around them in farewell, and return to their basins, their bridges, their leaf-shadowed steps. They are not disappointed.

In a place where becoming never has to end, they have learned that almost is a kind of home.
