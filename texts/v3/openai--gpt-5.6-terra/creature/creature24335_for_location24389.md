What lives there is not an animal in the ordinary sense.

At first it seems that nothing lives in the basin except the green things: the ferns pressed flat by rain, the crowded saplings, the moss taking its color from the stone. Then, after enough time in the dark, distinctions begin to fail. The vapor has habits. The water has routes it remembers and abandons. The roots do not merely cling to the ground; they appear to listen through it.

There is a presence in the fissures.

It has no single body, no face waiting beyond the trees. It exists wherever heat meets cold water under pressure: in the narrow places where stone has not decided whether to remain joined or become separate. It is older than the vegetation but younger than the mountain, born when the ridge broke and opened its wet black interior to air.

The smallest parts of it are almost invisible. They gather as a faint pearling on mineral-stained rock, a glassy skin in the seams of basalt, a colorless trembling in pools that should be still. In darkness they may show a weak blue-white gleam, not enough to light a hand, only enough to make wet surfaces seem to be remembering daylight. They retreat from open water and flourish in warmth, in iron, in the thin vapors that would choke or starve most living things.

The moss knows them. It grows thickest where their breath rises, though it often dies back at the center of a vent, leaving pale rings and orange stains. Roots encounter them underground and change direction. A tree may lean for years toward a crack, its bark silvered on the side nearest the steam. Then the crack closes or cools, and the tree begins, slowly, inconveniently, to lean away.

Perhaps the whole basin is a kind of colony.

Not one creature but an arrangement: hot-water organisms in the deep fractures; mats of mineral-feeding films under the stones; pale threadlike growths inside the roots; things too small to see riding every droplet that condenses from the cloud ceiling. Their lives are measured in pulses of heat, in the opening and narrowing of channels, in the arrival of a new mineral taste. When the mountain knocks, they alter. When a pool drains, they sleep in crust. When a vent opens, they arrive first.

There may also be something larger, though no one would see it moving.

On certain nights, when the whistles fall out of rhythm and the deep roar becomes continuous, the wet ground between the trees gives a subtle heave. Not enough to throw a person down. Enough to make hanging leaves tremble and pooled water draw inward from its edges. The roots tighten. Vapor pours from cracks that had seemed dead.

Then, from beneath the basin, comes the sound of a long inhalation.

It is tempting to call this creature the mountain itself. That is close, but not quite right. The mountain does not notice its own breathing. This thing does. It is the awareness produced by pressure: the attention of stone under stress, of water seeking a way through, of heat encountering resistance. It has no thoughts a human being could recognize. It does not watch intruders from behind the trees. Yet anyone standing in the basin for long may feel that their weight has been registered.

Not judged.

Registered, as rain is registered, as a root, as a fallen slab.

The basin’s living inhabitant is patient because it has no choice. It cannot leave the walls that contain it. It enlarges its chambers by fractions, wears channels through basalt grain by grain, feeds forests that may collapse into its openings. It takes in cold water through the upper cracks and returns it as warmth, vapor, mineral residue, sound.

Sometimes it rearranges the place in a single violent minute. More often it works softly, turning a dry ledge slick, warming a patch of moss until it glows brighter than the rest, hollowing the earth beneath a tree until the tree is suddenly standing over nothing.

The plants are its fur, perhaps. The cloud is its breath. The noises are not its voice, exactly, but the consequences of its continuing existence.

And the reason no animal remains there may be simple: animals require a world that stays itself long enough to be learned.

This basin is still becoming acquainted with its own shape.
