What lives here has learned not to waste a voice.

The first inhabitants are the ones that pass for fish until they turn.

They travel in fields beneath the standing waves: silver, black, and glass-clear bodies arranged so densely that the sea seems to contain weather below its surface. From a cliff they may look like reflections of cloud, or broad pieces of daylight moving against the current. Then the field changes direction and thousands of bodies reveal their sides at once. It is not a flash exactly. It is an opening: a rectangular brightness, a grid of cold scales, a pale geometry turning through black water.

Each animal is narrow as a knife blade and longer than an arm. Their eyes lie deep under transparent skull plates. Along their flanks are lines of pressure-sensitive pits which tremble with the great regular impacts of the waves. They do not navigate by sight alone. They read the fixed sea as one might read a clock. The pulse of the breaker, the underwater grind of machinery, the recurring thunder of an engine cycle: these are their landmarks.

When one platform’s piston knocks, they descend.

When the deep bell sounds, they spread.

When the engine stops without fading, they turn as one body and move toward another tower.

There are predators among them, though they are rarely seen clearly. Pale-backed animals rise in the troughs beyond the breakers, immense and slow, with wet skin folded in heavy seams around their necks. Their exhalations are not plumes so much as sudden absences in the wind: vapor bursts from them, is caught and flattened, and vanishes. They feed beneath the foam, where the silver schools are driven into dense spinning pillars by the platform currents.

Some have old harness scars.

A few carry pieces of machinery embedded in them: a rusted ring around a flipper, a length of black cable trailing from a shoulder, a numbered metal tag swallowed into an ear. No one knows whether they were caught by the works or once belonged to them. In the deep water they make low sounds that cannot be heard above the surf. The basalt receives those sounds, however. On certain nights, a hand placed against the cliff can feel slow calls traveling through the stone: not rhythmic like the engines, not regular enough to be a signal, but patient.

The white seals on the lower shelves are not seals, exactly.

They are shore-bound only in the sense that they return to shore. Their bodies are too thick to move gracefully on rock; they drag themselves up through spray in silence and arrange themselves along the black ledges. They have no visible wounds despite the sharp basalt. Their hides seem waxed, almost unfinished, and their black eyes remain open even while they sleep.

At the turn of night, they gather near the welded shelters.

Not entering. Waiting near them.

A person who watched carefully would see that the animals arrive before the submerged engines begin. They lift their heads toward the red marker lamps. Their bodies tighten. Then the deep machinery starts: a vibration beneath the entire basin, too large to call a sound. The seals lower their heads again, and one by one slip backward into the water.

Nothing has ever been found in their stomachs except fish, shell fragments, strands of cable insulation, and once, according to a record nailed inside a switch house, a human tooth with a gold filling.

The black-winged birds belong to the vents.

They nest where warm air leaks from the rock, in cuts too narrow for a person to enter. Their feathers are not truly black; close up they have the dull blue sheen of burned metal. Their heads are nearly bare, the skin beneath their eyes gray and wrinkled from the constant cold. They circle machinery more faithfully than carrion. A tower that goes dark will lose its birds within days. A newly active vent will acquire them before the first week ends.

They eat what the sea gives up: pale seal afterbirth, blinded fish, strips of tissue that rise from somewhere too deep to name. But they also peck at the mineral crust on pipes and cable housings. Their beaks are blunted from it. When they are startled, they do not cry. They open their wings and make a dry shuddering noise, like sheet metal flexing in wind.

There are people, too, although the place does not encourage certainty about this.

The old installations were designed for crews. The evidence remains everywhere: bunks bolted inside concrete rooms, lunch tins fused shut with salt, safety slogans faded to pale ghosts on corridor walls. In a few of the lit platform windows, shadows still cross at intervals. A shoulder. A hand raised to a face. Something pausing beside a gauge.

No boat comes for them.

No radio signal has ever been received from them that contains language.

The nearest thing is a repeating burst of tones heard on certain frequencies during the engine cycle: three low pulses, a gap, then seven higher ones. It may be diagnostic equipment. It may be an automated warning. It may be someone counting.

On the high shore, behind the welded doors and beneath the switch houses, there are maintenance passages descending into the volcanic rock. Most are flooded. Some end at steel hatches whose hinges are warm to the touch. Others continue downward in clean concrete stairwells, lit by strips of weak yellow light that have no visible power source. The walls are painted with arrows. The arrows point both down and toward the sea.

Those who live below do not often come out.

When they do, they wear layered black suits stiff with frost and masks with dark flat lenses. They move in groups of two or three, never more. They carry no lamps, even at night. They seem to know the ground by memory, or by the vibration of the water through their boots. They inspect red marker poles, strike ice from valve housings, and stand for long periods facing the waves as if waiting for a particular pattern of foam.

They do not acknowledge observers.

Perhaps they cannot hear anything above the sea.

Perhaps, after enough years in that noise, a human voice becomes an internal thing: felt in the throat and skull, no longer intended to cross distance.

There are reports of one figure living in the old crane gantry on the western platform. It appears whenever the clouds break at dawn: a narrow person in a pale oilskin standing high above the water with one hand resting on the crane arm. The figure remains there through wind strong enough to tear spray sideways and through cold that locks exposed metal in ice. At the exact moment the offshore engine begins, it turns toward the basin’s center.

Then it is gone.

Not descending. Not walking away.

Gone between one breaker and the next.

The deepest inhabitants may not be animals at all.

The machinery has been operating too long to be merely functioning. It has adapted itself to the sea’s repetition. The cables that vanish from platforms into the black do not hang slack; they flex in intervals, tighten, release, and tighten again. Pipes emit warmth according to patterns no weather explains. Underwater bells ring from locations where no station is marked. At times, a whole section of sea will rise by a fraction, not as a wave but as a slow inhalation, and all the surface breakers above it will continue unchanged.

Something below is moving vast quantities of water.

Or holding them in place.

Perhaps the industrial towers are not extracting anything. Perhaps they are feeding the sea, regulating its temperature, teaching it the shape of its endless waves. Perhaps the ridges are young because the basin has never permitted them time. Perhaps the cold abundance below the surface is a cultivated one: schools managed by pressure and sound, pale giants tagged and guided, migrations triggered by bells.

And perhaps the human-shaped workers, if they are human-shaped workers, serve the same purpose as the red lamps.

They do not inhabit the place in the ordinary sense. They mark it. They maintain the boundary between a world that changes and this one, where the same breaker is always breaking, where rust advances without consuming, where a light can remain on for decades without anyone remembering who first switched it on.

The sea itself lives here most completely.

Not as an animal lives, with hunger or fear, but as a system that has become aware of its own repetition. It speaks through impact. It breathes through the engine shafts. It dreams in blue-green migrations under the dark. Its waves are not identical because they are dead; they are identical because they are practiced.

Everything else—the white animals, the black birds, the silent workers, the towers with their buried bells—lives inside that practice.
