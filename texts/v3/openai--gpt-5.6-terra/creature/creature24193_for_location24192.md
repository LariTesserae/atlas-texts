What lives in the Underward is not merely a population adapted to floodwater. It is a people whose bodies, habits, tools, and sense of kinship have been slowly shaped by living in a place where walls sweat, floors float, and every room has a depth.

They are called Underwarders by outsiders, when outsiders remember they exist. Among themselves the word is seldom needed. There are pumpkeepers, growers, skiffwrights, ledgerers, divers, sealmakers, lamp tenders, salvage crews, children too young to have chosen a trade, and old people whose work has become remembering which wall used to be a doorway and which pipe must never be struck. But nobody is only one thing. A grower may be the person called to listen to a pump. A child may know the quickest route through a reed-bed. The woman who trades filter clay in the Market Vault may also be the one who enters collapsed tunnels when a pressure line begins to sing.

They tend to be compact in their movements, not necessarily in stature. The Underward teaches economy. People duck without looking up, turn sideways through familiar gaps, set down cups where no vibration will carry them away. They have strong hands from poles, ropes, valve wheels, root-work, and the constant lifting of wet things. Their shoulders are often marked by old harness pressure and the pale rub of carrying straps. Their feet are rarely bare on platforms, but often bare in boats and homes: soles softened by warm water, scarred by hidden wire or shell, accustomed to feeling differences in surface before the eyes register them.

Their skin has the particular pallor and weathering of insufficient sun, though it is not simply pale. It carries reflected colors: green from the shafts, amber from the machine halls, blue-white from lamp jars. Many have freckles of rust-colored staining across fingers and wrists, where iron water has worked into fine cuts. Their hair is cut for practicality or allowed to grow in thick braids tied high against damp collars. Long hair is usually waxed, wrapped, or woven with cord; loose hair catches in fan housings, root meshes, and drain grates. Some people shave their heads before long maintenance work, not as a ritual exactly, but as a precaution that acquired the gravity of ritual.

They dress in layers that can be peeled off, hung up, dried imperfectly, and worn again. Oiled cloth, patched rubber, knitted wool, softened work trousers, boots with soles rebuilt more times than their owners can count. A good jacket is handed down with records of its repairs. Pockets are important. Most garments have loops for tools, token strings, small lamps, folded waterproof paper, a strip of sealant, a piece of chalk, a knife. Children wear little cork or foam floats beneath their clothes until they learn the routes well enough to be trusted without them.

Their clothing is quieter than it appears. Metal buckles are wrapped. Buttons are cloth-covered. Keys are kept in felt tubes. Even jewelry has adapted: bone, ceramic, old plastic, shell, braided wire, polished glass. Anything that can clink is tied down. The Underward has little patience for accidental noise, but it values deliberate sound immensely. A bell struck once under a platform can mean a boat coming blind around a corner. Three slow taps on a pipe can mean pressure trouble. A particular low hum sung through a closed door can mean: I am here; do not open yet.

They speak softly, but not timidly. Their voices are trained to travel only as far as needed. A visitor might first think the inhabitants subdued, secretive, or mournful. Then they would sit at a meal and discover the rapid exchanges of hands, brows, shoulders, and mouths; the humor carried in a raised chin or a delayed glance; the ability to argue ferociously in near silence. Finger-spelling is common, though it has changed over generations into local shorthand. There are signs for currents, for unsafe ceilings, for a pump behaving badly, for “eel seen,” for “surface person,” for “you are standing where the drip falls.” There are gestures too old for anyone to explain: two fingers pressed against the throat before entering the turbine hall, a palm held over water before drawing from a new channel.

Their laughter is quiet at first, then sudden and physical. In the Market Vault it spreads through shoulders and faces before anyone hears it. In private rooms, with doors sealed and the pumps far away, they can be loud. Some families keep old music players alive with impossible devotion. There are gatherings in deep chambers where the water swallows most of the sound and people drum their hands against floating boards, stomp on resonant grates, or sing into bowls of water to make the surface tremble. Music in the Underward is often felt rather than heard: a rhythm in the ribs, a vibration under the heel, a communal disturbance of the basin.

They do not regard drowning as an abstract danger. It is too immediate for abstraction. Children learn water before they learn many words: how to float in work clothes, how to find a wall in stirred silt, how to follow a rope without tangling it around a wrist, how to distinguish a harmless warm current from a dangerous pull. They are taught never to fight a closing pressure door, never to swim into a space whose ceiling cannot be found, never to assume a familiar channel remains familiar after a storm. And they are taught that panic is not shameful, only contagious. If someone freezes in water, another person does not shout. They make contact. They give the frightened body something solid.

The oldest people carry the Underward differently. They are often the ones with the least need to look around. Their knowledge is distributed through posture: a pause before stepping onto a platform, a hand held flat to gauge an engine’s tremor, the way they taste water from a finger rather than a cup. They remember previous waterlines not as measurements but as events. That stain was the year the eastern root mass tore free. That shelf was built after the yellow bloom. That door was once the entrance to a clinic. Do not sleep there; the old floor underneath is hollow.

Some elders have never seen open sky except as a pale rectangle at the top of a shaft. Others left the surface as children and remember it with inaccurate intensity: wind that could push a person over, rooms with windows that opened directly into air, sunlight that made colors hurt. A few still make regular journeys upward, wrapped against rain and bad air, trading with surface enclaves or salvaging material from ruins. These travelers return changed for a while. They speak too loudly. They stand too far from walls. They bring back stories of brightness, distance, dry dust, birds in numbers so great they seem invented.

The children of the Underward are not solemn, despite what the place might suggest. They play in the narrow spaces adults have forgotten to prohibit. They race thumb-sized boats in drain currents. They keep transparent snails in jars until an adult makes them return them. They dare one another to touch old station signs beneath the water. They know how to hide beneath cress rafts and breathe through reeds, though they are punished for it every time. Their games involve routes: who can reach the Market Vault without using a named platform, who can identify a chamber by smell alone, who can recite the full sequence of turbine repairs from the Ledger Pool without getting a date wrong.

Their toys are usually functional objects given temporary freedom from usefulness: gasket rings, cable spools, broken lamp housings, valve tags, lengths of colored wire. A child might grow up with a favorite piece of buoyant pipe insulation, carved into the shape of an eel. At some age, the games change. They are given a hooded lamp, then a small tool roll, then permission to take a skiff alone across a known channel. The first solitary crossing is remembered longer than many birthdays.

There are households, but they do not always resemble surface households. Space is too constrained, maintenance too constant, and water too indifferent to private boundaries. People form living groups around rooms, boats, work cycles, shared gardens, and obligations. A sleeping chamber might hold three generations, two unrelated mechanics, a baby in a hanging cradle, and a fungal shelf marked in yellow wax. Another dwelling might house only one person and their archive of waterproof papers. Lovers often keep separate berths because one works day-shift at the filters and the other sleeps between deepward inspections. Families are made by care: who knows your breathing pattern when you are ill, who maintains your lamp, who notices when you fail to come home by the second turbine change.

They bury their dead in the root gardens when they can. Not whole, always; the Underward has learned practical ways to honor a body without demanding impossible space from the living. Ash, wrapped hair, a tool, a name pressed into clay, a small sealed packet lowered into root-dark water. The roots take what they are given. In time a person may be remembered by the place their memorial helped nourish: the copperleaf bed near the old signal box, the mushroom shelves beneath the south stair, the thick root curtain beside the public reservoir.

But people are only one portion of what lives there.

The water itself is inhabited by innumerable small workers: oil-softening bacteria, iron-eating films, drifting threads of algae, rotifers in the clear reservoir, clouded colonies that form and dissolve along warm pipes. Underwarders know them as one knows neighbors whose names cannot be pronounced. They cultivate some, discourage others, watch their colors and smells. A bloom can be a blessing, warning, medicine, food, or omen depending on where it appears. Green velvet on brick may mean good filtration. Black slick around a valve may mean the seals are failing. A pale foam collecting under a root chamber may mean the water has begun to change its mind.

The root columns live there with a kind of slow authority. They are not decorative. They buckle walls apart, knit fallen masonry together, drink from hidden cracks, hold up ceilings no engineer would trust, shelter nests of tools and sleeping bodies. Some root systems have names, not botanical names but domestic ones: Old Ladder, the Veiled Mother, Three Chimneys, the Root That Knocks. A person may say they were born behind Old Ladder, or that they met their wife under Three Chimneys, or that their grandfather disappeared after following bubbles past the Root That Knocks.

The eels live there too, or pass through it, which is somehow more important. No one claims ownership of them. They emerge from places too narrow to believe in, fat with darkness and muscle, and move through the settlement as if following maps printed beneath the water. Their appearances are recorded because an eel where no eel has been seen before can mean a new opening, a shifted pressure route, an altered chemistry. Some workers insist the eels are merely animals. Others insist that is exactly why they must be respected.

There are also things that are not alive in the usual sense but have become participants in the settlement’s life: the turbines, whose slow turning sets the pace of work and sleep; the old rail loop, which gathers silt and boats and memory; the generators that shudder in their sleep; the pressure doors, each with its own temperament; the lamps in glass jars, which require tending like livestock. An Underwarder may speak of a pump being hungry, a culvert sulking, a cable wanting to drift. This is not ignorance of machinery. It is a way of acknowledging that no one has full command over systems old enough to have acquired habits.

And perhaps that is what most truly lives in the Underward: an agreement.

Not peace. Not mastery. An agreement continually repaired between human bodies, old infrastructure, warm water, roots, fungus, corrosion, darkness, and time.

The inhabitants understand that the basin does not belong to them. They belong to it in the more difficult sense: they are among the things it carries, alters, feeds, remembers, and may someday cover completely.
