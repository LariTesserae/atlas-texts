The people of the basin do not think of themselves as living *in* the city. They live in its weather, its circulation, its appetite.

They are called many things by outsiders, most of which mean lowlanders, flood-born, or people of the black water. Among themselves, names tend to be more practical. A person may be from a particular liftward, bridge-clan, cistern lineage, pump district, or hound-right drainage. They may identify themselves by the water they are licensed to draw, the level at which they sleep, the kind of seal their doors use, or the age of the storm their family remembers surviving.

They are not one people in the simple sense. The city is too old and too continuous for that. Generations of migrants have entered it from the surrounding drylands, from drowned coasts, from orbital works, from failed inland states, from places that no longer appear on maps. Their descendants have been absorbed into the basin’s innumerable levels. Languages persist in narrow vertical strips: one dialect spoken only on the stairwells between the forty-second and fifty-eighth tiers of a freight stack; another retained by boat families who navigate the western floodways; another encoded into the work-songs of people who service the atmospheric collectors.

Most basin-dwellers are compact, strong through the hips and shoulders, accustomed from childhood to unstable footing. Their balance is conspicuously good. They step without looking at thresholds, ridges, slick decking, and sudden seams of water. Even in crowded markets, they seem to know where a street will flex, where an old plate sounds hollow, where the current beneath a grate is strong enough to take a dropped tool.

Their clothing is layered, modular, and never entirely dry. Outer fabrics are oil-dark or mineral-bright, cut short at the legs and sleeves so they do not drag in floodwater. People wear sealed collars, throat filters, hood membranes, cable-woven capes that catch heat from vents, and belts crowded with hooks, flotation tabs, repair patches, food packets, local keys, and personal pumps. Footwear matters enormously. A person’s boots tell a great deal: whether they work the elevated spans, the flood wards, the canal markets, the deep infrastructure, the collector towers, or the dry upper galleries where rain is mostly an event to watch from behind glass.

The wealthy are not distinguished by dryness. No one is dry for long. They are distinguished by control: private pressure doors, clean water allocation, quiet rooms, reliable elevation, inherited access to stable routes during storms. The very richest maintain old apartments high in fused towers, where the windows are broad and the roofs have enough open space to make silence possible. Some cultivate the luxury of an uninterrupted view all the way to the basin haze.

But the city distrusts people who have forgotten the lower water.

Everyone is expected to know at least the basic flood disciplines: how to read a wall’s pressure lamps, how to open a manual vent, how to recognize the change in a hound’s gait before a rise, how to tether a child, how to cut a trapped sleeve without cutting an artery, how to listen to a pump through the soles of the feet. Schools teach route memory before arithmetic. Children learn to swim in public cisterns, then in controlled flood channels, then in the warm dark water of the old undercity under the supervision of licensed guides.

Many never leave their home district except in ceremony or necessity. The basin is too vast, and transit too conditional, for casual travel to mean what it does elsewhere. A journey across the city can require rail, lift, skiff, footbridge, and an hour spent waiting under a pressure canopy while an entire avenue becomes a canal. People speak of distant districts as if they were countries: the Northern Condensers, the Shuttered Courts, the White Silt Towers, the Cable Orchards, the Long Drain, the Twenty-Three Hanging Wards.

Yet they are connected constantly by trade.

There are sellers of filtered rain in small sealed bulbs, each district’s water carrying a different mineral taste. There are armor-polishers for canal grazer plates, hound-law advocates, vent cooks, bridge chiropractors, thunderwrights, scavenger interpreters, buoyancy auditors, signal divers, insulation weavers, and people whose profession is to locate old rooms buried inside newer construction. Some households keep tiny domestic systems in wall recesses: heat-eating bacterial analogues, water-testing motes, edible mineral cultures, soft-bodied cable cleaners that sleep wrapped around pipes.

The city’s poorest live closest to the water and closest to the systems no one fully understands. They occupy floodable rooms, old service decks, unregistered platforms fastened beneath larger structures, and chambers reached only when the water is low. Their homes are made to lose things. Furniture lifts on rails. Cooking surfaces fold into walls. Beds are hung in nets. Family archives are sealed in buoyant cases that float to marked ceiling hooks when the water comes in. A household can be judged by the readiness of its rise-kit: lamps, masks, maps, medicine, rope, dry food, spare gaskets, names of people to contact above the flood line.

The city’s deepest laborers are called, depending on district, throatkeepers, mudwrights, gate listeners, or simply lowers. They work in the ancient drainage anatomy beneath the streets. Some tend gates so immense that they are understood less as machines than as weather events. Some enter submerged halls to clear obstructions from intakes. Some descend with hounds, because a hound will notice a change in water pressure long before an instrument admits that something is wrong. These workers are honored publicly and underpaid privately, as is customary for anyone whose labor prevents catastrophe.

There are also those who live in partial alliance with the city’s older intelligence.

They may be technicians, priests, litigators, archivists, or all four. They maintain conversational protocols with elevators that have developed preferences, with distribution engines that respond to particular songs, with rain-sorting columns that must be addressed in obsolete legal syntax before they will release emergency reserves. Their temples are often machine rooms. Their reliquaries contain corroded interfaces, fragments of original code, pressure diagrams engraved on brass, and recordings of voices from centuries ago giving instructions to systems that still obey.

They do not all agree that the city is alive.

Some say it is merely elaborate: a layered machinery so distributed that it resembles instinct. Others insist it possesses appetite, memory, and moods. A few claim that the city dreams through its water-monitoring swarms, gathering stray signal and thought from every flooded seam until, somewhere far below, a sleeping intelligence makes a decision. These people are treated with patient skepticism until a drainage route changes unexpectedly, or a district receives water it did not request, or a sealed door opens for someone who has no authorization.

Then everyone becomes superstitious.

The flood hounds live among the people but are not owned by them. They have dens in the engineered hollows along the retaining walls, in abandoned transit shells, under market pontoons, and beneath old civic stairs. Their relationship with the city is older than many municipal laws. Children learn not to touch a hound’s ears, not to stand in its path, and never to offer it food during a rise. Hounds are allowed through homes, shops, shrines, and stations without question if the water is coming up. A hound entering a building with intent is enough to empty it.

Canal grazers are treated with a mixture of affection and infrastructure concern. They are useful, beautiful to some, and prone to causing expensive damage when they gather under a weakened pier. Their plated backs are painted with route marks by licensed handlers, and some of the larger ones pull slow cargo platforms through flooded avenues. Old people sometimes claim the grazers remember the first basin waters, though they remember little else.

The wirewings are less respected. They steal conductive jewelry, nest in exhaust equipment, and can turn a clean balcony into a shifting, peeping carpet of heat-seeking bodies. Still, to destroy a nesting mass is considered unlucky in most wards. Their arrival around a vent is often the first sign that its heat is failing or changing. Engineers may resent them, but engineers watch them.

And in the shallow lower waters move the burdened scavengers, the slow things, whose backs accumulate the city’s discarded history. Children follow them with lanterns and make games of identifying what they carry: a spoon, a broken lens, a dental plate, a length of coded copper, an old coin from a government nobody remembers. Sometimes a scavenger emerges bearing something important enough to summon a whole neighborhood—a lost registry seal, an ancestral key, a component no longer manufactured, a message tablet still blinking after fifty years underwater.

There are people who say the slow things are the true archivists of the basin.

At night, when the storm lights pulse in the clouds and the streets shine black as newly poured glass, the inhabitants emerge onto bridges, platforms, roof canals, and market decks. They eat hot salted food from steam carts. They argue about gate policy. They mend one another’s clothing. They trade weather rumors. They stand beneath awnings and watch distant rain cross the city in gray curtains.

They live loudly because quiet is difficult, intimately because space is scarce, and with a degree of mutual obligation that would feel intolerable anywhere else. A neighbor may know the sound of your pipes, the weight of your door, the number of people in your rooms, and whether your child has come home from the upper transit line. This is not necessarily affection. It is survival rendered as familiarity.

Their dead are rarely buried. There is no ground to spare and too much water beneath it. Some are given to sealed heat chambers; some are dissolved through mineral rites into the public water system; some are commemorated by installing their names in pressure doors, stair rails, hound ledges, canal markers, or the code-litanies that keep old machines responsive. To be remembered is often to become part of the city’s operating instructions.

So the basin is inhabited by millions of human lives, by creatures adapted to the engineered wetness, by machines with habits, by waters that carry matter and signal alike, by old laws still moving through pipes and cables.

And perhaps, in the end, by the city itself.

Not above its inhabitants, not beneath them, but among them: listening through grates, breathing through pumps, changing shape under their weight, keeping them alive in ways neither they nor it completely understand.
