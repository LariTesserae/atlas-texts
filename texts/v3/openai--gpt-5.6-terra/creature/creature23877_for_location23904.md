What lives there is not a people inclined to announce itself.

The basin has made them attentive before it has made them anything else. They are listeners of runoff, readers of dampness, keepers of little continuities. A visitor from a dry or populous lowland might first mistake them for reserved, even melancholy. They speak quietly in public, close shutters before weather arrives, pause on stairways to let a laden person pass. They do not crowd thresholds. They leave room around springs.

But their quiet is not emptiness. It is an old courtesy toward stone, water, and the labor of other hands.

They are a people of the slope. Even those born in the lowest terraces learn the city through ascent: which stair holds ice longest; which handrail sweats before rain; where a shortcut turns treacherous in mist; which old woman on a third-floor landing keeps a kettle for anyone caught in a squall. Children learn to gauge distance less by streets than by changes in height and sound. They know that the bakery under the blue drain-spout is three flights below the wool court, that the schoolyard is above the cistern with the cracked lion head, that one can smell the upper reservoir before seeing it.

Their bodies tend toward the practical adaptations of generations spent in cold air and steep streets. They are sure-footed rather than swift, broad in shoulder from carrying, with hands altered by rope, stone, wet wool, and tools. Their oldest people often move slowly but rarely seem lost. They know routes through interiors that younger residents have forgotten: passages through old storage rooms, stairwells behind workshops, galleries built over channels. A grandmother can disappear through a doorway beside a fishmonger’s stall and emerge, twenty minutes later, five terraces uphill with no visible exertion and a bundle of herbs no one saw her buy.

Their clothing is layered, repaired, and deliberately plain at a distance. Waxed wool, heavy linen, felted coats, leather softened by repeated rain. The colors are mostly those already present in the city—charcoal, lichen green, brown-red, the blue of weathered slate—but almost everyone keeps one clear mineral color somewhere: a yellow cuff, a red scarf, a collar the deep green of copper salts. In fog this is useful. It is also beautiful in a way they do not bother to explain.

They have no sentimental notion that the city is permanent. They know too much about walls to believe that. A wall is a question asked repeatedly of rain and gravity. A roof is an agreement. A stair is a public promise. Their pride is not in making structures endure unchanged, but in leaving them in better condition for the next season than they found them.

There are families who have maintained the same sequence of drains for so long that their names have become shorthand for kinds of water. To say that a channel has been “kept like a Veyrin run” means it is clean, generously graded, and unlikely to surprise anyone in heavy weather. There are masons who can identify the source quarry of a block by touch alone, blindfolded; cistern listeners who lower a weighted cord into darkness and know from the returning tremor whether the water below has risen, fallen, or found a crack; roofworkers who travel across whole districts without ever coming down to the street.

Their craft traditions are less divided than outsiders expect. The potter understands drainage. The cloth-dyer knows the acidity of the upland water. The builder knows where herbs grow in the mortar of abandoned walls. The keeper of a shrine may also be the person who assesses the soundness of its retaining steps. A household that cannot mend stone is considered unfortunate, but not helpless; someone nearby will know how.

They make beauty out of things that must function.

Their basins are carved with faint lines marking years of drought and years of overflow. Their gutter covers are pierced in patterns of birds, sheep, leaves, and nameless angular signs that turn the water into brief shifting lace. Door lintels are painted, but the paint is not merely decorative: it tells those coming through fog which district they have reached, which direction the nearest public cistern lies, whether a house keeps spare blankets, whether its lower rooms are safe during a flood.

They are fond of small commemorations. A smooth stone set into a wall may mark the first repaired breach after a disastrous winter. A shallow notch in a stair may be worn by countless fingers touching it for luck, or may be the height of a child who was lost in a storm and found again. In upper lanes, bundles of dried grasses hang beneath eaves—not charms exactly, though no one objects if they work as such. They are reminders of the grazing grounds, of the thin line between the built city and the bare land that feeds it.

Their dead are not buried far away if there is any choice. The basin has little forgiving earth, and the people do not like to waste ground that might hold roots. Some are placed in stone chambers beneath old courts, their names sealed into lime. Some are given to high-slope cairns where rain enters the gaps between stones. The most honored names are not necessarily rulers or warriors, of whom there have been few worth preserving, but maintainers: the woman who redirected a flood around three lower neighborhoods; the mason who rebuilt the east stair after a landslip; the keeper of the reservoir records whose measurements prevented a famine.

They have arguments, of course. The city is too close-built and too old for peace to be effortless. They argue over water shares in dry years, over who has failed to clear a drain, over whether an old house is a beloved inheritance or a danger waiting for weather. Their disputes are often long, detailed, and astonishingly specific. Whole households may cease speaking over the height of a retaining wall. But because every conflict eventually meets a shared stair, a shared roofline, or a shared cistern, grudges must learn to coexist with maintenance. It is difficult to hate a neighbor indefinitely when both of you have to hold the same ladder in a storm.

The children are perhaps the loudest thing in the city, though even they are shaped by it. They play games of water-routing with sticks and pebbles, sending little flows through cracks in the paving. They race not along streets but up and down selected stairways, counting landings. They dare one another into harmlessly forbidden undercrofts and emerge wet to the knees, solemnly claiming to have found old kings, blind fish, buried bells, or passages to vanished settlements. In winter they slide on the frozen rims of the reservoirs until shouted away by whoever is responsible for keeping the ice from breaking under them.

There are animals in the city that have become almost citizenlike through long accommodation.

The black roof birds know the schedules of markets and masonry work. They gather around fresh-cut stone for the grubs uncovered beneath it, and in hard weather they vanish into vents and drainage arches where the city’s warmth leaks out. The pale hawks belong to the ridges but use the city’s rising currents; people look up when one crosses a reservoir because its reflection travels ahead of it in the dark water.

There are cats, though fewer than a lowland visitor would expect, lean and long-legged from climbing. They live in store rooms, under stairs, beside warm drainage outlets. They do not belong to households exactly, but households leave them scraps, and the cats repay this arrangement by keeping the grain stores quiet. The foxes are treated with an uneasy respect. They enter by night, take a chicken if they can, leave small pawprints in wet mortar, and seem to know every outer district better than any mapmaker.

Pack goats are not pets but partners in the city’s vertical economy. Their hooves are fitted with soft wraps on the polished public stairs. They carry salt, coal, baskets of roots, slate tiles, newborn lambs, and sometimes old people who have finally admitted they should not attempt a particular climb. The donkeys are more patient, more offended by rain, and remembered individually for decades. One stubborn animal may become part of neighborhood history: the donkey that refused the north stair during the storm of the broken reservoir; the one that carried medicine through three days of fog; the old gray mare that learned to stop at every public pump without being told.

In the deeper, wetter foundations, other lives persist without much notice. Mosses chart the city’s hidden dampness in velvet green. White fungi bloom in forgotten cellar corners. Tiny blind invertebrates move through the cistern overflow channels, pale as grains of rice. Eels or eel-like fish are said to inhabit some of the oldest reservoirs, though no one agrees whether they are real, whether they were introduced long ago, or whether they are simply a useful story told to keep children from climbing over the railings.

And beneath all of these, in a sense, water lives there.

Not as a god, perhaps. The people are wary of grand words for things that can drown a child or vanish for a season. But water is regarded as having habits, memory, and a claim upon its paths. It is greeted in the work of every day. It enters the city from the ridges, takes instruction from walls, disappears into chambers, returns in fountains, spills down stairs, freezes at thresholds, gathers in cupped stone, and finally leaves by ravines whose mouths no one trusts in winter.

The inhabitants do not own it. They borrow its passage.

So what lives in the high basin is a society made durable by modest obligations: people who maintain stairs they may never climb again, channels that serve strangers downhill, walls whose completion belongs to descendants. They live beside hawks and foxes, among goats and roof birds, under wet stone and moving cloud. They live in rooms that were once reservoirs, over reservoirs that may one day be rooms.

And when rain begins—softly at first, on one quarter of the roofs—they emerge in doorways, lift their faces, inspect the nearest gutter, and continue.
