What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense, nor a single great organism that can be pointed to and named.

It is a commonwealth of plants that have forgotten how to be separate.

The mats, towers, fans, bundled trunks, reed fields, and swelling bulbs are all distinct in shape and function, but beneath the surface they share more than roots. Their fibers touch, graft, exchange fluids, pass mineral salts, sugars, heat, and alarms. A wound opened in one slope is sensed far away as a change in pressure or chemistry. Growth is diverted toward it. Spores are released. Anchor-roots begin their slow crossing. The plateau closes itself.

Individual forms do exist. One may die: a tower may collapse in wind, a rotating crown may freeze at its hub, a pale-stemmed grove may drown when a channel changes course. But death is absorbed as material rather than recognized as departure. The dead become scaffolding, wet pulp, dark feed for new tissues. Nothing is mourned because nothing fully leaves.

The oldest inhabitants are probably not the largest visible plants, but the buried networks: cords of black-green fiber running under slopes and through mineral shelves; pale filaments threaded through the roots of everything else; soft vascular masses in the deep basin where water rises and falls. They are slow enough to be mistaken for geology. Some may have been alive before the present valleys took their shape. They do not move in ways an eye can easily catch, but they direct movement everywhere.

The tall mist-towers are their organs of thirst. The fan crowns are their organs of weather. The reed basin is perhaps their lung, or heart, though neither word quite applies. Its sinking and rising is not breath in the animal sense. It is pressure moving through a body too broad to know its own outline.

Among this immense vegetal labor, small lives have made accommodations.

The nearly unseen grazers are soft-bodied, cold-tolerant creatures, gray or green enough to vanish against wet moss. They live in the warm corridors between root-fields, where fermentation beneath the litter gives off a little heat. Their mouths are narrow and precise, made for shaving only the youngest leaf margins. They do not chew deeply into any one plant. To take too much would be to lose the shelter that keeps them alive. Their clipped trails are not damage so much as pruning, and the plants may use them: grazed edges branch more thickly; grazers carry spores in the folds of their feet; their droppings make small dark islands of richer soil.

There are insects in the pools, though they have become more like floating seeds than animals. Transparent, hinged things cling beneath the rosettes. They drink from the undersides of leaves where condensed water gathers nutrients. At certain times they emerge in brief, nearly silent clouds and mate within the sheltered vapor over the water. Their wings are too fine to make a sound. Their bodies are eaten almost immediately by the carnivorous blue-leaved plants along the shore.

Higher up, under the pale trunks, there may be larger creatures that no one would readily call animals.

They move only when weather shifts. They are lengths of furred root and folded leaf, perhaps, or colonies of small grazers joined beneath a traveling cloak of moss. In stillness they resemble fallen vegetation. In motion they make a deliberate, low ripple through the thickets, parting stems without breaking them. They feed on rot, fungal swellings, and the soft interiors of exhausted towers. If threatened, they flatten themselves into the root-mesh and become indistinguishable from it.

The black birds that cross above the cloudline are visitors rather than residents. They come for the seasonal bursts of pollen insects, or for the warm vapor issuing from the hollow trunks. They do not land often. The plateau offers no branches in the usual sense, no dry stone, no dependable open ground. A bird that settled carelessly might find its feet slowly gripped by closing leaves and exploratory tendrils.

There are also things that live in the flows.

The hidden channels carry pale worms with frilled bodies, blind shrimp-like forms, slow colonies of gelatinous feeders that pulse in tannin water. They inhabit the dark below the apparent land, where roots form ceilings and walls. They feed on decaying fibers and on the fine particulate soil the plateau manufactures endlessly. When a buried channel floods, some of them are forced upward into pools; their faint moving lights, seen beneath floating rosettes, might be mistaken for reflections.

But the dominant inhabitant is the process itself.

It lives in compression, in circulation, in the continual conversion of cold water and mineral stain into leaf, root, peat, and new ground. It is not intelligent in a manner that would recognize a face or answer a question. Yet it has preferences. It reinforces certain slopes. It keeps some lanes warm. It turns the wind-facing crowns toward mist. It permits a ravine to deepen and refuses another. It remembers injury as altered growth.

A traveler entering the high country would eventually feel watched, though there would be no eyes.

Not watched by an animal hiding in foliage, nor by a mind standing apart from the land, but attended to by the whole fabric of contact. Their warmth would change the condensation. Their steps would compress fibers and interrupt water routes. Their breath would add strange carbon and salt to the vapor. Small leaves would orient toward the disturbance. Spores would adhere to clothing. Warm corridors might soften beneath their feet, not opening exactly, but yielding along paths already prepared by centuries of flowing water and moving pollen.

If the traveler remained, the land would begin to use them.

It would take the heat first, then the shed skin, the hair, the crumbs, the metal taste of tools left in damp ground. It would learn the shape of their pressure. Moss would creep over their camp. Roots would gather beneath the places where they slept. Their footprints would fill, darken, green, and disappear.

Not out of malice.

The plateau has no emptiness in it, and no category for anything that is not eventually joined.
