What lives there is not quite a plant, though the chamber would insist that it is.

It is young in the way the hollow is young: unfinished, overgrown with possibilities, still deciding which of its parts are necessary. It has no fixed body. At one time it is a knot of pale roots gathered around the black water; at another it is the pressure behind a patch of moss, a thickening in the wall, a vine laid deliberately across an opening. It does not move from place to place so much as persuade the place to move around it.

The roots are its first limbs. Not all roots—only certain ones, distinguishable by their unnatural attention. They turn toward warmth that is not sunlight. They pause against stones. They curl around objects with enough gentleness to test their shape, then either bury them, split them, or keep them. A root of its body may emerge smooth and green-white from the wall, make a slow blind arc through the air, and settle upon the floor. By morning it may have become a rigid support, a drinking tube, a net of fine hairs, or nothing at all: reclaimed, dissolved, fed back into the wall.

It has a center only when it needs one.

Then, beneath the roots, there can be seen something like a small animal crouched in the dark soil: a pale, pear-shaped mass of leaf tissue and soft bark, no larger than a sleeping hare. It has folds that suggest shoulders, a blunt downward-facing head, perhaps several heads nested inside one another. From its back rise unfinished fronds, colorless at first, gradually becoming green where the filtered daylight finds them. Its underside is webbed into the earth by hundreds of thread roots. If uncovered, it trembles. If touched, it contracts with startling speed and becomes a mere root burl, featureless and convincing.

There are no eyes on its body, unless the water depression is one.

When the thing wishes to attend, the black skin of water stills. The root above it begins to drip more slowly. The green-gold light gathers around the little pool and deepens. Then every surface of the chamber seems to listen at once: leaflets turn, fungal threads withdraw into the moss, sap changes direction in the clear stems. The creature sees through dampness, pressure, vibration, changes in the taste of air. A visitor is known not by shape but by weight, heat, breath, and the minute falling of skin flakes onto the soil.

It is especially interested in hands.

Not with hunger, at least not at first. Hands make separations. They move one thing away from another. They hold a root apart, turn over a stone, carry water. The resident of the hollow has begun to imitate this understanding. The stripped thorn stems near the pool are likely its work. It has learned that a stiff stem placed across a swelling root can delay the root’s closing. It has learned that stones retain warmth. It has learned, imperfectly, that a sharpened thing can enter bark and make sap come out.

Its tools do not remain tools because it does not understand possession. Once used, they are fed. The stem grows. The stone is warmed. The bone is wrapped and given back a pulse. In time, perhaps, the creature will make implements that are alive enough to return when called: thorn needles that crawl through mulch, root wedges that expand at a signal, leaf cups that close around rainwater and carry it uphill.

It may have begun as a seed that fell into a crack before the hill had fully formed. Or as a graft where several unrelated plants met in darkness and failed to remain separate. Or as an injury in the root system: a place where growth, encountering too much growth, turned inward and became aware of its own obstruction.

It does not think in images, language, or plans. Its nearest equivalent to thought is a sustained pattern of growing. Curiosity is a root extended into unfamiliar soil. Fear is the sudden hardening of every tender surface. Memory is not stored in a brain but in recurring arrangements: the same radial stems laid around water; the same moss panels cultivated along the lower wall; the same opening kept from sealing, season after season, though no sunlight reaches it directly.

It may not know that it is alone.

The blind cricket is tolerated because it carries soil in the joints of its legs and because its tapping tells the chamber about distances beyond the roots. Sometimes the creature opens a seam for it. Sometimes, after the cricket has gone, a pale tendril explores the tiny prints it left behind, tracing them with reverence. Larvae are permitted in the seedpods because their feeding makes hollows. Mites are allowed in the moss because they move spores. Anything that only consumes without rearranging is quietly absorbed.

A person entering the chamber would not be attacked. Not immediately. The place would become more attentive. The warm air would thicken. New leaves would open around the visitor’s ankles and test the smell of cloth, metal, blood, smoke. A hanging root might brush the back of a hand. Water would rise upward once or twice, not as a display, but because the chamber had temporarily forgotten which way it had been flowing.

If the visitor were gentle, if they sat still and did not tear at the walls, the resident might make room for them.

A root would ease aside. Moss would soften into a cushion. The green light would settle. Perhaps the pale knot beneath the soil would surface nearby, only partially, trembling with the effort of becoming visible. It would offer something: a bead of clear sap, a newly opened seedpod, a cup-shaped leaf full of rainwater saved from the last storm.

And if the visitor returned often enough, the hollow would begin to grow around the idea of them.

It would preserve the shape where they sat. It would learn the rhythm of their footsteps. It might cultivate a passage through the hill, not for escape or welcome exactly, but because it had discovered that certain absences can be kept open.

In this way, what lives beneath the hill may eventually become larger than the chamber and less distinguishable from it. The hollow is its body. The hill is its unfinished body. The daylight trapped in the roots is its slow, distributed mind.

For now, though, it is small.

It is learning how to hold an empty space without filling it.
