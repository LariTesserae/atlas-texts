What lives on the plateau is not one people, though the people who work there tend to speak of it that way.

They say *the plateau* as if it were an employer, a weather system, an old animal, a country with no capital. They say the plateau is hungry, the plateau is listening, the plateau has closed a road. They do not mean it metaphorically, not entirely.

The human inhabitants are called many things elsewhere: pumpkeepers, rail hands, marsh surveyors, fungal gardeners, cablewrights, silt workers, weather clergy. Most have no use for these distinctions. A person may spend the early shift clearing algae from a turbine intake, the middle hours carrying messages along a flooded rail line, and the night sitting silently in a watch hut beside a pool that must not be measured. Work is not divided by profession so much as by what the ground permits that week.

They live in small stations rather than towns. A station may be three metal buildings on piles above a steaming bog, connected by catwalks and wire handrails. It may be a dozen houses inside an abandoned filter plant, each family occupying a former sedimentation chamber behind curtains and sheet walls. Some are built into cliff faces, where old inspection tunnels have widened into warm, dripping neighborhoods. Others cling to railway embankments, their foundations continually adjusted as the peat shifts beneath them.

No settlement is permanent in the ordinary sense. Children grow up knowing which rooms will flood in the long rains, which roofs need re-lashing before thunder season, which walls are only walls until the moss gets too thick and begins to move them. Buildings are dismantled, floated, hauled by cable, or simply abandoned with their lamps still hanging inside. The plateau reuses them. A derelict signal box becomes a nesting place for blue beetles. A workshop roof collapses and grows a forest. A flooded dormitory fills with blind fish that feed on the pale residue of old insulation.

The people are shaped by this impermanence. They build lightly where they can and heavily where they must. They distrust straight lines on maps. They memorize sound.

A good worker can tell, from the vibration through a boot sole, whether a rail is carrying a train, a pressure wave, or something walking beside the embankment beneath the mud. A good marsh guide can distinguish rain from cliff-sweat by the smell of it. A child can hear when an exhaust fan has changed pitch because a winged hunter has nested in the ducting. Old people listen to the nighttime calls and know which ones are animals, which are rupturing stone, and which must be answered by shutting down machinery.

They have a language dense with forms of wetness. There are words for water that has touched iron, water that has passed through roots, water that is safe before dawn but not after, water that carries spores, water that remembers a buried channel. There are words for the shades of industrial light in fog: lamp-light that guides, lamp-light that lies, lamp-light that has been reflected off a creature’s skin.

They seldom name the largest beings directly.

These are not gods, exactly. Nor are they animals in any comforting sense. The plateau’s great presences move in ways too slow, too broad, or too intermittent to fit neatly into either category. Sometimes one crosses a marsh, leaving a stepped basin of warm glowing water behind it. Sometimes a line of forest rises and settles over the course of an hour, as though something beneath the canopy has changed its position. Sometimes the deep call comes from so far away that it seems to travel through the pipes and bedrock before it reaches the ear.

The workers call these beings by functions, if they call them anything: the Wader, the High Back, the One Under the Roots, the Cliff-Breather.

No one claims to have seen one whole.

The long-necked marsh herds are believed to follow their paths. The luminous beetles gather around their footprints. Certain fungi bloom only in soil compressed by their passing. Some industrial crews have found their pipelines bent upward from below, not crushed but gently lifted, as if an immense hand had decided not to break them.

The plateau people do not worship these things. Worship would imply confidence in being noticed. Instead they practice courtesy.

They ring bells in the forest. They keep lamps low near reed fog. They do not drain pools that swell without weather. They leave portions of every harvest untouched: a tray of mineral silt at the edge of a settling pond, a warm basket of fungus cakes on a maintenance platform, a spool of bright copper wire hung from a pylon. These offerings are often gone by morning, though whether taken by animals, scavengers, workers from another station, or something else is not discussed.

There are also those who are less visibly human.

In the hot galleries where fungal mats are cultivated, pale maintenance creatures have become accustomed to the workers. They were once thought to be a kind of lizard, though they have too many joints and a disturbing talent for finding leaks before instruments can detect them. They live in the walls, feeding on mold, cable insulation, and the soft mineral crust around pressure valves. They make a faint chiming sound when content. The fungal gardeners tolerate them, even feed them, because a silent gallery is considered dangerous.

In the drowned terraces live the glass-backed crabs, some broad enough to carry a lantern on their shells. They scrape mineral scales from roots and culvert walls. Children catch the small ones and paint signals on them, releasing them near flooded crossings; when the crabs return to shallow water, their shells carry traces of the chemistry below.

In the old rail tunnels, there are colonies of blind, furred things that live on warm condensation and the insects drawn to signal lights. They have faces almost human in their arrangement, which has made them unlucky. No one kills them now. It is said that in the first industrial years, a foreman ordered a nest cleared from a tunnel and lost three trains in a week to floods, derailments, and a darkness that no lamp could penetrate. Whether the story is true is irrelevant. The nests remain.

The most respected residents may be the mosswrights.

They are people, mostly, though many are descended from families who have lived so long in the wet high country that outsiders find them difficult to read. Their skin is often marked with pale fungal scars and mineral staining; their hair is kept cropped, braided, or wrapped against the spores. They tend the boundary between structure and vegetation. They decide which moss may cover a pipe, which vine may be trained over a roof, which fungus will eat oil residue without spreading into the sleeping rooms.

A mosswright can look at a green stain on concrete and tell whether the building will survive another decade. They know how to make insulation from reed pulp and animal hair, how to persuade roots away from a foundation, how to seed a landslide with plants whose tangled underground fibers will hold it together. The factories call them contractors when paperwork is required. Everyone else calls them before they call engineers.

They are often accompanied by small black dogs with reflective collars. The dogs are not pets in the sentimental sense. They are path-keepers. They refuse certain ground, bark at lights that move incorrectly, and will sit facing a closed door for hours if something is passing on the other side of it.

There are children everywhere, though visitors often fail to see them at first. They are taught not to run toward voices in fog, not to touch water without asking what kind, not to stand beneath an unfamiliar cable. They play on the high, dry parts of rail embankments and in the upper levels of machinery sheds. They build tiny shrines from washers, beetle shells, and fragments of colored safety glass. They keep beetles in jars, learn the schedules of the freight lines by ear, and dare one another to put a hand on the warm walls of the oldest pump houses.

Some eventually leave for lower, brighter cities. Many return after years away. Those who stay develop the particular patience of the plateau: an ability to wait through rain, to repair what will fail again, to recognize that being surrounded by vast unknowable systems does not excuse one from cleaning a filter, mending a boot, comforting a frightened child, or shutting down a pump when the forest calls.

And beneath all of these lives something older than the work camps, older than the rail lines, older perhaps than the plateau’s present shape.

It does not live *under* the land exactly. It lives in the exchanges between heat, water, pressure, root, and stone. It is present in the warm springs that appear overnight, in the sinkholes that open with no warning, in the subtle changes to the course of underground rivers. It might be a distributed organism. It might be the land’s own metabolism. It might be nothing more than a pattern so immense that minds small enough to walk upon it mistake the pattern for intention.

The old maintenance records refer to it only as the Deep System.

The oldest residents call it the Host.

They do not imagine it kind. Kindness is too personal a quality. But neither do they think it cruel. It has allowed forests to grip its skin, marshes to gather in its hollows, animals to follow its warmth, people to tap its steam and raise lamps in its darkness. It accepts intrusion as it accepts rainfall: by absorbing it, changing around it, and remaining.

That is why the people who live there do not say they own the plateau.

They say they have been given another shift.
