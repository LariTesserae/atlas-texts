What lives there is a people accustomed to sufficiency.

They are not austere by principle, though strangers sometimes mistake them for it. They enjoy sweetness when it can be had, a bright scrap of cloth, a well-told account of some fool in the lowlands, the rare pear softened beside a stove. But they have lived too long where every useful thing must be carried, caught, patched, dried, or remembered to confuse abundance with comfort. Comfort, to them, is a roof stone that does not shift in wind, a barrel hoop that holds through winter, a child who comes home before fog closes the paths.

There may be two hundred of them in the town, perhaps fewer in a bad year, perhaps more after one of the valley families sends a daughter uphill to marry or a caravan leaves behind someone who decides not to go back down. No household is entirely separate from another. Walls meet walls. Roofs overlap. Smoke from one kitchen enters another’s courtyard. A person can spend a lifetime saying they are only going out to borrow a pinch of salt and return after dark knowing the illness of three elders, the progress of a roof repair, and who has been avoiding whom.

They know one another by use before personality. This is the woman who can set a bone straight. This is the man whose goats will follow him through sleet. This is the girl who remembers every jar in the lower storehouse and can tell, by tapping a lid, whether its grain has taken damp. This is the old one who knows which side of the pass keeps snow longest. This is the household with the good kettle. This is the boy whose hands are too clever with latches and should not be trusted near the communal lockbox.

Their names are used, certainly, but in the town names gather work around them. A name is rarely alone. It becomes Mara-of-the-spring, Oren-who-mends-sieves, Tavi-from-the-red-door, Sel-the-younger, even once Sel-the-younger has gray in her braid and her own grandchildren on the steps outside her house. Nobody considers this rude. It is how a life is kept from being lost among the lives beside it.

The people are leaner than those below, generally, and weathered early. Their faces have the fine creases made by glare, wind, and looking into distance. They wrap wool around their necks even in seasons visitors would call mild. Their clothes are repaired so often that a coat may contain the work of a grandmother, two parents, and several children: elbows of darker cloth, cuffs turned twice, buttons replaced with bone, horn, carved wood, or little loops of cord. The finest garments are not the newest but the oldest things still whole—wedding shawls, a black coat worn by three generations of mourners, a pair of boots resoled so many times that only the shape of the original leather remains.

Children live everywhere in the lanes, though they are taught early not to run near the cistern, not to climb the fog screens, not to frighten goats along the main ascent, not to waste a candle merely to see their own shadows move. They play with pebbles, knucklebones, thread spools, broken pottery, and small carved animals whose legs have long since snapped off. They race water down the channels after rain. They dare each other to touch the wet stones around the spring. In dry weeks they become quieter, as if the whole town has taught them, without saying so, that thirst is not something to make noise around.

The old live visibly among everyone else. There is little room to set them apart, and no habit of doing so. An elder whose knees no longer trust the stairs is moved to a lower room; a window is widened; a stool is placed in the patch of midday sun. They shell beans, twist rope, sort nails, tell children which plant can be eaten and which only smells as though it should be. They remember who owned a house before its current wall was built through the middle of it. They remember winters when the spring thinned to a thread, summers when the pear trees bore enough fruit for jam, years when the dead were buried too close together in the north field.

They do not speak much of history as a grand procession. History, in the town, is practical. It is the reason the third cistern lid has two handles. It is why no one stores grain against the eastern wall. It is why the shrine’s basin is never emptied completely, even for cleaning. A stranger may hear someone say, “We do not do that since the year of the cracked barrels,” and learn that this is explanation enough.

The keeper of the water is not a ruler, though people listen when she speaks. The work passes from one careful person to another, usually after years of assisting: measuring the cistern with a marked rod, inspecting seals, recording each household’s draw on narrow tablets, deciding when washing is permitted and when it is not. The keeper’s hands are often chalked pale from mineral residue. Everyone complains about the keeper in private, especially during dry months. Everyone becomes protective of the keeper if an outsider questions her judgment.

There are other offices, some formal and some merely inherited through competence. A bell is rung when someone dies, though it is a small bell and its sound is swallowed quickly by the ravine. Someone keeps the gate beam smooth. Someone has the key to the grain room. Someone knows the old songs used for walking in fog, where each line ends in a word that carries farther than ordinary speech. Someone maintains the grave field, straightening slabs after frost and making sure that no new grave cuts too close to an old one.

The dead live there as well, though not in the manner of hauntings.

They live in the habits they left: a particular way of stacking slate to keep wind out, a recipe for nettle broth, a shelf worn down by a hand that reached for it every evening. They live in names given to children who bear no close resemblance to them. They live under the north wall, each with one upright stone, where the earth is so thin that digging is slow and every burial is a work shared by many hands. The town does not imagine the dead have gone far. There is nowhere nearby to go that they did not already know.

At the spring, something else lives: the moss, the mineral-dark water, the small pale insects that cling under the basin rim. Children say the spring has a temper. Adults say it has a course, a pressure, and a source somewhere in the mountain. Both are careful around it. No one spits into the water. No one draws from it without a reason. On certain mornings, when fog has settled low and the basin is overflowing by the width of a fingernail, people leave a twist of herb or a clean white pebble on the shrine ledge—not as payment exactly, nor prayer exactly, but as acknowledgment.

The goats belong to households, but only approximately. They know their own doors, their own children, and the sound of the buckets in which peelings are carried. Yet a goat in trouble belongs to whoever sees it first. The old dog belongs to no one so clearly that people have stopped trying to say. It sleeps in a different doorway each week, eats where it is fed, and appears whenever a lamb is caught in thorn or a stranger comes up the path after dark. Its muzzle has gone white. Its hearing is poor. It has outlived several expected endings.

Ravens live above the settlement with a more complete independence. They know the refuse pit, the grain room vents, the moments after slaughter, the wind patterns along the cliff. A raven’s presence is not read as omen unless it behaves unusually, and ravens behave unusually often enough that this distinction occupies more conversation than it should.

There are mice in the walls, beetles under stored wood, spiders in the corners of shutters, and pale little moths that emerge from wool chests when they ought not. There are lice, too, and everyone is familiar with them without discussing them much. There are no creatures so grand that the town can afford to pretend itself untouched by smaller life.

What lives most deeply there, perhaps, is attention.

Attention to a cloud gathering beyond the pass. To the sound a barrel makes when lifted. To the change in a neighbor’s gait. To the color of the moss, the weight of a water jar, the date of the last rain marked with charcoal under a lintel. People elsewhere may call such attention caution, thrift, superstition, or narrowness. In the town it is simply the form affection takes when there is little room for error.

And beneath all this, in the joints of the walls and the hollows of the stairs, lives the town’s confidence that it will continue.

Not forever; nobody there is foolish enough to speak that word lightly. But through the coming frost, through another dry month, through the repair of the western retaining wall and the birth of a child whose first cry will be heard by three households at once. Through the next market and the next burial and the next thin rain ticking on slate.

It lives because its people have made a practice of remaining.
