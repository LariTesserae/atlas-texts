What lives in the High Dry Garden is not one people, nor one ecology, but a compact treaty of hungers.

The gardeners themselves are called, in the few languages that have names for them, the **Still-Walkers**. They do not call themselves that. They have no use for a name that can be spoken in calm air, because calm air is a temporary condition and therefore an unreliable witness. Their real names are sequences of taps, chime-notes, reflected flashes, and the particular way a person sets their weight against a gust.

An outsider might see only a cloak shifting beside a bell-tree and mistake it for shed foliage. A Still-Walker is often lean, broad-footed, and wrapped from head to ankle in overlapping plates harvested from the garden’s mineral leaves. The plates are polished on one side, dull on the other. In hot wind they wear the bright sides outward, becoming fragments of moving sky; in cold wind they turn the dark sides outward and vanish among the stone. Their faces are veiled not for modesty, but because breathing too deeply is considered rude to the garden. The air belongs first to the mouths of the plants.

Their skin is gray-brown from dust and mineral staining. Their hair, where it can be seen, is often threaded with copper wire, seed-filament, and the hollow quills of the winged creatures. Many wear little resonant tags at their wrists and throats: bits of bell-tree petal, cut bone, glassy root-stone. A person can be identified in a storm by the sound their body makes when struck by wind.

They are not farmers in the ordinary sense. They do not plant straight rows, weed, irrigate, or harvest according to seasons. They listen for pressure changes in the crater walls. They map the migrations of wiregrass. They crack open old root-stone and bury lightning-char beneath it. They move nests away from vents before the vents begin their breathing. They prop wounded bell-trees with ribbed frames of bone and stone, not to save the tree—which may regrow in a day—but to keep its falling crown from crushing a shelter or silencing a pasture.

Their oldest skill is not cultivation. It is **timing**.

A child of the High Dry Garden learns to tell, from the feel of grit against their teeth, whether a storm will charge the tower or split it. They learn which ringing of the bell-trees means ripeness, which means pain, which means that something large is moving under the root-stone. They learn never to stand between two blue bushes when they are glowing, because the bushes occasionally exchange lightning through anything taller than the grass.

The children are raised in the low shelters, though “raised” is not quite right. They grow in intervals, tucked into the pauses between wind events. Infants sleep in hammocks made of cured orange-dish skin, which shrinks tightly around them when static rises. Toddlers are tied to stone posts with soft braided grass until they learn not to chase the rolling creatures. Older children are sent out in pairs with mirrored masks to herd the ash-gray grazers away from the lightning gardens. Their first independent task is to retrieve a single seed-kernel after an explosive flowering without being cut, burned, or lured downhill by the vent-winds.

Few of them die in childhood from hunger. The Garden is generous in its harsh fashion. They die from misjudging movement.

Their dead are not buried. The ground is too dry, too restless, and too full of roots that would turn a body into a mineral scaffold within days. Instead, the dead are taken to the outer slopes beneath the crater walls, where the wiregrass gathers naturally after storms. There they are wrapped in leaf-scales and laid among the moving red loops. By morning the wiregrass has usually drawn a veil over them. Over weeks, the garden takes what it can: salts, bone, cloth, metal, memories carried in the scent of resin and smoke. Eventually a pale knot of root-stone forms where the body lay.

Those stones are kept. A household may have three or four ancestor-stones set into its shelter floor. They are warm during electrical weather. Sometimes they ring faintly.

The Still-Walkers believe the dead do not become plants or animals. That would be too simple. They become **directions**. A grandmother becomes the warning felt in a wind from the east. A hunter becomes the impulse not to follow a wounded grazer into the haze. A child becomes the sudden turning of a chime when no wind is moving it. To live well is to make yourself useful to the garden after you have stopped moving through it.

There are perhaps several dozen people in the crater at any one time. Perhaps a hundred, after a quiet season. They do not keep a fixed count. Counting is dangerous: it encourages the belief that the Garden is made of separate things.

Yet the human inhabitants are not the strangest citizens.

Beneath the bell-trees live the **root listeners**: pale, flat-bodied creatures like jointed hands with too many fingers. They are rarely seen above ground. During still periods they press themselves against the undersides of root-stones, absorbing vibration. When bell-trees ring, the listeners emerge in clusters and climb the trunks, laying their bodies across cracks in the mineral petals. They do not repair the trees exactly. They tune them. A bell-tree inhabited by listeners gives a lower, cleaner note and is less likely to shatter in a storm.

The gardeners leave the listeners offerings of sweet blue nodules and the powdered shells of the rolling animals. In return, the listeners warn of deep tremors. Their warning is subtle: every listener in a grove turns its pale underside toward the same point on the ground. The Still-Walkers have learned to see this even through haze.

There are also the **glass moths**, though they are neither glass nor moth. They emerge only after green lightning. They are translucent, palm-sized things with four angular wings and bodies that glow dimly from stored charge. By day they cling to cliff faces and look like flakes of mica. At night they drift through the crater in great slow clouds, settling on the orange dishes and drinking whatever light the dishes have collected.

A glass moth that lands on bare skin leaves a black branching mark, painless at first. The mark fades after several days, unless the person has recently touched lightning-char. Then it remains for years, a delicate map of veins across the hand or face. Such marks are considered fortunate, though the marked are forbidden to climb the tower for a full turning of the season. Lightning recognizes its own handwriting.

The winged creatures nesting under the rim are called **bowl-kites** by travelers, but the gardeners call them the Rim’s Children. They are not hunted except in famine, and even then only those found dead after storms are used. Their bones make the best wind-chimes because they retain a memory of height. A chime cut from a bowl-kite’s wing-bone does not merely indicate wind direction; it distinguishes between air entering the crater, air leaving it, and air that has passed over the old vents.

The adults are unpleasant, powerful things: blind in the copper haze, guided by heat and echo, with hooked feet and mouths full of small crushing teeth. They circle endlessly because landing is difficult and because the garden’s rising currents carry edible insects and seed-filaments toward the rim. The young scream when wind shifts because they have not yet learned that the world can move without breaking.

Sometimes an old bowl-kite falls.

When it does, the entire crater notices. The bell-trees close. The wiregrass pulls away in red ripples. The ash-gray rollers gather at a distance, inflate themselves, and wait. The larger grazers stand with their translucent chests brightening and dimming. Even the people stop moving.

Then the carcass is claimed in stages.

First come the small grinding creatures, stripping resin from the wing membranes. Then the root listeners, if the body lies near a grove, come up through the powder and enter through the eyes. Last come the gardeners, who take only the bones that have not been cracked and the tendon-sinew needed for nets. They leave the skull untouched. A bowl-kite skull, they say, must stay open to the sky.

And in the vents downhill—where no one follows the rolling creatures—there may be something older.

The vents do not breathe on any schedule that humans can predict. Most are narrow black seams between rocks, exhaling hot mineral air that smells of old coins. But a few, after storms, make sounds. Not whistles or pressure groans: voices, almost. Long inhalations followed by tones too low to hear properly, felt instead in the teeth and sternum.

The Still-Walkers place stones over these vents. The stones are carved with hands, spirals, and shut eyes. Each year some stones crack from underneath.

No one claims to know what lives below. The gardeners say only that the Garden has roots deeper than its plants, and that some roots remember fire.

Occasionally, after a violent storm, a thing is found near a vent: a shed skin of black translucent material, a spiral shell with no creature inside it, a clutch of hard red eggs that never hatch but grow warm when held. Such things are not brought into the shelters. They are taken to the tower and placed beneath its copper crown, where lightning may decide whether they belong to the world above.

The High Dry Garden’s true inhabitants may therefore be the wind itself.

It is everywhere: in the plant mouths, in the translucent lungs of the grazers, in the taut membranes of the bowl-kites, in the throat-chimes of the people. It carries minerals that become leaves, charge that becomes food, spores that become weather, and voices that become warnings. The Garden does not resist the wind. It has made a civilization from being scoured by it.

So the place lives through countless small acts of listening.

A bell-tree closing one crystal petal before a discharge.

A herd spacing itself farther apart as dust thickens.

A child hearing the wrong note in a roof-chime and waking the household.

A root listener turning beneath the ground.

A cloaked figure, nearly invisible in the copper haze, standing beside the lightning tower with one hand on the stone, waiting to feel whether the next storm will feed the Garden or try to erase it.
