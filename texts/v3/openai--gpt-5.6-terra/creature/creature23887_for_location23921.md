What lives there is not a settled community so much as a succession of specialists: creatures able to use a place before it is fully a place.

Closest to the stone are the smallest lives. Films of bacteria darken wet gravel and loosen minerals grain by grain. Algae cloud the edges of shallow pools, even where ice returns at night. In the black paste between stones, fungi extend invisible threads through what little organic matter the wind, water, and animals have delivered. A dead sedge blade, a tuft of winter-killed hair, the body of an insect caught in a rain channel: nothing remains insignificant for long. It is taken apart, slowly, under cold water.

The mosses are among the basin’s first patient inhabitants. They do not require much depth of soil, only a crease that stays damp, the shaded underside of a stone, a shelf where wind cannot scour them entirely away. In their small green-brown cushions live mites, springtails, nematodes, larvae pale as rootlets. They are abundant beyond sight. A hand pressed into a moss bed might feel only cold wet softness, but under that softness is a dense traffic of feeding, burrowing, laying eggs, dying, and being eaten.

Flies are everywhere during the brief workable season. Midges rise in thin wavering columns above still pools when the air is calm enough. Crane flies stumble through the grass. Dark stoneflies run across water-slick surfaces and disappear into cracks. There are beetles beneath nearly every flat rock: black, hard-backed, quick to freeze motionless when exposed, then suddenly fast when the shadow returns. They feed on carrion, on insect eggs, on whatever the weather has killed and not carried away.

The pools have their own temporary populations. Some are deep enough to last through summer; others are no more than rain held in a hollow of bedrock. In the lasting pools, small aquatic larvae cling to submerged gravel, and tiny crustaceans pulse through brown-green water. Water beetles and backswimmers make a life in places that may vanish in a week. Their eggs endure the dry or the freeze. Their young hatch whenever the basin gives them enough water to begin again.

Birds make the place seem fuller than it is because they are always moving between its separate opportunities.

Along the wet margins, small ground-feeding birds work with quick, anxious purpose. They run rather than walk, pause upright, then lower their bills among sedge roots and moss. Their plumage is the color of wet earth and old lichen, so that they disappear whenever they stop. They nest in shallow scrapes tucked beside stones, trusting camouflage more than concealment. A sudden sleet squall can take a whole brood; another pair will try again if the season permits.

Higher up, where snow lies late in the gullies, there are birds that turn white when winter comes and mottled gray-brown when the ground opens. They feed on buds, tough leaves, insects, whatever can be found close to the surface. In bad weather they crouch behind rocks or press themselves into snow hollows, becoming almost part of the weathered landscape. Their existence is quiet, but they leave signs: neat tracks in fresh snow, droppings beneath a sheltering ledge, a scatter of feathers caught in heath.

The waterfowl are more conspicuous. They arrive in pairs or loose family groups, settling on broad pools with the caution of creatures that know open water offers no cover. Some graze the wet margins, tearing at sedges and submerged stems. Some tip headfirst into the brown water and come up dripping, their calls carrying over the basin. Their nests are often hidden in the only places that can hide anything: a fold of grass, a mossy shelf above flood level, the lee side of a boulder warmed by weak sun.

On the cliffs live birds that regard the basin as a feeding ground rather than a home. Ravens pass overhead with deliberate wingbeats, black marks crossing gray cloud. Smaller falcons hold themselves in the wind above the scree, then fold and drop toward a fleeing bird or rodent. In the higher fractures, where stone has enough ledges and old nesting material, larger raptors may keep a nest for years—not because the mountain is stable, but because the ledge happens not to have fallen yet. Their presence is known first by the sudden silence of smaller birds.

The mammals are shaped by concealment and repetition.

Hares live among the boulder fields and rough grass, choosing forms beneath overhangs or in shallow depressions that offer a little shelter from wind. Their coats shift with the season, though never perfectly enough to defeat the changing ground. In spring, when snow retreats in broken patches, they can look strangely unfinished: white at the flanks, brown across the back, neither hidden in snow nor stone. They feed at dusk and before dawn, leaving tracks that seem to begin and end without explanation among the rocks.

Small rodents occupy the protected parts of the basin with great energy. They tunnel where turf is thickest, use old gaps between stones as runways, and gather dry grass in chambers that remain barely above freezing. Their population rises quickly during good summers. Then the predators become more visible. A fox may cross the wet flats with its feet placed carefully between pools, listening more than looking. A stoat slips through the boulder margins, thin enough to enter the places rodents trust. In winter, both survive by knowing the old routes under snow and by finding the pockets where life has concentrated.

The ungulates are the basin’s most obvious claimants. They move across it with a practical confidence that makes the raw ground seem briefly domesticated. Their hooves find purchase on angled stone, packed mud, and narrow shelves where a human foot would hesitate. In the early season they descend to the wetter ground for new growth: sedges, grasses, the tender beginnings of heath. Later they spread onto higher slopes, browsing wherever the snow has recently withdrawn.

They are not numerous in a way that softens the landscape. A herd may be visible all morning as scattered pale shapes on a distant shelf, then vanish behind weather without ever coming close. Their paths accumulate year after year. A trail through scree becomes darker where hooves disturb fresh stone. A crossing at a stream becomes a churned black patch. A narrow ledge gains a ribbon of dung, shed hair, and trampled moss. The animals do not conquer the mountain; they simply repeat their necessary journeys until the mountain begins to remember them.

Some give birth in the quieter folds of the basin, where sightlines are long and escape routes multiply among rocks. The young are unsteady only briefly. A place like this does not permit a long infancy. They learn the texture of wet stone, the smell of approaching snow, the warning behavior of older animals. They learn that a slope can move beneath them and that fog can erase the herd five paces away.

There may be fish in a few of the more permanent waters, but only if some older connection carried them there: an outlet stream below the basin, a lake downstream, a route available before uplift or ice rearranged the drainage. Most pools are too young, too cold, too unreliable. They belong instead to insects, amphibians where the season allows them, and the birds that feed on both. A frog, if one lives here, is not a creature of the open basin. It survives in a precise wet pocket warmed by dark mud, returning each year to water that may have shifted a few yards since the last thaw.

In winter, much of this life withdraws without leaving.

The insects persist as eggs, pupae, or larvae beneath ice and stone. Rodents remain in tunnels under snow, feeding on stores and roots. Hares travel the hardened surface during the cold hours, their tracks blown half away by morning. Ungulates descend if the high shelves are buried or glazed beyond use. Birds leave in layers: first those dependent on open water and insects, then those unable to find food beneath snow. A few remain—the white birds of the gullies, ravens, perhaps some hardy scavenger moving from cliff to cliff—but the basin becomes less inhabited in the visible sense.

It is never empty. Water continues moving beneath ice. Fungal threads remain in the soil. Seeds lie against rock, waiting for a season that may not favor them for years. Roots hold a little ground together. Lichens continue their almost immeasurable work on exposed faces. Beneath a drift, a rodent breathes. In a crack under snow, an insect waits in a state so slowed it resembles death.

The living thing most characteristic of the basin may be the route rather than the individual creature: the narrow passage from snowfield to pool, from pool to sedge bed, from sedge bed to sheltered stone; the annual descent of hoofed animals; the bird corridor along the cliff base; the fox’s circuit of likely places; the drainage line that gathers larvae, seeds, feathers, and silt.

Everything there lives by reading change quickly.

No inhabitant expects permanence from a bank, a pool, a grass patch, a snow bridge, or a slope. They use what holds. They leave before it fails, if they can. And in their tracks, nests, tunnels, droppings, feathers, roots, and small buried bodies, they contribute to the slow manufacture of a more enduring country.
