What lives in the basin is not one creature, at first. It is a congregation of small survivals: things that prefer seepage to stream, shade that does not last all day, stone warmed from beneath and above.

The frogs are the easiest to name. They are dark, round-backed little animals, nearly the color of the peat, with throats pale as wet ash. They do not make a chorus. Each keeps its own pocket of water beneath moss or sedge roots and gives a single click when the conditions seem right—when a drop enters its pool, when a cloud opens, when the underground knocking stops. Their calls travel oddly in the bowl. A frog may be close enough to touch, yet sound as though it were hidden on the far wall; another may call from the rim and seem to speak directly beneath a listener’s feet.

There are pale lizards in the dry intervals between wetness. They have long, narrow heads, soft gray bellies, and backs patterned like the grain of old bone. In the morning they are slow and grave, laid across black stone with their toes spread wide, accepting warmth. By noon they move in swift, impossible little distances: present on one ledge, absent, then visible several arm-lengths away where there seemed to be no route between. They eat flies, moths, and the smaller creatures that nest in fern stems. They drink not from pools but from the undersides of leaves, taking water bead by bead.

In the central wet ground live creatures too small to distinguish without kneeling. Water beetles move through the brown cups of peat. Thread-thin larvae cling to submerged roots. Black snails, no larger than grains of barley, travel over stone in the hours after rain and vanish into cracks when the sun reaches them. There are pale worms in the saturated soil, translucent enough that their dark internal threads show faintly through their skins. They turn dead grass, fallen leaves, drowned insects, and the edges of old branches into the black, rich earth beneath the sedges.

A particular kind of midge hatches in the basin’s mist. It is almost invisible except when a low sun catches the cloud of it: a faint tremor above a pool, as though the reflection has begun to breathe. The adults do not travel far. They rise, circle, mate, settle, lay their eggs in water so shallow it scarcely seems capable of holding life, and disappear. The dragonflies hunt them.

The dragonflies are not truly transparent. They only appear to be when they are still.

At rest, their wings hold a thin film of the basin behind them: the green of sedge, the black shine of stone, the gray-blue shape of sky. When they shift, the image shifts a fraction after the wing. In flight this makes them difficult to follow. A dragonfly crosses a pool, and for one instant the pool seems to have a clean notch cut from it. The animal is elsewhere before the eye has restored what it expected to see.

They lay their eggs in the places where water goes briefly uphill.

There is a small hoofed animal that visits at night, though it does not belong to the basin in the way the frogs do. It is a mountain deer, perhaps, or something close enough that a traveler would use the same word. It is slight-bodied, brown in summer and nearly black when wet, with narrow hooves that leave a divided mark no bigger than a thumb joint. It comes down one of the easier outer slopes after dark and drinks from a particular central thread of water. It does not drink from the deepest pools.

It has learned, over generations or through some private intelligence, that the pools do not always return what leans over them.

A deer that drinks from the wrong one may lift its head startled, looking not toward the rim but toward a place among the stones where no path begins. It may stand listening for a long time, then leave before dawn by a route it did not use to enter. In the morning its tracks may approach the pool and stop. New tracks begin several paces away, pointed in the wrong direction.

Above the basin, in the loose stones and outward-growing trees, lives the dark bird. It is a small corvid, though its shape is leaner than a crow’s and its bill more finely made. Its feathers are black in shade but carry a greenish iron color when the sun catches them. It comes down rarely. Usually it crosses the open mouth of the bowl with purpose, calling once, as if announcing something to the stone, and goes on.

Occasionally one remains.

Then it will perch on a bare branch at the upper rim and watch the water for an hour without feeding. It tilts its head at the underground knock. When the knock comes, the bird’s feathers lie flat. It does not call again until the frogs begin.

There are nests in the basin, but they are difficult to find because the nest-makers have learned restraint. Small brown birds weave grass into hollows under the shrubs. Moths leave powdery eggs on the sheltered faces of fern stems. A mouse, or some relative of a mouse, keeps a dry chamber in the roots of a twisted tree and fills it with seeds gathered beyond the rim. It comes out at twilight and moves through the open places in fast, careful lines. It has a blunt nose, glossy eyes, and ears so thin that light shines pink through them. It knows every dry stone, every channel that can be crossed without sinking, every place where an owl’s shadow might arrive.

No owl is known to nest inside the bowl. Yet the mouse fears one.

Perhaps owls cross overhead. Perhaps the memory of them has become part of the animal’s shape.

The oldest inhabitants are not animals.

They are the mosses that seal water into stone. Some form cushions bright as newly cut fruit; others spread low and dark, their surfaces velvet-soft and cold beneath the hand. They are crowded with worlds: minute insects, spores, eggs, rootlets, the temporary drowned bodies of things that fell and could not climb out. Moss holds the basin together more quietly than any root. Where it grows thickly, the water is slowed. Where the water is slowed, peat begins. Where peat begins, the sedges take hold. Where sedges take hold, all the small creatures find places to hide.

The ferns are older-looking but less patient. They seem to arrive in sudden abundance wherever a seam opens and water makes its mind known. Their green is intense, almost excessive, in the narrow wet courses. Their curled new growth appears in spring like a row of small listening heads. In late summer their undersides darken with spores, and the basin’s air carries a bitter, green dust whenever wind reaches them.

The few trees at the rim possess their own kind of animal life. Their bark shelters tiny pale spiders and bark-colored beetles. In the forks of their roots, rainwater collects with leaf fragments and the bodies of drowned flies. Their leaves are eaten in neat, crescent bites by insects that emerge only after mist. Their roots hold the shallow earth in the mountain’s fingers. Each tree is alone, but their roots may meet below the stone, or may not. The basin gives no easy answer.

And below all of these—below sedge, peat, seep, black rock—there may be something that is not alive in the familiar sense, but is living enough to make the distinction feel thin.

The muted knock does not come from an animal’s body. It is too deep, too unhurried. It may be a stone shifting as water enters a cavity. It may be trapped air finding a route through ancient mineral channels. It may be the mountain settling itself by degrees too small to notice until sound is made.

But the living things hear it.

The frogs go silent. The lizards flatten themselves against the warm rock. The dark bird, if it is there, attends. Even the dragonflies alter their flight, rising slightly from the pools and holding in the air as though waiting for the basin to resume.

Then, after a while, a drop falls from the rim.

Another answers from a fern.

A frog clicks once beneath the moss.

And everything that lives there continues its slow, particular work: drinking, rooting, hatching, watching, dissolving, holding on.
