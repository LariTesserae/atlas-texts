What lives there has learned not to distinguish between machine and season.

The smallest lives are almost invisible until the mist comes. Then the black leaves acquire motion: pale thread-worms rise from the folds along their veins and drink from the beads of water. They are not worms precisely. Each has a ring of little gripping limbs and a translucent back through which a line of orange heat can be seen traveling from head to tail. In dry intervals they retreat into the plant tissue, becoming indistinguishable from a blemish or a seam. The birds eat them, though only after the mist; at other times the worms are too hot to touch.

Brass-backed insects live under the warm panels and around the mouths of pressure valves. Their shells are not metal, but they have taken on the colors of their surroundings: old gold, green corrosion, burnt umber, the blue shine of heated steel. They gather in trembling masses around leaking joints. When a valve begins to fail, they appear before any gauge notices. They cover it, rasping and clicking, and their collective body absorbs some small amount of heat or vibration. Whether this is repair, feeding, courtship, or merely coincidence is unknown. The machines usually survive them. The insects always do.

The plate-winged swifts are the garden’s most visible citizens. Their translucent feathers are veined with faint red, as if every wing contains a map of hot pipes. When they circle, they make almost no sound against the stacks’ roar. But on quiet thermal shifts, one can hear their wingplates ticking together like cooled glass.

They have no songs in the ordinary sense. Instead, they tune themselves to the stacks. A flock will settle along a cable, facing into the updraft, and open the small slits beneath their throats. The air passing through them produces a chord: thin, metallic, nearly lost beneath the great industrial note. The chord changes when pressure changes. A few old maintenance records—those not ruined by damp—refer to them as *auxiliary indicators*. It is unclear whether the workers named them for their usefulness or whether the birds had already been doing the work long before anyone noticed.

Their young are born blind and gray, with soft skins rather than plates. They must be kept in nests of insulation and oily fiber until their first molt. During that vulnerable time, the parents bring them water held in the cupped overlap of their own wing feathers. No bird drinks from the pooled water on a hatch. No bird drinks from a pipe.

The flower-faced grazers are more difficult to understand.

They are gentle only at a distance. Their bodies are low and long, covered in a velvet that seems to drink light. Their faces remain folded most of the time: layered flesh, pale on the inside, arranged like closed petals around a dark central mouth. When feeding, they open slowly. The interior of each face is patterned with fine, radial teeth suited to shaving moss from hot surfaces and gathering fungal foam from the lips of seals.

They move in family lines through the undergrowth. The smallest follow by touching their noses to the jointed tails of the adults, which give off occasional dry clicks. Those clicks are not speech, but the grazers respond to them with unnerving precision: a click pattern travels down the line, and every animal turns at once, or crouches, or climbs into the warm hollows beneath a casing.

They are born knowing the deck’s dangerous places. They never step on the yellow-and-black plates, even where moss has obscured them. They avoid certain vents before they open. They sleep in circles around old inspection hatches that have not been opened by human hands for decades.

Sometimes one will stand beneath a red emergency cable, unfold its face toward the vine-covered line, and remain there for hours. Nothing happens. The animal eventually leaves. But the vine around the cable will have grown a little farther by morning.

The larger thing beneath the deck is called different names by those who have heard it: drain-ox, bellows-beast, root-hog, the old maintenance animal.

None of these names are adequate.

It does not come fully into the garden. It lives among the underside girders, the runoff channels, the blind spaces where the deck’s warmth falls away into cloud. There are glimpses: a curve of dark hide moving below a grate; a wet, pale eye opening between pipes; something like a shoulder plated in lichen and mineral scale. Its breath enters through the drainage network and exits through places that have no visible connection to it. When it inhales, loose leaves drag toward the grates. When it exhales, the garden receives a warm wind smelling of iron filings, spores, and old rain.

The thing feeds on what falls through: fruit, dead insects, shed feathers, failed bolts, scraps of insulation, perhaps the occasional animal that has become too weak to move from the drainage edge. It may also feed on the machinery itself, not by consuming it but by taking something from its vibrations. When the great pistons deepen their rhythm, its breathing answers. When a fan stops briefly for inspection, it makes a low sound beneath the deck, almost grief, almost hunger.

No one has seen its head.

There may not be one head. There may be a body threaded through the whole underside of the weatherworks, grown around ducts and braces over generations, too large now to emerge through any opening made for it.

There are other presences that do not fit easily among the animals.

In the abandoned control booth, something leaves moisture on the six chairs. Not puddles: six damp impressions, as though occupants had risen moments before. The impressions appear after the mist and vanish by noon. The dead gauges sometimes twitch during these intervals. Their needles move toward readings no longer labeled on the faded faces.

A shape is occasionally seen moving inside the uppermost inspection windows of the stacks. It passes behind steam and old glass, tall enough that its head should be visible above the frame, but never is. Some say it is a person in a maintenance suit. Some say it is only a hanging length of cable seen at the wrong angle. The garden animals look upward when it passes. The swifts become silent. The grazers fold their faces shut.

The vines know it best.

They are not ordinary vines, although they began as such. Their oldest roots run through the seams of the deck and into cable housings where no soil exists. Some have entered the walls of the stacks. Their stems are warm to the touch. At night—if night can be said to occur at that altitude, where cloud and reflected industrial light confuse the hours—the leaves sometimes turn toward the machinery rather than toward the sky.

When a pipe begins to corrode, vines gather around it. When a hatch has not been opened for a long time, they seal its edges with roots. They have swallowed warning signs without obscuring the symbols beneath; the yellow paint remains visible through leaf and tendril, as if the plants consider it part of their own pattern.

The people who once maintained the works may have planted them as filters, stabilizers, insulation, or ornament. Perhaps they intended to cultivate a controlled green refuge in the upper machinery.

But the vines no longer behave as cultivated things.

They react to pressure changes before the gauges do. Their tendrils tighten around rails when storms approach. During electrical disturbances, pale blossoms open along the red emergency cables, each one facing outward toward the cloud. And when the stacks are singing at their deepest, most settled pitch, the vines seem almost asleep.

It is the silence that wakes them.

No one knows what the garden does when the stacks stop. There are no reliable witnesses. The scratched warning on the booth door is old, but not as old as the garden; beneath it are marks made by many different hands, most too faded to read. One sentence remains:

*They turned toward us.*

Perhaps it refers to the plants.

Perhaps it refers to the animals, all of them at once: birds leaving their nests, grazers unfolding their faces, insects pouring from warm seams, the hidden thing below lifting itself toward the deck.

Or perhaps the garden has one more inhabitant, one that normally exists only as a distributed attention among leaf, root, feather, breath, steam, and vibration.

When the machinery sings, that attention is occupied. It listens to the weatherworks. It feeds on their heat. It dreams in their rhythms.

When the machinery falls silent, there is room for it to hear anything else.
