What lives there is not an animal.

It has no body that can be found in one room, no nest, no spoor, no need to eat the fruit that ripens untouched over the courtyards. It does not move through the city in the way a person moves. It moves as the city moves: by continuing.

At first a traveler might mistake it for an effect of exhaustion.

After hours among the falling water, with the roar entering through the feet and teeth, it becomes possible to notice a figure on a distant balcony: someone standing beneath a vine-hung arch, hand resting on the stone rail. The figure is dark against the bright wet air. Its posture has the patient slight forward bend of someone watching rain.

Then the traveler reaches the balcony.

There is no one there.

Only a wet handprint on the rail, if the traveler is fortunate. Or an oval patch of warmth on the stone. Or a row of droplets traveling against gravity toward the place where the figure stood.

This is how the city is inhabited.

Its inhabitants are called, by the few who have entered and returned, the **stillfolk**, though no one agrees whether they are folk, or still. They are not ghosts. Ghosts remember being alive and resent the interruption. The stillfolk do not appear to remember an earlier state. They seem instead to be persons made by the city’s expectation of persons: the answer produced by a doorway that has waited too long for someone to pass through it.

A stillfolk may begin in a room.

Not arrive there: begin.

A house that has remained empty for an age will acquire certain small arrangements. A chair, though no chair was present before, may be found facing an open window. A cup of clear water stands on a sill. A pressed hollow appears in moss on the floor. In the next room a cloak hangs from a peg, woven from root fibers, rain threads, strips of black leaf. It is always damp. If touched, it has the temperature of skin after fever.

Then, perhaps, someone is wearing it.

The stillfolk are almost human in silhouette, but that likeness is a courtesy extended toward human sight. Their faces are difficult to retain. Looking directly at one, a traveler sees features made of many incompatible things: mineral streaks, folded fern fronds, water sliding over dark glass, the pale undersides of leaves in cloudlight. Their eyes are usually closed. When they open them, they are full of depthless moving water, and whatever stands behind the viewer is reflected there with great clarity.

They have hands.

Hands are important to them.

A stillfolk hand may rest against a wall for centuries, though the person attached to it is only glimpsed for an instant. Beneath such hands the stone softens, becomes porous, lets out its stored warmth. New roots emerge from the wall in the shape of veins. Canal water changes course by a finger’s width. A door that led to bare cliff will, on the following day, open into a room containing an old table, a staircase, and a view of rain falling upward over the interior basin.

The city rearranges itself around their touch, though rearrangement is too quick a word. It is more accurate to say that the city discovers what it had already been.

The stillfolk do not speak aloud. Nothing small survives in the sound of that place. But they communicate by making occasions in the water.

A fountain may alter its unchanging shape only in the eye of the observer, briefly forming the image of a hand opening. Condensation may gather across a wall in lines that resemble writing. A canal may carry a single yellow leaf uphill, against the leafless green current, until it reaches a traveler’s feet. If the traveler follows it, they may find a door that was not there before.

Sometimes the message is only a change in warmth: a cold corridor becoming warm on one side, as if someone has just passed. Sometimes it is a room full of rain that does not wet anything. Sometimes it is the sudden certainty that one should not descend the next staircase.

Those who ignore such messages are not punished. The city has no malice. But they may walk for days and return to the same intersection, older by no measurable amount and unable to remember the names of their own companions. The city does not trap visitors. It merely has difficulty distinguishing departure from arrival.

The stillfolk have no fixed number.

In the lower districts, where the city’s old plazas are broad and its houses dense beneath vines, they are numerous. One may be seen in every third archway, every empty market stall, every window overlooking a canal. In the upper inward-facing terraces, where the clouds drift through rooms and the waterfalls rise toward the false sky, they become vast and sparse. There, a figure might stand across an entire hillside: its robes composed of suspended rain, its head a dark hollow among tree ferns, its fingers descending as five separate streams into the basin below.

Or the traveler may realize that the enormous face in the cliff has been watching since morning.

There are smaller inhabitants as well, if they count as separate beings.

The warm pools contain no fish, but sometimes the mineral sheen gathers itself into the outline of a silver creature and glides beneath the surface. It has no wake. It is gone when looked at from above. In cisterns, wet rootlets braid themselves into pale, child-sized forms that sit with their knees drawn up, listening to water travel through the walls. They crumble harmlessly if approached, becoming roots again. Along certain air-canals, folds in the water move like long-winged birds flying upstream. They are not birds. They are the water rehearsing the possibility of birds.

The plants are involved.

No fern is merely a fern there. The great fans opening from masonry joints turn toward empty rooms. Orchid mouths close when a stillfolk passes. Bromeliads fill themselves before rain has arrived. Vines will sometimes withdraw from a doorway, leaf by leaf, exposing a threshold for a single afternoon. In the highest quarter, the silver tree ferns produce pale fronds shaped like hands, palms upward, as though waiting to receive something descending from the clouds.

Perhaps the stillfolk grow them. Perhaps the plants grow the stillfolk.

Perhaps both are organs of the mountain’s long interior dream.

There are places where the inhabitants gather.

The Hall of Seven Arches is one. No traveler has seen them enter, but at certain hours the spaces beneath the arches contain seven upright absences, darker than shadow and outlined in spray. The waterfall beside the hall continues to descend in its fixed white folds. Yet in the center of its roar, one can feel a pressure like attention: seven people listening to something very far away beneath the stone.

Another gathering place is the market of drowned stalls. Water hangs in vertical walls between the pillars, and behind each wall stands a merchant-shape with its goods arranged before it: bowls of wet black seeds, polished fragments of cloud, bricks that pulse with subterranean heat, fruits made of translucent green stone. No exchange occurs. The merchants do not beckon. But if a traveler leaves behind an object—especially an object made elsewhere—it may be gone upon return.

In its place there may be something the city has made from the memory of it.

A compass becomes a circular leaf whose veins point inward.

A knife becomes a slim black root with an edge of glass.

A written letter becomes rain falling in the shape of the handwriting, over and over, without touching the ground.

The stillfolk appear most clearly to those who are lonely, but not because they feed on loneliness. They are drawn to it as water is drawn to a crack. A solitary traveler may wake in an empty house to find a companion seated at the far side of the room. The companion will not look up. It may spend the whole night combing its long wet hair with a comb made from a fern stem. By morning it is gone, but the traveler’s boots have been cleaned, their clothes dried without becoming dry, and the route ahead is visible in bright beads of moisture across the floor.

No returned traveler can say whether such kindness is intentional.

That question may not mean anything in the city.

The mountain forgot it was a mountain, and in forgetting, it made space for streets, rooms, gardens, waiting places. The city forgot it was empty, and in forgetting, it made inhabitants. The inhabitants may be nothing more than the city’s ability to imagine someone standing in its doorways.

But imagination, sustained long enough by water and heat and stone, becomes difficult to distinguish from a population.

And perhaps that is why there are no animals.

An animal has to leave marks: teeth, tracks, droppings, nests, absence. It must take something from the world and carry it into itself. The stillfolk do not take. They are made from what remains: the warmth in a wall after a hand has been removed, the shape left in rain by a body that never arrived, the unspent welcome of ten thousand rooms.

They live there because the city has room for them.

They live there because every avenue expects to be crossed.

They live there because, somewhere behind the visible waterfalls and gardens and endless architecture, the mountain is still trying to remember who it was built to shelter.
