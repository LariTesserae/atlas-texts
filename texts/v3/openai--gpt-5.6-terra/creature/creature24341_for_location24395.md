What lives there is not quite a people in the usual sense, though they call themselves people, and have words for household, lineage, debt, marriage, injury, song, and the ownership of a good mud bank.

They are the **Shelf-Dwellers**, the children of the first firm ground.

They are short by the standards of drier worlds, broad through the feet and hips, and built for balance. Their feet are wide, with toes that spread when they stand on wet clay; the oldest among them have soles so thickened and polished by silt that they can cross surfaces which would swallow a stranger to the ankle. Their skin ranges through clay colors—pale yellow-brown, dark umber, gray-red, almost blue-black where it is wet—and nearly all of it bears a permanent sheen. Sweat, condensation, rainless damp, and the vapor of the ground itself keep them gleaming. In the low light their bodies look freshly molded.

They shave little. Hair is useful: it keeps biting flies from ears, absorbs sweat, provides something to twist into cords and mourning knots. Most wear it bound high, often stiffened with clay or ash. Children go bareheaded until they are old enough to cross the upper walkways without being held; after that they receive a family wrapping of reed cloth and a comb made from fish bone, shell, or fired ceramic.

Their clothes are simple because nothing can remain dry. Reed skirts, woven fiber wraps, softened fish skin, bark cloth brought from some distant place none of them have seen. In the denser districts, clothing is dyed with soot, shell-white, iron-red mud, and the blue-black stain of certain marsh fungi. The colors are less decorative than practical: a household’s workers must be recognizable across a crowded platform, through steam, smoke, birds, and bodies. A potter’s people wear broken bands of red around one arm. Stake-drivers bind their calves in black. Fish-trap families favor narrow white cords across the chest. Those who tend the sick wear no color at all but wet gray.

They are not farmers.

That fact has shaped them more deeply than they understand.

They have no reverence for seed, no calendar of planting and harvest, no fantasy that the ground can be commanded to multiply. Their oldest myths do not begin with a field or a tree. They begin with a footprint filling with warm water. They begin with someone placing a plank over it. Their central moral word is not *grow*, but *hold*.

To hold a roof.
To hold a wall.
To hold a child above the water.
To hold a neighbor’s ladder while they cross.
To hold the mud together long enough for another day.

Their wealth lies in things that can be remade: fired brick, shell, stakes, rope, mats, tools, fish traps, animal stock, kiln rights, access to a cleanish pool. A rich family is one with many hands, many sound platforms, many animals that know the way home, and a storage room raised high enough that its jars have never sat in water.

They eat a vast variety of small things. Fish in every form: smoked, boiled, mashed with clay-salt, fermented in sealed jars, dried in smoke towers, pounded into paste with eggs and reeds. Frogs, turtles, snails, eels, bird eggs, fat marsh grubs, the milk of goats, pig flesh on rare feast days, the blood and offal of animals too old to work. They make flat cakes from ground shellfish and fishmeal, which outsiders would call food only after hunger had taught them respect. They brew cloudy drinks from reeds, honey gathered from wall-hives, sweet roots traded across distances, and whatever grains arrive by boat from lands that perhaps do have fields.

For there are boats.

Not graceful river boats, because there are no rivers to honor them. These are broad, ugly, shallow-bottomed things: reed hulls plastered with pitch, plank trays poled through channels, floating platforms burdened with jars. The boat people are a distinct caste of temperament if not blood. They know the submerged routes between settlements, the invisible ridges, the places where water is deep enough for an ox to vanish. They carry clay, shells, animals, spouses, songs, gossip, and a few goods whose origins have become nearly mythical: copper, hard timber, obsidian blades, woven cloth that smells faintly of sunlight.

Copper is not the material of ordinary life. A copper awl passes through generations with a personal name. A broken copper hook is searched for in ash heaps and latrine pits. A person may be buried with a copper object only if the household has accepted that it will never again be useful, which is considered a form of grief almost too severe to speak of.

The Shelf-Dwellers are not still, even if their city is.

They marry, age, quarrel, make jokes, keep grudges for decades, lose children, raise animals, cheat at measurements, sing while working, and try to make their dwelling platforms marginally better than those of their neighbors. Their elders remember events with astonishing precision, because the landscape offers few markers of before and after.

“This was when the third east kiln cracked.”

“This was the season the white ox learned to climb the fish stairs.”

“This was when your mother fell through that roof but did not spill the jar.”

Such occurrences become anchors in time. A generation may be named after a particular bird infestation, a famous roof fire, an argument between two boat guilds, or the day a child heard a new tone from a shell bell. Since the sky does not change and the waterline does not move, human memory supplies weather to history.

They keep records, though not in the manner of dry civilizations. Fired tablets would be too precious and too easily repurposed as fill. Instead they mark obligations on long strings of shaped clay beads, shell disks, knotted fibers, and notched stake-ends. A household might possess a bundle recording three hundred years of goat exchanges, canal labor, marriages, and compensations for damaged roofs. Only certain old people can read the whole language of knots. They work with their fingers close to their faces, murmuring aloud as if the records might otherwise drift away.

The dead do not leave the city easily.

There is nowhere dry to bury them, and no height from which to send them into the air. Bodies are wrapped in reed mats, weighted with brick fragments, and placed in sealed chambers beneath platforms or within old walls. Some are submerged in clay-lined pools with their tools and a shell over each eye. Others are fired after death—not burned to ash, but dried and hardened slowly in the outer chambers of a kiln, becoming dark, fragile, earthen figures. These kiln-dead are kept in family alcoves until they crack, at which point the pieces are mixed into road foundations.

The Shelf-Dwellers do not find this cruel.

They believe a person has three bodies: the wet body, which walks and hungers; the remembered body, which lives in other people’s speech; and the holding body, which remains in whatever structure receives their material. A grandmother may be in the lintel above a doorway. A dead boatman may be beneath the crossing where his grandchildren conduct trade. A child who died young may be part of the floor upon which the household sleeps. To be incorporated is to continue helping.

Their gods are similarly practical.

They do not worship the sky much. The sky has never made a promise and has never broken one. They respect it as one respects a ceiling.

Their most beloved powers are the Low Ones: beings imagined beneath the clay shelves, under the water, in the pressure where stakes go down and do not reach bottom. The Low Ones are said to have hands of rootless reed, backs of packed shell, and eyes like bubbles rising through mud. They do not create the world. They keep it from remembering that it could be fluid.

Each district maintains its own versions. One is invoked before driving pilings. One receives the first bricks from every kiln firing. One watches animals crossing narrow walkways. One is asked to keep sleeping infants from rolling toward dark water. Their shrines are low, damp, and crowded: a niche in a retaining wall, a platform under a stair, a sealed jar sunk beneath a threshold. Offerings are not grand. A fish head, a pinch of salt, a small clay bell, the first milk from a goat, a child’s broken bead.

The bells matter because the Low Ones are believed to be mostly deaf.

Or perhaps not deaf—simply surrounded by too much earth to hear ordinary speech. A struck pot, a booming slab, a rack of clattering shells: these send vibration downward through posts and foundations. Every ringing is therefore both signal and prayer. The city does not distinguish neatly between the two.

There are creatures in the city which may be animals, spirits, or citizens of a more secret kind.

The most respected are the **wall eels**: pale, thick-bodied fish that live in water trapped inside old foundation courses. They emerge only in the dimmest hours, slipping through drainage gaps and disappearing beneath houses. A family that finds one in its courtyard does not kill it. They feed it scraps and wait for it to leave. If it remains for three days, the household assumes something below the platform has shifted.

There are also the **mud-white crabs**, blind things with soft shells, found in pits that should have contained only clay. Children collect them in jars and listen to the clicking of their claws. Some claim they are born from the nails of the dead.

In the outer pools, huge fish move beneath the surface, broad-backed and slow, their skins marked with pale scars. They are never caught. Nets tear around them. Oxen avoid their shadows. The boat people say they are older than the first shelves, and that they feed not on flesh but on the city’s lost objects: dropped knives, broken beads, dead lamps, wedding rings, the small goods of daily life. Somewhere in the deep mud beneath the plain, they say, the great fish carry whole households of lost belongings in their bellies.

And then there are the children born with webbing between their fingers.

Most grow into ordinary workers. Some become famous swimmers, ditch-clearers, animal catchers, or boat guides. A few develop an unusual sensitivity to vibration. They can place their palms on a wet wall and tell whether a piling is loose, whether a canal gate is blocked, whether an ox is struggling in a submerged pen. These people are not treated as magical exactly. They are treated as indispensable, which is both higher and more burdensome.

They are called Listeners.

A Listener may belong to no household completely. During danger they are summoned across district boundaries. Their hands are washed in warm water, rubbed with ash, and pressed to walls, planks, jars, the ground itself. Then they name what is wrong.

“There is an empty space under the west lane.”

“The third post beneath this room is splitting.”

“Something large has passed through the basin.”

“Your child is not ill. The sleeping platform is humming.”

They are often right.

The city is full of laborers, but its truest inhabitants may be the things that labor has taught to cooperate with it. Goats know the roof routes better than their owners. Dogs patrol the edges of plankways and bark at unfamiliar footing. Pigs turn refuse into dryness. Birds strip fish racks clean if not watched, but their sudden silence warns of something moving below or above. Dragonflies gather over pools where the water has turned foul. Mosquitoes are cursed, slapped, smoked, and endured, yet their swarms are read like signs: a dense cloud at a doorway may mean rot in the wall cavity beyond.

Even the bricks live, in a manner of speaking.

Every brick bears the fingerprints, straw marks, shell grit, and kiln scars of its making. People recognize particular batches. They know which walls contain the unreliable clay from the southern pits, which roadbeds are laid over the remains of an old market, which platform was once the roof of a house that sank without sinking. When a wall is dismantled, its bricks are greeted by name and sorted according to their former service. Some are too honored to become fill. Some are too unlucky to be placed near sleeping rooms. Some have been fired so many times that they ring when tapped, and these become bells.

The city has no wilderness beyond itself, because it has made the plain into an extension of its body. Yet the plain has not surrendered. It waits in every gap between boards, in every footprint, in the cool water under every floor.

The Shelf-Dwellers understand this without fear. They do not imagine themselves separate from the rawness beneath them. They are another layer of it: reed, mud, shell, bone, smoke, animal warmth, brick dust, breath.

Their city persists because they persist in making it, and they persist because the city gives their hands something to do.

Under the fixed gray sky, amid the unending bells, they are the warm, restless creatures of the first ground: never arriving, never departing, forever holding.
