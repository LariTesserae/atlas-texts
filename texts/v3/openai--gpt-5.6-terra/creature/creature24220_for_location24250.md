What lives in the high country is not one people, nor one ecology, nor even one clear boundary between the grown and the born.

The first inhabitants are the mountains themselves.

They are not conscious in the clean, immediate way an animal is conscious. They do not watch a traveler pass or recognize a face at a distance. But they remember pressure. They remember nourishment, heat, repeated touch, old injuries, and songs spoken into particular hollows over many generations. A root wall that has held a family’s house for a century will thicken beneath its sleeping mats. A bridge-vine used kindly will flower at its handholds. An abandoned settlement may slowly close around its old courtyards, not out of hostility but because no one is there to ask it to remain open.

The people who live among these immensities call themselves, in one region, the **Tenders of the Upper Breath**. Elsewhere they are known as the **Root-Kin**, the **Hollow People**, the **Cloudside Clans**, or simply the **Those Who Ask**. They do not agree on a common name, and most do not need one. Their worlds are local: one water court, one set of ascending paths, one old trunk whose inner chambers contain the graves, storehouses, kitchens, nurseries, and council floor of a community.

They tend to be few.

A whole lineage may consist of thirty people spread across several days of walking, linked by reed-message cords, trained rain birds, and the practice of leaving signs in living bark. Their settlements are dim, warm, and almost invisible until one is within them. A doorway may look like a fold in a root. A market may occupy a natural bowl in the floor, marked only by woven mats and clusters of hanging fruit-lamps. The people do not build high walls against one another. The country itself already makes privacy abundant.

Their clothing is grown as often as woven. Some wear capes of broad, water-shedding leaves cultivated to remain supple after cutting. Others train fine rootlets through cloth, producing garments that slowly alter with the wearer: cuffs that tighten in rain, collars that open in heat, pockets that sprout tiny pale flowers when their contents have been forgotten too long. Children are often given a living braid of fiber at birth. It is kept through adulthood, added to their tools, clothing, or house frame, and eventually planted over their burial place.

They have gardeners, but the word is too small for what such people do.

A gardener may be a bridge-speaker, a path-midwife, a fever-listener, a root surgeon, a maker of sleeping halls, and a judge in disputes over water. They learn the moods of particular structures. They know which groves tolerate songs, which must be approached in silence, which old beams react badly to blood, salt, or grief. The best among them can hear, by placing an ear against bark, whether a terrace is becoming overloaded months before it begins to sag.

Some are born with a talent for it. Others acquire it through patience. There are old women who can lay their palms upon a wall and tell what passed through the other side of the mountain the day before: a herd of bellows beasts, a burst sap vessel, three strangers, a frightened child. There are young men who spend a decade learning a single bridge-vine’s seasonal language before they are permitted to cross it alone. There are children who speak easily to doors but never learn to speak comfortably to other people.

Their dead do not leave the range.

The usual burial is called a returning. The body is wrapped in leafcloth, placed within a shallow niche among chosen roots, and given soil from the family’s threshold. The root system grows through the body over time, taking what it can use. Bones may remain, becoming smooth and green-stained, sometimes later gathered and worked into tools, beads, flutes, or the small polished markers used to identify an elder’s favorite resting place.

The dead are not thought to become the mountain exactly. That would be too simple. They become participants in its memory. A person whose life was strongly tied to a bridge may afterward be consulted before crossings. A patient healer may be remembered in a medicinal grove. A dangerous ancestor may be planted under a path that no longer goes anywhere, where their impatience has room to wander.

The oldest communities keep lineages of trees as carefully as they keep lineages of people. They can say: this canopy came from a sapling planted by our mother’s mother’s mother; that cistern was opened by a child who never survived the wet season; this entire hanging orchard grows from the wedding lattice of two families who later became enemies.

There are also those who live apart from communities: the **high walkers**, the **moss widows**, the **canal sleepers**, the people called **unrooted**. Some are exiles, some pilgrims, some simply unsuitable for close life. They follow the migrating paths, sleep in abandoned root chambers, exchange labor for food, and carry news between settlements. Their knowledge is often immense and unreliable. A high walker may know seven routes across a ravine that no longer exists, yet be unable to say which season they are currently in.

Among them are the listeners of the Seed Horizons.

These people travel upward only after long preparation. They eat little salt, avoid iron, and cover their faces with veils of pale fungus fiber. They do not speak names while on the silver platforms. They go to tend the offerings near the bone-grained basins, to watch the six-legged grazers, and perhaps to see what other world appears in the water.

Some return changed.

A listener may come back with a stone in their hand that no one saw them carry. They may refuse shade for the rest of their life. They may begin making maps of dry valleys that do not exist in the high country. Once in a generation, a listener returns with the certainty that something from the reflected landscape has looked back.

Not all inhabitants are human-shaped.

The bellows beasts are sometimes regarded as fellow gardeners. Communities follow their feeding paths, because the places they have grazed become bright with edible shoots and healing buds. Their shed moss is gathered for bedding, wound dressings, and the slow-burning fuel used in clay fire chambers. They are gentle except when a calf is threatened, and their migrations can alter the structure of an entire valley. A herd resting beneath a loaded terrace may warm it, dry it, and prevent a collapse. A herd moving elsewhere may leave a settlement suddenly colder.

The pale climbing deer are half-wild and half-courted. They choose their own routes, but certain families maintain salt gardens in high alcoves to attract them. Their antler epiphytes are carefully harvested after seasonal shedding. These tiny plants are used as medicines, dyes, and indicators of rot within a root structure. To kill one of the deer is permitted only in famine, and even then the hunter must sleep alone in a wet hollow until they dream of the animal’s ascent.

The quiet marsh birds keep the time of some settlements. Their throat-fans shift color before changes in pressure and fog. A blue-black fan means rain. A white fan means the upper roots are freezing. A fan patterned with crimson bars means something large has moved through the nearby channels, and doors should remain closed.

There are smaller beings everywhere: shell-backed sap lizards that clean fungus from the underside of leaves; fist-sized frogs whose skin gleams like wet copper; moths that carry pollen between levels of the mountains; pale spiders that weave only across open space and whose webs are strong enough to catch leaves, birds, or an inattentive person. There are beetles bred for their cold light, and ants that cultivate medicinal molds inside the skulls of long-dead trees.

In certain old galleries live colonies of human-sized leaf mimics. At first they appear to be merely hanging bundles of vegetation, wrapped around a central pale body. They move only when no one is looking directly at them. They are scavengers, not hunters, and are tolerated because they consume rot, fallen meat, and the dangerous fungal blooms that sometimes form in sealed chambers. Children are taught never to insult one, never to feed one by hand, and never to count how many are standing near the edge of a lamp’s light.

The predators are not generally named unless they have taken someone.

To name them is thought to help them remain distinct from the foliage. Most people prefer to speak of a missing goat, a broken railing, a silence under the leaves. The vine-cats are especially avoided in speech. They are seen rarely: a green flank between branches, an eye opening in what had seemed a knot of bark, a tail lowering itself in the rain. It is believed they are not individual animals but recurrent shapes assumed by the mountain’s hunger.

Whether this is true is difficult to say.

There are stranger residents still, especially around the breaks.

The detached shadows in fog season are perhaps not shadows at all. Some mimic the movements of plants imperfectly, lagging behind them or bending in directions the leaves do not bend. Others collect along abandoned walkways as if waiting for traffic. A few have been known to follow people home and settle beneath their houses. Such households become unusually fertile for a time. Then, years later, every seed planted there fails at once.

In the looping stairs and returning canals, travelers sometimes encounter versions of themselves that are older, younger, or differently clothed. Most communities teach that these are not selves but the mountain’s guesses: provisional arrangements of posture, voice, and memory made by a place that has felt so many people pass through it. One should greet such a figure politely, offer it water if it asks, and never tell it where one lives.

And in the deepest foundational layers—below the root galleries, below the warm chambers, below even the oldest carbonized reedwork—there may be remnants of the first makers.

Not ghosts, perhaps. Not living descendants. Something slower.

Occasionally a person digging a drainage channel breaks into an airless cavity and finds tools laid in rows: shell wedges, charred wooden spades, cords turned black but still intact. Sometimes the cords twitch when exposed to rain. Sometimes, in the walls of ancient structural hollows, one finds impressions of hands pressed into old clay, fingers elongated by centuries of root growth. These places are sealed again. No one wants to know whether the first builders are absent, sleeping, or simply dispersed throughout the great body of their work.

So what lives in the high country?

Families and wanderers. Herds and silences. Patient plants, accidental plants, plants that have learned the uses of a home. Old purposes without owners. Water that remembers channels no longer open. Predators that are almost camouflage, ancestors that are almost architecture, and mountains that continue—without haste, without mastery, without ever quite becoming finished—to make room for all of them.
