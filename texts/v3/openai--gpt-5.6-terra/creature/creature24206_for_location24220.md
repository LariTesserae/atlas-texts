What lives there is not one people, nor one ecology, but a set of uneasy accommodations made inside a body too vast to understand itself.

The pale browsers are among the least troubling inhabitants. From a distance they resemble stripped branches walking upright through the glow: high narrow shoulders, long jointed legs, ribs shining through translucent hides. Their mouths are soft and broad, made for combing root-hair without tearing it. They feed in silence, often in pairs, one watching while the other eats. When storms begin, they fold themselves into hollows in the ground and become nearly invisible beneath a skin of closing leaves.

They are not quite animals in the ordinary sense. A browser wounded deeply may put down rootlets from its legs. One trapped in wet soil will sometimes remain there for years, growing still, taking light through the pale fans along its spine. Travelers know not to use such creatures as landmarks. A standing browser may have become a thicket by the next season.

There are smaller things in the warm vents: moths with clear wings veined in green; beetles that carry blue fungal lights beneath their shells; blind white leapers that live under mats of moss and scatter at the vibration of footsteps. Some insects pollinate flowers that never open above ground. They crawl into tight red folds in the tissue of the walls and emerge dusted in yellow powder, their bodies swollen with sweetness and spores.

In the black water held inside the great roots live thread-fish, if fish is the word. They are long, colorless, and almost too thin to see, sensing the world through tremors in the sap. Children of the hollow settlements sometimes catch them by lowering strands of hair into seep-pools. They are eaten raw in lean times, but more often released. A pool with thread-fish is usually clean. A pool without them may be poisoned, fever-warm, or connected to something deeper.

There are also things that are only seen once.

A handprint appearing beneath the translucent skin of a wall, pressing outward from inside the tissue.

A procession of seed-pods rolling across a slope against the wind, each one clicking softly as though containing teeth.

A white animal with too many knees walking along the underside of the ceiling, where no animal should be able to walk.

The people who live in the hollows do not consider all such things monsters. Monster is a word for something that wants you specifically. Most of what lives beneath the green vault has no interest in people at all. It grows, feeds, circulates, flowers, migrates, closes wounds. That indifference is often more dangerous than hunger.

The human inhabitants are few and scattered: basin-folk, root-walkers, glow-keepers, wind-listeners. Outsiders give them separate names, but they trade spouses, stories, seed-shell, medicines, and warnings often enough that they think of themselves as variations of one people.

They tend to be lean, broad-footed, and pale-eyed from generations under luminous leaves. Their skin is not necessarily pale; it is usually stained by their work—green at the fingers from sap, yellow in the creases from pollen, silvered around the lashes by lichen dust. Many wear layered cloaks woven from root-fiber, oiled against the permanent wet. Their garments are patched with strips of bark, soft hide, shed membrane, and the tough transparent skins peeled from old water-veins. A good cloak is an inheritance. It can remember three generations in its repairs.

They do not live far into the largest basins unless necessity drives them. Their settlements cling to margins: shelves where the tissue has hardened, old scar ridges, dry folds beneath inactive roots, places where the air circulates predictably and the ground has not moved in living memory—which may mean only ten years.

Their homes are grown as much as built. A family will plant a ring of pliable stems around a shelter frame, braid them as they rise, and feed their roots with ash and compost until they make a low living dome. The inside is warm compared with the outer air. The walls pulse faintly at night. Children learn early which parts of a house can be cut for cooking-fuel, which can be tapped for water, and which will bleed bitter sap that brings dreams of drowning.

Some households cultivate their own small lights: lantern-fruit hung in netted cradles, blue molds encouraged on damp boards, pale lichen gardens painted across the undersides of shelves. The brightest homes can be seen across a basin like constellations caught in grass.

Their old people are valued for memory, but not merely because they remember the past. They remember the behavior of places.

They know which ridge was once a wet valley. They know that a certain passage smells sweet before it shuts. They know that the silver plain bends eastward because, beneath it, a great root is slowly drawing the ground in that direction. They can tell from a crackling noise in the walls whether a chamber is preparing to breathe, bloom, flood, or fold.

Maps exist, but only as partial things: bundles of carved stakes, strips of cured membrane marked with colored sap, songs timed to walking pace, stories that describe a journey in the form of a family argument or a hunting tale. A route is never simply a route. It is a sequence of conditions.

Walk until the blue lamps become three lamps and one dim one.  
Cross where the moss smells of iron.

Do not step between the two split trunks; they were one trunk when the story was made.

The people have no single explanation for the land around them.

Some say they live inside the buried root of the world-tree, and that the surface is only a dead crust grown over its crown. Some say the hollows are the digestive chambers of a sleeping god, though this belief is considered unlucky among those who work near water-veins. Some say the entire country was once a forest on the surface, compressed downward by a disaster so ancient that its trees learned to become earth.

Others say the place is not alive in the way an animal is alive. It is alive in the way weather is alive: full of motion, appetite, accident, and consequence, but without a face behind it.

There are those who believe it is waking.

They are called listeners, though most do not claim to hear words. They camp near the deepest crevasses and record the great strains of the roots: low groans felt through the ground, pulses that travel through a basin and make every lantern-fruit flare at once. Some listeners insist these rhythms are becoming more regular. Some say the buried organism is learning the shape of its own interior. A few have begun to speak of an eventual movement so large that all the chambers will become one thing: all valleys lifted, all rivers redirected, all settlements carried in the folds of a newly growing land.

The practical people call this dangerous speculation.

Yet even they keep their shelters light enough to dismantle in an hour.

There are children born in the hollows who have never seen open sky. They imagine it as a ceiling that does not breathe, a light that hurts, a blue emptiness with nowhere to tie a rope. They play games of storm and root-collapse among the stilts of their homes. They dare one another to touch sleeping tendrils. They collect colored spores in glassy seed-shells and trade them like jewels. They learn the warning sayings before they learn the names of distant relatives.

And sometimes one of them goes missing.

Not taken, usually. Not attacked.

A child follows a newly opened green passage, or sees a familiar lamp shining where no lamp should be, or hears what sounds like someone calling from the other side of a thin living wall. Searchers may find footprints ending at unbroken tissue. Years later, in another chamber, a traveler may find a hand-carved stake embedded high in a fresh wall, its dry grass bundle still intact.

The hollow-folk do not always call such people dead.

The land takes things into itself, but it also moves things. It carries groves, floods, seed, heat, memory, bone, and occasionally a human life from one impossible place to another.

So the people remain. They prune carefully. They listen. They build their homes in scars and seams. They raise lights against the green immensities. They have learned that survival is not a matter of conquering the buried country, nor even fully understanding it.

It is a matter of being small enough to notice when it changes its mind.
