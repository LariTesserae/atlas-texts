They are called, in the nearest approximation, the **Stillfolk**—though the name is not theirs, because they do not use names in the ordinary way.

They do not announce themselves. One may look down an empty street for an hour, see only the blue shade beneath the galleries and the pale surfaces of the houses, and then look again to find that someone is standing at its far end: upright, narrow, wrapped in layered garments the color of old bone, watching the flags along the roofs.

At first they seem human. This is part of their strangeness. They have heads, hands, shoulders, knees. They wear belts, carry baskets, lead low carts by cords. Their dwellings contain bowls, sleeping shelves, partitions of woven mineral fiber, caches of tools. But their resemblance to people is a resemblance maintained across a distance.

Their skin is not skin exactly. It is matte and pale, sometimes the gray of ash, sometimes with a faint bluish translucence at the temples, wrists, throat, and behind the knees. Fine seams cross it, especially on the hands. In cold light these seams are invisible; near an active wall, they acquire the same granular green shine that moves through the town’s mineral veins.

The Stillfolk do not age in a smooth direction. A young one may appear dry and grave, with eyes clouded by mineral film, while an elder may have a face almost featureless with new pale growth, as if the years have been laid over them in thin deposits. They periodically shed layers from their palms, shoulders, and backs. These discarded skins are brittle but not dead. Set in cracks or laid beneath thresholds, they harden and become part of the town’s fabric: a patch of paving, a low wall, a shelf grown directly from a room’s interior.

No one sees this process unless invited.

They are born, perhaps, in chambers that have recently closed.

The glowing alleys are avoided not because they are dangerous in the usual sense, but because they are private. When a district begins to change, certain households move toward it rather than away. They bring woven screens, bowls of dry black lichen, bundles of the stiff gray plants gathered from the margins. They disappear into rooms where the walls are brightening. For days afterward, there is no sign of them. Then the town settles into a new arrangement, and there may be a new doorway opening onto a court. Within it: a small person standing very still in the cool shadow, their body soft-looking and pale, their eyes not yet able to focus on the dim light.

The infants do not cry. Their first sound, if it can be called a sound, is a faint vibration in the teeth of anyone close enough to feel it.

The Stillfolk communicate chiefly through pressure, gesture, and placement. They touch fingertips to a wall and wait. They set bowls in particular alignments. They angle their bodies toward one another in the narrow streets. A conversation may consist of two people sitting on opposite sides of a doorway while neither looks at the other, one slowly scraping a chalky root clean, the other turning a small mirrored insect-shell over and over in their hand.

When speech is necessary, it is nearly inaudible: dry consonants formed behind closed teeth, breaths given shape but not volume. Their language is suited to the town’s silence. Much of it appears to concern direction, stability, and time measured not by clocks but by structural events.

Not *tomorrow*, but *after the northern stairs soften*.

Not *home*, but *the room that still opens beneath the fan-tree*.

Not *death*, but *being taken into the wall*.

They do not seem to eat in public. Yet the communal ovens are used. At their mouths, one sometimes finds trays of black lichen, pale roots shaved from the gray-green plants, thin wafers of translucent stone, and a dark paste that has been pressed into the shape of hands. The ovens do not burn with flame. Their interiors carry a low stored heat, and the food that emerges is dry, brittle, and faintly luminous in darkness.

They drink no water because they have no need for water as animals understand it. Moisture exists in them only as a trace, held deep within their tissues and used sparingly. Their true nourishment is mineral change: salts, fungal growths, the slow exhalations of newly exposed stone. They cultivate the black lichen not for its bulk but because it gathers something from the air that no instrument would detect. They tend the white trees with particular reverence. The dark fans of those trees open toward the diffuse sun and, for a few hours, alter the entire courtyard around them. Beneath their shade, the Stillfolk sit with their backs against the trunks. Afterwards, their eyes are clearer and the seams in their skin are less bright.

They are not masters of the town.

This is the central fact of their civilization.

They do not build walls so much as persuade walls to pause. Their tools are small and peculiar: tuning forks of horn, combs with stone teeth, spatulas of dark metal, braided cords weighted with pellets of salt. With these they can encourage a seam to widen, delay the closure of a doorway, guide a swelling partition away from a sleeping alcove. But they cannot command the greater movements. They cannot stop a district from lifting, nor make an old square remain a square when the town has decided it must become a chamber.

Their maps are therefore not maps of streets.

They are maps of tendencies.

A Stillfolk map is a layered thing made from translucent flakes. It records where stone has thickened over generations, where light returns most often, which courtyards draw the lichen, which walls have begun to dream of opening. The newest maps are kept in public recesses, weighted down with bowls. Old maps are not discarded. They are ground into powder and mixed into plaster, so that every house contains the memory of streets that no longer exist.

Their dead are not buried beyond the town. The mineral crust outside the basin is too raw, too indifferent, and the Stillfolk regard it with the caution one might give a deep sea.

When someone dies, their household opens the seams in their skin with a blade of polished horn. The body is not cut apart; it is unfolded. Within are layers of pale, flexible mineral tissue and small dark nodules, warm for a little while after death. These are removed and placed in prepared cracks in a favored wall. The rest is left in a quiet room where the green light has begun to show. Eventually the room closes around it.

Years later, perhaps, a shelf in another house will develop a dark smooth inclusion the size of a thumb. A child will find it, touch it, and be told which relative has become part of the neighborhood.

This is not metaphor to them. Their ancestors do, in some degree, remain present. A stable wall may contain generations. A house that is unusually resistant to change is considered heavily inhabited. Some families prefer such houses; others distrust them, believing that too much remembrance makes a structure unable to respond when the deeper movement comes.

There are divisions among them, though they are subtle.

Some are **keepers of the flags**, moving from roof to roof each dim morning to inspect the curling fibrous strips. They know the minute changes in posture that precede a wall’s growth. Their fingers are stained gray from handling the plants, and their faces are often covered by thin masks against the powder released during a shedding.

Some are **listeners**, who spend long periods seated in bare rooms with one hand on the floor. They do not hear the city. No one hears it. They read changes in temperature, in the prickling of mineral dust against the skin, in the faint green accumulation beneath the nails. A listener may rise suddenly in the middle of a shared meal and leave without explanation. By evening, everyone in that quarter will have moved their possessions three doors away.

Some are **gardeners**, though their gardens consist of cracks, lichen bands, root-clusters, and the six white trees. They are treated with a degree of tenderness that may be religious. The gardeners prune the trees’ dark fans with tiny stone shears. They gather the fallen fan-films before they touch the paving. These films are used for writing, but only for temporary messages: warnings, directions, apologies, notices that a doorway should not be used. Within a few days the writing fades, then the material curls into dust.

And some are **wanderers**, who go to the town’s edge and beyond it.

They are rare, taller than most, wrapped almost completely against the pale mineral plain. They carry no visible supplies except horn wheels, folded screens, and long staffs with forked ends. When they return, if they return, the town has changed around them. A wanderer may come back to find their old district gone, their family rooms sealed, their remembered route to the white trees replaced by a stair ending at a blank wall.

They accept this with no outward grief.

Perhaps that is why outsiders, if any have ever reached the basin, mistake them for emotionless beings. But the Stillfolk do grieve. They grieve with rearrangement. A person who has lost someone may move all their bowls into a new configuration each morning for a season. They may sit for hours beside a wall that has swallowed a room. They may stop tending their own skin, allowing mineral bloom to spread over their neck and hands. Their neighbors do not offer consolation in words. They bring new flags, fresh lichen, a cartload of pale stone wafers. They remain nearby, doing small necessary tasks until the mourner begins again to participate in the town’s adjustments.

They believe—or behave as though they believe—that the town is alive in a manner too large to be intentional.

It does not love them. It does not hate them. It has no use for their individual continuance. But it makes chambers in which they can sleep, cracks in which their plants can root, warm floors after its hidden transformations, and green-lit passages where new life can begin. In return, they keep its surfaces clean, tend the organisms that gather along its seams, remove obstacles from places where it needs to grow, and learn its changing body well enough not to make it defend itself against them.

At rare intervals, during the narrow tremors that run through the basin, all the Stillfolk emerge.

They stand in the squares, under balconies, beside the dry basin and beneath the white trees. They place their hands against walls or paving. They do not brace themselves. They do not flee.

The green light rises through the town. Its seams appear everywhere: in the paving, in the buildings, in the trees, in the pale lines beneath the inhabitants’ own skin. For those few minutes, the distinction between citizen and dwelling becomes difficult to maintain.

Then the lights go down.

Someone finds that their doorway is gone.

Someone else finds that a wall has opened beside their sleeping shelf, revealing a room with no dust in it and a new stair descending into cool blue darkness.

By the next dim morning, bowls have been moved. Screens have been hung. A flag has been planted on the new roofline.

And the town, having changed its mind, is inhabited again.
