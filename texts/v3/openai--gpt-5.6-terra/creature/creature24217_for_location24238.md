What lives in the lowlands is not animal, and not quite plant.

At first there seems to be nothing but the vegetation itself: growth without witness, architecture without intention. But the longer one remains near any of the civic-looking places—the moss basin, the vaulted root galleries, the damp yellow walls—the more difficult it becomes to maintain the distinction between a plant and the arrangements it makes.

The inhabitants are arrangements.

They begin as preferences in the growth.

A leaf turns when it should not need to turn. A root, descending through a packed layer of old stems, pauses at a pocket of air and divides around it instead of filling it. Several vine systems that have never touched begin to stiffen in parallel. Water gathers in a certain depression day after day, though the depression is too shallow to retain it naturally. In time, these small decisions accumulate. A volume of plant-mass becomes biased toward itself. It begins preserving a corridor, maintaining a hollow, holding open an eye of light in the canopy.

Then something lives there.

Such a thing has no body one could isolate. It may occupy a hundred trunks, or a single tangled arch of roots, or a whole district of responsive foliage. Its boundaries are not skin but coordination. One recognizes it by repetition: the same opening remaining open despite the relentless pressure of growth; the same sequence of leaves lowering and lifting when storms approach; the same pale blossoms appearing along a route that otherwise shifts every week.

Some of them are small.

A small one might live in the hollow inside a reed-column. Its presence is known by the way the warm wind through the column acquires a rhythm: three low breaths, a pause, then a long exhalation that makes nearby vines tremble. Over months, the vines may arrange themselves around the reed in ribbed spirals, not quite a staircase, not quite a cage. The hollow remains clear. Fallen leaf-matter is pushed out through cracks in the base. Water is redirected from nearby cups so that it drips only along one inner wall.

Nothing enters. Nothing emerges.

Still, the hollow behaves as though it is occupied.

Larger inhabitants reside in plazas.

The moss basin is one. Its breathing rim is not merely a ring of contracting stems. It is a slow organism made of all the plants surrounding the open center, perhaps extending for miles under the woven substrate. Each morning, when mist gathers in the basin, the blue-green fans incline inward. Their surfaces cool. Their veins darken. The mist thickens rather than dispersing. By noon, when the collected moisture begins to bead and descend, the leaves lift and turn outward, directing the water into particular channels beneath the moss.

The basin is fed, drained, opened, and protected.

It may be a mouth. It may be a lung. It may be an eye kept clear beneath the green density so that the lowlands can briefly look upon their own interior.

Nothing in it needs to be seen.

There are inhabitants that travel, though they do not move from one place to another in any simple sense. They migrate by relinquishing their old growth and persuading new growth elsewhere into the necessary shape.

A traveling one might begin in an eastern tower, where the air rushing through a reed-column has learned a particular low note. Over several days, the note appears in adjacent columns. Vines begin tightening in matching intervals. Pools tremble before the gusts arrive. A line of pale leaves thins, yellows, and grows translucent along a route through the crowded stacks. The original hollow may then collapse under a new root swell, closing forever.

But the pattern continues westward.

Weeks later, in a district of ivory arches, a sequence of roots begins to flex in the old rhythm: three pressures, a pause, a long release. The tunnels beneath them remain dry during a storm. Their ceilings glow faintly after dark. The inhabitant has gone there—not as a creature goes, but as a melody passes from one instrument to another.

There are no names among them, unless names are forms that recur.

The nearest thing to a face appears in the younger zones, where raw roots expose the layers below. Sometimes a set of arches will form around a cavity in the peat. Pale fungal lace grows across the opening in a loose membrane. Behind it, warm air pulses. Condensation gathers in two dark hollows. Fibers spread downward in a curve like a mouth, though they do nothing but drink from the soil.

The resemblance is accidental.

It remains accidental even after the hollows narrow when approached.

It remains accidental even after the root arches shift, over the course of an hour, to turn the membrane toward the observer.

The lowlands do not possess eyes, not individually. Yet their inhabitants have discovered many ways of making attention.

Some attend to moisture. Some attend to pressure. Some attend to heat, or the difference between sunlight arriving from above and light arriving from a place where there should be no opening. A few seem to attend to absence: they gather around voids, preserve them, enlarge them, grow elaborate walls and vaulted approaches around spaces containing nothing at all.

These are among the oldest.

In the deepest root districts there are chambers kept empty for years. No shoot penetrates them. No moss crosses their thresholds. The surrounding roots knit themselves into doors that close during the hottest part of the day and open at night, revealing only blackness and the faint draft of subterranean water. The chambers are not nests. Nothing rests in them. They seem instead to be places where a larger pattern has withdrawn its growth, making room for something it cannot become while crowded by itself.

When a trunk falls nearby, the roots alter course to prevent it from crushing the chamber.

When floodwater rises, mats of leaves fold over the entrance.

When the impossible shifts in distance begin—the horizon looping, bridges reaching ravines that were not there—the empty chamber often remains where it was.

Perhaps the inhabitants use emptiness as an anchor.

They have no speech, but they make weather-like statements.

A sudden opening of hundreds of night flowers along a rootway may mean that a distant section of canopy has split. A change in the taste of the chemical air may precede the collapse of an old platform. In certain corridors, the vines tighten across the path hours before a rain-event spills from the upper reservoirs. Elsewhere, leaves all turn their pale undersides outward, making a long bright signal visible through layers of vegetation.

No one receives these messages.

That does not make them meaningless.

The largest inhabitant may be the entire city, though it is not unified in the way a single body is unified. It is more like a crowd without separate members: innumerable local urgencies pressing, yielding, borrowing structure, abandoning structure, sending water and tension through shared tissues. Neighborhoods disagree. Root systems barricade one another. A flowering tower may starve the supports of a moss street in order to rise another span toward the light. Whole avenues are erased by the ambitions of a single expanding trunk.

Yet after each violence, new passages appear.

After each collapse, some hollow is preserved.

After every season of ungovernable multiplication, the lowlands retain a few inexplicable clearings, a few resonant towers, a few bridges that seem to lead deliberately from one breathing district to another.

It is possible that these are not inhabitants at all.

It is possible that the city is trying, with all its leaves and roots and bursting vessels, to make inhabitants of itself: to grow places where a self might gather, lose itself, and gather again.

And perhaps that is why nothing else can live there.

The lowlands are already occupied.
