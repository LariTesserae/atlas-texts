The garden is tended by a thing that was not made to be a gardener.

Once, perhaps, it was a maintenance unit: a human being altered for long work in wet places, or a machine built in the loose likeness of one. There are records of such custodians in the lower offices, sealed in plastic envelopes and drifting through drowned filing rooms: *hydraulic attendants, biological mitigation personnel, corrosion stewards.* But the garden has had a very long time to misunderstand its instructions, and so has its keeper.

It lives in the pump housings.

Not in one of them exclusively. It moves among all three through passages beneath the water, and no one has found the route. At times a pale oval can be seen behind a mossed grille, then the pressure changes and it is gone. At other times, during the indoor rain, it stands on the central bridge with its head lowered, letting the torrent strike its back.

It is tall when it stands upright—taller than the welded door, though it seldom does. Most often it travels folded over, using its long front limbs to test the water before it. Its body is covered in overlapping plates that might once have been protective workwear: dark rubber, stitched canvas, greened metal mesh. Every surface has been colonized. Moss grows in the seams. A few of the lacquer-dark vines have threaded through its shoulder frame and rooted there, so that it carries leaves with it. Small orange shelf fungi rise from the ridge of its spine. In the yellow lamp-light, it can be mistaken for one of the islands until it blinks.

Its face is the least certain part.

It has a round lamp set deep in the chest, behind a crazed glass cover. This is where its light is. The head, bowed beneath a hood or carapace, bears no reliable features except a number of fine, pale sensory filaments that comb continually through the air and water. When it lifts its face toward someone, they may see two wet black eyes. Or polished inspection lenses. Or the blank oval of a mask filmed with condensation. The garden does not agree on this point.

The birds know it as a warm structure. They perch in the vines along its shoulders and pick insects from its moss. The crabs shelter beneath the hems of its plates when the rain falls. Fish follow it in a silver procession, especially when it wades through the shallower channels, because its passing stirs loose food from the sediment. The eels do not fear it, but they do not touch it. They make room.

It does not feed in any recognizable way.

At dawn—if the slow brightening of the bulbs can be called dawn—it kneels beside one of the brass inlet mouths and places both hands around the pipe. For several minutes it remains there as warm water passes through the wall. Its chest-lamp dims. Its leaves tremble. The little mechanisms inside it make soft, patient clicks. Then it rises again with droplets streaming from its sleeves.

Sometimes it opens its own body.

There is a door in its abdomen, flat and rectangular, concealed beneath vine growth. When the keeper unlatches it, the garden smells sharply of copper, old rain, and charged dust. Inside are pressure dials, nests of rootlets, a small reservoir where fish fry occasionally circle, and a bundle of black cables that pulse gently like sleeping organs. The keeper cleans nothing in there. It only reaches in and removes obstructions: a dead moth, a curl of mineral scale, the translucent skin shed by an eel. It places these things carefully into the water.

It tends by allowing.

This is the principle it has inherited from its lost makers, though perhaps they never intended it to mean what it means now. The keeper never prunes a vine; it persuades the vine to choose another path by moving a warm pipe or hanging a scrap of wet insulation in its way. It never kills a crab; when crabs breed too thickly beneath the panels, it opens a small valve and lets a thread of colder water into their colony until they migrate. When moss blocks a relief vent, it waits until the moss has fruited, then lifts the entire soft mat and lays it on another island.

It is especially attentive to rust.

At night, it walks the bridge rail with a little tool in one hand: not a scraper but a tuning fork of tarnished brass. It touches the fork to corroding bolts and listens. Each bloom of rust makes a different note. Some ring high and thin, some give only a dull wet thud. The keeper marks certain places with loops of red thread. By morning, crabs will have gathered there, and in a week the rust will have spread in a branching, orange pattern through the metal.

The thing in the hatch watches the keeper.

When the upper hatch mutters and the water beneath it grows black, the keeper becomes very still. Its chest-lamp shutters. The birds fall silent. Even the rain, if it is raining, seems to descend around that patch of darkness without entering it. There is an old relationship there—not friendship, not predation exactly. The keeper will sometimes bring an eel to the ladder and release it upward, hands cupped around its slick body. In return, something may leave behind an object: a toothlike piece of white ceramic, a strip of human fabric, a corroded badge whose letters cannot be read.

The keeper hangs these gifts from the vines.

There may have been people in the garden once. The bridge has handrails at a human height. The welded door bears old marks where someone repeatedly struck it with a tool. Beneath the central pump housing, there is a small metal locker, dry inside despite the flood. It contains a pair of boots, a whistle, three paper labels sealed in resin, and a photograph gone nearly blank from damp. The keeper visits this locker during the quietest interval between rains.

It cannot open it.

Or will not.

Instead, it kneels before it with its head bent, while its internal relays tick through a sequence that might be a prayer, a diagnostic routine, or the remembered sound of a name.

If a visitor enters the garden, the keeper does not immediately reveal itself. First come signs: a loose chain lifted clear of the water to make a safer crossing; a jammed valve turned just enough to reduce the current; a cluster of birds suddenly departing the fan housings and wheeling toward the far wall. Then, from behind the nearest pump island, the pale chest-lamp appears.

It does not speak. Its voice system may be drowned, or it may have decided that speech is too blunt an instrument for this place.

But it can make invitations.

A bulb will brighten where it wants someone to look. A line of fish will gather and swim toward a hidden step. A broad lily leaf will turn slowly in a current that does not touch any other leaf. If the visitor is injured, the keeper will lead them to the warm spill beneath the third brass mouth, where the water has a mineral softness and the moss grows silver at the edges. It will sit nearby, enormous and dripping, until they are able to stand.

It has never been known to leave the chamber.

Perhaps it cannot. Perhaps somewhere beneath the growth, below the roots and the rust and the layered maintenance skin, there is a tether running down through the tower: a cable, a pipe, an umbilical of old command. Or perhaps it remains because the garden is the only thing in all the flooded waterworks that still needs it.

Not to survive.

The garden would survive almost anything. It has learned too much from the machine around it.

It needs the keeper because every living place eventually gathers a witness: something that notices the third red rivet, listens for the deep engines, remembers which leaks are fertile and which are dangerous, and stands beneath the falling rain so that the storm is not alone.
