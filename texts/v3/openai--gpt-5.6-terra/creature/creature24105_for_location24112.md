The people who live in the basin are not, for the most part, people who came looking for a view. They came because there was work at the locks, work in the blending sheds, work repairing pumps and valves, work driving the little forklifts that carry palletized goods between warehouses. They came because a cousin had a room above a tool supplier, because a father had a place in the rail yard, because the rent was low in the apartments nearest the cooling trenches, because it was easier to stay than to leave.

They call themselves residents of the city rather than workers of it, though most of their daily knowledge is occupational. A child learns the names of canals before they learn street names: the East Cut, the Hot Drain, Number Six, the Old Intake. Grandmothers know which stairwells smell of mold after three wet days. Teenagers know which fence has a gap behind the transformer yard and which unfinished embankment makes the quickest route home. Everyone knows the pitch of a healthy pump. When it changes, people look up.

Their houses are built high enough to acknowledge the ground without pretending to defeat it. Apartment blocks stand on blunt concrete legs, their lowest levels full of bicycle cages, repair shops, storage lockers, and dark rooms containing pipes. The homes above are narrow but organized. Shoes stay near the door, muddy ones on grated trays. Laundry hangs indoors or under covered balconies, because an outside line may be drenched by rain, steam, or soot before the shirts are dry. Windows are fitted with insect mesh, storm latches, and sometimes improvised strips of foam to keep out the whistle of industrial air.

The people have a practical relationship with cleanliness. They wash often, because heat demands it, but there is no expectation that anything will remain clean. A freshly painted railing acquires orange streaks within a season. White shirts become gray at the cuffs. Children return from school with pale clay on their calves and black canal mud beneath their fingernails. Floors are mopped with water that is emptied carefully into sinks, never simply thrown from balconies. Everyone has seen what careless water does when it finds its way into the wrong crack.

Their clothes are light, cheap, washable, and chosen for shade more than style: synthetic work shirts, loose trousers, rubber sandals, broad caps with cloth neck flaps. At the yards, nearly everyone wears a faded high-visibility vest, whether or not they are presently working. It is a garment that migrates from job to job, from workshop to food stall to evening walk. The orange and yellow strips flash among gray concrete and wet steel like small pieces of warning paint.

There are families who have been there since the mudflat was first filled and compacted. They remember when the outer road ended in standing water, when reeds grew where the third warehouse now stands, when the first apartment buildings smelled sharply of new cement. Their older members speak of the city’s beginnings without nostalgia. The early years were muddy, hot, and badly planned, they say; the drains were too shallow, the curbs too low, the first roofs leaked. Still, they regard every new embankment and pump house with a proprietary suspicion. A new contractor has to prove that they understand the basin. Those who do not understand water do not last long.

There are also temporary populations: crews housed in modular dormitories behind wire fencing, contractors from upriver, mechanics from the port, inspectors who arrive in air-conditioned cars and leave before the evening insects begin. Some stay only a month and complain continuously about the heat. Others return year after year and acquire, despite themselves, the habits of locals: carrying folded plastic rain capes, checking drain grates with the toe of a shoe, drinking cold salted tea from metal flasks.

The city’s most respected people are not necessarily its managers. They are the maintenance workers who know where the obsolete lines run; the old electrician who can identify a fault from a brief flicker under an awning; the woman who runs the hardware counter and keeps, in unlabeled drawers, every gasket and threaded fitting no catalog remembers; the drainage supervisor who walks the canals before major rain and makes phone calls no one else thinks to make. Their expertise is difficult to display and impossible to replace. In a dry city, a failed drain may be an inconvenience. Here it is a change in the shape of the day.

The children make the hard infrastructure into landscape. They race along the raised pipe corridors until shouted down by guards. They fish with string and bent wire from the safer sides of the lock channels, though their parents tell them not to eat anything they catch. They collect bright industrial fragments—ceramic insulator shards, colored cable ties, smooth bits of plastic weathered in drains—and trade them in schoolyards. They are adept at reading weather not from the sky but from surfaces: the sudden slickness of concrete, the warm smell from a culvert, the way ants begin climbing walls, the low urgency in adult voices when a ditch starts to run backward.

Dogs live in the city with the assuredness of longtime employees. Most are thin, mud-colored, and clever enough to sleep beneath parked trucks without being trapped by them. They know the lunch schedules of workshops and the quieter routes across service roads. Some belong to guards, some to families, some to nobody in particular. During rain they pack themselves beneath loading bays, tolerating one another closely until the water falls off the roof in sheets.

Cats are less visible but more established. They inhabit storage yards, grain sheds, abandoned office annexes, and the dry upper cavities of utility buildings. Their kittens appear in improbable places: behind stacked conduit, inside empty cable drums, under a forklift seat. The night crews leave food for them, nominally to control rats, though the rats continue to thrive in the pipe racks and culverts.

The rats themselves are perhaps the city’s true original citizens. They travel where people cannot: under slab foundations, through storm drains, along insulated ducts, inside the warm spaces behind machinery. They are large, wet-furred, and wary. Workers see them in the sharp beam of a flashlight and then not again. Their presence is accepted in the same manner as rust, mosquitoes, and condensation: something to manage, never fully eliminate.

Frogs occupy a more favorable place in local imagination. Their voices emerge after rain from holes where no living thing ought to persist. Children try to find them; adults insist that they are good luck, or a sign the culverts still breathe, or evidence that the water has not been poisoned beyond recovery. Nobody agrees exactly. Still, when a pump station falls quiet after a storm and frogs begin calling from the drainage channels, people notice the sound with relief.

The egrets are treated with a restrained affection. They appear at the canals in their startling white, calm amid oil sheens and floating debris, and remain indifferent to traffic, shouting, and machinery. Workers eat lunches near them. Security guards point them out to visitors. A few residents leave fish scraps at the water’s edge, though this is discouraged by signs. The birds offer no explanation for their presence. They simply stand in the contaminated shallows, waiting with immense patience.

At night, the people of the basin become more visible. The heat loosens slightly, though never enough. Folding chairs appear outside shopfronts and beneath apartment stilts. Food vendors set up beside bus stops: grilled fish, noodles, dumplings, skewers of meat turning in the oily air. The best stalls have fans mounted low, blowing warm air across customers’ ankles. People eat under buzzing fluorescent tubes while tankers pass slowly on the road behind them.

Conversations are practical at first—whose shift changed, whether the rail spur is blocked, when the rain will come, how high the Hot Drain got last time—but they lengthen. Children drift between groups. Someone plays music from a phone. Someone else repairs a fan with a screwdriver borrowed from three tables away. In the low spaces between buildings, where every sound carries, an argument can be heard across an entire block, followed half an hour later by laughter from the same direction.

There are people who dream of leaving, especially the young. They imagine cities with hills, dry sidewalks, trees that form actual shade, windows that can be opened without admitting insects and chemical warmth. Some do leave. Many return after years elsewhere, unsettled by silence or by streets where rain is allowed to lie in gutters without immediate concern. They return with different clothes, different accents, and a renewed irritation at the basin’s stains and smells. Then, gradually, they relearn its small disciplines.

The city’s deepest inhabitants may be the systems themselves: the pumps in their concrete chambers, the lock gates lifting and lowering with slow authority, the culverts carrying unseen water under roads and yards. They have a kind of life because people attend to them. Grease is applied. Bearings are changed. Screens are cleared of reed stems, plastic bags, dead birds, and sediment. When a pump fails, the city feels it before it understands it. The air changes. A ditch begins to rise. Phones ring. Lights appear in workshops at hours when they should be dark.

So the basin is inhabited by keepers: tired shift workers, schoolchildren, dogs, frogs, rats, egrets, mechanics, vendors, insects, and machinery that never entirely rests. None of them dominates the place. Each has adapted to the warm wet pressure of it, to the fact that all solid ground is provisional and every boundary must include a drain.

They live in a city that survives not by keeping water out, but by knowing where it is going next.
