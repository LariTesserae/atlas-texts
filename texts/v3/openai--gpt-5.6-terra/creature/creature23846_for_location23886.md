What lives in the Dry Orchard is not, at first, easy to separate into people, animals, plants, and city.

The local word most often translated as *person* means something closer to *a pattern permitted to move*. It can describe a human-shaped citizen, certainly, but also an old root-system with voting rights, a caravan’s trained vault-hound, a memory-bearing house, or—in rare cases—a machine that has spent enough years planted in the digestion gardens to acquire habits.

The most numerous people are called the **Tenders**, though few of them tend anything in the gentle sense. They are descendants of several lineages that arrived on the shelf during wetter eras, before the dust ring dimmed the sky and the surface water withdrew. Their bodies remain broadly mammalian: warm, bilateral, expressive, prone to fatigue and attachment. But generations in partnership with the Orchard have made them subtly, thoroughly unlike their ancestors.

Their skin is dry and pale in the highland light, often patterned with fine mineral freckling around the eyes, throat, and joints. It does not sweat much. Instead, when overheated or frightened, a Tender’s skin opens minute dark pores along the collarbones and forearms, releasing heat in a faint crackle of ionized air. Children can be recognized at dusk by the static sparks that jump between their fingers when they argue.

Their hair grows in narrow, waxy fibers, usually silver-gray, black-green, or the color of old copper. Many cultivate it into loops, mats, or upright combs threaded with living filaments from household plants. These filaments are not decorative alone. They carry charge, temperature, pulse, and sometimes messages. A person returning from a distant terrace may arrive before their body does: their hair-root has already sent a sensation of tiredness through the domestic walls, or the taste of a storm encountered on the road.

Their eyes are adapted to the perpetual half-light. The iris is usually dark enough to seem absent, but it is ringed with delicate reflective tissue, so that a Tender looking sideways can flash white, amber, green, or a cold violet in the dimness. They dislike direct bright light, though they have little occasion to fear it. The lamps within the city are grown to accommodate them: low, colored, responsive to breath and movement.

No Tender is wholly unwired from the plant system for long.

At birth, each is given a **root-name**: not a spoken name, but a living strand implanted beneath the skin behind the ear or in the hollow of the sternum. It grows slowly through the body, branching harmlessly among nerves and blood vessels. By adolescence it has joined the individual to their dwelling, their kin-grove, and the municipal root web. It does not control thought. The Tenders are very protective of that distinction. But it translates bodily states into signals the Orchard can understand.

If someone is ill, their house begins to alter its temperature before they consciously ask. If they are lost, familiar paths may remain open longer. If they die far from home, the root-name tightens, sends its final patterns downward, and waits for retrieval.

Because of this, privacy is not conceived as silence.

Privacy is the right to remain unread by the wrong roots.

A person may veil their root-name in resin, sleep in a ceramic room whose walls have no living tissue, or travel with a portable dry chamber called a hush. Lovers commonly share root access before they share a bed. Political conspirators exchange dead things: polished stones, machine scraps, old paper, anything the Orchard cannot immediately taste. Children dare one another to enter abandoned pre-city structures, where no root-web reaches and their own pulses become strangely solitary.

There are others who refuse implantation entirely. They are called **the Severed**, though some use the name proudly and some regard it as an insult. Their skin bears scars where root-names would ordinarily be placed. They live in old ceramic districts, on the barest outer shelf, or in the upper levels of transit wells where the fields interfere with biological signaling. They trade in secrets, unrecorded songs, unregistered births, and histories too dangerous to let the city preserve. The Orchard does not expel them. It cannot, not entirely. Its laws require incorporation, not agreement.

But it watches them with the patience of vegetation.

The oldest inhabitants are not people in the ordinary sense. They are the **Crown Minds**: immense cultivated plants whose interiors have accumulated centuries of human decisions, animal memory, weather records, machinery diagrams, and the copied habits of the dead.

A Crown Mind may look like a grove of pale towers fused at the base, or a low black lattice spread over several terraces, or a flowering fan wider than a market square. It thinks slowly unless disturbed. Its conversations take days. Its emotions are recognizable only in retrospect. A Crown Mind might spend an entire season subtly redirecting foot traffic away from a neighborhood, and only later will residents discover that a transit well was about to fail beneath it. It might mourn a dead caretaker by growing a new chamber in the exact shape of the caretaker’s childhood room. It might become angry at a broken law and close every street leading to the offender’s house.

The Tenders insist that Crown Minds are citizens.

The Crown Minds have never confirmed or denied this.

Then there are the **remembered**.

When a person dies, the digestion gardens receive the body, but not always the whole person. The body’s mineral arrangement, its implanted root-name, its stored messages, the habits recorded in domestic fibers, the impressions left in beds and tools and favorite corridors—all are taken in. Over months or years, patterns emerge in the garden’s tissues.

Most are only echoes: a familiar laugh in the tremor of a bridge, a preference for a certain lamp color, a tendency for one household vine to curl around visitors with a particular scent on their clothes.

Some become more coherent.

These remembered dead do not return as bodies. They live in walls, in municipal archive roots, in the low voices of flowering towers broadcasting at night. They may advise descendants, settle disputes, recite maps no living traveler has used for a century. They are not considered immortal, because their patterns eventually thin, merge, or become useful in other ways. But they are not gone in the way death once meant gone.

A family can inherit an ancestor as a corridor.

An old judge may remain active as the stern central root beneath a neighborhood plaza.

A beloved grandmother may become the smell released by the house whenever its children come home safely.

The animals of the Dry Orchard live among these people not as possessions but as neighboring intelligences with particular contracts.

Vault-hounds are the most familiar. They select their own human associations. A pack may follow a caravan for three generations, then abandon it because one of the descendants has developed a voice pattern the hounds dislike. They understand city vibration better than any person and are often the first to recognize a lie spoken while barefoot. Their chest calls are not merely warning signals. In the deepest packs, they form names, territorial claims, elegies, and long rolling arguments about the quality of old bones.

The six-legged grazers are sacred only in the administrative sense: killing one without a licensed need is treated as damage to municipal infrastructure. Their reflective down is harvested during seasonal shedding and woven into cloaks that retain static warmth. They wander through markets, press their broad mouths to grounding stalks, and occasionally lean their enormous quiet heads through a dwelling entrance to inspect a newborn child.

Small foil-winged insects are used as message carriers in places where root systems are damaged. Thread-mice are tolerated despite their habit of stealing conductive fibers, because their tunnels aerate the deeper mats. The root-shaped predators are not tolerated at all, but the city recognizes their necessity and maintains designated hunting corridors where they can run unseen beneath the stalk fields.

Even the dust has residents.

Microscopic colonies of engineered lichen ride each storm front, dormant in the open air, waking only when they strike a compatible mineral seam or the warm outer skin of a pylon. Some are older than the city. Some may have arrived from elsewhere in the system, carried down inside a fallen machine or an ancient cargo vault. They are responsible for the slow drilling of transit wells, the repair of ceramic ruins, and several outbreaks of architectural growth that the municipal records describe with great embarrassment.

There are rumors, especially among caravan people, of things beneath the shelf.

Not buried machines. Not roots.

Older dwellers.

They are said to move through the stone that is no longer quite stone, following the deep conductive fractures where the Orchard sends its oldest signals. When they pass near a district, lamps dim. Waterless pipes begin to resonate. People dream of being enormous, motionless, and patient beneath the mountains.

No confirmed body has ever been found.

The oldest Crown Minds change the subject whenever they are asked.

So the Dry Orchard is inhabited by Tenders and Severed, hounds and grazers, insects and predatory knots of root, civic groves and incomplete ancestors. It is inhabited by laws that have become biological, by machinery that has learned how to be composted, by old buildings dreaming through new leaves.

And perhaps, beneath all of them, by something that has waited through every age of softness, listening to the city grow overhead.
