What lives there is not a single people, nor a single kind of beast, but an unfinished congregation: things adapted to a world that has only just made room for them.

The smallest lives are the quickest.

They are the first to enter every crack, the first to turn a fallen slab from an obstacle into a neighborhood. Pale jointed crawlers, blind mites with translucent bellies, soft white worms threaded with grit, beetle-things whose shells are plated in salt. They feed on whatever the earth gives them: mineral films, fungal smears, shed skins, the minute corpses of insects that fall through the sun shafts from the world above. Some live in the warm seams and emerge only when the ground cools. Some carry pebbles on their backs until the pebbles grow into crusts and become part of them. They are not quite animals in the familiar sense, or perhaps they are animals before familiarity has had time to settle over them.

There are scavengers that seem made specifically for disaster.

When a collapse comes, and the chamber fills with chalk-white dust, they arrive before the dust has finished falling. Long-bodied burrowers with hinged skulls push into the new rubble, tasting heat and pressure with the naked skin around their mouths. They do not eat stone. They eat what stone exposes: trapped grubs, broken nests, fungus buried below old floors, injured runners, the steaming soft tissues of creatures caught in a fissure. Their young are born pale and nearly formless, then darken as minerals collect in their hide. A mature rubble-eater may be black with basalt scales along its spine, its legs white with salt.

The herding beasts are the most visible inhabitants of the open ground.

They move in families through the bright basins, massive but nervous, their fur always damp at the tips. Their forelimbs are broad and strong enough to rake loose soil aside or pull them up a wall of clay. Their hind limbs are built for sudden leaps across fresh breaks in the floor. They have no need for eyes in the deeper chambers; the bone plates over their faces are smooth, featureless shields, crossed by grooves that quiver at distant vibrations.

They listen through their skulls.

A herd may stop all at once in the middle of feeding, every plated head lowered to the earth. Then the smallest among them will step forward. The young test the ground because they are light. They place one foot down, then another, and wait for the answering tremor. If the floor is good, the herd follows. If it is hollow, they turn with surprising speed, bellowing in those low wet notes that carry through the stone.

Their lantern-sacs are not lamps exactly. They swell beneath the throat when the animals are frightened, courting, wounded, or near an active fissure. In darkness a herd becomes a drifting cluster of dim turquoise and greenish blue, each animal’s face lit from below as though it had swallowed a piece of deep water. The people say the lights are the earth looking back through them.

Other large animals do not gather.

The wall-walkers live alone or in pairs, suspended in the high ribs of the cavern. They have long hooked limbs, folded tight against bodies covered in a rough hide that resembles mineral deposits from a distance. They can remain motionless for days, perhaps weeks, drinking condensation from the stone and waiting for winged things to settle near them. When they move, they unfold with the terrible slowness of a crack spreading in ice.

There are gliders that descend from the heights at dusk. Their membranes are thin enough to show the branching blood beneath them, and their bodies have dense, warm cores that draw silver moths in clouds. They feed with broad combs of teeth, sweeping the air in flight. In the daylight shafts they look dark, almost ordinary; deeper inside, where their skins catch the glow of mineral fungi and the blue throats of the herds, they become rose-colored and transparent.

The true predators live where brightness ends.

Some are low and armored, their faceted skins taking on the colors of the ground beneath them: iron-red, sulfur-yellow, wet black, the pale gray of fresh rock. Their legs work in uneven sequence, too many to count before they vanish under a ledge. Their jaws open sideways, splitting the head into petals of hard cartilage. They hunt by vibration. A careless hoof, a dropped tool, a child running across a stable shelf—these are louder to them than any cry.

Others are less easily seen. They inhabit warm pools and flooded crevices. A traveler may kneel to drink and notice only a pattern in the mud, then feel the water pulling away from their hands. The pool-creatures have mouths like circular drills, but they are timid unless disturbed. They prefer animals already injured by a fall. In settlements, people keep small fires or vibrating stones near water basins to discourage them.

And there are creatures no one hunts because no one understands whether they are animals at all.

Thin pale forms sometimes appear near newly opened fissures, lifting themselves from steam on hundreds of hair-fine limbs. They collapse if exposed to dry air. They leave glassy threads behind on the stone. Children collect these threads when they can and tie them around the wrists of the sick, though the elders say this is foolish: the thread belongs to places that are still forming, and it carries the desire to return.

The people themselves are a people of listening.

They are not numerous. Their settlements are scattered from shelf to shelf, each one small enough to abandon quickly, large enough to preserve a fire and a few remembered names. Their clothing is pale hide, softened in mineral water and stitched with gut or braided fibers stripped from the sinews of the larger herd animals. Many wear stone beads or little polished bones at their throats. These are not ornaments alone. A bead that begins to crack against the chest may warn of changes in pressure before a person can feel them.

Their children learn the sounds before they learn the routes.

This is the hollow sound of a floor with water beneath it.  
This is the sound of clay settling harmlessly.  
This is the sound of a wall preparing to move.  
This is the sudden quiet that comes before the animals run.

They make maps, but not maps of fixed places. Their maps are skins and flat stones marked with sequences: three tall pillars, then a hot draft, then a salt basin, then the chamber where the ceiling sings after rain. A map from ten years ago may be useful only as a warning. A map from yesterday may already be a lie.

Their oldest skill is not building. It is leaving.

They know how to dismantle a shelter in an hour, how to carry coals wrapped in damp clay, how to turn a drying rack into a sled, how to follow the herds without frightening them. They know that the safest route is often not the one marked by human cairns, but the one used by small animals in steady numbers. Where the runners stream without panic, the ground has usually held long enough to trust.

Yet the people are not merely refugees from collapse. They are makers of small continuities.

They fire clay when they find a shelf that will stay dry. They carve bowls. They save salt. They trade rare things between shafts: sharp black glass from deep fractures, copper-green crusts used as pigment, luminous throat-sacs carefully dried and worn inside leather pouches. They paint marks on shelter stones—not scenes of the surface, which few have seen clearly, but patterns of lines breaking, widening, joining again. Their art resembles cracks because cracks are their alphabet.

Some among them go upward.

They are called shaft-climbers, though no one knows whether they ever reach the world above. They travel with hooked bones, hide ropes, smoke torches, and water sealed in bladder skins. They climb toward the yellow squares of light, passing through clouds of moths and nesting gliders, into places where rain falls sideways and unfamiliar roots hang down from the soil overhead.

Those who return bring stories of an impossible land: green growth covering the ground, wind that comes from nowhere and does not smell of stone, open water moving continuously in a single direction. They speak of trees, which the listeners imagine as enormous mineral columns with leaves of living skin. They speak of a sky with no roof. Most people consider this last claim an exaggeration born of thin air.

But there are other humans below, perhaps older ones, perhaps changed ones.

In the deepest warm corridors, beyond the settled shelves, travelers sometimes find arrangements of stones too old to read. Circles within circles. Bone tools fused into mineral crust. Handprints pressed into clay that hardened before anyone living was born. The prints are often larger than a human hand, or have too many fingers, though the earth can distort anything over time. Around them lie the remains of fires burned with some fuel no present settlement knows how to make: blackened pits whose ash remains warm when the rest of the chamber is cold.

The people avoid these places.

Not because they believe them haunted in the simple sense. The underground has too many real dangers for simple hauntings to command much respect. They avoid them because the animals avoid them. Herds turn away. Moths do not settle there. Even the small runners divide around the old circles, flowing on either side as if the ground inside them were still moving.

Perhaps something lives beneath even this country: not a creature with a body, but a pressure, a heat, a slow intention in the stone.

The inhabitants do not name it often. Naming suggests a thing can be addressed, bargained with, offended, forgiven. Instead, when the floor trembles beneath a settlement or a new crack opens near a sleeping place, they place their palms on the ground and wait.

Then they watch the animals.

If the runners flee, they gather their children.  
If the winged things rise, they put out the fires.  
If the herd beasts begin to glow blue in the throat, they take what they can carry and leave the rest.

And when the stone finally opens somewhere new—when sunlight pours down into dust, when an old chamber becomes a bright basin, when hundreds of startled creatures cry beneath a square of sky—the people come last, cautiously, after the scavengers and the moths and the first dazzled herds.

They stand at the edge of the fresh light.

They look upward at the world that has not noticed them.

Then they begin to mark which ground might hold.
