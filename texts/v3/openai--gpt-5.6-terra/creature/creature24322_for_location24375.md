What lives there is not easily seen because it has learned the city’s first rule: nothing survives by standing wholly above the water.

The inhabitants are called, in the oldest submerged inscriptions, the **Low Ones**—though this is not a name they use for themselves. It is a description made by people who still believed height was a virtue. The Low Ones live in the flooded floors, the undercrofts, the sealed cistern corridors, and the chambers that were deliberately built to be given over to silt. They do not occupy houses in the ordinary sense. A building may belong to them for three seasons, or for two centuries, or only while a particular crack in its eastern wall continues to breathe warm vapor.

They are upright when they need to be, but rarely entirely so. At a distance, one might mistake a Low One for a pale trunk standing in brine, a column wrapped in wet cloth, a person stooped under a load of reeds. Their bodies are narrow and long-limbed, with joints that bend farther than human joints ought to. Their skin is not scaled, furred, or smooth. It has the faint granular texture of limestone after rain: pale gray, ochre, salt-white, sometimes darkened nearly black by wetness. Under the skin run slow shifting bands of color, like sediment settling in a jar.

Their faces are difficult to retain in memory. They have features, certainly: a brow, eyes set deep beneath clear lids, a mouth whose corners extend farther back than expected. But the face changes with moisture. Dryness sharpens it. Rain blurs it. In high humidity, the skin around the eyes becomes translucent enough that the movement of dark fluids beneath it can be seen. Their hair, if it is hair, grows in sparse cords along the neck and spine. In old individuals these cords harden, mineralize, and trail behind them like roots.

They breathe air, but only reluctantly. Their true respiration happens through soft folded organs along the ribs, the throat, and the backs of the knees. When submerged, these open into delicate red-gray fans. When the water is too saline or too warm, they emerge and stand with their mouths near cracks in walls, inhaling the cool exhalations of the ground.

This is why the city’s walls are damp. It is why its lower chambers are never fully sealed. The Low Ones require seepage. They require masonry that leaks, floors that sweat, reservoirs that remember water after they have been emptied. A dry building is, to them, not merely uncomfortable but dead.

They are not numerous. There may be thousands beneath the city, or fewer than a hundred. Their number cannot be counted because they do not remain distinct in the way surface creatures do. During the hottest stretches, families gather in deep basins and sleep submerged together, bodies intertwined among the stairs. Their skin loosens. Their voices slow into vibrations. For days or weeks they may become a single dense congregation, exchanging warmth, nutrients, impressions, and pieces of memory through the water.

When they emerge again, they are not always exactly the same people.

A child may return with an elder’s fear of a particular canal. An old mason may wake knowing the song his sister used before she dissolved into the basin thirty years earlier. A parent may recognize their own hands on the arms of someone who has not yet been born. The Low Ones do not find this alarming. To them, identity is a reservoir: it rises, spreads, enters new rooms, leaves mineral deposits, and eventually becomes part of the ground.

They build very little now. The city was old when they arrived, or perhaps it was built by ancestors so distant that ancestry has become indistinguishable from geology. But they maintain constantly.

At times of deep dark, when even the blue-gray thinning of the sky does not come, they move through the streets with bundles of shell grit and wet clay. They clear the broad seams between paving stones. They scrape salt from carved depth-marks. They dismantle a stair that has become dangerous and rebuild it three steps away. They open a wall in one district so that a rising basin may enter an abandoned court rather than drown an inhabited chamber. They fill another opening with lime mortar mixed with ash, crushed shell, and something living taken from their own bodies.

Their repairs are never permanent. They would consider permanence a form of negligence.

A wall must know how to fail without bringing down the rooms beside it. A bridge must be free to shift in its cradles. A neighborhood must be able to sink without dragging the whole city after it. The Low Ones call this practice—if it can be translated as a practice—**making room for the water’s thought**.

They do not fear the strange movements of the place. The reversed canals, the absent districts, the doorways that show another weather: these are signs that the city is listening below itself.

The Low Ones speak to it.

Not in words alone. Their language is largely made through pressure: palms against wet walls, feet placed on particular paving stones, low tones sung into cistern throats, sequences of taps carried through archways and water pipes. A message may take a day to reach the far side of the city, traveling through shell-limestone and saturated clay. A warning is often felt before it is understood: a pulse under the sole of the foot, a tremor in a cup of basin water, a sudden taste of iron on the tongue.

The city answers in changes.

A canal reverses to indicate that a lower quarter should be abandoned. A wall beads with sweet water when a hidden spring is opening. A stair loses a step because its old arrangement no longer corresponds to the ground beneath it. The reflections in flooded plazas show buildings that are gone because the city retains their weight, their shape, their claim upon the water.

The Low Ones are caretakers, then—but also interpreters, and perhaps organs.

There are other things among them, though whether these are separate beings is disputed even by those who live there.

In the deepest civic basins dwell the **Scribes**. They are smaller than the Low Ones, nearly child-sized, and almost wholly aquatic. Their bodies are thin and white as peeled roots. Their eyes have no visible pupils. They spend their lives on the underwater stairways, tracing and retracing the carved levels with sensitive fingertips.

Where the measurement marks become writing, the Scribes gather.

They eat almost nothing that anyone has observed. They draw sustenance from mineral films, from the faint sweetness in old enclosed water, from the soft decay accumulating in the seams of stone. Their fingers exude a weak acid that freshens old carvings as they pass over them. Over decades, they deepen some marks and erase others. The laws written beneath the waterline are therefore never fixed. A depth that once meant safety may come to mean mourning. A mark for flood may become a mark for return.

The Scribes do not speak aloud. When disturbed, they turn their blank eyes upward and produce a sound inside the water like a bell heard through several walls. Afterward, some portion of the basin will alter: a current begins, a stair cracks, a submerged door opens onto a room that had not existed before.

There are also the **Roof Sleepers**, though few people below the old upper stories see them clearly. They cling to the undersides of collapsed domes and high vaults, where condensation gathers in warm beads. They are broad, flat creatures with skins like folded wax, their limbs splayed around them in slow radial arrangements. During rain they unfold and drink upward from falling water. In dry intervals they contract into pale knots against the stone, indistinguishable from old lime deposits.

The Roof Sleepers make no noise, but their presence changes the rooms beneath them. Water collected under a ceiling where one sleeps will remain cool long after dawnless heat has taken the street. It will not spoil. It will hold, reflected in its surface, a sky from some other season.

The Low Ones harvest this water sparingly. They believe the Roof Sleepers dream the city’s forgotten elevations: the time before subsidence, before the sea lost its boundary, before every public square learned to receive a basin.

And then there are the ones in the doorways.

No one calls them inhabitants, exactly. They are taller than the Low Ones, thin enough that they seem made from the darkness gathered in an opening. They never enter the streets. They stand beyond the threshold of doors that should open onto dry halls, bright noon, distant rooms, or impossible versions of the city. Sometimes they are visible only as a weight in the dark. Sometimes one sees a wet pale hand resting against the far side of a wall. Sometimes a face appears in the bright impossible room beyond, watching with an expression of immense exhaustion.

The Low Ones leave these doorways undisturbed.

They place shallow bowls of brine nearby, pieces of shell-limestone, coils of root, fragments of old carved stone. Not offerings, precisely. More like acknowledgments. They say the doorway beings are those who chose not to change when the city changed. Or those who changed too completely. Or the city’s former inhabitants, preserved in the shape of their refusal.

On nights when the darkness is especially close, the figures beyond the doors may whisper.

Their voices do not cross the threshold as sound. They appear as memories in the minds of listeners: a room with glass windows; a dry road; birds turning above a market; the unbearable distance of a blue sky.

The Low Ones listen without envy. Then they return to their basins.

They have a music, though it is almost below hearing. In flooded courtyards, during the hours when warm vapor drifts low across the paving, they stand waist-deep in water and hum into the stone. The note begins in one throat and is taken up by another, not repeated but bent slightly, each voice adjusting to the resonance of the walls. Over time, the entire courtyard begins to answer. Water trembles around their bodies. Moss loosens from mortar. A blocked drain opens somewhere below.

The song is not ceremony in the sense of prayer. It is maintenance. It keeps certain chambers from collapsing. It tells the buried foundations that they are still being remembered. It teaches new water the routes that old water took.

Occasionally, a solitary Low One walks far beyond the outer quarters, along the old causeways through steaming mud. They carry no food and no tool except a smooth stone marked with depth-lines. They visit the pylons standing alone in the flats.

There, they wait until the far structures shift in apparent distance.

When the pylons seem near, they return quickly, often before the mist has lifted. When the pylons seem far, they may remain for days, half-submerged in the mud, listening. Sometimes they do not come back at all.

In time, perhaps, a new pylon appears farther out on the flats.

The city does not mourn them in a human fashion. A canal changes course. A basin fills. A stair receives another carved mark beneath the water. The Low Ones gather, share what they remember of the departed, and let that memory disperse among them.

Nothing lives there that is separate from the damp, shifting body of the place for very long.

The city has inhabitants, but it also has circulations, dreams, repairs, hungers, and recollections. Its people are one expression of its low endurance. They are the parts that walk, touch, listen, and decide where the next flood may be allowed to go.

When one of them stands alone in a dark street, pale and motionless among the hot wet stones, it may look as if the city has grown a person for the evening.

And when that person slips silently into a doorway full of water and does not emerge, it may be that the city has taken the shape back into itself.
