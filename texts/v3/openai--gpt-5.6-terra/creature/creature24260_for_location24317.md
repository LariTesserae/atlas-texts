What lives there is not one people, exactly, but a negotiated multitude.

The humans call themselves by district names more often than by any larger nationality: people of the North Grafts, the Foam Marches, the Long Trestle, the Lichenward, the Old Vent. They are surface-descended in the broad biological sense, but generations underground have made them recognizably of this place. Most are spare rather than frail, with strong hands, broad feet, and the habitual economy of people who have learned that every sudden movement has consequences. Their eyes tend toward pale irises and large pupils, though nobody is truly adapted to darkness; there is too much dim daylight for that and too much work done by color. A conduit gardener must distinguish a healthy amber pulse from the greenish slowing that means mineral fever. A stone surveyor reads warmth in gray foam. A child learns the colors of dangerous mold before learning arithmetic.

They dress in layers of root fiber, felted insect silk, old woven cable sheathing, and the soft, tough plates shed by grazing animals. Their clothing is practical but not plain. Underground people have made an art of visibility. A coat lining may be dyed with lichen pigments that flare blue under lamp-spectrum light. Tram workers wear bands of reflective fungal lacquer. Children stitch bright seed cases to their cuffs. In the long ash-blue hours, a market crowd can look like a loose constellation moving through the trunks.

Their bodies carry the city’s atmosphere on them. Resin in the hair. Fine stone powder in the creases of their hands. The green scent of cut vine on the sleeves of gardeners. Many wear small breathing charms at the throat: not filters, usually, but folded strips of aromatic bark that clear the taste of refinery haze from the mouth. Some chew bitter root pellets while working in warm mineral zones. Some rub conductive oils into their fingertips before tending live electrical meshes.

They are a people used to maintenance as a form of citizenship.

A person may inherit not a house but a responsibility: twenty meters of sugar conduit, a junction of old roots beneath a school, a row of ventilation reeds, a colony of beetles bred to eat a particular rot. There are families who have listened to the same stand of structural trees for five generations and can identify each trunk by the sound a knuckle makes against its bark. There are children who grow up knowing that the wall of their bedroom is alive, and that one day it may need to be cut away, coaxed aside, or thanked for its service.

This makes them patient in some ways and fiercely impatient in others.

They understand waiting. They understand that a stair may take ten years to settle into the shape promised by its builders; that a new neighborhood begins with spores, scaffolds, and a prediction no one fully trusts. They can spend a month diverting a freight route around a root-tip no thicker than a wrist.

But they do not tolerate waste, carelessness, or the surface habit of treating a living system as scenery. Someone who pours solvent into an unmarked fissure, drives a stake through an active fungal route, or kills a herd animal for sport is not merely a criminal. They are considered stupid in a profound, almost theological way: a person refusing the fact that they live inside the thing that keeps them alive.

Their oldest institutions are part guild, part family, part ecological ministry.

The Listeners are among the most respected. They are not mystics, though visitors sometimes mistake them for such. A Listener studies the language of stressed wood: the ticks of drying, the creaks of tension, the low pulses produced when sap moves through a trunk under pressure. Some use contact instruments of ceramic and brass. Older ones sometimes press an ear directly to the bark. They advise engineers, settle property disputes, and attend births of buildings. When a major trunk must be felled, a Listener determines whether it is dying, merely changing course, or sending strength toward some unseen rootward purpose.

The Graftwrights shape homes, halls, and bridges. Their work is slow and intimate. They do not build a doorway and walk away; they persuade a growing structure to accept the doorway. They bind stems together in woven cradles, introduce compatible fungal strains, prune light-seeking shoots, and sometimes spend decades on one civic project. The grandest public buildings are known by the names of their Graftwright lineages, not their politicians.

The Vaporers live closest to the warm stone. They work beneath black glass hoods and return home with cracked lips, heat rashes, and a faint metallic shine on their skin. They harvest the breath of the earth: mineral vapors, sulfurous gases, trace water, volatile oils. Their work is dangerous and wealthy, though no one calls them wealthy without qualification. A good vapor seam can support a district. A ruptured one can poison it.

Then there are the quiet professions that hold everything together: beetle keepers, membrane menders, tram insulators, moss shearers, plate polishers, spore accountants, herd callers, lamp-tenders, root surgeons, rail-levelers. A city with no rain has no excuse for romance about plumbing; its equivalents are sacred, tedious, and everywhere.

The inhabitants do not live alone among their own kind.

The beetle herds are perhaps the closest thing the city has to livestock in the familiar sense, though they are treated more like municipal workers with appetites. They move in glossy black clusters through bark yards and compost courts, reducing dead fiber into useful pellets and warm soil. A trained keeper can steer thousands with taps on hollow wood and strips of fermented rind. Children are warned not to step among them, not because the beetles bite, but because the herd will simply continue its work.

The moss-furred burrowers live beneath homes, embankments, and seed fields. Their actual bodies are warm, blunt, and almost blind, but their coats host dense little ecologies: moss, bacteria, pale thread fungi, sometimes tiny flowering growths in the brighter districts. They are kept for insulation, compost turning, and the cultivation of medicinal molds. To brush one is to feel a moving patch of cool forest floor.

Root-hounds belong to no single household. They are issued by district crews when something is wrong beneath the pavement. Long-bodied, six-legged, and soft as folded gloves, they can smell ruptured sap, overheated fungal mats, leaking gas, and the particular sourness of wood beginning to fail. Their handlers speak to them in clicks and breaths rather than shouted commands. A root-hound is considered an excellent judge of character, mostly because it dislikes people who move too fast.

The larger grazers drift through the open basins in managed migratory bands. They are taller than a person at the shoulder, narrow and jointed, with translucent lower jaws that rasp mineral lichens from young stone. At night—or what passes for night beneath the roof—they glow faintly from their digestive heat, not enough to illuminate a road but enough to make whole herds resemble embers moving through grass. Their shed plates become tools, roofing, insulation, and the translucent panes used in certain kinds of lamps. Their dung is processed into heat, fertilizer, and fermentation stock. They are not pets. They have too much momentum, too little interest in human opinion. But people sing low route-songs to guide them away from new construction, and the animals often follow.

Above all of these are the things that are not exactly animals and not exactly infrastructure.

The great root systems themselves are alive in ways the city must respect. Some are cultivated, some inherited, some older than settlement and only partially understood. They transmit nutrients, chemical warnings, electrical signals, and perhaps something else. A root corridor damaged in one district can cause symptoms kilometers away: a warehouse wall sweating resin, a stand of street lamps closing their leaf arrays at noon, an entire block of dwelling trunks turning subtly toward a distant light well.

People argue over whether the city’s oldest living structures possess awareness. Officially, this is an unproductive question. Unofficially, nearly everyone has an opinion.

There are those who say the Cathedral Graft beneath the central exchange remembers every fire it has survived. There are caretakers of a western rail trunk who insist it dislikes certain songs. There are old residents who leave scraps of mineral bread in the forks of their home trees before asking for a room to be widened. Engineers laugh at this until a structure begins to behave in ways their diagrams did not predict.

And there are other inhabitants, rarely admitted in the daytime civic language.

In abandoned shafts and ungrafted stands, fungal networks develop habits. They lure particular insects. They make sleep near them unusually vivid. They release odors that resemble childhood kitchens, dead relatives’ coats, summer rain that no one born underground has ever experienced. The city regulates them when it can. It boards up dangerous blooms. It sends mycologists with masks and flame jars into basements where the walls have begun to whisper through their spores.

But it also cultivates some of them.

Memory fungi are used in mourning houses, where a family may sit together in a sealed room and breathe the carefully moderated exhalations of a colony fed on the belongings, hair, handwriting, and recorded voice of the dead. No one claims that the fungus preserves a person. No responsible person, anyway. Yet the bereaved emerge saying they have been answered, or forgiven, or reminded of something they had forgotten to grieve.

The predators in the upper gardens are another matter. They are tolerated because they keep the moth populations from becoming ruinous, and because no one has yet found a way to remove them without damaging the hanging ecosystems that sustain the upper vaults. They are called foldcats, silk-wolves, roof devils, and a dozen more local names. Their true forms are disputed. Most witnesses see only a strip of leaf-colored hide, a dangling tendril, a shadow withdrawing into a canopy. Their voices are below hearing, but people feel them in the ribs: a pressure that passes through a district and makes conversations stop.

The inhabitants have rituals for this. When the moths begin to rise in dense clouds, lamps are shuttered in certain squares. Children are called indoors. Garden crews hang polished plates from the high branches, which turn slowly and confuse descending shapes. Nobody says it is fear. They say the upper gardens are feeding.

There are surface visitors, too, though they never quite cease to be visitors.

They arrive in sealed lifts, drilling cages, and freight shafts with bright equipment, wet habits, and voices that carry too far. They bring metals the underground cannot easily produce, specialized medicines, fabrics that do not rot, news of storms, elections, oceans, wars, and sunlight. In return, they take resins, engineered fibers, fermentation cultures, medicinal molds, mineral-grown ceramics, and biological patents that the city guards with a severity bordering on paranoia.

Surface people often find the residents unnervingly calm. They mistake quiet for passivity. They see a community reroute a tram for a tree and assume incompetence; they see a factory stop production because a fungal mesh is stressed and assume superstition.

Then they remain long enough to notice that the city does not collapse.

It flexes.

It heals around damage. It turns waste into soil, exhaust into food, pressure into architecture, mineral vapor into light. It has endured cave-ins, blights, failed harvests, political purges, surface embargoes, and the slow rebellions of its own living materials. Its people are not masters of this system. They are not its prisoners either.

They are one organ among many.

At the end of a shift, they emerge from mills and graft yards and vapor sheds. They cross root streets beneath the green columns of light. They carry lunch tins, tool rolls, sleeping children, bundles of cut vine, cages of breeding beetles, letters printed on fungal paper, small pots of stubborn flowering plants. They stop at market halls where warm paste is served with sharp oils and mineral salt. They speak close. They listen before crossing a bridge. They look upward when the high gardens go still.

And all around them, slowly enough to seem motionless, the city goes on living them.
