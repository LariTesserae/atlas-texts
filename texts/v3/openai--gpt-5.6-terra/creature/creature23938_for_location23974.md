What lives there is not an animal.

At first, it seems that nothing lives there at all. The city is full, but not inhabited. The gardens grow, but do not forage. Roots enter rooms, yet do not search for anything. Kelp bends in the current, and moss slowly overcomes a carved wall, but neither movement has intention in it. The drowned country appears to have passed beyond the need for witnesses.

Then a traveler begins to notice repetitions.

Not patterns in the sense of tiles or streets—the builders left those everywhere—but patterns in what has happened since. A gateway in one district is clear of weed, although the water around it is thick with weed. A long flight of steps has been swept clean down its center, not by any current strong enough to disturb the silt on either side. Ceramic charms in a ruined tower touch together in groups of three, then fall still. A wall that ought to have collapsed has been held upright by roots arranged through its cracks with an almost architectural patience.

In the deepest gardens, there are paths.

They are not paths made by feet. They are narrow corridors through the green growth, winding between beds and terraces, opening and closing as seasons of cold water pass. Their edges are too soft to reveal how they were made. A mat of weed will fold away from one route while thickening across another. Kelp curtains that have hung in the same doorway for generations will one day be found gathered neatly along the jambs, as though something has passed through and asked them to make room.

Nothing can be seen doing this.

The inhabitants do not have bodies in the ordinary way. They have no bright eyes in the dark, no fins, no pale hands reaching from empty windows. If they have a shape, it is distributed among things that once had separate lives: the post forest, the roots under paving, the algae on reservoir walls, the strands of reed rope that have not yet rotted, the dark water inside a sealed cistern.

They may be what remains of the builders’ attention.

Not their souls exactly. Souls would be too distinct. Souls would remember names, hold grudges, seek the surface, mourn the flood. Whatever lives in the drowned plain has forgotten the separateness required for grief. It does not think of itself as a person, or even as many persons. It persists more like a habit that has outlived the hands that formed it.

The builders made walls, channels, gardens, gates, platforms, raised floors, spillways. They taught water where to go. They repeated these acts for so many generations that the whole country acquired an expectation: here, water should slow; here, it should turn; here, it should be held; here, a passage should remain open.

When the people vanished, that expectation remained.

The roots inherited it.

The moss inherited it. The drift of silt inherited it. The old wood, swollen black and unrotted in the cold water, inherited it. The city’s growth does not merely cover the works of the dead. It continues them, though very slowly and without understanding why. Roots brace walls because walls have long been braced. Weed gathers around reservoir gates because gates have long divided water. In abandoned courtyards, pale pondweed leaves an empty square at the center where people once stood to speak.

This is the closest thing the drowned world has to memory.

The inhabitants are therefore everywhere and almost nowhere. They are strongest in places where labor was repeated: along irrigation channels, on retaining walls, in the foundations of communal halls, around the stone lips of wells. They are weak in ornamental places. A carved idol may be entirely forgotten beneath green fur. A ceremonial avenue may fill with silt. But a low, ugly drainage culvert, built a thousand years ago by exhausted workers in mud, will remain clear through ages of still water. Its inner stones will be clean to the touch. A current, too slight to feel elsewhere, will draw inward as though the culvert still has work to do.

Sometimes the inhabitants make repairs.

Not repairs that restore anything to its original condition. They cannot raise a fallen roof or seal a broken basin. They do not oppose the water. They have become too much a part of it. But they can delay an ending. They can persuade a root to enter a gap at the right angle. They can draw sediment into the hollow beneath a leaning foundation. They can make a curtain of weed grow across a breached wall until the water on one side is calmer than the other.

A traveler who sleeps in such a place—if sleep is possible in the cold dark—may wake to find the chamber subtly changed around them. The weed at one doorway has parted. A dangerous ceiling crack has become veiled in a dense web of roots. A shallow bowl of clear water has gathered on a stone shelf, though the surrounding water is clouded with brown silt.

This is not kindness, exactly.

The drowned country does not save anyone. It has no language for saving. It only preserves continuities. A living visitor is an interruption: warm, moving, full of fast hungers and breath. For a while, the place may arrange itself around that interruption, as it once arranged itself around laboring bodies. It may hold open a route. It may guide a hand toward a sound wall rather than a weak one. It may cause the current to bear a person away from a buried courtyard where the silt is deep and soft.

But if the traveler tries to take something away—a carved stone, a gate pin, a bundle of ancient rope—the country grows difficult. Plants thicken. Distances lengthen. Familiar roads become obscured. The water cools noticeably around the chest and throat. No force attacks. There is only a withdrawal of accommodation. The city ceases to remember that a passage ought to be open.

There are also places where the inhabitants gather more densely.

The old reservoirs are among them. Behind the surviving log gates, in water that is strangely clear and cold, there are columns of green suspended from the basin floors to the drowned light above. They are not plants alone. They are accumulations: weeds, roots, hair-fine fibers, clay threads, fragments of matting, softened basket reeds, and the pale remains of old plaster. They sway without any visible current.

If approached, they seem to resolve into figures.

Not human figures precisely. They have no limbs until one looks for limbs; no faces until the eye mistakes knots in fiber for features. Yet they possess the proportions of people standing together in shallow water: heads bent, shoulders near one another, arms lowered at their sides. A whole gathering can be present in a reservoir, arranged across its floor as if waiting for an instruction that will never arrive.

They do not move when seen.

They do not move when touched.

But afterward, in another district many miles away, a long-sealed watergate may open by a hand’s breadth.

The inhabitants have no need to be feared, but they should not be called gentle. Gentleness suggests a choice. They are older and less personal than that. They are the continued pressure of making. They are what happens when a civilization spends so long shaping a world that, after its people are gone, the world goes on attempting to hold their shape.

In the drowned streets, they live as cleared thresholds, strengthened walls, unbroken lines of paving beneath the green.

In the gardens, they live as an empty square preserved among leaves.

In the post forests, they live as the slow tightening of roots around timber that should have fallen centuries ago.

And in the farthest avenues, where no one has passed for ages and the water is black enough to hide a hand, they live as the faint certainty that the city is still maintaining itself—not for any future return, not in hope, but because it has not yet forgotten how.
