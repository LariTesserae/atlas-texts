The people who live in the basin are called residents only on forms.

Among themselves, when speech is possible, they use narrower words: bridgefolk, pump hands, upper-floor people, those of the sixth bell. Their identity is less a matter of family than of elevation, shift, and route. A person is known by which warning horn can reach their window, which pedestrian span they take home, what kind of animal passage lies beneath their building, and whether they work during the water-sharing interval.

They are not numerous. There may be a few hundred in the town proper, perhaps fewer. Counting them is difficult because addresses are not always occupied by people. Some houses are maintained for a single sleeping worker between shifts. Some contain only relay equipment, signal lamps, stores of dry cloth, emergency ladders, or old municipal furniture. Some are kept empty because the pipe-shelled herds pass through their ground floors twice a day, and it is simpler to let them.

The residents live mostly above the first level.

Their homes begin at stairs: narrow brick flights rising from the broad streets to doors with iron thresholds. The lower floors are sealed, grilled, or converted into workshops. Windows begin high. Balconies are practical things, wide enough for a handcart or a child in a rain cape, connected by bridges whose rails have been polished smooth by generations of hands.

Every home has hooks near the door for horn masks, waterproof capes, signal paddles, and climbing belts. Every kitchen has a kettle designed to be heard over turbines: not a whistle, but a little shuttered lamp that flashes when water boils. Beds are built into interior walls, where the vibrations are least severe. Cups have heavy bases. Shelves have lips. Nothing fragile is placed near a window unless it has already survived there for years.

They are pale or soot-darkened according to occupation, with rain-creased faces and a habitual way of listening through the soles of their feet. Children are taught to stand still on certain paving slabs and recognize the approach of the larger herds by the change in vibration. Adults can tell, from a tremor in a handrail, whether the south pumps are merely laboring or whether something has shifted below them.

Their spoken language is compressed by the noise. It has many gestures and very few wasted phrases.

A raised palm means wait.

Two fingers angled downward mean the street is feeding.

A closed fist tapped twice against the chest means something beneath has turned in its sleep.

There are gestures for apology, hunger, marriage, grief, and shift change, but also for more particular necessities: *do not close that gate yet; the white-necked ones have young among them.* *The black rollers are warm and will seek grates.* *Someone is trapped below street level.* *Do not run; running makes the membrane things descend.*

People here do not often run.

Their work is devoted to continuation. They tend pumps, boilers, rails, signal towers, vents, cisterns, machines that crush mineral, machines that separate it, machines that carry it away. They scrape salt from the insides of pipe joints. They oil bearings. They inspect meshes over reservoir channels. They replace shutters bitten through by small airborne animals. They sweep mineral dust from upper walks, though it always returns. They keep records in ledgers swollen with damp: reservoir heights, horn calls, breakages, births, deaths, migration delays, the duration of the underwater note.

The town’s oldest profession may be that of listener.

Listeners work in the pumping house or in small insulated rooms beneath the public clocks. They place their hands on brass rods driven into the basin floor. They read the deep knocks, the long low tremors, the changes in the engines’ reflected vibration. No one agrees on what exactly they listen to. The official explanation is pressure variation in old subterranean chambers. The unofficial explanations are numerous, but none are spoken loudly.

There are people who believe the basin rests upon the back of a living thing.

There are people who believe the living thing is not beneath the basin but is the basin: the retaining walls, the mineral seams, the wet stone, the network of pipes gradually becoming organs.

There are old residents who say it is neither of these. They say the town is inside something, and that the grey inland sea beyond the embankments is only what can be seen through its teeth.

Most residents have no interest in choosing.

Belief is a luxury below the second floor. The gate must rise at the right hour. The boilers must not overheat. A child must be retrieved from the west bridge before the barrel-creatures roll beneath it. The white-necked animals must be given their water, because if they are denied it they will stand against the pumping house walls and knock until mortar dust falls from the ceilings.

The fauna does not belong to anyone, though each kind has its custodians.

The pipe-shelled crawlers are watched by culvert hands. They are warm, slow, and not unintelligent in any way people can use. Their shells shed flakes that are collected, ground, and mixed into furnace linings. They remember pathways with absolute faithfulness. A culvert hand can redirect a herd only by changing the temperature of the paving ahead of it or by sounding a sequence of low strikes through a steel plate. Touching one directly is forbidden except in emergencies. Their undersides are covered in fine articulated limbs that can strip cloth, skin, and boot leather with patient, impersonal motion.

The rolling black creatures are managed by grate wardens. They seem featureless until one sees them close: their smooth bodies are interrupted by small breathing slits and a ring of wet, pale sensory pits. They seek warmth, vibration, and the exhalation of underground air. In winter they gather over steam vents in heaps so dense that entire streets disappear beneath them. The wardens place heated iron bars along alternate routes to keep them from smothering intake shafts. Some of the creatures bear old municipal markings burned into their skins, though no current worker admits to making them.

The tall white-necked animals are treated with the nearest thing the town has to reverence.

They are not birds, though their heads have the stillness and sudden violence of birds. Their bodies are bulky and pale, scaled in patches like wet plaster, with long folded forelimbs held tight against their sides. Their necks rise above roofline. Their beaks are not quite beaks: more like two opposing plates of white enamel, capable of producing the resonant knocks that travel through railings and pipes.

They are said to be older than the houses. Their young, when they appear, are small enough to pass beneath a bridge and soft-looking enough that children once tried to follow them. No child has done so for decades. The animals do not hunt people, as far as anyone can determine. But they do not yield to people either. When a white-necked gathering blocks a road, the road ceases to exist until they move.

The creatures in the sky have no custodians. No ladder reaches them. No net has held one for more than a few seconds. They enter chimneys, exhaust stacks, open windows, and occasionally the mouths of sleeping animals. Their membranes shine silver in rain. At night, their undersides catch factory light and make slow revolving constellations above the roofs.

Some residents insist they are young of the unseen things below.

Others insist they are fragments of weather.

The town’s children call them turners.

Children are perhaps the most adapted inhabitants of all. They play not in streets but on upper platforms, behind mesh, in the long corridors between second-storey apartments. Their games imitate municipal necessity. They build tiny bridges from scrap metal and demand tolls of one another in buttons. They make horn calls with cupped hands. They lie flat on wooden floors and guess which herd is passing below. They collect porcelain fragments from crawler shells, iridescent films from oil puddles, shed membrane scraps caught in gutter screens.

They draw no trees.

When given a blank wall, they draw pipes, bridges, towers, great pale necks, black round bodies at grates, the square geometry of reservoirs. Sometimes they draw a huge dark outline beneath all these things, not quite animal and not quite landscape. When asked what it is, they often shrug. It is simply what is under.

The oldest inhabitants are not necessarily old in years. They are the people who have remained through enough repetitions to see the town’s strange permanence become personal. They remember the bakery window before its crack. They remember when the east horn tower lost a note and the crawlers came early for three days. They remember a winter when the rain belts stopped moving and hung over one district until roofs collapsed beneath their weight. They remember, or claim to remember, a visitor descending from the western platform despite every warning.

No one agrees what happened to that visitor.

Some say the person joined the night shift and never left.

Some say the barrel-creatures covered them in the street and rolled on.

Some say there was no visitor at all; only a coat caught on the railing, flapping in the steam.

The residents are not prisoners exactly. Trains arrive and depart through their covered trenches. Mineral leaves. Tools arrive. Occasionally people leave with the freight crews, carrying small cases and wearing coats that seem too clean for the basin. A few return years later. Most do not.

Those who return are changed in subtle ways. They look at open soil with alarm. They find birds impossible: the casualness with which ordinary birds perch on a wire, lift into air, scatter over a field. They dislike the sight of grass moving in wind. They sleep poorly without machinery. They speak too loudly at first, then learn again to use their hands.

There are animals beneath the town that no one sees, and perhaps never has.

Their breathing determines the clocks.

Their movements, if they move, are so slow that a life can pass between one adjustment and the next. A foundation cracks, a pipe changes angle, a reservoir lowers by the width of a thumb, and the town accommodates the change as it accommodates rain or herd passage. The listeners mark it. The repair crews receive new instructions. Bolts are found loosened. Bricks are found waiting.

Whether these deep animals are alive in any familiar sense may not matter. The town behaves as though they are. The factories keep their rhythms. The water is portioned. The gates rise. The horn towers speak. Above, below, and among all of it, every resident—human, shelled, white-necked, rolling, membrane-winged, unseen—continues along an inherited route.

That is what lives there:

not a population sharing a town,

but an arrangement that has learned to wear the shapes of people, animals, machines, and weather.
