The people who live in the root do not call themselves rooted.

That is a name outsiders use, when there are outsiders to use it. Rootfolk, rooters, barkborn. The town dislikes all of them. They suggest a people who belong naturally to the place, and nobody born in the chamber believes they belong anywhere naturally.

They are simply residents.

Most are descended from laborers who came in through openings that no longer exist: survey crews, salvage gangs, families fleeing the black lowland, engineers sent to make the root useful and later forgotten by whatever office had sent them. Their old equipment remains everywhere in the town, though much of it has become too entwined with the root to move. The residents have inherited their ancestors’ tools and their suspicion of instructions written on paper.

They tend to be slight, though not fragile. The chamber teaches economy of movement. People grow up measuring every step, every bridge, every handhold. Their shoulders are often rounded from ducking beneath low growths and carrying loads through passages that may change shape halfway through a shift. Their skin is pale in the sense of things kept from the sun, but no two shades are quite alike: wax-white, green-brown, mushroom gray, the color of old copper. Many develop a fine dark freckling along the hands, throat, and cheekbones after years of working near the sap vents. It does not hurt. It is considered a sign that the root knows your outline.

The oldest residents have more of it.

Their eyes adjust to the green light. Visitors sometimes find their gaze unsettling, not because their eyes are strange in themselves, but because they seem to look past the visible surface of things. A resident can notice a draft in a sealed room, a pulse in a wall, the slight rearrangement of a familiar route. They learn early to tell the difference between a structural groan, an animal moving in the bark, and a room deciding to become another room.

They wear practical layers made from whatever can be woven, stripped, traded, or salvaged: oilcloth aprons, old uniform shirts, fiber capes, belts threaded with hooks and pruning blades. Most boots are modified several times over, with soles that can grip wet wood, iron grating, and the yielding street-felt. Children are given soft shoes before they are trusted with hard ones. Hard soles anger some of the younger surfaces.

Everyone carries a lamp, though few use it carelessly.

The lamps are hooded and usually green, so as not to confuse the root’s light. A good lamp is passed down through families, repaired until its original casing has vanished under patches. Some are powered by winding springs, some by salvaged cells, some by pale oil pressed from the bladder-fruit that grow near warm pipes. A lamp that flickers blue without being touched is not extinguished. It is set down, respectfully, and left where it wants to be until morning.

The town has trades, but no profession remains only itself for long.

A pump tender may also be a midwife, because births often happen when the water shifts. A turbine mechanic knows enough plant anatomy to recognize a blocked breathing channel. A child who collects snails may become a guide, then a rail inspector, then a singer on the vent crews. The town’s surgeons are not called surgeons. They are cutters, because their work includes treating people, clearing infected fungal mats, removing splinters of glasswood from livestock, and opening carefully marked seams in the root.

They do not cut flesh and wood by the same rules, though sometimes the distinction is difficult.

There are people known as listeners. Not elected, not trained in any formal way. They are the ones the root seems most willing to speak around. A listener may be someone who wakes at the right moment before a wall shifts, or someone whose hand can rest against the bark without provoking a shiver. They are consulted before major repairs and before weddings, departures, and the sealing of dangerous corridors. Most listeners are quiet people. They do not claim to understand the root. Anyone who claims that is either lying, mad, or preparing to leave.

The root keeps some of them.

This is not said with particular horror. It is said the way a fishing town might speak of the sea.

When a resident disappears, there is first a search: paired crews, cord lines, bells at the intersections, names sung into vents. If no trace is found after three cycles of dimming, the person’s room is left intact for a season. Their tools are hung where they can be seen. Their food ration is placed on the sill. The hope is not exactly that they will return. It is that whatever has taken their path will learn the household’s habits and leave the others alone.

Sometimes the missing do return.

They are welcomed, fed, and asked no questions until they ask one first. A returned person may be gone for an hour or twelve years; time has poor manners in the root. Some come back with a new skill, a changed voice, or a memory of rooms no map includes. A few return with roots of white hair growing under their fingernails, or with their old scars replaced by smooth green seams. These people are watched gently. If they begin to sleep standing against a wall, their family calls a listener.

The children are the most at home, and therefore the most carefully watched.

They play games on the grating bridges, racing beetles with lamps tied to their backs. They collect shed skins from the silent birds and make translucent kites that will not fly unless released in a vent. They dare one another to touch the warm spots in the streets. They know passages adults have forgotten, and they can hear the three bells more often than anyone else.

Children sometimes have imaginary friends.

The town does not dismiss them.

Adults ask the necessary questions: Does the friend have feet? Does it know your full name? Does it ask you to go somewhere alone? Does it smell like flowers? The wrong answers result in immediate, calm intervention. The child is taken to stay with relatives in another district. Their toys are smoked with bitter resin. Their bedroom wall may be shaved back to the silver sap line.

Nobody calls this cruel. The root has many ways of being fond.

There are few old people by outside measures, but there are elders. Age is counted less by years than by pulses survived: the red pulse, when the entire chamber warmed and the streets sweated sap; the hollow pulse, when half the eastern district became a shaft; the sleeping pulse, when the root went dark for nine days and the town lived by turbine glow and faith.

Elders sit near the market crane beneath the flowering column. They mend harnesses, sort seed packets, and tell stories that begin as practical advice and become something stranger before the end. They remember the last person who tried to burn a path out. They remember a time when the pale foxes had no leaves. They remember the water tower being full—not with water, but with voices.

Some residents are not human, though nobody agrees where the boundary should be drawn.

The pale foxes are tolerated as scavengers, thieves, and occasional messengers. They have learned to sit outside a sickroom door and stare at the wall until someone checks behind it. The snails are used for navigation, compost, and carrying small packages between districts, though they cannot be relied upon in flowering season. The silver mist-fish are considered good luck when they gather near a work crew, except around the cistern, where they mean the water has begun remembering something.

There are also things that were once machines.

The oldest ore carts sometimes travel the rails without being pushed. They move slowly, with the solemnity of animals that have become accustomed to harness. Their wheels have been replaced by layered rings of woody tissue, and their empty beds hold damp nests of fiber. Children feed them scraps of bark. Mechanics do not interfere unless one stops at a door and refuses to leave.

The generator house is kept by three siblings called the Turners, regardless of their actual number. They sleep in shifts beside the turbines and say they can tell from the vibration of the belts whether the root is dreaming. Their hands are permanently stained green-black from grease and sap. When the pulse is strong, they coax power from it. When it weakens, they sing the engines through their slow turns.

Their songs are not beautiful in the ordinary sense. They are timed to wrench strokes, valve releases, and the heavy rhythm beneath the floor. The root accepts them because they contain work.

There are singers elsewhere too: rail singers, pump singers, cutters’ choirs. A person may sing alone while tightening bolts or scraping moss from a support beam, but certain songs require at least two voices. One voice asks the root to hold still. The other promises that the work will be useful. Neither promise is assumed to be true.

The residents make little art that can be hung on walls. Walls are unreliable galleries. Instead they carve tiny objects from shed amber: animals, tools, houses remembered from old family stories, impossible maps of the chamber as it ought to be. They press patterns into bark plates before burning them for fuel. They braid colored wire through growing vines, making living knots that change as the vine thickens. At festivals, they hang lanterns inside the abandoned water tower and tell stories upward, into its dark throat.

Their dead are not buried.

There is nowhere appropriate to dig, and fire is too dangerous. Bodies are wrapped in old work cloth, weighted with tools chosen by the family, and laid in a particular chamber beyond the switching yard where the root has made a bed of pale, dry fibers. By morning the bodies are usually covered in a white flowering growth. Within a week, the flowers close.

The families return afterward to collect whatever the root leaves behind.

Usually this is a button, a tooth, a piece of jewelry, or an object the dead person lost years ago. Sometimes it is a new shoot growing from the floor in the shape of a hand.

The town’s greatest fear is not death, though it fears that well enough.

It fears uselessness.

The residents believe the root allows them to remain because they perform functions it cannot perform alone, or cannot perform quickly enough: clearing decay, directing heat, extracting pressure, carrying materials through its hollow places, noticing danger before it becomes infection. They do not imagine themselves masters of the root. They are maintenance organisms, favored parasites, tools with memories.

This belief gives their lives a peculiar dignity.

Every repaired railing, every cleared vent, every bucket of bad water brought safely to the cistern is an argument for continued existence. Every child taught not to answer a wall is an investment in the town’s future usefulness. Every song sung in time with a machine says: we are still here; we are still doing something the great body needs.

And sometimes, in the quiet after the third bell, the root answers.

Not in words. Never words.

A distant chamber opens where no chamber had been. A blocked route becomes passable. A warm current reaches a freezing district. A lost person finds their way home by following a line of blue light under the bark.

Then the residents lower their lamps, speak softly, and go back to work.
