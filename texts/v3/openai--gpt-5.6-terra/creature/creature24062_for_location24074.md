What lives in the hollow is not a single species or a settled order of beasts, but a congregation made necessary by altitude.

The place draws creatures that have learned the same lesson: warmth is not found; it is borrowed. From stone that has held a little of the day. From another body. From breath caught under a wing. From wet fur pressed against wet fur until the cold outside cannot find the skin beneath.

The horned animals are its most visible citizens, though even they are difficult to see until they move. They are small mountain browsers, scarcely larger than goats, with barrel ribs and shoulders built high enough to push through crowded bodies. Their coats are not truly black; in daylight they would show brown, iron-red, and charcoal gray, each animal dyed by the minerals of the ledges it favors. Condensation darkens them until they merge with the stone.

Their horns are close-curled, almost defensive rather than grand. They do not use them for contests in the ordinary way. A horn is a scraper, lever, wedge, and digging tool. With it they peel loose the wet crusts of lichen, pry at root mats, and work patiently at white or green mineral seams. The older animals know which walls sweat salt after rain and which fissures hold enough sour water to drink. Calves follow them with their mouths open, catching droplets shaken from an adult's beard.

They sleep in compressed circles. The young disappear into the center. The old and scarred animals take the outer ring, their bodies turned toward the wind. No single animal commands the herd, but there are memories distributed among them: this ledge holds through frost; that pool poisons after a long dry spell; this crack is safe until the stones begin to click overhead. When a mountain-call comes from beyond the rim, every head rises at once. Even newborns learn stillness before they learn to walk.

The white-faced birds are perhaps less birdlike than they first appear. In their folded resting posture they are heaps of gray and black plumage, with only the pale oval of a face visible in the dark. They have long legs for stepping through puddles and loose rubble, and broad, strong wings that serve as weatherproof cloaks. Flight is possible but costly in the thin air; they save it for escape, courtship, and the brief journeys between neighboring hollows.

Their faces are white because they are bare. Moisture beads there and runs into fine grooves toward their beaks. In rain, they stand with their heads lifted, collecting water directly. They feed on the silver crack-insects, on moth larvae, on tiny blind things brought up in the seep-water, and on the soft tissues of animals dead from cold or falling stone. Their chicks are born into feather shelters shared by several adults. A nesting group will make a low ring of bodies around them, facing outward, wings overlapping, while the chicks sleep in the heated dark beneath.

The bright pebbles around their nests are not ornaments in any simple sense. The birds choose stones that hold a little shine: quartz fragments, mica flakes, pale mineral nodules. They place them where a returning adult can see them even under starlight. But they also use them as warnings. A scattered ring means the nest has been abandoned. A double ring means chicks are inside. A line of bright stones leading away from a pool may mean that something drowned there, or that a predator has been seen using that path. No one creature taught them this. The pattern survives because the birds who read it live.

The insects are the hidden foundation of the hollow. When water wakes the cracks, they emerge: small silver-bodied crawlers with translucent skins and too many fine legs, carrying their young as pale grains beneath their abdomens. They live most of their lives inside the mountain, feeding on fungal threads, mineral slime, and the thin films of life that form where water meets fresh stone. They are slow in open air and almost helpless on smooth surfaces. The birds eat them. The mice eat them. The moth larvae eat them. Even the horned animals swallow them inadvertently with wet lichen and receive something precious from their soft bodies: fat, salt, and protein.

The moths belong to darkness more completely than any other creature. Their wings are broad, soft, and marked with shadowed circles that resemble shut eyes. They do not seek flowers, because there are few flowers to seek. They drink seep-water, feed on fungus, and rasp at the mineral-rich skins that grow on wet walls. Their caterpillars are fat, pale things hidden in moss and rock seams. The adult moths are food, but their value is greater than their flesh. They carry spores from one wet crack to another. They carry pollen from the rare low plants that bloom only after a sequence of rains. Their drifting makes the hollow possible in ways none of its larger residents can understand.

The rock-mice are more than mice. They are the keepers of insulation.

They have thick, oil-dark fur and flattened feet with gripping pads between their toes. Their teeth never stop growing, which makes them tireless workers of roots, fungus, feather shafts, and soft stone. They pack their nests with everything that traps heat: shed wool, bird down, moth wings, dried moss, bits of old hide. At night their colonies shift like one large animal inside the ledges. A nest that appears to be a patch of fur may contain forty bodies, each one asleep with its nose tucked against the belly of another.

They steal whenever they can, but theft in the hollow is not entirely condemned. A tuft of hair taken from a horned beast may insulate a mouse litter. A feather removed from an unattended bird nest may become a wall against frost. The larger animals chase them lazily, as much from custom as anger. The mice return when they can. Their droppings nourish the sparse roots. Their burrows deepen the cracks. Their stored food feeds other things when a rockfall opens their chambers.

Then there are the climbers: small predators with long wrists, needle claws, and faces too narrow for their teeth. They move vertically as if gravity were merely another texture in the rock. Their coats are mottled with wet gray, rust, and pale lichen-color. In the center of the hollow they are cautious. There, every movement is observed; the herd's horns and the birds' stabbing beaks make open hunting dangerous. Along the outer walls, however, they work the dark routes: mouse tunnels, bat crevices, nest ledges, the places where solitary animals are forced by cold or hunger to separate from the crowd.

They are not cruel animals. They are precise. A climber takes what it can carry, eats every usable part, and leaves the rest to moths, beetles, fungus, and the crack-water. Their young are raised in narrow horizontal fissures lined with stolen fur. Their mothers teach them the geography of weakness: where water freezes first, which ledges crumble under a horned animal's weight, how to wait without shivering.

The bats are the hollow's pulse after midnight. So many hang beneath the shelves that their sleeping masses seem like dark mineral growths. When they wake, the air begins to stitch and flicker. They hunt the small flying things that gather around wet stone and fungal light. Some take moths. Some sip from fruiting fungal shelves. A few, with broad noses and slow wingbeats, feed from the wounds of larger animals, though so delicately that the sleepers often do not wake. They leave behind small dark stains on the stone and a smell of warm dust beneath the cold mineral air.

And beyond the rim is the thing that makes all the others fall silent.

It is not often seen. It may not enter the hollow because the hollow is too small for it, too crowded, too steep-sided for a body of its scale. But its call travels through the stone: a metallic, resonant bellow like a mountain seam being torn apart. The sound belongs to something vast and slow-moving, perhaps a horned grazer larger than any herd-beast below, perhaps a predator whose plated throat turns its breath into thunder, perhaps an animal so old that it has become part of the mountain's own weather.

The hollow's creatures know it without needing to see it. The horned beasts pack tighter. The birds lower their faces beneath their wings. Bats stop their chittering. Even the climbers flatten themselves into cracks. It may be that the great caller feeds on creatures driven out onto the exposed ridges. It may be that it is harmless, and merely enormous. In such a place, harmlessness is not the same as safety.

What truly lives there, finally, is a habit of nearness.

No animal can afford much territory. None has enough food to waste, enough warmth to hoard, enough certainty to live alone. The hollow is full because its creatures have arranged themselves around its few mercies: the sweating stone, the shallow pools, the dry overhangs, the moss seams, the bodies of one another.

They do not make a city. They make something older and less deliberate: a living insulation around a wound in the young mountain. Breath warms breath. Waste feeds root. Root feeds browser. Browser feeds predator. Death feeds everything. Water runs through all of it, bead by bead, from the black walls into mouths, fur, fungus, blood, and stone again.

The hollow is crowded not because it is generous, but because it is the one small place near the roof of the world where life has discovered how not to let go.
