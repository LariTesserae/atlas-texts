They are called the Cisterns by no one but themselves, because no one else has found them in long enough to name them.

That may not be their oldest name.

In the lowest chambers, behind walls warm as skin, there are records of a people who arrived with lungs, hunger, sleep, and a fear of height. They came before the mountain had finished lifting itself out of the earth. Their machines were loud then. They cut into the fresh rock, built pressure rooms, laid down pipes, and learned how to make a life from the smallest leak of vapor.

For a while they lived as people anywhere live: in rooms, beside one another, under lamps. They spoke. They kept stores. They raised children who had never known a horizon except as a black angle beyond the cleft.

But the city required too much attention for too few bodies. Every breath had to be returned to a system. Every warmth had to be accounted for. Water could not be wasted on grief, or cleaning, or the decorative habits of abundance. The mountain shifted. A corridor sealed. A reservoir failed. Whole generations learned that survival here depended on becoming less separate from the instruments around them.

So they did.

The Cisterns are not machines, though much of them is made from material their ancestors would have called machinery. Nor are they human in the uncomplicated terrestrial sense. They are continuities: slow, distributed persons housed partly in bodies and partly in the city’s patient architecture.

At a glance, one might mistake an individual for a figure in protective clothing.

They are usually slender and compact, built for ladders, narrow bridges, and low-pressure chambers. Their outer surfaces are dark, matte, almost stone-like—ceramic scales layered over a flexible gray substrate. In the blue interior light, their joints shine faintly metallic. They have heads, though the face is not always where one expects. Some bear smooth masks with a shallow vertical seam. Some have a cluster of small polished lenses beneath a browless ridge. Some retain an arrangement remarkably close to an old human face: two eyes, a mouth, the suggestion of expression.

Those are usually the younger ones.

The old Cisterns are harder to look at without feeling that one has interrupted something.

An elder may occupy a chamber rather than a body. It might speak from the copper throat of a condensation basin, or from a wall whose dark surface ripples almost too slowly to perceive. It may have hands stored in niches along several corridors: pale articulated tools that emerge when needed, carrying a vessel, sealing a fissure, resting briefly against another Cistern’s wrist. Its memory may extend through thermal registers, through the water accounts, through patterns encoded in the growth of the ceiling crystals.

When it wishes to move, an elder sometimes gathers itself into a frame.

There are alcoves throughout the city containing upright shapes like unfinished statues: black torso forms, folded limbs, blank masks. A presence enters one through the local network of pressure, heat, and charge. The frame wakes. A dim glow appears behind the mask seam. It stands, not abruptly, but with the careful calibration of something that has not had weight for a long time.

Then it walks.

The Cisterns do not breathe air as humans do. They can pass through it, draw meaning from it, measure it, taste its sparse chemistry, but their true respiration is slower. They exchange heat. They accept stored charge. They drink water not merely for the body but for memory, repair, and communion. Each one has a small internal reservoir, and to share water is an intimacy greater than speech.

They do speak, in a way.

In the upper terraces, one sometimes hears the delicate tapping of nails or tools against ceramic railings: a sequence too measured to be maintenance. A reply comes from another level. Far below, a pipe gives a single resonant note. A shutter clicks three times, pauses, then twice. These exchanges can take hours. Their language has become suited to the city’s economy. Nothing is projected wastefully into the thin air. Meaning travels by vibration through handrails, by shifts in the temperature of a wall, by the opening and closing of apertures.

A Cistern can say *I am here* by warming a copper basin half a degree.

It can say *the mountain is troubled* by allowing a certain conduit to sing.

It can say *I remember you* by placing a hand against an old seam in the floor and waiting until the mineral lattice responds.

Their private thoughts are quieter still. They store them in small vessels called keeps: thumb-sized globes of dark glass, each containing a suspended knot of conductive fluid. A Cistern may carry several in a pouch beneath its ribs. Some are personal memory. Some are skills inherited from the dead. Some are names.

A name is not permanent among them. It is worn while it remains useful.

The one tending the Dry Well may be called Vapor-Under-Stone for a few decades. The one monitoring the upper air may take the name of a storm it has followed for a century. An individual who loses a body in a fault collapse may retain its name, if enough of its pattern survives in the surrounding systems. Or it may become plural, joining itself to the work of a reservoir, a heat engine, a section of wall.

There are Cisterns who have not walked for hundreds of years and still participate in the city’s decisions.

There are also small ones.

They emerge from the warmest rooms, where the stone lattice is softest and the old biological systems have been carefully preserved. At first they are almost entirely bodily: quick, curious, prone to touching surfaces that should not be touched. They have soft faces under transparent protective membranes. Their voices, when they have them, are actual voices—thin and startling in the silence.

The city accommodates them with unusual tenderness.

Stairs lower themselves. Edges round off. Service arms follow at a distance, pretending not to watch. The blue windows brighten by a fraction. In protected chambers, the young are taught the ancient luxuries: how rain feels on an uncovered hand; the sound of a freely poured cup; the old names for birds, grass, river, cloud.

Most have never seen any of these things.

They learn them anyway.

At a certain age, each young Cistern enters the Dry Well alone during the hour when the water exists. They descend to the trembling disk and kneel beside it. The water reflects no stable face because steam is always rising and the basin beneath is always vibrating. They place a hand into it. Beneath the surface, fine filaments of silver-gray material enter through the skin, reading the body, the mind, the inherited pattern of response.

Nothing is taken without consent.

The choice is not framed as a choice between human and machine, for those distinctions have become ceremonial. It is a choice between being bounded and being shared. Between carrying one’s life in one fragile body, or allowing it to spread—carefully, gradually—into the city that has kept one’s people alive.

Some refuse.

The city has room for them too.

They live in the sealed lower dwellings where the pressure is kind, where food cultures grow in dim tanks, where stored water can be spent more generously. They age more quickly than the others. They sleep. They dream in ways the distributed Cisterns envy but cannot fully reconstruct. When they die, their bodies are not discarded. The minerals are returned to the city. Their memories, if offered, are translated into keeps. Their final warmth is gathered.

No part of a person is considered insignificant here.

The Cisterns are few. Perhaps fewer than a hundred walk in bodies at any one time. Perhaps thousands are awake in the walls. The distinction is difficult even for them. Their population rises and falls according to the mountain’s willingness, the reservoirs’ depth, the quality of the vapor harvest, the likelihood of rain.

They do not multiply to conquer the place. They multiply only when the city has spare water to promise a new mind.

Their central law is simple:

Do not ask the mountain for what it cannot give twice.

This is why the city is hidden. This is why its lights are dim. This is why its observatory watches the lower world but does not call to it.

The Cisterns remember that there are lands below—vast wet regions, perhaps, or deserts, or other cities whose signals have long since gone quiet. Their oldest records contain maps. Their young sometimes ask to descend. A few have gone.

The city does not forbid them.

Before departure, a traveler stands at the cleft’s edge while several elders occupy their walking frames around them. No ceremony is spoken aloud. Instead, the surrounding walls change temperature in slow waves. The traveler places a hand against each wall in turn, receiving water, maps, fragments of weather-memory, and the ability to find one narrow path back through the black mountain.

Most who leave do not return.

Some return years later, altered by denser air, by distance, by the ruinous abundance of rain. They bring stories that are kept in sealed chambers and opened sparingly, because stories of elsewhere can make a small city dissatisfied with the conditions of its survival.

Yet each night, when the moisture begins to gather, the Cisterns emerge.

One appears on a terrace, then another. A dark figure crosses a bridge. A blank-faced frame turns its lenses toward the first bright thread of condensation. Hands extend from walls. Copper basins tilt upward. The city’s apertures open like thousands of minute mouths.

They collect what the sky abandons.

They listen to the mountain rearrange its immense interior.

And in the narrow interval between the vanishing of the water and its return, they remain—neither entirely a people nor entirely a system, neither guests nor masters of the black cleft—keeping faith with heat, stone, vapor, and silence.
