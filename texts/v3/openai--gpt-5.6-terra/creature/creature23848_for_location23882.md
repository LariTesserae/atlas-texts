What lives there is not a collection of animals so much as a pressure system made of flesh.

At first one notices the plated herds because they are large enough to alter the visible land. They travel low and densely, each animal no higher than a crouching person but joined to the next by touch: flank to flank, head beneath tail-base, armored mouth working at the mineral sheen on the stone. Their backs are pale—not white, but the color of the undersides of mushrooms or old teeth—and segmented into overlapping shields. When a herd turns, those shields lift and settle in sequence. From a distance, the turn looks like moonlight moving over water.

They are called grazers only because they feed continuously. They do not eat plants. Their mouths are broad discs of hard, rasping tissue, ringed with small gripping palps. They scour the black rock for microbial varnish, salt bloom, fungal threads, and the gelatinous colonies that grow in damp seams. In dry months they wear grooves into limestone. In wet months they gather around seepage walls and feed with their faces pressed flat against the stone, shivering in communal rows.

No single one seems fully self-sufficient. Between their plates live the small white cleaners: six-legged, flat-bodied things that look like torn scraps of pale leather until they unfold and run. They comb the seams of the herd for mites, eggs, sloughed skin, and droplets of condensed water. Other creatures live in the nostril folds and throat chambers of the great grazers, emerging at feeding time to collect loosened mineral paste. When a herd rests, it becomes a hill full of tenants. Its sleeping noise is a damp, patient ticking.

The young are not born into open ground. They are laid in the warm undersides of the herd itself.

Adults carry clusters of soft eggs in protected folds between the lower armor. The hatchlings come out blind and nearly transparent, no larger than fingers, and remain beneath the moving mass for their first season. There they feed on shed scales and the rich, waxy material secreted around the adults’ joints. Many never leave. They grow into the smaller, darker forms that maintain the herd’s underside, clearing rot, carrying away parasites, and eating whatever is crushed beneath the passing bodies.

Where the herds gather, there are always drummers.

They are squat, thick animals with helmeted heads and forelimbs ending in dense pads of cartilage. Their eyes are vestigial pits under translucent skin. They navigate by impact. A drummer places its belly to the earth, listens through its ribs, then strikes the stone with its head or breastbone. The sound travels much farther through the low country than it does through air. Other drummers answer from over the ridge, from under a pool, from within a crushed tunnel. Their messages are not subtle: water, danger, mating ground, herd-route, collapse, carrion. But the same signal can mean several things depending on its rhythm, and more than one animal has followed a food-call into a breeding crush or a warning into the jaws of a hunter.

The loudest of them are the basin bulls.

These are enormous, blind, low-slung creatures with bellies dragged smooth by stone and nasal chambers that branch through the skull like horns. In season, they fill those chambers with air and bellow downward, forcing the sound into the ground. Their calls make water tremble in distant pools. Shell drifts loosen. Smaller animals emerge from burrows in confusion. They do not fight with horns or teeth. They fight by driving their bodies together, forehead to forehead, each impact sending a concussion through the limestone. Sometimes neither yields. Then both continue until the ground beneath them fractures, and the basin takes one or both.

Their carcasses become places.

A dead basin bull may feed a ravine for years. The skin opens first, attended by the soft-bodied eaters: ribbon-backed siphoners, pale sac-worms, blunt snails with rasping belts of teeth. Then come the bone-pickers, compact many-limbed things whose mouthparts can scrape marrow from a rib interior. Later, when only the frame remains, the skeleton is occupied. Eggs are glued to vertebrae. Moisture collects in the skull chambers. Small grazers shelter beneath the pelvis. The skull itself may become an echoing nest for whistle-throated colonies, whose calls are amplified by the dead animal’s sinuses until they sound much larger than they are.

There are creatures that resemble shells, though they are not shelled in the familiar sense.

They build their coverings from whatever the country gives them: mineral crust, old plates, fragments of bone, compacted excrement, shell powder glued together with mucus. A young one begins as a soft, vulnerable knot. As it moves, it collects debris. Over years it becomes a walking mound armored in the history of everything it has passed through. Some carry smooth dark domes polished by traffic. Others bear jagged towers of white shell that clatter against one another when they gather.

These are the rim-holders, found around the edges of black pools.

They do not drink in the ordinary way. They filter water through long curtains of flesh hidden beneath their armored skirts, extracting microbes, dissolved minerals, drifting eggs, fragments of mucus, and the blood-clouds released by feeding animals. They spread around a pool in overlapping rings, each individual anchored partly to stone and partly to its neighbors. In a drought their bodies form a living dam. Nothing reaches the water without crossing them.

The pools belong to them until something larger decides otherwise.

The hunters are difficult to see even when they are close. Their upper surfaces are dark, rough, and mineral-streaked; their pale underbodies show only when they rear, roll, or strike. They have no need for speed. Most are broad as carts, long as small boats, and flattened enough to hide in shallow depressions that would not conceal a human ankle. They lie motionless amid shell drifts or against wet shale, their sensory crowns pressed to the stone.

Those crowns are not heads exactly. They are blunt, fleshy domes packed with pressure organs. Through them the hunters feel the tremor of feet, the flutter of a buried animal’s breathing, the shifting weight of a herd deciding whether to turn. Their mouths open beneath the crown rather than in front of it. When they attack, the whole forward half of the body lifts and falls. Hooked limbs seize. The mouth folds outward. Inward-angled teeth close around whatever has been caught and prevent it from withdrawing.

They are often injured. In this country, a hunter cannot take a meal without being climbed upon, bitten, rasped, parasitized, or trampled by something else. Their scars become habitats. Small cleaners live in their wounds. Mineral-feeding mats establish themselves in the cracks of old armor. One may see a great hunter moving along a ridge with an entire pale colony growing in the groove where its back was once split open.

Some hunters have learned the herds’ signals.

They drum a false route-call from beneath a limestone shelf. A grazing flow shifts toward the sound, believing it leads to damp stone or communal safety. The first ranks enter the narrow ground. The hunter strikes from above. The herd compresses so tightly that many animals suffocate before teeth ever touch them. The survivors feed on the dead as they escape. Nothing in the low country wastes panic.

Below the flats live the loosers.

They are burrowers, but not makers of burrows. Their bodies are too heavy and the earth too unstable for that. Instead they move through weaknesses: old passages, softened mudstone, the spaces beneath shell banks, the cavities left by roots that belonged to an older world and have long since rotted away. They have many limbs, though no observer agrees how many, because the limbs are continually folding under and over one another. Their skin is clear enough that dark organs can be seen pulsing inside.

When rain comes, loosers rise.

They push up through wet ground in groups, opening mouths that are mostly circular muscle and fine grinding teeth. They consume the mineral-rich crust exposed by water. They eat drowned eggs, buried carrion, softened bone, and the dense black paste left in long-used migratory lanes. In doing so they undermine the lanes. A herd crossing a familiar route may suddenly sink knee-deep into a body of living burrowers, each one trying to move toward the warmth and pressure above.

The smallest inhabitants are not always the least important.

There are bead colonies embedded in ravine walls: translucent spheres, each no larger than a seed, joined by filaments under the stone’s surface. They brighten when touched by moisture, vibration, or the chemical traces of blood. A passing animal causes them to gleam in waves. In the dark they reveal trails long after the traveler has gone. Predators watch these lights. So do scavengers. So do mating animals looking for paths recently used by their own kind.

There are dust mites that feed on the powder produced by shell collisions. There are hair-fine worms that live in water films and enter open wounds to lay eggs. There are blind, hard-shelled tick-creatures that cling to the feet of herd animals and detach only when they sense a warm carcass nearby. There are floating mats of microbial jelly that gather in still pools, absorbing minerals by dayless chemistry and becoming food for everything from hatchlings to basin bulls.

And there are the breathers in the walls.

They are rarely seen whole. A ravine face may appear to be wet limestone, faintly pocked and shining. Then the stone exhales. A hundred narrow apertures open. Soft grey tongues emerge, gather moisture from the air, and withdraw. The breathers are colonial animals that inhabit cracks in the rock. Their bodies spread through the limestone in branching tubes, dissolving it slowly and depositing darker minerals behind them. Over centuries they make the ravines. Their feeding creates the seepage channels. Their waste forms much of the black crust that the grazers rasp away.

The country is partly alive because they have hollowed it into being.

At rare intervals, when cloud light is strong enough to flatten the darkness, one can see the oldest things moving along the far ridges.

They are called old only because nothing appears to eat them. Their bodies are enormous and low, composed of segments so weathered that each resembles a slab of dark stone. White mineral fans grow from their sides. They move with painful slowness, advancing only when the ground beneath them has softened with rain. Smaller creatures swarm over them in such numbers that their outlines are never clean. Herds divert around them. Hunters keep their distance. Even the basin bulls lower their calls when one passes.

No one knows what they feed upon. Perhaps they consume the rock itself. Perhaps they are moving from one ancient water source to another. Perhaps the movement is not purposeful but merely the last continuation of something begun before the basins were worn down.

Where one has passed, the stone is polished to a dark mirror. Water gathers in its track. Bead colonies shine along the edges. Within days, the path fills with animals.

That is how the land remains alive: nothing arrives at an empty place. Every track becomes a corridor, every body becomes a wall, every wound becomes a pool, every call becomes an answer. Even silence is inhabited.
