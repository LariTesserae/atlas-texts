What lives in the basin is adapted less to hardship than to repetition.

The people are the most visible form of life, though they are not numerous. They do not gather in crowds unless a conduit fails or a death requires witnessing. Most of the time they exist in pairs, in work crews of three or four, in families occupying two warmed rooms above a service corridor. The city’s population is known with precision by the Registry, but no one else asks for the number. To ask is considered faintly rude, like asking how much heat another household receives.

They are called basin people by the few records that mention an outside, though none of them call themselves that. They call themselves by district, shift, trade, or conduit.

“I’m on Lower Seven.”
“My mother was Furnace Access.”
“He came up through East Return.”
“She works pressure.”

A personal name is used mostly indoors. Names are soft things, for kitchens, sickbeds, and the intervals before sleep. Outside, among the concrete walls and lamp poles, people use functions. It is useful to know who can mend a seal, who is authorized to enter a hot shaft, who has the key to a particular junction cabinet. The city has lasted by making usefulness a form of kinship.

Their bodies tend toward the same general shape over generations: narrow in the chest, compact in the limbs, pale or gray-brown from a life spent under sodium lamps and mineral dust. Their eyes are large enough that outsiders in old photographs might have found them striking, but within the basin no one remarks on it. The eyes have simply learned the economy of dim light. A person can recognize another at a great distance by the reflected pinprick of their gaze, before they can see their face.

Children are taught darkness before they are taught writing.

They learn how to stand still at the edge of a lamp’s reach and wait for their sight to arrange the world. They learn that darkness has textures: the open dark of the basin, the close dark beneath a stairwell, the granular dark beyond moving machinery, the oily dark of an unlit shaft. They learn to count paces between poles. They learn never to follow a sound unless it repeats itself in a way that proves it comes from a machine.

For a long time, every child believes that lamps grow naturally from roads.

The adults do not always correct them.

Their clothing is thick but not particularly warm. The city supplies warmth; clothing protects against dust, sharp edges, old grease, radiation from certain conduits, and the dry abrasion of the basin wind—which is not wind exactly, but a rare movement of pressure across the flats. Coats are repaired until their original fabric disappears beneath patches. Gloves are inherited. Boots are rebuilt from soles, belts, sealant, and layered scraps of synthetic hide. The oldest workers wear garments that contain material from machines no longer found anywhere in the city.

There are ceremonies of repair.

Not formal ones, at first glance. A broken handrail receives three people, a lamp housing receives two, a cracked pipe jacket receives whoever is nearest with the proper compound. Yet the work is done with a concentration that approaches reverence. Nobody says the city is sacred. The word is associated with old texts, failed cults, things that demand belief without providing heat. But everyone understands that a repaired object is a thing brought back from the edge of neglect.

Neglect is the nearest thing the basin has to death.

The people die, certainly. They die in warm rooms, in pressure incidents, from cancers caused by older industries, from slow mineral diseases that stiffen the lungs and joints. Sometimes they die far from habitation, found seated beside a road barrier or curled against the sheltered side of a substation. The city’s silence makes death difficult to conceal. When a person stops arriving, their absence acquires weight quickly. Someone notices the uncollected meal allotment. Someone hears that a tool has remained checked out. Someone sees a lamp burning behind a door through too many shift changes.

The dead are not buried in the ground. The crust is too hard, too warm, and too full of pipes whose routes are only partly known.

They are lowered instead into the old cooling wells.

These are vertical shafts lined with dark tiles, descending beyond the reach of ordinary lamps. They were once part of the city’s heat-exchange system, or perhaps were built for something before the city’s present purpose. Each has a steel collar, a hoist, a set of worn safety markings, and a ledger kept in a cabinet nearby. The body is wrapped in ceramic cloth. Family members place small objects within the folds: a tool, a cup, a strip of colored insulation, a handwritten schedule, a piece of polished slag.

Then the hoist lowers the bundle into the warm dark.

No one knows where the wells end. The ropes go down farther than any resident has ever descended. Some say the dead are given back to the engine under the basin. Others say the wells are merely storage, and that all the bodies remain stacked in dry, intact chambers deep underground, waiting for someone who never comes.

There are songs, but they are not sung loudly.

The music of the basin is built for enclosed rooms. It is tapping on pipework, breath through narrow ceramic whistles, low strings made from tension wire, voices held close to the throat. Most songs have no verses. They repeat a few lines until the repetition begins to alter the listener. Songs are used for calibration, mourning, sleep, childbirth, and long maintenance tasks. A worker tightening bolts in an access tunnel may hum a line that tells them when to stop turning. A mother may sing a sequence of valve names to a child until the child sleeps.

The people do not dream much of sunlight.

Some do, particularly the young, after reading old manuals with diagrams of blue sky printed in degraded inks. But sunlight is too abstract to carry emotional force. It is treated like an extinct industrial process: interesting, wasteful, poorly suited to the basin. In the oldest residential blocks, there are murals based on inherited descriptions—yellow fields, trees, clouds. The trees are generally rendered as tall green conduits with many branching outlets. Clouds look like white ash settling upward.

What the people dream about instead is failure.

A lamp going out one pole ahead of them, then the next. A familiar hallway lengthening. A pressure gauge rising without sound. A train arriving from the northern tunnel with every carriage lit and no one inside. Pipes speaking in a voice just below comprehension. The commonest dream is of a door opening onto a room that should not fit inside the building.

The city itself may be alive in the way a reef is alive: not through one mind, perhaps, but through accumulated response.

It notices pressure. It redistributes heat. It seals fractures, bypasses broken lines, wakes pumps that no person remembers activating. It has systems beneath systems, some marked on maps, some appearing only as a vibration underfoot or a light seen through a grated floor. There are buildings that have been cold and abandoned for generations but whose internal clocks continue to click. There are sealed panels that occasionally display fresh diagnostic text in obsolete alphabets. There are elevators with no assigned power source that rise, empty, to levels no one has entered.

The maintenance crews call such behavior inheritance.

They do not call it intelligence. That would require answering questions no one wants asked: what does the city want, and why has it kept them?

Beneath the power stations live the small pale feeders.

They are known as ventlings, though they are not young versions of anything. A ventling is usually the length of a person’s hand, with six jointed limbs arranged too evenly along its sides and a body clear enough to reveal pale granular organs working inside. Their black mouths can rasp mineral deposits from warm stone. They are found alone in cracks near heat exchangers, in the cavities beneath boiler plates, sometimes curled inside discarded pipe insulation.

They are harmless unless handled.

A ventling picked up with a bare hand will adhere briefly to the skin, not biting but testing. The contact leaves a pattern of numbness that can last days. Old workers say the animal is tasting the body for minerals. Children are told that ventlings count bones.

No one has seen one hatch, mate, fight, feed at length, or die. Dead specimens have been found, but when opened they contain only black powder and fragments of transparent shell. They are protected by municipal rule, not because anyone feels tenderness toward them, but because they are older than the current machinery. In some stations, technicians will delay an entire repair if a ventling has settled near a faulty seal.

“It knows something,” they say.

They do not specify what.

The larger beings on the upper slopes have no agreed name.

Officially they are designated Transit Interruption Organisms, a phrase stamped on warning placards and recited during safety briefings. In common speech they are called walkers, tall ones, rim-things, or simply them. The oldest name, used only by people who have worked outside the city boundary, is the Glass Leg.

A walker appears first as an error in the darkness.

There will be a line where no line should be, moving between two distant lamps. Then another. The creature’s legs are long enough to stride over abandoned fences and shallow pits without changing pace. Its body, if it has one, is seldom seen. Some witnesses describe a narrow pale hull suspended between the legs. Some describe no central mass at all, only articulated limbs meeting in dark air. Others insist the thing is not walking across the basin but descending through it, placing its limbs carefully on a surface humans cannot perceive.

When a lamp catches one of its joints, the joint gives off a weak, milky shimmer.

Walkers make no noise. Yet after one has passed, people often report a low pressure in the teeth, as though an immense bell has been struck below hearing.

They never approach the inhabited districts directly. They pass beyond the outer conduits, along routes that have become known through observation. The city’s roads, rails, and pipes seem to have been arranged around those routes from the beginning. No construction is permitted across them. No explanation is offered.

Once, according to a maintenance record, a survey team attempted to follow a walker’s tracks after it crossed a field of fine crust. They found prints shaped like shallow six-pointed depressions, each large enough to contain a house. The prints continued for less than a kilometer before ending at a vertical wall of black stone. There was no visible ascent, no tunnel, no break in the rock.

The team returned with a sample of powder from the final print. It was entered into storage. The storage room has been locked since.

Other things live in the basin, though they resist being called creatures.

There are filament colonies in certain abandoned ducts: hair-thin mineral threads that grow toward warmth but withdraw from light. They form delicate white fans in the corners of pipe chambers. If disturbed, they collapse into dust. If left undisturbed, they slowly knit themselves into shapes resembling diagrams—grids, spirals, branching channels, once even a crude outline of a human hand. The city periodically burns them out, then later regrets it, because their presence often predicts a pressure leak months before instruments detect one.

There are black motes that gather above the open mouths of some shafts. They can be seen only when a worker’s lamp passes beneath them. They do not fall. They do not rise. They turn in place, a slow dust-devil without wind. Anyone who walks through one comes away with a faint metallic taste in the mouth and dreams, that sleep, of places they have never seen: broad halls beneath white light, wet stone, a horizon.

There are voices in a few of the unused telephone lines.

Not messages. Not static. The receivers, if lifted, produce the faint sound of someone breathing at a distance. Occasionally a voice says a number. Sometimes it says a person’s name, though never the name of the person holding the receiver. The municipal instruction is to replace the handset, document the line, and notify no one outside the communications office.

People obey the first two parts.

The city has its own social predators as well: the collectors, the hoarders of obsolete access, maps, identification tags, discontinued medicines, old batteries that still hold a charge. They are tolerated because every family eventually needs something no longer made. A collector may live in a sealed storehouse packed floor to ceiling with unlabeled drawers, sleeping in a space scarcely large enough to turn over. They know which valve key fits a vanished station. They know which records were falsified. They know that some buildings have more floors than their exterior allows.

Their prices are rarely monetary.

A collector may ask for a week of labor, a family story, a promise to leave a particular door unopened, or the right to hear about a dream. Dreams are valuable in the basin because they are one of the few things that still change without being repaired.

And at the center of all this, below the administrative hall and the dry fountain of black glass beads, something lives in the heat.

No worker has seen it. No instrument has reached it. The deepest boreholes melt, collapse, or return readings that cannot be made to agree with one another. But the oldest infrastructure seems to converge there. The conduits pulse outward from it. The city’s temperature settles around it. The pipes tick in patterns that resemble adjustment, then patience.

Some believe it is a machine left by the builders before the builders were forgotten.

Some believe it is the planet’s exposed organ: a mineral heart, slow and inexhaustible, dreaming heat into the basin.

Some believe it is the thing for which the city was built as a shelter, a nest, or a restraint.

The inhabitants do not agree. They continue replacing lamps. They sweep the powder from the roads. They descend into access shafts with tools slung over one shoulder and hooded lights in hand. They pause at boundaries. They listen when the darkness changes shape.

And beneath their feet, beneath the warmed rooms and the silent factories and the old black stone, the buried presence continues its work: not sleeping, not waking, simply enduring—while the people above it make a life from the small, lit portion of the world they have been permitted to keep.
