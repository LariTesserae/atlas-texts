What lives in the Low Exchange is not exactly a population. It is an arrangement of survivals.

There are people, certainly, but few enough that each one becomes part of the chamber’s weather.

The regulars are known less by name than by pattern. The courier who comes down from the east lift at the same impossible hour every third night, wearing a waterproof coat even in dry weeks. The two maintenance siblings who work separate shifts and leave messages for each other in grease pencil beneath a handrail. The tea vendor who owns no stall, only a folding counter that unfolds from the wall beside Platform Three whenever the rail is expected. The vendor’s kettle is induction-heated, officially permitted, and their cups are heavy ceramic things with blue salt stains around the rims.

There are inspection crews in jointed reflective coveralls, their helmet lamps turned low to preserve their night sight. There are drainage technicians who smell permanently of copper and antifungal solvent. There are guideway cleaners, structural listeners, sensor auditors, people whose work has no upper-city equivalent because the upper city has forgotten that it rests on anything.

Some of them live in the narrow perimeter rooms: old service apartments converted from pump attendants’ quarters, transit offices, emergency shelters, abandoned retail cubicles made private with curtains and insulation foam. Their windows, if they have windows, look onto the basin. They can tell the weather above by the color of seepage, by the pace of the pumps, by whether the wall behind their beds has begun to tick.

These residents are called lowfolk by people who do not live there. Among themselves they use more practical distinctions: dry-side, basin-side, rail-side, wall-born. A wall-born is someone who grew up beneath grade, whose first sense of distance was a corridor narrowing into dark and whose first sense of sky was a ceiling too high to find.

Children are rare, but not absent. They learn early not to chase moths toward grates, not to touch an unlit door, not to step on the thin red inlays in the floor even when they are dormant. They play quiet games in the galleries: counting the seconds between the rail’s tremor and its arrival, guessing which pump has failed by the shape of the sound, inventing names for the lights that blink but are never used.

They are sometimes afraid of the upper city. It is too exposed. Its rain falls all at once.

The oldest human inhabitants are not necessarily old in years. They are old in adaptation. They have learned the chamber’s immense etiquette. They pause when a wall hum changes key. They avoid sleeping too deeply during heavy weather. They carry cloth masks not for poison but for dust, because dust is information: chalky dust from harmless settling, black dust from insulation, glittering dust from a stressed bracket somewhere beyond sight.

They know which doors are truly sealed and which merely wish to be left alone.

There are also those who come to the Low Exchange because it is one of the few places left in the city where absence can be rented cheaply. People waiting for hearings, for transit clearance, for someone to return a message. People who have been evicted from higher levels but not yet removed from municipal records. Musicians who sit near the warm ventilation plinths and play instruments that can be heard only in pauses between the machinery. Lovers who meet beneath the unlit stairwells because nobody looking down from above can see them there.

The chamber does not welcome them. It makes room.

The moths are perhaps its truest citizens.

They are white only in direct light. In shadow they become the color of old paper, mineral film, skin beneath water. Their larvae feed on the lint caught in filter housings, on fungal threads in ducts, on whatever settles in the warm places where air slows down. They emerge in small, unspectacular clouds and gather around lamps with a solemnity that resembles devotion.

The blind grate-insects live lower still. Their legs are long enough to bridge trickles of water; their bodies are black, smooth, and jointed like machine parts. They communicate through tremors in metal and stone. When a train passes, they freeze. When a structural warning begins, they vanish so quickly that the drains seem to empty themselves.

In flood season, other things arrive.

Pale crustaceans come through the runoff channels, washed down from reservoirs, flooded foundations, ornamental water systems nobody maintains anymore. Some are no larger than fingernails. Others are broad-backed and translucent, carrying dark organs like dropped ink inside them. They survive briefly in the basin’s temporary water, sifting algae and sediment from the tiles. The tunnel heron knows their schedule better than any human does.

The heron is not a resident in the official sense. It cannot be registered, summoned, or convinced to avoid restricted areas. Yet it returns. It has made a territory of platforms, drain mouths, and the basin’s old waterline. It watches doors. It watches workers. It watches the rail passing above its head without flinching.

Maintenance workers say it has been the same bird for fifteen years.

Others say it cannot be. They say each flood brings another, and the chamber simply makes them look alike.

Behind the walls, in the warm wet seams inaccessible to people, there are colonies of translucent moss, bacteria that plate pipes in iridescent films, tiny worms in mineral deposits, blind mites in insulation. The city’s engineers classify these things as biofouling, growth, contamination, maintenance burden. But the Low Exchange is full of systems that have begun to depend on them. Moss slows condensation. Fungal mats trap dust. Microbes consume traces of oil and heat-resistant coolant. A pipe collar left too clean can begin to sweat more violently than one furred with green.

The machines have their own life, if life is permitted to mean persistence, appetite, repair, and response.

The pumps sleep in shifts. The structural lattice listens constantly. The doors recognize and refuse. The vending unit remembers preferences longer than some residents remain registered. The fern-tending machine makes its rounds with a patience no person could sustain, pausing beneath each pale frond to examine its edges with a blue inspection light.

It has been seen, on occasion, stopping beside a sleeping person in the service recess. Not touching them. Merely waiting until they wake, then continuing on.

Nobody knows whether that is a fault, a safety protocol, or kindness inherited accidentally from some older code.

And deeper than all these things are the unconfirmed residents: pressure pockets, water tables, old tunnels full of air that has not mixed with the city’s air in decades. There may be animals in those places. There may be nothing but machinery and stone.

The Low Exchange does not require monsters to feel inhabited.

Its largest resident is the ground itself: warm, burdened, and awake in the only way stone can be awake. It shifts in increments too small for panic. It receives the weight of the vanished city above and answers through its embedded lattice, speaking in notes that pass through rails, tile, pipe, bone.

Everyone who lives there learns, eventually, to listen back.
