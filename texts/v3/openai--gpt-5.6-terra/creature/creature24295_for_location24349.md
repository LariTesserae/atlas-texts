What lives there first is not a people but a habit: the habit of refusing to give the water an empty place to enter.

The inhabitants call themselves, in the old administrative language, residents. In work orders they are pressure-rated personnel, domestic units, minors, dependents, temporary submergence workers, air-account holders. In the ordinary language of kitchens and schoolrooms, they call one another *inside people*. A person from a dry district—if there are still dry districts—is an *above person*, whether they have ever stood beneath open sky or not.

Most of the residents are human, though the word has become practical rather than philosophical. They are descended from people who knew weather as something that struck the outside of a house, not something that could be redirected through its stairwell. The oldest among them remember cities with streets that remained level and rain that went down drains without first being sorted, heated, chemically interpreted, and assigned a route. They remember opening a door and encountering air without checking its pressure history.

Their grandchildren do not.

Children here learn to swim before they learn to write, but not in the way surface children once learned. They learn neutral posture first: how to go slack when a current takes hold, how to find the pull of an intake by feeling the skin tighten between the fingers, how to make themselves small against a wall during a pressure adjustment. They are taught the municipal chord as babies. Each tower's note has a name, and each name implies an instruction.

The lowest note means: remain where you are.

The wavering note means: seal the mouthpiece.

The note that rises by a fraction means: do not use the stairs.

There are lullabies built around these tones. There are games in which children identify pump failures from recordings. Teenagers dare each other to sleep through a harmless calibration cycle, waking with their hair floating around their heads in the brief soft lift of water through the bedroom's balancing chamber.

They grow compact and strong through the chest, though not all in the same way. Some have been altered medically for pressure tolerance: reinforced middle ears, unusually quick circulation, blood chemistry that handles abrupt compression better than their grandparents' did. Others wear their adaptations externally—neck seals, implanted ear regulators, tiny maintenance ports beneath the collarbone. The wealthier families prefer discreet devices, smooth and internal. Workers in the tower districts often have visible hardware: brass-colored pressure gauges set into harnesses, old valve scars around the ribs, emergency breathing fittings at the jaw.

No one is fully at ease in the water, not even those born there. Ease is considered suspicious. Water is necessary, beloved in narrow ways, studied, cursed, listened to—but it is never trusted. A resident may speak affectionately of a warm current that carries laundry capsules upward, or of the clear blue channel that runs past the nursery windows, but they will also check its taste, its turbidity, its schedule, its authority code. They know that every current has been given permission to exist by someone, somewhere, and that permissions expire.

Their homes are dry when they can afford dryness.

A domestic apartment is usually a cluster of pressure cells: a main room, a sleeping blister, perhaps a kitchen alcove, each divided by round doors that close with the soft, wet kiss of gaskets. The rooms are small because dry volume is expensive. People decorate the inside surfaces compulsively. Painted skies are common. So are murals of forests, old train stations, fruit markets, beaches no one expects to see. Some households keep a single wall bare, coated in a mineral plaster that subtly sweats and cracks with pressure change. It is called an honest wall.

There are people who collect obsolete things that cannot survive the city well: paper books sealed in resin sleeves, wool garments that never leave storage boxes, rusted keys, clay bowls, photographs printed on polymer film. A grandmother may own a real wooden chair, swollen and repaired a dozen times, taking up more room than a sleeping capsule. It is not comfortable. It cannot be allowed near an unregulated moisture source. It is kept because wood, to many residents, is proof that the world was once capable of holding a shape without assistance.

Food comes in coils and trays, in damp green racks, in fermented blocks, in pale roots grown under agricultural lamps. There are mushrooms cultivated in warm exhaust chambers; algae breads; small, costly fruits raised in private membrane gardens. Meat exists, but tends to be vat-grown, stringy, and heavily seasoned. The smell of frying is almost unbearable in tightly filtered living blocks and is treated as an occasion. Families cook onions when they have something to celebrate. The scent enters ventilation systems and travels through walls; neighbors can tell who has received good news.

There are no pets in the usual sense, though people try.

The engineered water excludes fish, and anything with fur is a burden of air, waste, and grief. Some apartments maintain tiny colonies of snails in sealed glass loops. Others keep translucent cleaning organisms that browse mineral buildup from pipes. Children name them. Adults insist they are not animals, only biological maintenance. When one dies, it is often buried in a planter beside a celery stem, with the same quiet attention once given to a dog.

More numerous than the people are the things that serve them and have begun, in the long accumulation of service, to resemble a population.

There are repair swarms that live in cracks until called by voltage. They are not intelligent individually, but they make choices as a mass: whether to bridge a fracture, whether to consume exposed metal, whether to abandon a failing seal and build another behind it. Their movement is almost invisible until they gather in sufficient number to become a silver frost over stone.

There are inspection eels—long flexible machine bodies, black and jointless, that slip through pipes too narrow for a human arm. They have no faces, but many have accumulated decorative markings from technicians: stripes, eyes, obscene slogans, names of lost relatives. They return from the dark passages dragging hairlike samples of corrosion and silt. Children press their hands to observation glass to watch them pass.

There are buoyancy moths, little triangular maintenance drones that cling beneath ceilings and drink condensation from the air. Their wings are not wings but radiators. In domestic quarters they are allowed to remain visible because people find them reassuring. If the moths are active, the air system is working. If they settle all at once, someone checks the alarms.

The maintenance capsules are treated almost as fellow citizens. They travel the rails with sealed human occupants when necessary, but many run empty, carrying tools, meals, waste cartridges, replacement membranes, infants in emergency incubators, the dead. Their passage is announced by the metallic cry of their bearings. Residents can tell from the sound whether a capsule is freight, medical, tower crew, or school transport. In older neighborhoods, people pause their conversations when a red-lit capsule goes by.

The city also contains those who do not fit its intended schedules.

There are unregistered families in abandoned service volumes, living in spaces the planners believe are permanently flooded or structurally inaccessible. Some have learned to borrow power from old cable trunks and condense drinking water from warm pipes. Their rooms are lit by stolen work lamps. They are called seepers by officials, though many were born in the city and have never seeped anywhere. They know routes through maintenance shafts that no formal map preserves. When a district seal fails, it is often a seeper who reaches the breach first—not out of civic virtue, necessarily, but because they understand that the city cannot afford to lose any pocket of survivable air.

There are tower workers who spend so long beside the pressure engines that the four-note chord follows them into sleep. Their hearing changes. They become exquisitely capable of recognizing deviations that instruments miss: a ring half a millimeter out of alignment, an intake full of gravel, a distant cavity opening beyond the approved excavation zone. They are respected and avoided. In public they speak quietly, often with one hand resting against a wall, feeling through it.

There are membrane growers, whose work is part chemistry, part gardening, part veterinary practice for structures that are not quite alive. They tend translucent building skins, feed them mineral slurry, cut away infections of unwanted algae, encourage thickening around old brick and damaged joints. Their suits are pale and soft-looking, padded against accidental puncture. They are said to have the cleanest hands in the city and the dirtiest dreams.

There are current clerks who assign water its routes.

They sit before displays in dry rooms no larger than closets, watching colored maps of flow through streets, ducts, towers, reservoirs, waste loops, heat exchangers, and construction chambers. Their decisions determine whether a household receives warm washwater at dawn, whether a school corridor is passable, whether a new foundation can print safely, whether a dead zone of stagnant water is allowed to remain still for one more hour. A bad clerk can drown a workshop without ever leaving their chair. A good clerk is almost invisible.

The city has priests, although they do not always use the word.

Some serve old religions, adapting prayers to pressure locks and flood schedules. Some bless the dead before they are sent upward in weighted capsules to the reclamation chambers. Some keep vigil at the sinkhole mouth, staring through green daylight at the drowned reeds and the slight dimpling of rain. Others believe the pressure towers are not sacred exactly, but are the closest remaining example of something larger than human intention that can nevertheless be listened to.

Their rituals tend to involve silence. Silence is rare and expensive here. Whole blocks will sometimes power down nonessential systems for a minute, creating an almost unbearable hush beneath the machinery. In that hush, people hear water touching the outside of their walls. Many cry. Many do not know why.

And there are the newly arrived: survey crews, construction workers, debt migrants, families relocated from places where the flood has eaten the foundations but not yet provided replacement rooms. They come through the upper conduits with crates, rolled bedding, seed packets, old identification records, and the stunned expression of people who have not yet learned that a building can have an upstream side.

They are the ones occupying the raw chamber first.

They sleep in temporary cells while pale foundations spread beneath them. They hang photographs on walls that are still being printed. They complain that the lights are too bright, the pressure changes too frequent, the water too loud. Then, gradually, they begin to distinguish one pipe from another. They learn which stair carries an uphill stream, which plaza floods on cleaning days, which window has the best view of the dim surface. Their children begin naming the local machines. Someone opens a kitchen. Someone starts growing herbs in a rationed tray. Someone paints a blue horizon on a membrane wall.

By then the chamber has become inhabited.

Not safe, not settled, not natural.

But inhabited: full of bodies, tools, rituals, arguments, borrowed warmth, stubborn plants, small repairs, and people who have learned that survival is not merely remaining alive inside the water.

It is making the water pass around a table without taking the table away.
