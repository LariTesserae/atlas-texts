What lives on the plateau is not quite animal life in the familiar sense. It is a civilization of appetites made durable enough to survive without water, wood, leaf, or soft earth. It has learned to eat heat, pressure, oxidation, vibration, and the slow disclosures of buried stone.

The oldest creatures are the **furnaceback herds**.

From far away they resemble moving sections of the plateau: low ridges advancing across the crust with no visible urgency. Up close, each is a many-legged body forty or fifty meters long, built beneath an overlapping armor of mineral plates. Their shells are not grown all at once. They are assembled over lives that may last centuries. The animal feeds at a vent, incorporates silica, iron, sulfur, and metals into tissues beneath its skin, then extrudes a new plate along its back. Old plates crack, tarnish, and are colonized by tiny scavengers. Some carry entire temporary ecologies: silver membrane pelts, nests of heat mites, pebbled colonies of shell-borers.

Their legs are jointed in too many places, each ending in hooked, black feet that can grip hot glass or crumbly slag. They do not lift themselves much above the surface. Heat is valuable; their bellies are broad collectors lined with dark conductive scales. When a herd rests over a warm seam, the ground beneath it can dim visibly. Their shovel-mouths open in layered sections. First the outer plates fold back, then a throat of rotating mineral teeth lowers into a fissure. They drink vapors. They chew brittle deposits from the crack walls. Sometimes they remain with their mouths in the earth for months, becoming indistinguishable from a row of wrecked machines.

Inside them, chemical chambers pulse and turn. Their digestion is audible. A furnaceback does not have a heartbeat so much as a cycle: the deep compression of gases, the clatter of internal sorting plates, the abrupt release of heat through vents along its flanks. At night, or what passes for night, these vents glow through the seams of their armor. A sleeping herd can be seen from a hundred kilometers away as a low constellation crawling over the ground.

They communicate through the rock.

A young furnaceback begins by tapping: a foot held against the crust, a pressure pulse sent downward, another returned from somewhere beyond the horizon. Adults can strike the ground with a whole row of legs in sequence, creating messages that travel through ridges and trench walls. The pulses are too low for most ears. One feels them in teeth, sternum, and the waterless cavities of the skull. To the herds, the plateau is not silent stone. It is a resonant body, alive with routes, warnings, feeding records, and the slow names of places.

A basin is not merely a basin. It has a tone.

A certain cracked red ridge has a pulse that means *warm metal near the surface*.  
A line of blue slag means *cross only during the second cooling*.  
A shadowless column means *the depth has opened its eye*.  
A ringing field whose needles strike in unison means *wait until the sky has chosen one shape*.

The furnacebacks remember these things communally. A herd loses individuals continually to fissures, collapse, predation, or simply exhaustion of its internal chemistry, but it does not lose a route. The route exists in the herd’s rhythm. Calves walk within the armor-shadow of adults, feeling the messages pass through their feet before they understand them. By the time they become large enough to travel alone, they know the map of the plateau as a sequence of pressures in their bones.

The calves are astonishingly small: dark, many-legged things no larger than a clenched fist, hatched from clusters of cooling slag beneath the adults’ bodies. For their first years they feed on scraps from vent mouths and on the scale-dust shed by older animals. They swarm over their parent’s back, entering narrow channels between plates and cleaning away corrosive crystal growths. At a distance, an adult furnaceback sometimes appears to be shedding black smoke. This is not smoke. It is hundreds of young rearranging themselves across its shell.

Not all armored creatures are herd creatures.

There are the **black cutters**, which are mistaken for stones until they move. Their bodies are flattened ovals of articulated shell, each perhaps the size of a hand, perhaps much larger; perspective is unreliable near the basins. They travel in loose fields, thousands or millions together, spreading outward across new mineral deposits. They have no obvious heads. The leading edge of each body opens into a ring of hard teeth that rasps material from the ground. Behind it, fine jointed limbs pass the shavings inward.

They are the plateau’s refiners.

Where a vent has coughed black needles across the plain, cutters arrive. They turn the needle dunes over and over, separating fragile crystal from heavy metal, conductive grain from worthless glass. Their waste emerges in regular pellets, sometimes arranged unconsciously into vast dark spirals by the slope of the land and the weak pull of local magnetic fields. These spirals can remain visible for centuries. Herds avoid them. The cutters do not like to be disturbed while sorting, and in sufficient numbers they can strip the lower armor from a resting furnaceback.

The black cutters have a peculiar defense. When threatened, they lock together shell to shell until a whole swarm becomes a single dark sheet. It may cover hectares. The sheet hums as the animals pass vibration through one another, and its surface becomes so smooth and black that it reflects the bleached sky. Travelers who see such a field are warned not to approach. A reflection on the plateau is rarely only a reflection. The cutters know this instinctively. They wait until the image above them is still before they separate.

Living among them are the **needle mites**, creatures so small that their movements are visible only as a darkening of a surface. They thrive in the heat plumes around vents and on the warm undersides of large animals. Each is a grain-sized, six-limbed shard with a mirror-bright abdomen. They eat the sharpest, most reactive materials first: fresh condensates, metallic films, the exposed edges of newly fallen mineral needles. Their immense clouds make a delicate sound, like rain falling onto a roof of dry leaves.

When they gather thickly over a carcass, they can polish it clean.

This is why death does not linger on the plateau. A furnaceback that has failed will first attract the cutters, who open its seams. Then come the mites, then pale dissolvers, then burrowing spindles that enter through the emptied joints. The carcass becomes less animal with each hour. It loses its heat. It loses its noises. Its internal struts are removed one by one, carried down into the crust, and eventually the location is marked only by a patch of unusually dark dust and a subtle alteration in the herd’s path.

The **burrowing spindles** are rarely seen fully. Their upper bodies are narrow and polished, shaped like black drills with rings of pale sensory pits. They emerge from the ground only long enough to sample the air or drag something below. A spindle can sense vibration through kilometers of stone. It knows when a vent is preparing to open. It knows when an animal has collapsed. It knows, perhaps, when a place is about to disagree with itself.

They leave fans of powdered glass behind them, delicate at first, like frost. The fans point in directions no wind has taken. Some plateau creatures use them as signs. The glass-winged kites will settle beside a fresh spindle fan and hold themselves perfectly motionless, their six wings spread to drink the warmth rising from the tunnel. Furnaceback calves are taught not to step across one. A fan is an invitation downward, and what waits below has already begun to eat.

The kites are among the few inhabitants that appear graceful.

Their bodies are small, pearl-gray, and almost weightless-looking, suspended below six thin mineral wings. The wings are not feathers. They are lattices of transparent crystal membrane, ribbed with dark conductive veins. In daylight, they are nearly invisible except where they interrupt the glare. At evening, they catch strange colors from the breaking sky: indigo, copper, green-white, the dark blue of impossible depth.

Kites do not flap unless a thermal fails. They ride heat currents from columns and fissures, circling for days, sometimes weeks. Their mouths are little more than fine combs. As they pass through vapor plumes, they filter suspended particles and reactive aerosols, feeding on whatever the air has not yet burned away. Their droppings fall as tiny glass beads, and these are prized by certain smaller animals. A single kite can seed a wide area with mineral growth.

They have one behavior that none of the larger animals understand, though all of them respect it: when the sky begins to produce its second horizon, the kites climb.

They gather above basins in silent spirals, rising past the height at which heat should support them. There, against the pale dome, they become a dark rotating stain. Sometimes their reflection appears below them first. Sometimes the reflection becomes more numerous than the flock. When this happens, the real kites descend quickly and vanish among cracks and overhangs. The reflected ones may continue circling until night.

No creature eats them during those intervals.

There are also the **silver pelts** on the north faces of monoliths. Calling them creatures is uncertain, but they react, feed, reproduce, and flee. In cool conditions, a pelt begins as a faint brightening on stone. Then thousands of crystal membranes unfold, each no thicker than smoke and shaped like a leaf only by accident. They spread across the surface in branching films, drinking the difference between cold rock and warm air. The whole colony trembles with electrical activity. It can pass a charge from one end of a cliff to another.

At dawn, silver pelts are soft-looking, luminous, almost beautiful. By noon they may have turned brittle and gray. Herds gather before this happens. Small scavengers climb over them. Cutters rasp them from the stone. Furnacebacks use their broad mouths to scrape whole sections free. The pelts do not resist; indeed, they may depend on being eaten. Fragments lodged in a grazer’s armor travel to new monoliths, where they wait years for the right temperature and unfold again.

Some pelts seem to remember the shapes of animals that fed on them.

A monolith may briefly shine with the silhouette of a creature that died generations earlier: a long armored body, a kite with outstretched wings, a many-limbed crawler whose species is no longer found anywhere on the plateau. Whether this is memory, mineral accident, or a symptom of the place’s uneven reality is not known. The herds will stand facing such a shape for long periods, their feet pressed to the ground, receiving or sending a slow sequence of subsonic knocks.

Deepest in the basins live the **column attendants**.

They are seldom observed directly because they resemble the columns they serve. They are tall, segmented things of dark stone and reflective metal, rooted around the base of each central extrusion. Most of the time they remain still, their limbs folded close, their bodies ribbed like mineral formations. When a basin coughs vapor, however, they wake. A ring of them unfolds from the darkness around the column, and each begins to move with terrible slowness toward the rising plume.

Their faces are smooth shields interrupted by circular apertures that brighten when exposed to heat. Their arms divide into many narrow tools: rakes, graspers, blades, soft-looking tendrils that harden on contact with air. They do not appear to eat in the ordinary way. Instead, they collect material from the vapor fall and press it into the column’s base. Over decades, perhaps centuries, they build rings of sorted deposits there: iron-black, salt-white, sulfur-yellow, red with oxidized metal.

No smaller creature willingly enters an active attendant ring.

The furnacebacks will walk around an entire basin rather than cross it. Kites divert their thermal paths. Even burrowing spindles avoid tunneling toward the columns. Yet the attendants do not hunt, do not pursue, and do not react to nearby life unless it crosses into their circle. Then the intruder is not destroyed quickly. It is taken apart with such methodical care that the watching creatures become silent.

The attendants may not be animals at all. They may be a phase of the plateau’s own metabolism: mobile organs formed when the central columns require maintenance. Or they may be survivors of some older process, workers left behind after every recognizable maker disappeared. Their bodies are worn by centuries of mineral weathering. Some have cracks filled with silver pelt. Some bear cutter colonies on their lower limbs. One can stand beside a moving attendant and hear, beneath the grinding of its joints, a faint internal sound like distant voices speaking through stone.

There are things even the plateau’s creatures avoid.

In certain smooth places, where the fused crust has become dark and mirror-flat, something sometimes rises beneath the reflection. Not from below the ground, exactly, but from below the image of the ground. The animals know before it appears. Cutters lock into sheets. Kites fold their wings. Furnacebacks halt in mid-step, their many legs held rigid for hours. The needle mites disappear into cracks.

Then the reflection moves.

It may show a herd traveling in the wrong direction. It may show a basin where no basin exists. Sometimes it shows no creatures at all, only a dark opening in the sky below. The plateau waits. Eventually the surface clouds, fractures, or gathers dust, and the reflected place loses its coherence.

Only then do the animals resume their work.

Thus life continues: not triumphantly, not comfortably, but completely. Every vent is grazed. Every fallen shell is divided. Every heat difference is inhabited. Every mineral rain becomes food, armor, offspring, signal, structure, or waste. The creatures do not oppose the plateau’s violence. They have entered into it. Their bodies are among its instruments.

From a great height, if height can be trusted there, the plateau might appear empty: cracked ceramic, dark ridges, glowing seams, a few slow shadows crossing impossible distance.

But closer, the shadows have legs.

The ridges breathe.

The black fields sort themselves.

And beneath the crust, answering one another through stone, the herds continue to say: here is warmth; here is danger; here is the way down; here is the way that still remembers up.
