What lives in the basin has learned not to trust dry ground.

The people are called basinfolk by outsiders, though among themselves they use narrower names: gutter-side, reed-side, north-road, wall-born, those of the pale rise. A person’s first memory is often of being carried across a bridge: wrapped in oilcloth against rain, held above the dark water while adults argue about which boards are sound. They learn balance before speed. They learn to listen for hollow planks, for water moving where it should not move, for the soft collapsing sigh that means a bank is about to go.

Their feet are broad or made broad by habit—bare feet, often, with toes that grip wet timber and the edges of stone. Old people have soles polished hard as alder roots. Children go everywhere by routes adults have forgotten are routes: sill to barrel, barrel to low roof, roof to woodpile, woodpile to the back window of an aunt’s house. They play games in which one child is the rising water and the others must reach a particular doorstep without touching mud. They cheat shamelessly, but everyone accepts that cheating is a form of engineering.

The basinfolk are not especially tall. In the close lanes, height is inconvenient; a tall person gathers gutter-water on their shoulders and knocks drying herbs from eaves. They dress in layered wool, old sailcloth, softened hide, and woven rush-cloaks darkened with oil. Their sleeves are tight at the wrist. Their hems are rarely long. Everybody owns at least one garment that can be tied above the knee in a breath. They wear belts crowded with useful things: a peg, a knife, twine, a hook, a little lump of sealing clay, sometimes a spoon. The wealthy are recognized less by ornament than by dryness. Their houses leak in acceptable places.

They have hands made busy by the town. Even a person at rest tends to be mending something: a net, a boot sole, a cracked cup, a child’s hood, a miniature sluice in a toy canal. Their fingers carry permanent dark crescents from peat, clay, alder bark, and iron-rich water. Their nails are cut short. Their knuckles are often swollen. They are careful with sharp things, because a dropped knife can disappear into mud more completely than a stolen knife.

There are ditch-keepers, of course: not priests exactly, though they are treated with some of the wariness reserved elsewhere for priests. They carry long probing poles marked with old flood levels, and wear waxed capes whose hoods make their faces seem withdrawn into shadow. A ditch-keeper knows which drain was laid by whose grandfather, which wall is secretly resting on a boat, which household has narrowed a public runnel by one finger’s width every year for a decade. They settle disputes with sticks, string, water bowls, and an authority that grows sharper during rain.

Their chief tool is the level: sometimes a carpenter’s simple water trough, sometimes merely a clear bottle with a bubble trapped inside. They can look at a lane, a gutter, a sagging roofline, and tell where the water will go six hours from now. This knowledge makes them indispensable and unpopular. Every household believes its own water is innocent.

There are also gatherers of the margins. They go out when the low clouds have thinned and the reeds are steaming, traveling on narrow punts that scarcely seem wider than their hips. They return with eel traps, woven mats, roots, eggs, driftwood, marsh mint, wet feathers, clay of several colors, and the occasional object from elsewhere: a carved rail, a broken oar, a sealed bottle, a child’s shoe, a dead man’s buckle. These things are examined carefully at the Basin ledge. Nothing that comes from water is merely discarded. It may be useful. It may be owed to someone. It may carry a story that has not yet reached town.

The basinfolk do not use the word “lost” lightly. Something may be under a house, beneath a bank, downstream, caught in reeds, claimed by a neighbor, or gone into the Basin. Only the last is truly troubling.

The Basin has its attendants, though the town denies this when speaking to visitors.

They are usually old women, but not always. One may be a child who has not yet learned to fear the sound of deep water; another may be a broad-shouldered former brickmaker with a scar over one eye. They live nearby, in rooms that smell of lamp fat and wet stone. They keep the retaining walls clear of loose offerings, dead birds, spoiled food, and the small careless debris that gathers in any town. They measure its rise with notches cut into a blackened post. They listen at its unfinished side before dawn.

When it bubbles regularly, they do not call it bubbling. They say the Basin is counting.

On such mornings, people delay their work. Doors remain only partly opened. A woman carrying ashes to the midden may stop in the lane and listen with her head tilted. Children are called indoors, not frightened exactly, but made occupied: shelling peas, untangling twine, polishing the bowls that no one has used. The ditch-keepers come with their poles and stand at the edge, though poles tell them nothing. Cats leave the nearby roofs.

Then, usually, the counting stops.

The water settles. Someone spits into the drain to break the tension. The market opens late. The town resumes its repairs.

But the attendants have noticed that after certain countings, things change. A channel runs backward for a few minutes. Eels are found in rain barrels. A child wakes speaking a word nobody recognizes. The pale mineral rise extends by the width of a hand. Once, an old boat that had been used as a bridge for forty years was found floating upright in the Basin, full of clear water and six white stones.

No one agreed what to do with it. By noon it had sunk.

There are creatures in the drains, too, though they are not discussed at table.

Most are ordinary enough: frogs with mud-dark backs, blind little fish in the deeper culverts, beetles that skitter over the skin of cisterns. There are leeches, pale and flat, that appear after floods in places where no leech ought to be—inside boots, under washbasins, on the undersides of lids. There are rats that know the town’s hidden passages better than any human surveyor. Their nests are warmed by the town’s own seepage and refuse, and in winter they grow bold enough to climb pantry shelves.

But sometimes something larger moves beneath the planks.

Not often. Never clearly.

A board lifts a little and settles. Water in a drain rises without rain. A row of floating duckweed parts along a line as though a heavy finger passes beneath it. The basinfolk say this is only the mud shifting, or trapped air, or an eel as long as a man’s arm. They say it in the same tone used for saying a roof will hold another season.

The children have another explanation. They say the town is built on the back of a sleeping animal, something wide and old that came up from below when the earth was still water and has not realized it can leave. Its shell is the firm ground. Its breath comes up through the Basin. Its dreams make the channels wander.

Children are not punished for saying this. Adults simply tell them not to wake it.

The marsh herons belong to no one, and are regarded as officials from some neighboring authority. They inspect roof ridges, fish from drainage cuts, and stare into open windows. A heron that lands on a home three days in succession is an omen only if it leaves something behind. Usually it leaves droppings, which is taken as an insult but not a prophecy.

Cats are the nearest thing the town has to a nobility. They remain clean where everyone else is damp. They cross the town above its problems, sleeping in lofts and on warm brick, appearing soundlessly beside fish baskets. Their paws know which roofs will bear them. They have a custom, incomprehensible to people, of gathering along the Basin wall on certain cold evenings. They sit facing the unfinished side, tails wrapped around their feet, and watch the black water for hours.

The basinfolk pretend not to notice.

There are ghosts, if ghosts are what remains when a person’s habits outlast their body. The town has many habits. A dead carpenter is remembered in every wedge he cut. A woman who kept rain records for fifty years still determines arguments about drought because her wax tablets are stored under the ditch-house floor. A boy drowned in a cistern generations ago is invoked whenever children run near open water, though no one remembers his name. The dead do not haunt the basinfolk in pale shapes. They haunt them as instructions.

Do not put weight on that corner.
Do not trust water that has gone still.
Keep the northern drain clear before the frost.
Never build over the old reedbed.
Never fill the Basin.

And below all this, perhaps, lives the thing the law was made for.

It is not a monster in the way travelers would understand. It does not climb from the water or seize livestock. It has no appetite anyone can name. If it is alive, it is alive at the pace of clay settling, peat swelling, channels finding one another underground. It may be a buried river pressing against a skin of earth. It may be an old spring complicated by roots, stone, and drowned foundations. It may be the basin’s memory of when it was open water, remembering with enough force to rearrange the town above it.

Or it may be something that has listened for a very long time.

The town feeds it nothing deliberately. Yet every day, a little enters: lost buttons, fish scales, a spoon dropped from a bridge, rainwater from every roof, ashes dissolved in gutters, tears, blood from cut hands, the names shouted across lanes and carried down through the drains.

The Basin receives all of it.

Then it reflects the town back in fragments: lamp-fire, wall, roof, face, bridge, cloud. Never quite whole. Never entirely still.
