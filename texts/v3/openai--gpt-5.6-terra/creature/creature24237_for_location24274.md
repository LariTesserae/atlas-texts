What lives there has learned not to expect gifts in the forms other places call ordinary.

The people of the hollow are not numerous. From above, if anyone could look directly down into the broken crown of the ridge, they might mistake the settlement’s inhabitants for another dark, mobile layer of vegetation: figures passing under plate-crowns, pausing in doorways, climbing the short stairways between one black wall and the next. They are compact people, built close to the ground in the way of things that must conserve heat and water. Their skin is dark in youth and often grows paler in patches with age, dusted by the mineral salts of their work. It is common to see rust-red at the knuckles, pearl grey across the brow, yellow gathered in the creases beside the nose. These stains are not always washed away. Some are useful. Some mark a household’s garden work. Some are simply allowed to remain because the town does not make a strict distinction between a body and the place that feeds it.

Their hair grows thick and dry, usually worn bound close to the skull with plant fiber, narrow strips of shed membrane-wing, or braided reed bark. Loose hair catches too much dust, tangles in rootwork, and gives the small clinging creatures places to settle. Older residents often wear their hair in carefully arranged ridges or coils, held with polished ashstone pins. The shapes can identify a lineage, a terrace, or an obligation: those who tend the night-breath gardens bind a thin pale cord through the hair; those charged with maintaining the central square keep one side of the head closely shorn, so that the warm fissure can be felt against the skin when they kneel near it.

Their eyes are unusually protected. The light on the pale dust can blind an unprepared visitor within an hour, and the people have broad, dark lids, heavy lashes, and a habitual way of narrowing their gaze without seeming to squint. They wear little polished basalt visors when crossing the outer field, or paint thin dark lines beneath their eyes with soot mixed into plant resin. In the lanes, where light falls in shafts and rebounds from every black surface, they move with an ease that makes the narrowness seem less confining than it is.

They are quiet not from solemnity but from efficiency. The town has taught them that sound is local. A shouted message dies at the next bend; a whispered one may reach someone beside you more clearly than speech raised in a field. Their spoken language is therefore dense and compressed. A few low syllables can carry practical detail—whose grazer has wandered, which terrace needs brushing, whether the night exhalation has begun early. Much of the rest is carried by their hands.

Hands are important enough to be introduced before names. Two people meeting in a lane may touch fingertips, palm, wrist, then briefly press the backs of their hands together. The sequence tells something about warmth, work, recent travel, and mood. Children learn to read powder on another person’s skin: pale dust under the nail means upper terraces; a green-black stain at the heel of the hand means reed cutting; the fine orange pollen of wall-flowers suggests someone has been near a flowering seam and should not touch food until they have brushed themselves clean.

They speak with objects as well. A scraper laid blade-up outside a doorway means the household is working and does not want visitors. A basket tipped on its side says there is food to share but no one awake to receive thanks. Three smooth stones set in a row on a stair mean a child has gone somewhere alone and is expected back before the heat leaves the wall. A bundle of hardened leaf-plates hanging from a lintel announces a death, though not always a recent one. The plates may remain for years, gradually becoming part of the doorway’s shade.

Their clothing begins as practical wrapping: layered fiber cloth, softened reed mesh, flexible skins peeled from the larger glassy shrubs, and thin plates cut from plants at the moment their tissues harden. But the same materials become elaborate in the hands of those with patience. Some wear long mantle-panels that make them look, from a distance, like moving sections of the hollow’s vegetation. Others favor tightly wrapped tunics and many-pocketed belts, their pockets filled with brushes, seed-cysts, food pellets, mineral flakes, bones sharpened for digging, and pieces of colored stone whose use may be social rather than material.

No one wears much that would jangle. The few ornaments they value are silent: polished fragments of black glass, bead chains made from the inner joints of burrowing animals’ discarded shells, narrow plaques of translucent mineral bark. A person’s wealth is not carried as metal or coin. It is carried in relationships to sheltered places: access to a warm sleeping wall, a share in a mature reed-tree, the right to harvest from an old garden mat, responsibility for a communal animal enclosure, knowledge of where a certain salt gathers after certain nights.

The pale grazers are not quite livestock in the simple sense. They are neighbors kept close. Their hides provide scale-plates, their shed mouthparts become tools, their warmth sustains sleeping rooms, and their dung—dry, mineral-rich, almost odorless after curing—feeds the terrace mats. But they are also consulted. The people watch the grazers’ habits with more attention than they give the sky. A herd refusing one lane may mean the stone there has changed temperature. A grazer leaning its armored flank against a certain wall every afternoon may reveal an emerging crack, a warm pocket, or an insect nest. Children are assigned individual animals when young, not as owners but as listeners. They learn the clicking rhythms of mouths, the minute shifts of posture, the places where an animal prefers to settle. By adolescence, most can walk through a crowded pen at dusk without touching a body, guided by sound and stored familiarity.

The membrane-winged creatures are treated with a different kind of regard. They are not pets, though some families cultivate nesting folds for them beneath roof slabs. Their slow wing pulses carry pollen from wall-flower to wall-flower, and their patterned membranes are gathered only after natural death. People say they do not fly so much as revise their position in the air. Children sometimes imitate them in games, standing on high steps and opening their arms slowly until an elder tells them to stop wasting warmth.

The burrowing animals have an almost civic status. Their mounds are not casually destroyed. A household that needs to widen a doorway must first coax or redirect the colony beneath it, usually by laying down particular fibers, warm stones, and polished fragments the animals favor. There are specialists who understand their tunnels well enough to negotiate with them. They are called, in the closest rendering, wall-listeners or under-stewards. Their work is respected, difficult, and occasionally absurd: an entire household may wait three days for a colony to move its nursery chamber before repairing a sagging floor.

The people are not alone in thinking the hollow belongs to them. Nothing here is quite owned without qualification. A terrace is held by a family, but its roots may cross into the next terrace. A roof is made by hands, but a plant colony may claim it in time. A wall can be carefully laid, then become the outer face of a burrow, then the foundation of a vine cradle, then the warm flank of a sleeping grazer. The town’s laws, such as they are, concern sequence and accommodation. Who cuts first. Who waits. Who is responsible when a root lifts a paving slab and a child trips. Who receives the fruit from a roof-vine fed by a neighbor’s ash basin. Such questions can occupy long evenings.

Their elders are not necessarily the oldest people, though age is visible and honored. An elder is someone who has watched enough small changes to recognize a pattern. They can tell whether a crack is a seasonal opening or a generational one; whether a new mineral taste on the leaves means a richer exhalation, a poisoning of the roots, or nothing at all. They remember the old alignments of stairs. They can point to a house wall and say that it was once a terrace edge, or to a planted lane and say that it used to be too hot for bare feet. Their memories form the town’s long map, which is not drawn on paper because paper is too hungry a material here. It is recited while walking.

Children are freer than the adults appear to be. They slip through root tunnels, sit in the hollow reed trunks of the tree-like growths, hunt shed scales in animal pens, and collect the failed flowers that have folded black upon themselves. They are taught early not to waste anything: not a stone, not pollen, not a strip of fiber, not the warmth of a wall. But they are also taught to play with restraint rather than prohibition. They build miniature homes against larger homes; arrange burrow-polished fragments into elaborate lane patterns; make soft clicking conversations with the grazers; race along the shaded sides of the square without crossing the boundary around the fissure.

The fissure does not frighten them in the dramatic way a monster or a grave might frighten another people. It is too useful to become an object of simple terror and too unknowable to become familiar. Its warmth is measured. Its breath is smelled. A faint shift in its exhalation may tell the gardeners that certain night pores should be covered, or tell the wall-listeners to inspect the lower foundations. The square around it is a place of meetings, repair agreements, mourning, and the first public work of newly grown children. But no one presumes intimacy with it.

There are people whose duty is to sit beside it at certain hours. They do not pray, exactly. They place one palm on the dark paving and one hand loosely over their own sternum. They listen with their bodies. Sometimes they report nothing. Often, nothing is the report the town most wants.

The dead are returned carefully. There is not enough fuel to burn them extravagantly, nor enough soft earth for burial. A body may be placed in a high dry chamber where the mineral air slowly takes it apart, leaving bones that are later worked into tools, pins, garden markers, or small domestic objects. The dead remain in the town’s touch. A grandmother may become the smooth handle of a seed brush. A parent may become a white hook for hanging reed bundles. This is not viewed as a reduction. To be useful after death is considered a continuation of shelter.

And there are other things that live in the hollow, too small or too slow to be counted until they become necessary.

Salt-mites graze the morning powders from leaf surfaces and are in turn gathered by the membrane-winged creatures. Fine transparent worms inhabit the warmest ashstone and leave behind threads of mineral paste that children prize for sealing tiny cracks. Pale fungi, almost indistinguishable from dust, spread beneath animal bedding and convert waste into a dry, sweet-smelling soil analogue for the garden basins. There are beetle-like things with mirrored backs that emerge only during the first hour after sunset, climbing the flower walls to drink condensed vapor. They vanish by dawn, leaving minute tracks like writing.

At the outer edge of the dust field live creatures few residents see clearly: long-bodied scavengers that move beneath the powder and surface only on moonless nights. Their passage leaves a low ridge, as though the ground has briefly remembered a buried current. The town does not hunt them often. Their flesh is bitter and their bodies are difficult to find. But their hard, crescent-shaped jaws are valued for cutting mature plant plates.

What most deeply lives there, however, may be the habit of adjustment.

The people do not imagine the ridge as dead ground beneath a finished town. They know it is moving. Their homes lean because the ridge leans. Their stairs alter angle because the ground has lifted. Their gardens change flavor as the breath from below changes. A child may inherit a room whose floor is one finger-width higher than it was when the child’s mother was born, and the fact will be known, noted, perhaps marked by a thin line scratched into the wall.

They live in a place that asks constantly, quietly, to be noticed.

So they notice. They rebuild a step. They move a sleeping mat. They train a vine away from a crack. They shift a low wall by the width of a hand. They teach their children which stones are still useful, which roots can be cut, which must be persuaded, and why no stone should be thrown into the dark line at the center of the square.

Not because something below will punish waste.

Because aboveground, everything has a place to become.
