What lives in the basin is not one people, exactly. It is a place too full for a single kind of life to own it.

The herds belong to it in the most obvious way. They are small, deep-chested grazers, somewhere between antelope and mountain goat, with narrow black hooves made for broken limestone. Their horns are not grand: short swept hooks in the males, smaller crescents in the females, all rubbed pale at the tips from years of scraping mineral crust from stones. In mist they disappear except for these horns and the occasional white flare of a rump. They travel in long, soft files from dry grazing to wet hollow, from wet hollow to shade, from shade to the open places where the wind keeps biting flies away.

They are numerous, but not tame. Even while feeding, each herd has several animals standing with their heads high, ears turning independently toward every sound. Their alarm is a sudden held stillness. A thousand mouths stop tearing grass. A thousand legs cease their small movements. Then one animal stamps, and the pause shatters into motion.

The doglike hunters understand this pause better than anything else.

They are the nearest thing the basin has to a ruling species, though they do not rule. They are lean, long-legged animals with rough dun coats, dark muzzles, and ears too large for their heads. In the cool hours they cross the floor in family bands, not quite wolves and not quite dogs: more patient than wolves, less territorial than dogs. They know the basin’s recurrent routes so well that they often seem to be waiting for an event rather than seeking it. A herd will enter a narrowing between two slopes; the hunters will already be there, spread among the stones.

Yet they are not relentless. Their dens are shallow scrapes beneath overhangs, lined with dry grass, shed hair, and feathers. Their young spend much of the day playing at the mouths of these shelters, rolling in limestone dust and stealing strips of hide from one another. Adults sometimes leave a kill half-finished because beetles have come, or because the heat has risen, or because a pool has begun calling to them from somewhere beyond the grass. Hunger moves through them, but it does not make them cruel.

There are cats too, larger than a housecat but smaller than a leopard, with pale coats marked by broken charcoal bars. They keep to the ledges and the thorn shadows. Their faces are broad and strangely level, giving them an expression of permanent consideration. They hunt hares, birds, and the young of the hoofed herds when chance presents them. Often they simply lie on stone, receiving the cold or warmth of it through their bellies, their eyes reduced to narrow bright seams.

Around the pools live the wet things.

Frogs, dark-backed and broad-toed, crowd the mud after rain. Some are no larger than a thumbnail; others are heavy enough to make a small splash when they leap from a bank. They breed with astonishing urgency during the brief wet season, filling every shallow pool with ropes of eggs and every evening with a vibrating chant. Their young do not always have time to become adults before the water shrinks. Many are stranded in blackening mud. But enough survive, always enough.

There are otters in the hidden stream channels: small, long-bodied, brown as wet roots. They appear at dawn and dusk, slipping out from under stone shelves with fish or crayfish in their mouths. They do not make dams, because there is too little water and too much rock. But they are habitual users of the basin. Certain flat stones bear the white dust of crushed shells. Certain banks are crowded with their tracks. Certain shallow runs have been cleared of loose pebbles by countless passages of their bodies.

The birds give the basin much of its visible restlessness. Ground birds hurry through the sparse grass in family clusters, feathered in pebble-brown patterns so intricate that, when still, they become indistinguishable from the terrain. Waders visit the pools on thin pink-gray legs. Ravens and carrion crows occupy the higher air and the limestone shelves. They are clever enough to use the place deliberately.

The crows know which stones will break a shell, which cracks conceal beetles, which thorn bushes hold the nests of smaller birds. They follow the large hunters at a distance, waiting for torn hide. They watch otters crack shells. They watch one another watching. In some parts of the basin, a crow will carry a snail shell to a particular ledge and drop it onto a chosen slab. The ledges acquire pale scatterings of broken shell, not because anyone intends a monument, but because memory has settled in the birds and made certain rocks important.

And there are the low people.

At first a visitor might mistake them for another kind of animal among the brush: upright only occasionally, gray-brown in color, narrow-shouldered, long-armed. They are small compared with humans, but sturdier than monkeys, with broad feet splayed for mud and stone. Their skin beneath the sparse hair is a dull clay color; their faces have flat noses, deep-set eyes, and heavy ridges above the brow that give them a grave appearance even in play.

They are not many. Perhaps several dozen live across the entire basin, divided into loose kin-groups that gather and separate according to water, fruiting shrubs, insects, and the movements of the herds. They sleep in hollows under roots, in shallow caves, and beneath low overhangs where the rock holds the day’s warmth. They do not build walls. They do not cut trees. They do not mark territory except with the worn pathways of their feet and the occasional overturned stone.

Their intelligence is practical, immediate, and shared.

They know which pools turn bitter in late summer and which remain sweet beneath their mineral film. They know how to lift a flat stone without placing fingers where scorpions rest. They probe rotting wood with stripped branches. They crack roots open with rounded rocks. They carry fire only when lightning has left it available in dry grass, and even then not far or for long. By the next rain, their blackened patches are washed clean or swallowed by new growth.

They sometimes hunt, but awkwardly compared with the cats or the doglike hunters. A few will drive hares toward a waiting companion. They raid ground-bird nests. They gather insects in large quantities after rain, turning stones with quick, alert hands. Most of their food comes from roots, seeds, eggs, grubs, shellfish, small creatures at the pools, and whatever the larger predators leave behind.

They have a fondness for bones.

Not tools in a crafted sense—nothing shaped patiently into blades or needles—but bones are carried, tapped, chewed, used to poke into holes or scrape at mud. A long leg bone may pass through several hands during a day and then be abandoned by a pool. A skull may be rolled down a slope by juveniles, retrieved, rolled again. Nothing accumulates. The basin’s scavengers and weather redistribute every object before it can become a record.

Their voices are low and various. They hum while digging. They cough softly to locate one another in mist. When frightened, they make a sharp two-note bark that sends nearby birds bursting from the brush. At dusk, when several kin-groups meet near reliable water, they sit close together and produce long, wavering calls—not songs with words, perhaps, but calls with recognizable patterns. A mother answers an older daughter. A male calls from beyond the reeds and is answered by a companion on the slope. Young ones imitate the sounds badly until they are silenced with a touch or a cuff.

They know the deaths of their own.

When one of them dies, the others do not bury the body. The ground is too hard in some places, too transient in others, and nothing in the basin seems inclined toward burial. But they sit nearby for a time. They touch the face, the hands, the hair. Sometimes they bring food and leave it near the body; sometimes they drive crows away for a day or two, though never long enough to prevent the larger process from continuing. Then they go.

By the next season there is no grave. There may be a few pale bones under thorn roots, scattered by foxes and rain. A crow may carry away something small and bright. The place does not retain the dead in a human fashion. It retains them as nourishment, calcium, memory in the movements of survivors.

The low people do not seem to find this strange.

Nothing in the basin has a plan for becoming more than it is. The crows do not seek cities of broken shell. The otters do not redirect streams. The hunters do not clear the grasslands of prey. The low people do not lay out stones in circles or name the ridges into possession. They are capable of preference, fear, affection, jealousy, curiosity, and grief. They recognize favored pools and dangerous ledges. They know one another’s calls. But their minds turn outward rather than forward.

They live by attention.

A change in wind. The sound of hoofbeats becoming too sudden. A cloud thickening beyond the broken rim. A frog chorus stopping all at once. The cold breath of mist reaching the ankles. A loosened stone. A crow’s alarm. A pool that has begun to shrink.

To them, if they have any thought resembling history, the basin has always been in the act of happening. Rain comes down the same gullies. The grass returns in the same seams. Predators take the weak, carrion feeds the air, water disappears beneath stone and appears again elsewhere. The walls remain raw and pale. The sky remains a long opening overhead.

And each morning, from every shelter, burrow, scrape, nest, and shadowed shelf, life rises into it again.
