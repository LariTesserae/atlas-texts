What lives there is not one thing, but a way of surviving interruption.

The blind fish are the most obvious inhabitants, though they do not seem to own the chamber. They arrive in pale flashes where the light is strongest, turn their soft mouths into the descending water, and vanish into clefts below the shelves. Their eyes have long ago ceased to be eyes. Beneath the skin of their heads are two milk-white knots, no larger than seeds, which can sense brightness but not shape. They know the chamber by pressure: the tremor of a falling stream, the pull before a whirlpool forms, the low alteration in the water when a new crack opens somewhere in the stone.

They are born in air pockets.

When one of the temporary breathing rooms holds long enough, the fish gather beneath it. Their eggs rise in clumps and adhere to the wet ceiling of the hollow, where they tremble in the thin air. For a few days they are not wholly underwater creatures. Then the air closes, the ceiling floods, and the young fall into the current already knowing how to be carried.

The crabs live more deeply, under the mobile shelves of sediment. Most are not much larger than a thumb. Their shells are dark with embedded mica, and when they remain still they look exactly like bits of broken rock. They feed on what the mountain brings: drowned insects, grass seed, skins shed by fish, the pale bacterial velvet that grows on iron-rich seeps. Their true labor is stranger. They collect grains.

Each crab has a preferred color—red clay, black glass, white limestone, green river grit—and will labor for weeks to drag those grains into a crack beneath a shelf. There they build little mounds, unseen except when a storm strips the floor bare. The mounds are not nests. They are weight. The crabs seem to understand, in their crab manner, which cracks must be kept open and which should be packed shut before the pressure beneath them becomes dangerous.

Sometimes the people find a passage that was open on their last visit filled neatly with red grit. They know to turn back. They say the crabs have closed a door.

There are worms in the mineral foam.

No one sees them at first, because they are the color of mountain milk: translucent, chalk-pale, soft as threads of melted wax. They live in the newest deposits, where cold water rich with dissolved stone enters a crack and begins to leave its white crust behind. They eat what cannot quite be called food—mineral bloom, iron films, the faint organic stain carried down from soil and roots far above. They burrow through the soft foam and leave tunnels that harden behind them.

When a piece of mountain milk is taken into the outer air, the worms die or sleep; nobody agrees which. The valley people do not speak of them while gathering. They scrape the foam gently, never gouging into the crack. This is partly reverence and partly caution. A cavity emptied too deeply can become a mouth for water.

The transparent-fingered plants are alive in a more patient fashion. Their leaves are not leaves in the usual sense. They are thin sacs, clear enough that the dark line of a stem can be seen running through them. They open and collapse with changes in the current. During violent flow they flatten against the rock, almost disappearing. During slack water they spread into the chamber like hands held out for rain.

Inside their clear fingers live minute red organisms, so small that they resemble rust suspended in glass. The plants feed them light from the blue ice; the red organisms feed the plants whatever they make from it. Together they have learned to use the chamber’s noon-brightness before it vanishes into silt and roar.

The oldest of these gardens may be only a few years old. That is old here.

The more mysterious inhabitants are the ones the visitors do not name directly.

They call them the held breath.

A held breath is encountered first as an absence of violence. A traveler swimming through a lower passage may emerge beneath a shelf and find the water strangely still. Bubbles gather there but do not rise. A lamp flame, once lit, leans toward a darkness where no wind can be felt. The skin prickles. The water tastes less of iron.

Then there is a sound: not the mountain’s knocking, not the clatter of pebbles, but a slow inward-and-outward creaking, like a very large animal sleeping behind a wall.

Some say this is only the stone adjusting around an air pocket. Some say the chamber itself has lungs, and that the breathing rooms are its exhalations. The oldest gatherers say neither thing is quite true.

They say there are presences made of all the breaths left in the mountain: the trapped breath of birds swept through the upper fissures, of goats buried in avalanche caves, of miners who entered places they should not have entered, of children who shouted into wells and heard their voices altered on the way back. Those breaths travel with the water until they find a hollow where they can collect. There they wait, not alive, not dead, listening to the mountain make and unmake its rooms.

A person who rests too long in such a hollow may begin to remember a life that is not theirs: a roof beam, a cooking fire, a river crossing in summer, a name spoken by someone with a familiar voice. The rule is to leave before the lamp burns halfway down.

There is also the keeper of the rope ladder.

No one has seen it clearly. The platform is repaired between visits, though the valley people deny repairing it every time. New root cord appears braided into old strands. Fresh-cut branches are wedged under ancient blackened wood. Stones that had rolled loose are restored to their places. Sometimes the ladder has been tied with knots no living person in the upper valleys claims to know.

The keeper may be a person: a solitary gatherer lost during a flood, surviving in the breathing rooms and feeding on fish, moss, and whatever the torrents bring.

It may be several people, appearing only when the water is low.

It may be the crabs, if crabs have hands in the dark that are larger than the ones seen in daylight.

Or it may be the mountain’s habit of not allowing a useful thing to disappear until it has learned what it was useful for.

The people who enter the chamber leave offerings at the base of the ladder: a hook bent from bone, a little oil, a strip of dried meat sealed in waxed cloth, a child’s first shed tooth. They do not call this worship. They say only that anything which keeps a path open deserves to be fed.

Above all these small lives, there is the mountain itself.

Not as a god exactly. It does not judge. It does not speak in words. But it is young, and young things are hungry for form. The chamber is one of its thoughts: a bright flooded thought it cannot keep still. It makes shelves, breaks them, lays down white mineral skin, tears it away. It admits fish and birds and people only briefly, then rearranges the routes by which they came.

The inhabitants understand this better than visitors do.

The fish do not resist the current until resistance matters.  
The crabs seal what must be sealed.  
The pale worms harden the soft places.  
The transparent plants flatten themselves and wait.  
The held breaths gather where they can.  
And perhaps the keeper reties the ladder.

Nothing there expects permanence.

That is why, in the violent water beneath the blue ice, so much has managed to live.
