What lives there is not a single people, nor a hidden city waiting beneath the fronds. The drowned plateau has no need for a ruling species. Its inhabitants are arrangements of function: creatures that have become so fitted to the permanent currents, the fixed light, the returning sounds, that an individual life is difficult to separate from the work of the whole.

The first inhabitants are the plants themselves.

They are not passive growths, even where they resemble moss, weed, or forest. The great tower-plants have slow internal awareness, if awareness can exist without thought. Their pumping rhythms alter when a root colony is damaged. Their crowns incline fractionally toward the mineral upwellings that rise from below. A wounded stalk may close its leaf fans for years, redirecting current through its neighboring trunks until the injury is armored over with shell, fiber, and chalk. Whole groves share pressure through their roots. A tremor in one valley can produce a delayed tightening of leaves in another.

Their communication is not a language of signals but a language of load.

Weight changes. Flow changes. Salt changes. The small electrical prickling of an animal against a root changes. A forest feels these things and responds by opening, holding, filtering, releasing. It does not decide in the way an animal decides. Yet it makes room for what belongs, and slowly excludes what does not.

The pale cushion-plants are among the oldest minds in the water. They grow over stones, consume stones, and eventually become stone-colored themselves: enormous lobed masses whose interiors are threaded with channels large enough for fish, eels, and the occasional grazing animal to pass through. Their upper tissues drink the weak daylight. Their lower tissues feed on mineral seepage. Between those two hungers, they maintain a warm, dim interior full of small life.

Inside them live colonies of soft-bodied things called, by no one, lantern-broods.

They are fist-sized, translucent animals with rings of black eyes around a central mouth. They do not swim well. They cling to the inner membranes of the cushions, feeding on filtered particles and the skins shed by other creatures. At intervals, all the lantern-broods in a cushion loosen at once and drift upward through its internal canals. Their pale bodies collect in chambers near the surface, where they glow blue-white for several hours. This illumination is not mating display exactly, nor defense. It is part of the cushion-plant’s respiration. The brood light encourages fine films of living tissue along the chamber walls, and those films in turn feed the brood.

Thus an organism that looks like a colony becomes an organ, and an organ becomes a weather event.

In the root forests dwell the knucklers.

They are shelled filter creatures, each no larger than a human hand, but they form populations so immense that their collective movements shape the soundscape of entire valleys. Their bodies are enclosed in hinged plates of dull green mineral. They fasten themselves in lattices around roots and open and close their shells according to the pulse passing through the plant. When a tower trunk draws water upward, millions of knucklers open. When it releases pressure, millions shut.

From far away, this creates the mechanical clatter mistaken for machinery.

Up close, the sound is more intimate: a thousand tiny doors closing in a house with no rooms.

The knucklers have little mobility, but their larvae travel everywhere. They drift through the drowned country in slow clouds, settling only where a suitable rhythm can be felt. A young knuckler does not choose its root by taste or texture. It chooses by pulse. If the deep piston-beat inside a plant matches the rhythm stored in its soft body, it anchors itself and begins to grow its shell. The wrong rhythm leaves it floating forever.

There are free creatures, too—more recognizably animal, more capable of error.

The long-necked ridge-walkers are among them. Seen in the water, they appear gentle and improbable: bodies shaped somewhat like heavy seals, with four paddle-limbs ending in spread, gripping digits, and necks long enough to rise through several bands of light. Their skins are dark and smooth underwater, but on the exposed mountain crowns they dry into a textured gray hide. Around their throats swell the red air-sacs that allow them to carry surface breath down into the cold depths.

They are not truly amphibious in the ordinary sense. They do not belong either to water or to air. They belong to the crossing between them.

Each ridge-walker follows a particular ascent through its entire life. It knows—by inherited instinct, by scent, by pressure, perhaps by something stranger—the exact series of shelves, ledges, root bridges, and floating leaf-fields that will bring it from a deep feeding ground to an exposed ridge. It climbs that route repeatedly. Not every day, perhaps not every year in any meaningful measure, but always in the same order.

Young ridge-walkers follow beneath the bodies of their elders, pressing their narrow heads against the adult’s belly so they can ride in its pressure shadow. If one is separated, it may spend decades attempting to find the route again. Such lost animals become the only visibly distressed things in the plateau. They drift through unfamiliar forests, calling in low throat-tones. The forest does not answer them. It merely continues to pump.

Eventually, if they survive, they settle into another cycle and become part of a different herd’s route.

The great browsers are older than the ridge-walkers and less intelligible. They are sometimes called valley-mouthed, though no observer could be certain where their mouths end or their bodies begin. A browsing head may emerge from a curtain of fronds: pale muzzle, blunt sensory tendrils, a row of slow-turning plates that strip algae without tearing the plant tissue beneath. Then the head moves on, followed by an immense shoulder, perhaps a section of ribbed flank, perhaps the beginning of a body. The rest is concealed in blue distance or vegetation.

They carry ecosystems.

Their backs are ridged with living channels in which water collects and circulates. Mosses, shelled colonies, tiny grazing fish, and threadlike worms live there. Some tower-plant seeds attach to their hides and take root in the mineral deposits accumulated along their spines. A valley-mouthed beast may therefore wear a young forest for centuries before the growth becomes too heavy and sloughs away in a drifting mass.

The creatures are so large that they alter local current patterns. Their passing opens temporary clearings in the fronds. Their wake carries sediment into places where sediment has not otherwise reached for ages. Small animals gather behind them as they move—not to feed on scraps, though they do that, but because the wake is one of the few things in the drowned plateau that resembles novelty.

Even this novelty is repeated. The same beast makes the same opening in the same forest. The same cloud of shrimp rises through the same beam of green light. The same scraps descend. Yet to the shrimp, perhaps, repetition is not sameness. Perhaps their lives are short enough that a returning wake is an event.

The open-channel predators have more obvious individuality. They are called flank-eyes by the few smaller creatures capable of avoiding them, though the name has no voice behind it. Their bodies are narrow and segmented, black as wet obsidian, with jointed fins folding along their sides. Each bears a row of metallic sensory organs that resemble eyes but may detect vibration, heat, electrical motion, or the pulse of panic in nearby flesh.

When they hunt, the reflective organs ignite one after another along the flank.

A flank-eye does not chase prey for long. It executes a route. It enters a channel at a certain depth, passes beneath a particular root, flexes around a known pillar of basalt, and bursts into a fish-school at a precise angle. The fish explode, the predator takes one, perhaps two, and then leaves. If a fish-school is absent, the creature still performs the sequence. If prey is unusually abundant, it does not take more than its established share.

There is something almost ceremonial about this restraint. But it is not mercy. It is geometry preserved by appetite.

The smallest creatures are the ones most nearly everywhere.

Luminous shrimp occupy the shadowed spaces: beneath leaves, inside root curtains, in cave mouths, around the slow bodies of grazers. They pulse light in sequences that seem random until watched for a long time. Then patterns emerge. A cluster of three flashes may mean current change. A downward ripple of light may precede the opening of a root fan. A long, dim shimmer may signal the approach of a valley-mouthed browser.

Some shrimp are born with incomplete patterns. Their flashes stutter or arrive in the wrong sequence. These flawed individuals are not driven out; there is no social mechanism for that. But other animals fail to read them. They may be ignored by their schools, mistaken by predators, or lost in the dark. The drowned plateau is balanced, not kind.

Along the limestone caves live the note-nesters.

They are small, pale creatures with six limbs and soft, folded bodies, resembling aquatic moths without wings. They gather wherever water passing through hollow stems produces a sustained tone. The creatures attach themselves to the stems, arranging their bodies in rings. Their tissues thicken or thin in response to the vibration, slightly altering the interior diameter of the stems. Over generations, the nests tune the note.

A cave may therefore possess a single continuous sound for centuries because its inhabitants maintain it.

The note-nesters feed on microbial films and dissolved material caught in the stem fields. They breed by releasing clouds of dust-fine young into the resonant water. Most are carried away. A few settle in other singing caves and alter the pitch there by a fraction. In this way the plateau’s music changes, but slowly enough that no living creature can perceive the change as change.

The caves are full of their tuned notes: deep tones in the larger chambers, thin glassy whines in narrow passages, tremulous chords where several currents cross. Larger animals sometimes sleep near these sounds. Ridge-walkers fold their limbs beneath themselves on cave floors. Fish suspend in motionless schools. Even flank-eyes have been observed resting along the walls, their bright side-organs dimmed.

There may be no sleep in the human sense here. There is only participation at a lower intensity.

And there are beings that do not fit comfortably into the animal or plant world.

In the black fissures beneath the oldest terraces, where warmer mineral water rises into the cold, there are woven bodies called the patient reefs. They begin as mats of threadlike organisms around a vent. Over immense spans, mineral deposits harden through them; new tissues grow over the deposits; shell-creatures, fungi, rootlets, and soft filter animals join the mass. Eventually the reef becomes a vertical structure: a branching, pale-black formation taller than a tower-plant, porous throughout, continuously exhaling warm water.

The patient reef does not move, but neither does it remain the same.

Its branches close over dead channels and open new ones. Its mineral skin records the chemistry of the water in bands. Its inner cavities house entire populations. Some have openings shaped uncannily like doors, but they are not doors. Water enters them; water leaves them; the reef has no interior reserved for a visitor.

The nearest thing the plateau has to a citizen may be the drift-keeper.

These are long, ribbon-bodied animals that live in the broad clear avenues between forests. They are rarely seen because their bodies are nearly transparent. Only their internal organs show: a black central thread, glimmering buoyancy sacs, and a delicate red net that expands when they feed. A drift-keeper does not swim against current. It aligns itself with the local flow, using broad fins to hold a chosen position in the water column.

Its purpose, if purpose can be assigned, is to gather things that do not belong.

A dead leaf loosened from its proper loop. A clump of excess sediment. A dying animal carried into an unfamiliar channel. The drifting larvae of a species that has settled in the wrong depth. The keeper encloses such matter in folds of its body and carries it—not far, but exactly far enough—to where it can reenter the plateau’s established use.

They are scavengers, transporters, and corrections.

A drift-keeper may spend a century moving between the same two root forests, catching the same sorts of displaced material, feeding the same fungal mats. If one dies, the matter of its body becomes the work of another system. If too many die, debris begins to gather in the avenues, currents alter, and the forest responds. New keepers emerge from dormant eggs in the root beds.

They are not guardians because nothing needs guarding from outside.

They are custodians of recurrence.

Perhaps there are also people.

Not human people, and not necessarily tool-makers. In the deepest inhabited folds of the root country, among the curtains beneath the floating leaf-fields, some creatures gather in groups that are neither schools nor herds. They are tall, narrow-bodied beings with flexible ribbed torsos, four long arms, and heads enclosed in translucent hoods. Their skins carry slow bands of pigment that pass from one body to another when they touch.

They do not build structures. Instead, they cultivate living clearings.

A group will inhabit a pocket beneath a root canopy where the current is gentle. Over years they encourage certain fronds to grow apart and others to braid together. They stroke young rootlets with mineral-rich mucus. They harvest the shell colonies only after the colonies have divided. They guide luminous shrimp into particular patterns, perhaps for light, perhaps for meaning. Their dwellings are not enclosed spaces but maintained conditions: a room of current, brightness, sound, and food.

They are called, if they can be called anything, the held.

They seem to remember.

This is their strange distinction. Other creatures repeat a route because their bodies contain it. The held repeat things while recognizing that they have repeated them. They return to a certain cave note and become still. They gather when a valley-mouthed browser passes, placing their hands against one another’s hoods as the immense body crosses the distant water. They find the dead of their kind and carry them upward, fastening them in the underlayers of the floating leaf-fields, where the bodies are gradually taken apart by roots and small animals.

Their dead are not buried. They are distributed.

The held use no hard tools, though they sometimes carry polished stones, hollow stems, and plates of shed shell. A shell plate may be tapped against a root to produce a delayed echo. A hollow stem may be placed in a current to alter the song of a cave. They leave marks only in temporary arrangements: shrimp-light patterns, cleared silt, the angle of a folded leaf.

One might mistake these arrangements for ritual. They may be ritual. But they may also be maintenance, communication, play, mourning, or merely the pleasure of altering something small in a world where nearly everything else has already found its perfect form.

The held are not masters of the drowned plateau. They are among its smallest consequential inhabitants. A tower-plant can outlive their entire lineage. A patient reef can absorb the trace of their dwelling without interruption. A browsing beast can pass over their cultivated clearing and rearrange it in an hour.

Yet the held endure because they do not try to oppose the system.

They learn its repetitions. They listen for the returning sound in the valley wall. They read the small errors in the shrimp-light. They know when a root forest is tightening around an injury, when a drift-keeper has failed to arrive, when the fixed hunting sequence of a flank-eye has shifted by the width of a hand.

Such shifts matter to them.

They are perhaps the only beings for whom the drowned plateau is not wholly beyond change.

To the held, there are tiny changes everywhere: a new scar on a ridge-walker’s neck; an old cave note becoming slightly flatter; a child’s pigment bands forming an unfamiliar pattern; a leaf falling a little farther before being caught. Their lives are brief enough, and their attention close enough, that the eternal machine still contains surprises.

So what lives in the high water is not stasis.

It is a multitude held inside a rhythm too vast to notice itself: pumping forests, shell-lattices, luminous swarms, route-bound hunters, mountain-climbing breath-carriers, animals that bear gardens, reefs that grow from heat and stone, and quiet remembering beings who make rooms in the roots and call the smallest variations by names that vanish with them.
