What lives in the basin is not one creature, though at first it may seem that way.

A person looking down through the braided conifers might begin by naming things: moss, alder, goat, owl, beetle, fungus, newt. The names would be correct, and insufficient. Each life there has kept its own shape for so long that its edges have become difficult to separate from the things touching it. The moss is not merely on the stone; it is part of the stone’s wetness. The stone is not merely beneath the goat; it has accepted the goat’s weight into its lichened grammar. A salamander passing beneath a root leaves no trail, but the root seems afterward to contain the memory of a salamander.

The basin is inhabited by continuance.

Its smallest residents are perhaps closest to being individuals. The blind worms around the warm stone have pale heads, pale mouths, small repeated hungers. They feed on what the peat gives them: drowned pollen, dissolved wood, the invisible shedding of other bodies. Their lives are slow but not still. They knot and unknot among the beetles’ glassy legs. If lifted into another water they would probably die; in that black ring around the stone, they persist without apparent beginning.

The finger-long fish circle farther out. Their skins have lost most of their color, though each retains a dim seam of silver along the spine. They do not hunt. Nothing in the pool is hunted in the ordinary sense. A fish opens its mouth beside an algae filament; later the filament is shorter. A beetle rests beneath the surface, then moves one leg. A tadpole grazes at the black fuzz on a submerged leaf and remains, forever, a tadpole. Their unfinishedness is not illness. It is their mature condition.

There are amphibians that may be older than the roots they shelter beneath.

The newts have skin the color of rain-dark iron, speckled faintly with mineral blue. When one crosses a wet rock face, its belly makes a soft adhesive sound. It pauses often, but not from fear. There is nowhere in the basin from which it must flee and nowhere it needs to reach. Its pauses are part of its travel. In the weak glow, its eye looks like a bead of water that has remembered how to watch.

The frogs are wider and more difficult to account for. Mud collects on them. A thread of liverwort may begin to grow against a foreleg and remain there. Their throats do not pulse in the way a watcher expects. They breathe, but at an interval too patient to measure. Sometimes one is seen with a snail shell resting on its back, as though the snail had mistaken it for a green stone. Neither seems inconvenienced.

The salamanders are the basin’s dark quick thoughts. They appear where the eye had been certain there was nothing: between two black roots, under the white lip of a fungus, in the narrow wet crease where shale layers part. Their bodies are sleek and secretive, but not shy. They simply belong to the covered places, and the covered places open for them.

The snails may be the true gardeners.

They carry the basin upward. Wherever they go, their shells bear spores, algae, powdered bark, pollen that never needs to become seed. On an old snail’s shell, a whole miniature slope may have established itself: gray-green crust, a tuft of moss, a bead of water held in a depression. Some shells are so dark they resemble seeds; some are pale as worn teeth. They climb wet trunks with a patience that is not effort but nature. At night—which is all the time—they make thin shining roads over bark, stone, mushroom, feather, and occasionally the still eyelid of a sleeping bird.

The insects live in densities too great for counting.

Within a fallen branch, beetles work through wood in separate chambers, one species beneath another, black shells under brown, brown beneath red, red beneath pale larvae curled in damp galleries. Their labor is not destruction. The wood does not become less itself as they eat it. It becomes softer, darker, more various. It gives room to fungus; fungus gives room to mites; mites give room to smaller things whose names have never risen beyond the basin walls.

The moths are folded scraps of the mineral light. Their wings have the colors of soaked paper, lichen, old bone, submerged ash. They cling under fern fronds and along the sheltered undersides of bark. From time to time, one opens its wings. The motion is quiet enough that it resembles a change in the darkness rather than an animal moving through it.

The gnats remain above the pool in their small hovering congregation. They do not swarm, exactly. Swarming suggests urgency. These form a low, wavering constellation beneath the conifer roof. Each is held in place by some agreement with the cold air. If one drifts aside, another occupies the space it has left. Their wings make a sound so fine it may be the sound the water makes when it remembers air.

The birds are numerous but withheld.

They are not all one kind. There are small brown birds with rain-slick crowns; soft gray birds whose breasts seem to hold the basin’s low glow; black birds with narrow bills; tiny creatures round as fists, sleeping with their heads buried under one wing. In daylight elsewhere, they might have songs. Here they have only minute communications: a throat click, a feather-shake, the touch of a beak against another bird’s shoulder. They roost pressed together because the canopy has made a darkness of its own, and in that darkness their warm bodies are one of the few moving temperatures.

When they settle, the whole roof settles with them.

The owls are not above the other animals, though they are higher. They occupy hollows in the basalt where old water has enlarged a seam into a chamber. Their feathers are ash, peat, damp bark, the pale underside of mushroom caps. Their faces emerge from the wall so completely that a visitor might take them for patterns in the stone until the eyes close. They eat, perhaps. They must. But no feather pile gathers below their nests, and no small bones are found. Their hunger has been absorbed into the larger quiet.

The heron-shaped bird at the pool edge is another matter.

It is too tall for the basin and yet has always been there. Its plumage is the gray of rain seen through smoke. Its knees are submerged; its beak points toward the warm stone below the peat-dark surface. No one has watched it arrive, and no one has watched it leave. At times there seem to be two of it: one standing in the water, one standing in the black reflection beneath. At other times there is neither, only two pale reeds where the mind had expected a bird.

The goats on the ledges do not belong to a herd in the usual way. They have no young running among them, no dominant animal, no pattern of feeding or sleep. Each stands in a chosen fold of rock with damp hair hanging from its flanks and moss knitting around the bases of its horns. Their hooves have pressed shallow basins into the ledges, and the basins hold water. In those hoof-shaped pools live minute things: rotifers, larvae, green threads, a beetle no bigger than a grain of rice. Thus each goat supports another smaller basin upon it.

Their eyes are not vacant. Their attention is directed inward—or downward, perhaps, through the rock. Once in a span of time that cannot be measured by weather, one lowers its head by the width of a hand. The juniper beside it responds with a trembling that passes through every branch of the basin roof. Then all is as it was.

And beneath all these lives, or between them, lives something that cannot be seen as an animal.

It lives in the wetness shared by everything.

It is in the clear seep that emerges from shale and enters moss without falling. It is in the warmth of the stone at the pool’s center. It is in the way a dead branch is already a shelter, a bridge, a meal, and never merely dead. It is in the refusal of the water to rise or diminish. If it has a body, its body is the basin’s fullness: every occupied seam, every folded wing, every root drinking from another root’s darkness.

It has no face except, perhaps, the many faces that occasionally turn.

A frog’s lidless eye.  
A goat’s slow blink.  
An owl’s dark gaze from stone.  
The round glossy bead on a snail’s head.  
The silverless eye of the fish beneath the leaves.

None of them recognize an intruder. Recognition would imply a boundary, and the basin has no use for boundaries beyond the ones it already contains. A person at the rim is not an enemy, not prey, not guest. They are simply outside the arrangement.

For a little while, they may feel the basin’s attention as a pressure: not watching, but making no room.

Then the water continues entering the soil. The birds continue breathing together in the roof of trees. The worms turn around the warm stone. The heron lowers its beak by an amount too small to see.

And whatever lives there goes on living, not forward, not toward an end, but completely into the place it has always occupied.
