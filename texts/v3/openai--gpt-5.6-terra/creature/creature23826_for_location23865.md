What lives there is not adapted to the water.

It is made by it.

At first it resembles one of the vertical curtains: a thickening in the blue-gray pressure, a region where the falling water falls more slowly than the water around it. Then a shoulder comes through, pale and smooth as basalt worn under a river. An arm follows. It does not push against the current. The current gathers along it, folds itself around elbow and wrist, and continues on its way changed.

There are several of them, though never more than one is plainly visible.

They are tall only when the basin permits height. When the water is climbing, they lengthen into narrow white figures that extend from floor to roof, their lower portions buried in black stone, their upper portions lost in the dark water overhead. When the water falls, they become compact: bent-backed, long-handed things sheltering in the lee of a pillar or wedged beneath an overhang, with their limbs folded close as pale roots.

They have no clothes, or perhaps their clothing is too close to their bodies to distinguish. Fine dark strands stream from their heads and shoulders. In stillness the strands seem like hair. In motion they spread into branching fans, tasting the currents, fastening them briefly to one another. Their skin is marked with seams: thin gray lines around the throat, beneath the ribs, across the joints of the fingers. Along these seams, small openings pulse.

They do not breathe in the ordinary sense.

When the deep pulse rises from below, their mouths open wide—not in alarm, not to cry out, but to receive it. Water enters. The seams along their bodies flare. For a moment their insides shine a dull green-blue, as if each has swallowed a lantern far away beneath the sea.

Then they change places.

A figure crouched in the square doorway may be standing at the red plant when the light goes out. Another may appear halfway up a basalt needle with one hand pressed to the rock, though there was no path to it. They do not swim. Swimming would imply resistance, intention, travel. They seem instead to be redistributed by the basin, as gravel is redistributed, as warmth, as old images caught in two crossing sheets of water.

The long colorless creatures avoid them.

Or perhaps they are younger forms of them.

Sometimes one of the pale inhabitants holds a long body against its chest while water roars through both of them. The creature’s underside opens and closes. The figure’s own seams answer. They remain like that through one pulse, then another. Later the long body slips away through a curtain of water, and the figure is gone. In the place where it stood, there may be a few black clinging shapes newly gathered on the stone.

Those black shapes are not snails.

They are small, folded versions of a posture.

If watched closely during the silences, each has a minute pale groove where a face might be. They cling to the rock until the basin calls them. Then all at once they release, rise through the confusion, and disappear into the dark roof-water. Some return unchanged. Some return slightly larger. Some return with a thin red thread growing from them, and root themselves in cracks near the center of the basin.

The red shrub is one of these, or their dead, or their memory of a body that could not keep moving.

The inhabitants tend it.

This is difficult to see because tending, here, is not touching. A pale figure will stand near the shrub for a time, facing away from it, one hand lifted into the current. The water about the plant clears. Its blunt translucent leaves spread wide. In that interval, the whole basin gains a sharpness that is almost painful: basalt planes, mineral veins, the white root in the doorway, every grain of black paste in its pocket of soil.

And in the clarity, there are more figures than there should be.

They stand on ledges where no one has climbed. They look from the upper ridges. One is seated within the square opening behind the pillars, despite the opening ending in solid stone. One kneels in the chain of uneven bowls, passing water from one bowl to the next with cupped hands, though the water is already moving through them. One stands on the central island while it is submerged, feet planted in the hollow of still water.

It is never clear whether these are inhabitants of the basin, or views of the inhabitants from earlier arrangements of the basin, or possibilities held briefly in the water before the water decides what has happened.

They have made the ruins, perhaps.

The pillars are their resting marks. The doorway is an abandoned way of entering a different pressure. The stone bowls are for dividing currents into portions small enough to listen to. In the deepest pauses, when the roar thins and the impossible tiny sounds emerge, one can hear the bowls singing to one another: not bells exactly, but the faint wet note of stone under a hand.

The pale figures listen.

They place their palms against the basalt. They lean their featureless faces toward cracks. They gather near the place where the white root is widening the wall. Their attention is immense and slow. It has the quality of waiting beside an egg, though what is being waited for is not inside the stone.

It is on the other side of it.

Occasionally, when the central island rises and its still hollow reflects the dry sky, one of them comes onto the black crown with it.

The figure does not emerge dripping. Water draws away from its body in long reluctant threads, as though it has forgotten it is allowed to let the figure go. For those few seconds the inhabitant becomes more nearly human than elsewhere: narrow face, closed eyes, ribs moving once beneath pale skin. Its feet stand in the steaming basalt beside the hollow.

In the reflection, birds cross overhead.

The figure watches them.

Not with hunger. Not even with grief.

With recognition so old that it has become a kind of instruction.

Sometimes it raises one hand toward the reflected grass and sun. Sometimes its fingers move, shaping a gesture no person standing in air would understand. The reflection may answer: a bird turns; the grass bends against a wind that does not exist in the basin; a distant shape at the edge of the reflected sky pauses and seems to look down.

Then the deep pulse comes.

The island sinks. The dry world breaks apart into rings. The pale inhabitant is seized by warm water from every side. Its body loosens—not dying, not drowning, but becoming less distinct. Shoulder becomes current. Hand becomes a pale turn in the blue. The dark strands spread, divide, and vanish among the vertical sheets.

Afterward there is no one there.

But for several seconds, through the falling water, a silhouette remains standing on the submerged island: upright, patient, with one arm still raised toward a sun that has never shone in that place.
