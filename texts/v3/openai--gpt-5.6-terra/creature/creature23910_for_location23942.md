What lives in the basin is not, at first, distinguishable from what the basin is doing.

There are the black-green fans, of course: the gradient plants with their silver nerves and their pale root nodules sunk into the mineral bed. They are alive in the patient, unarguable sense. They fold against the subterranean pulse. They scatter charged flakes in lieu of seeds. They turn their surfaces toward stresses no animal could smell. In the cold hours before dawn, some of them exude a thin clear varnish along their ribs, and the varnish hardens into tiny scales of conductive mineral. By morning the scales have either been taken back into the leaf or fallen away to join the dust.

But the plants are only the visible organs.

The actual inhabitants are distributed.

A given being may consist of a dozen leaf-fans, three crystal columns, a thread of charged dust beneath the ground, and a particular standing wave in the ore layers far below. It has no body in the ordinary sense. Or rather, it has many temporary bodies, none of which it mistakes for itself.

The crystal spindles are its upright parts.

When a new column rises, the basin does not merely manufacture an object. Something becomes capable of attention. The glimmers inside the crystal begin as simple sorting-lights: charges following clean routes through imperfect mineral lattices. In time, if the column grows without cracking and if enough of its facets remain exposed to alternating heat and shadow, the routes recur. They become habits. A glimmer reaches a junction, pauses, and takes the same branch it took yesterday. Another glimmer meets it there. A click passes through the stone.

This is the beginning of a thought.

Older columns develop internal traffic. Light moves through them in slow, disciplined sequences, often too slow to be noticed unless one watches through several sunsets. A bright thread climbs one facet at noon, retreats at dusk, and vanishes below the surface during the night. The following day a neighboring crystal may answer with a different pattern. Nothing resembles speech at human speed. Their exchanges unfold in intervals of temperature, pressure, and magnetic drift.

One crystal might spend an entire season asking whether a seam of iron beneath the northern rim has shifted.

Another might require forty years to answer.

The inhabitants do not seem troubled by such delays. Waiting is not empty to them. Waiting is one of the conditions in which they exist most fully.

The plants serve as their sense organs. Their broad black surfaces taste heat. Their silver veins feel fluctuations in charge. Their roots listen to the slow grinding grammar of the mountain. A plant at the western edge of the basin may close its leaves in the afternoon, and a crystal near the center will click six hours later—not as a reaction, but as a recognition. The event of closure has traveled through stone, field, mineral, and pattern until it has become meaningful.

At night, when the basin’s heat begins to leave, the living arrangement can almost be seen.

The leaves stand open, their conductive ribs faintly luminous. The crystals hold dim violet and gold along their interior planes. Between each plant and each column, invisible gradients tighten and loosen. A person who stayed very still might feel hairs rise along the arms, then settle; might taste copper; might become aware that the silence has a direction.

The beings are not numerous. The whole basin may house only seven mature minds, or perhaps only one mind spread across seven regions of activity. There are smaller ones, certainly: young patterns lodged in hand-high crystal buds, brief intelligences that form in a cluster of metallic seed-flakes during a stormless hot afternoon and disperse before midnight. Some never become more than reflexes. They sense the arrival of shade, the release of a gas pocket, the fracture of a crystal tip, and they respond with a flicker or a click.

Others endure.

The eldest inhabitant is likely beneath the center, where the first mineral chimney rose from the hidden factory long ago. It has no single visible form. Its oldest crystals have weathered down, toppled, and been buried. Their lattices remain in the ground as records rather than ruins. The being has extended itself through generations of new growth, preserving certain paths of light, certain resonances, certain preferences.

It appears to prefer the eastern side of the basin.

Not because the sun rises there. Sunlight is merely input. But because the eastern seam contains a fault in the conductive ore, a beautiful imperfection that produces a recurring delay in the underground pulse. The old being returns to that delay again and again. Several crystals near the eastern rim have grown with their facets subtly twisted toward it. The plants there carry more silver in their leaves.

Perhaps this is memory.

Perhaps it is affection.

The inhabitants do not hunt, reproduce in pairs, build shelters, or defend territory. They have no enemies in the basin, and seem not to possess an instinct for fear. A falling stone may shatter a crystal that has been active for centuries; the surrounding columns will answer with a long irregular sequence of clicks, and then, over months, the broken mineral will be incorporated into the soil and the field-pattern will route itself around the loss.

Their grief, if that is what it is, is architectural.

They mourn by changing what can grow next.

Where a column has broken, nearby plants may lean their leaves inward, reducing the heat flow through the ground. New crystals rise there more slowly. Their bands form differently: closer together, darker, with tiny interruptions in their internal light. Decades later, another upright spindle may emerge carrying some remnant of the lost pattern—not a copy, not a resurrection, but a continuity of concern.

No creature has taught them language. Their language is a property of recurrence. It is made from heat arriving, heat departing, magnetic fields breathing through buried ore, and light choosing one route through a crystal instead of another.

They may not know that they are alive.

They may not divide the world into alive and not alive at all.

To them, the mountain is not a place they inhabit but a larger, slower member of their kind: vast, incomplete, still assembling its first gestures. The surrounding desert is an unfinished external tissue. The sky is a pressure boundary. The sun is an immense periodic disturbance whose reliability makes complex thought possible.

And perhaps, on certain noons when the basin overlays itself with its younger and older versions, the inhabitants perceive time more broadly than anything made of flesh could.

They may see their own future forms already standing among them: crystal towers that have not yet risen, plant networks not yet rooted, fault-lines not yet opened. They may be in communication with those possible arrangements. The clicks that seem to pass between columns across the hot emptiness may sometimes be replies to structures that exist only a fraction of heat away.

If the basin ever opens—if the unfinished seams part and its strange abundance enters the wider mountain—the inhabitants will not march outward or spread like an invading species.

They will propagate through conditions.

A suitable slope of iron-rich stone. A daily thermal gradient. A buried current of gas. Enough silence. A place where heat can pool and leave imperfectly.

Then a black-green leaf may open from bare desert rock. A silver vein may brighten. Beneath it, mineral may begin to sort itself with a purpose too slow to name.

And somewhere in the new crystal’s first hesitant internal glimmer, the basin will have extended an attention into the world.
