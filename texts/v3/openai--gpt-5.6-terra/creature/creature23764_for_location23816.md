What lives in the city is not one population but several overlapping accommodations.

First, there are the people who call themselves citizens, though citizenship here is less a matter of birth than of having learned the local adjustments: how to sleep through a settling wall, how to tell harmless fog from the kind that leaves grit on the teeth, how long to wait after the bells before crossing a bridge. They are miners, machinists, surveyors, tram conductors, gardeners, water-readers, cooks, teachers, inspectors of cracks, inspectors of inspectors, and old men whose sole recognized qualification is that they remember where a staircase used to go.

They tend to be practical in the way sailors are practical: superstitious only after exhaustive experience. A woman may calibrate a laser level to the thickness of a hair, then touch the doorframe three times before entering a newly opened tunnel. A structural engineer may dismiss ghosts while carrying a small bottle of milk for the thing that knocks inside the eastern pumping station. No contradiction is felt.

The city produces a particular kind of person: patient, strong-handed, good at listening. Its inhabitants pause in conversation when a cup rattles on a table. They know whether the tremor comes from the mill, the freight line, a nearby foundation correcting itself, or something deeper deciding it has not finished. Children learn these distinctions early. They are taught the official alarm tones, the evacuation routes, the safe wall colors, and the names of the streets that no longer exist.

Many families live in apartments altered beyond their original plans. A grandmother’s kitchen may lean into what was once a neighbor’s bedroom; a corridor may have become a greenhouse after a wall shifted and a window was swallowed by the building next door. People make use of these changes. They bolt shelves at angles. They grow beans in damp alcoves. They hang hammocks where floors have become unreliable. A household’s wealth is partly measured by its stock of shims, brackets, weatherproof cloth, spare tubing, and good rope.

There are newcomers as well: laborers from the low forests, engineers sent by distant ministries, traders who arrive with salt and medicine, scholars who want to study the moving reservoir, fugitives who find that the city asks fewer questions than the roads below. Some come intending to stay one season and remain for thirty years, held by work, by marriage, by debt, by the strange difficulty of descending once one has learned to live above the weather.

Then there are the municipal goats.

They are officially livestock, unofficially a public institution, and in practice something closer to a distributed intelligence with hooves. They know routes no survey map records. In bad weather, people watch which direction the goats face. If they leave a retaining wall all at once, nearby residents begin packing without waiting for an alarm. Their copper tags are stamped with district numbers, but no one has ever successfully assigned ownership. Every few years the department of slope management attempts a census. Every few years the goats return more numerous than before.

Foxes live among the freight yards, sleek and narrow as knives. Their silvered fur is not entirely runoff, though the city prefers that explanation. They steal gloves, electrical insulation, coins, pieces of bread, and sometimes objects that have no obvious value: a child’s drawing, an unmailed letter, a single false tooth. They leave these things in orderly piles beneath transformer boxes. Railway workers say the foxes are building a city of their own, only flatter.

The tunnel moths are another citizenry, if one is willing to use the word broadly. They arrive in thick pale clouds around lamps and emergency markers, coating signal glass, vent screens, and the faces of workers who stand too long beneath them. Their wings carry fine mineral dust from places people have not yet excavated. Survey crews sometimes collect them and read the powder caught in their bodies as a kind of premature map.

In the water system live creatures that are not discussed at meetings unless one of them has blocked a pipe.

The eels are blind, translucent, and longer than any eel should be. They move through the reservoir’s changing channels and appear in cisterns where there ought to be no connection. Some are marked with old scars that resemble letters. Children dare one another to touch them in flooded service passages. Adults do not, though they sometimes leave scraps of fish near drain mouths during droughts.

There are also the wall-thirsty things.

No one agrees on their shape, because no one sensible waits to see one fully. They inhabit old masonry, insulation cavities, abandoned lift shafts, and the spaces left when a building shifts away from its foundations. They make small noises: a wet breathing, a scrape of nails, a faint knocking in response to household sounds. They are not considered dangerous if given water and not addressed directly. Residents leave shallow bowls outside their doors, especially in buildings that have begun to lean. The bowls are often empty by morning. Sometimes they are moved several feet. Once in a while, something small and smooth is found at the bottom: a pebble, a molted tooth, a brass button from a uniform no one recognizes.

Above the city circle the great carrion birds, condors in outline and appetite but not necessarily in origin. They nest on shelves that did not exist the previous year. Their nests contain bones from animals that have never been seen in the basin, lengths of cable, pieces of mirror, and occasionally human tools lost during storms. They are watched with unease, but also with respect. A condor descending through fog is often the first warning that something large has died, fallen, or emerged in the upper country.

And sometimes, after particular weather, the antlered ones arrive.

They stand on tramlines, in quarry fields, at the mouths of tunnels. They have the posture of deer but too many joints in their legs, antlers that branch like roots, and eyes with the reflective depth of standing water. They do not eat. They do not startle. They wait, always facing uphill, as if listening for a call that comes through the stone. Nobody drives them away. Municipal crews place lanterns around them and suspend service. By morning they are usually gone, leaving no tracks except for long wet impressions in the gravel that lead toward slopes too steep for anything to climb.

The city’s dead are not exactly absent either.

Most are buried in the lower ground, where the soil is thought to be more settled, though the graves sometimes rise or shift sideways with the seasons. Families visit them with flowers weighted by stones. Some dead remain in the city in less formal ways: in recorded voices on obsolete tram announcements, in handwritten repair notes tucked behind panels, in the names given to cracks, bridges, and dangerous stairwells. There are streets named for people who vanished during surveys and returned only as local directions: take the Maren Cut, go down past Old Jovic’s Corner, do not use Hesh’s Lift after rain.

What truly lives there, perhaps, is a shared habit of negotiation.

The people negotiate with the mountain. The mountain negotiates with water. Water negotiates with buried channels and sleeping pressures. Moss negotiates with brick. Goats negotiate with gravity. The things in the walls negotiate with thirst. Even the factories are built to yield rather than win.

No creature in the city believes stability is permanent. The successful ones are those that can loosen their grip, move a little, brace themselves, and remain.
