What lives in the basin is not one population but several, layered together like the strata exposed in its pale walls.

There are the workers, first of all: kiln-men and yard hands, mechanics, drivers, fitters, clerks with damp cuffs, women who sort invoices in the office above the weighbridge, old men who know the sound of a failing bearing before the machine admits it. They have the look of people accustomed to carrying weight without making much ceremony of it. Their boots are always crusted with some local substance—clay, lime dust, coal grit, mud—and their houses have sheds full of things too useful or too potentially useful to throw away.

They live by schedules that have outlasted the people who wrote them. Shift bells, delivery days, firing cycles, the Thursday siren. They know which gutters overflow first, where the roof leaks over the tile press, which of the yard dogs will bite a stranger and which only barks because barking is its job. They have opinions about the stream, the railway, the quality of the aggregate, and the willow by the settling ponds. These opinions are often inherited.

Some of them came because there was work. Some arrived because there was nowhere else they could afford. Some were born in the workers’ houses and have never quite left, though they may have gone away for years and returned with different coats, different partners, different disappointments. The town is small enough that departure is always noticed and return is treated as if it had been expected.

There are children, too, though never as many as the town seems built to contain. They live in the margins adults have declared inconvenient or dangerous: behind the boarded shops, beneath the rail gantry, in the weed lots between foundations. Their pockets hold fired-clay fragments, rusty washers, snail shells, lengths of copper wire, smooth black stones from the ditch. They know the red rail gate can be climbed without touching the sharp part. They know which pond has carp, which has leeches, which shed contains swallows, and where fox cubs sometimes appear in spring.

They grow up with industrial noises so constant that silence feels suspicious. A press striking in the distance is as ordinary to them as a clock. The smell of wet brick is weather. The siren is not alarming unless it sounds at the wrong time.

The animals live close to the work because work leaves cover behind it.

Foxes make dens under warped platforms where the ground stays dry. They move along the rail embankment at dawn, red shapes between puddles, nosing at discarded lunches and rats from the grain store. In winter they sleep curled among insulation scraps and old hessian sacks. Their cubs play in the nettles behind the crushing plant, where no one can see them unless they stand still.

Rats live everywhere the town has tried unsuccessfully to seal. They occupy drains, boiler-room voids, stacked pallets, embankments, the dark hollows under sheds. They are sleek, practical creatures, nourished by spillages and neglect. They know the works’ rhythm better than anyone: when the canteen bins are emptied, when the night shift leaves, when the floodwater drives insects from the culverts.

Feral chickens roam with an air of municipal authority. They scratch at coal dust, investigate open doors, and perch on warm pipes. No one remembers who first kept them or when they ceased to belong to anyone. They lay small eggs beneath sheet metal and in the old tyre pile. People complain about them but leave grain out in cold weather.

The settling ponds hold an older kind of life, though the ponds themselves are young. Newts drift among drowned stems. Water beetles crease the surface. Carp feed in the cloudy depths, large enough now to seem improbable, their flanks flashing when rain strikes hard. Frogs gather in the reeds until their calling fills the basin with a sound like machinery that has learned to breathe. Reed warblers nest where the wire fence sags, singing with fierce confidence above water not entirely safe for anything.

In the banks around the ponds, rabbits multiply. Their warrens undermine warning posts and cause small collapses after heavy rain. At dusk they emerge in cautious groups, each one pausing with its ears raised, as if listening for the next geological event. They feed on grass growing from spoil heaps, on clover by the sidings, on the tender green things that insist on appearing wherever stone has been broken open.

Above them are rooks, crows, swallows, and the occasional kestrel.

The rooks claim the gantry and the crane as if these structures were ancient black trees. They drop shells, stones, and scraps of wire from their perches. Their arguments travel across the whole town. Crows patrol the raw slopes, picking through gravel for beetles and worms exposed by rain. Swallows make the engine house their summer cathedral. They pass through its broken windows at full speed, turn beneath the rafters, and vanish again into white daylight, indifferent to presses, steam, shouting, or men with raised hands.

At night, bats come down from the basin’s wooded rim and hunt along the watercourse. They skim over the square, cross the rail yard, and pass through the orange haze around the kiln windows. Moths gather at the lamps in dense, uncertain clouds. Spiders build webs between handrails and warning signs, rebuilding them each time the rain tears them loose.

There are also things that live in the town less visibly.

Mold lives in the back rooms of closed shops, in the curled paper of old ledgers, beneath wallpaper in houses where the heating has failed. Moss lives in every crack it can find and has begun to make the paving look older than it is. Lichens occupy the concrete walls, taking their time. Roots probe beneath foundations, beneath drains, beneath the railway sleepers. They do not distinguish between abandoned ground and occupied ground. They merely enter.

The stream lives there, too, in its own way. It is not large enough to command respect and therefore receives a great deal of it indirectly. Everything must negotiate with it: roads, culverts, gardens, sheds, fences, the foundations of the tile works. It changes colour with the weather. It takes whatever is carelessly left within reach. It feeds the willow, the alder, the ponds, the reeds, the insects, and finally the wet green basin itself.

And perhaps there are residents who belong to no official count: people in the upstairs rooms above shuttered shops; someone living in the old signal cabin with curtains drawn; a widow who keeps pigeons in a shed behind the square; a retired fitter who walks the yard every morning though he has not worked there in twelve years; young couples who rent cheap rooms for a season and leave behind a chair, a bicycle, a cracked mug in a windowsill.

The town keeps them all without much warmth and without much cruelty. It gives shelter where it can. It makes room in empty buildings, under platforms, in drainage channels, between stacks of pipe. It allows lives to continue in its pauses.

What truly lives there is persistence: feathered, furred, rooted, rusting, human, fungal, mechanical. Nothing in the basin is entirely settled, yet nothing seems inclined to leave.
