What lives in the constructed valley is not a single species, nor even a clean division between organism and machine.

First there are the things that were meant to live there.

The white cabinet insects are called **threadmoths** by the crews, though they neither fly nor seem to possess wings. They are little folded pale bodies, triangular when resting, with a fine arrangement of jointed feelers beneath them. In an open electrical cabinet they form in the corners like dust caught by static. If a technician puts a gloved hand near them, they withdraw one by one into the cable bundles, flattening themselves until they are no thicker than labels.

They feed, perhaps, on warmth. Or vibration. Or the microscopic bloom of conductive salts that grows around old contacts. They are most numerous in boxes that should have failed but have not. A few electricians leave them alone for that reason. Others crush them with insulated pliers. The crushed ones smell sharply of hot pennies.

Their pale bodies make a faint clicking when a cabinet is heavily loaded. Old workers say the clicking is how the insects pass current among themselves. Younger workers say it is only their shells expanding and contracting in the heat. Neither group opens the cabinet when the sound begins to organize itself into a rhythm.

Along the warm channels live the **brush colonies**.

From a distance they are black vegetation, thick as drowned fur, combed in one direction by the exhaust drafts. Close up, each brush is a mass of fine jointed filaments anchored around a piece of metal: a washer, a scrap of mesh, the grille of a long-discarded filter. The filaments are hollow. They carry water, heat, and whatever slow chemical exchanges let the colony grow. Their tips divide into tiny hooked forks that catch silt and corrosion dust. Over time, a brush colony builds itself a little bank.

A channel that has been left unattended for six months will change course around one.

The colonies seem to prefer places where water alternates between hot and cold. They retract during chemical flushes. They darken when a pump begins to cavitate. Some workers claim that a patch of brush will lean toward a leak before the leak becomes visible. The official position is that they respond to airflow.

Nobody disputes that the pale growth on the drainage markers is related to them, though it looks nothing alike.

The marker growth is a chalky crust, soft as skin when fresh, hard as porcelain when old. It gathers on painted arrows and depth measurements, leaving the numbers visible while swallowing the paint around them. It spreads most vigorously after floods. If scraped away, it returns within days, beginning as a cold damp bloom beneath the remaining flecks of paint.

There is a story that the drainage markers once led down to an older basin, a deeper one, before the collapse and the sealing. The pale crust, according to that story, is not growing on the markers. It is growing *through* them, using the signs as the last narrow openings left to whatever is below.

This is why the old workers will not let it be removed.

That, and because channels with intact pale growth rarely overflow.

The rat-shaped things are called **runners**, though almost nobody has seen one run.

They appear at the edge of vision on pipe racks and cable trays: a pale back, an angular haunch, a narrow head lowered as if drinking from a seam. They are usually the size of a small dog, but accounts differ wildly. One crewman insisted he had found a runner no longer than his hand sleeping inside a valve housing. Another swore that something the length of a freight cart had moved through the fog along the far marsh, its back plated in overlapping sheets of white mineral.

The smaller ones have been caught. At least, objects resembling them have been caught.

They have a soft outer surface, almost waxy, over a framework of wire, cartilage, and something like braided root. Their mouths contain little rotating plates, suitable for shaving oxidation off pipework. They have no eyes in the familiar sense. Their heads are crowded instead with black pits that open and close in time with nearby machinery.

A runner kept in a sealed maintenance locker does not die quickly. It becomes still. Its surface loses color. Its joints slowly arrange themselves into a shape resembling bundled cabling.

No one keeps them anymore.

Farther down, in the channels where the water is black and too cold to touch, are the **linefish**.

They move with a particular sideways hesitation, as if each one is listening for instructions. Some are unmistakably animal: thin bodies, dark fins, mouths that open against the current. Some have wires trailing from their flanks, or faint indicator lights buried beneath translucent skin. Some are square in outline, or segmented, or fitted with corroded sensor eyes that still rotate beneath algae.

The basin’s management records say autonomous inspection probes were introduced into the recovery water decades ago. The probes were designed to map obstructions, test acidity, and report heat gradients. Their batteries failed. Their housings cracked. Their navigation routines degraded.

The linefish do still map obstructions. They gather around a blocked grate days before the water visibly backs up. They avoid channels that will flood with steam. When a pump station begins its long failing scream, the fish leave the surrounding water all at once, sliding downstream in a black shining school.

Sometimes they collect beneath an inspection cart as it crosses a causeway.

They turn in the cart’s headlamps. They wait.

There are larger things in the water, but those are not discussed in shift briefings.

The human inhabitants of the valley are a scattered people: drainage crews, pump tenders, welders, lift operators, surveyors, unlicensed salvagers, and those who have found reasons not to go back above. They do not live in a village, exactly. They live in intervals between systems.

A person may sleep in the dry upper level of a pump house for three weeks, then move because the wall begins to sweat rust-colored water. A cook may establish a stove beneath a conveyor bridge, serving broth from reclaimed heat and packets of cultivated fungus, until a new cable run makes the place unsafe. A mechanic may occupy an abandoned signal shelter so long that her tools become part of its interior: wrenches hanging from ceiling hooks, boots placed beneath a dead control panel, a nest of blankets around the old relay cabinet.

Most keep no permanent address. They identify themselves by routes.

“I work the east drains.”

“I sleep under Lift Four when it’s down.”

“I’m from the hot side of the old spillway.”

The children of the valley, where there are children, learn the geography before they learn the city above. They know which frost will hold their weight and which only covers open grating. They know the sound of an approaching cart by the pitch of its wheel bearings. They can distinguish harmless vapor from the kind that burns the throat. They play a game called Siren, in which one child calls once, twice, or three times, and the others must decide whether to keep moving, climb, or cling to something fixed.

The game is forbidden in the shelters. It is played anyway.

There are also the **quiet crews**.

No roster lists them. They wear old rain capes with the reflective stripes cut away or painted black. Their lamps are hooded. They work after formal shifts end, moving along the marsh margins with hooked poles and shallow sleds. They collect drowned tools, dropped components, copper filament from the fronds, and things that come loose from the lower channels after heavy pumping.

The quiet crews sell useful scrap. They also sell less useful things: lengths of cable that remain warm without a power source; mineral plates taken from runner nests; black brush sealed in jars; linefish that have stopped moving but still emit a slow regular pulse.

Most respectable workers insist the quiet crews are dangerous thieves.

Most respectable workers know at least one of them by name.

At the deepest practical level of the valley, where the shelves flatten and the channels disappear into grated dark, lives something that has no accepted name.

It may be the basin’s accumulated intelligence: every sensor loop that was never fully disconnected, every emergency control system patched around a newer one, every failed machine learning routine left awake in a sealed cabinet. It may be a colony, distributed among the cable moss, the pale marker crust, the linefish, and the unseen roots of the brush. It may simply be the machinery responding in ways the machinery was built to respond, though not in combinations anyone anticipated.

It does not speak.

It changes pressure.

A worker alone in the lower shelves may notice a pump engaging somewhere it should not. A jammed gate opens just before the water reaches a shelter. A lamp flickers three times over a route that has become unsafe. A dead intercom produces a breath of static when someone is about to step beneath a pipe.

These events are not reliable enough to be called protection.

They are not random enough to be ignored.

The old workers say the valley notices labor. It does not care for people as people care for one another. It does not distinguish gratitude from use. But it recognizes weight, heat, repetition, repair. If someone cleans a drain for years, the basin learns the rhythm of their boots. If someone keeps a failing pump alive through winter, the lights near that pump will sometimes come on before they arrive.

And if someone tries to take too much from it—if they strip a marker bare, poison a channel, cut an old cable merely because it has copper in it—the valley becomes awkward around them.

Doors seize.

Lifts stop between levels.

Fog thickens.

Reflections appear where there is no water.

Beyond the active shelves, among the new foundations, another population is beginning.

There are weld-spiders no larger than hands, officially mobile seamers, which skitter over bare frames and leave cooling beads of metal behind them. They were designed to operate in groups, but some have lost contact with their charging racks. These continue working alone. They patch joints no one assigned them. They weld scraps into corners. They construct tiny bright lattices in the sheltered places beneath stair treads.

There are cable-fronds in the unfinished walls already, reaching toward the fresh electrical fields.

There is frost in the upper beams, laying down its pale geometry.

There is water gathering patiently in the floors, accepting soot, metal, dust, and the first drifting bodies of insects.

The new district will be inhabited before its doors are installed. It will acquire its own routes, its own warning sounds, its own things that count as animals. Its mud will begin as runoff and refuse, then become ground. Its workers will name it badly at first, using whatever designation appears on a temporary sign. Later they will call it something else.

And below it all, the constructed valley will continue to make room.

Not kindly.

Not cruelly.

Like a machine that has been running long enough to develop weather, habits, hungers, and a dark interior life of its own.
