What lives there is not, at first, a people.

It is a habit.

It is the habit of putting one thing beneath another before the first thing has finished existing. The habit of catching water before it falls. The habit of bracing a crack with a splinter, then bracing the splinter. The habit of making a cup for the overflow from a cup. This habit has roots deeper than the roots of the plants, and it moves through the room with more certainty than any animal could.

The room is full of hands, though hands are seldom seen.

They are present in thumbprints that remain soft in fresh clay. In the five shallow dents pressed into the rim of a water jar. In the smooth places on ladder rungs, where something has climbed again and again. In the little tied knots that appear overnight in a length of reed. Sometimes a hand emerges from the dark gap behind a stacked wall: brown, gray, greened by wetness, wrist wrapped in old fiber. It tests the air. It cups a drip. It holds a new nail against a piece of wood until the wood accepts it.

Then the hand is gone, though the nail remains.

There may be builders inside the constructions. Not behind them, not beneath them exactly, but threaded through their making. A person-shaped presence can be glimpsed in the brief pallor of lightning: a shoulder between two baskets, a cheek against a plaster wall, knees folded in the impossible hollow beneath a stair. They are too narrow for their apparent bodies. Their backs seem to continue into walls. Their hair, if it is hair, hangs in cords and roots and black wet strips of bark.

They do not seem imprisoned.

They seem occupied.

Each has a task so small that it has become endless. One tends a gutter no longer than a finger, guiding its water with a sliver of leaf. One sits with a clay brick in their lap, pressing its corners gently inward as though teaching it the shape it will need. One listens at a wall with both palms spread against it, waiting for the wet interior to tell them where the next support must go. One has been tying the same knot for a very long time. It grows more intricate and more secure, while the original cord softens to pulp in their fingers.

Their faces are difficult because the room keeps lending them features.

A carved guardian may look away when one of them looks up. A square mouth in a wall may fill with breath. Closed wooden eyes may shine with reflected lampfire, then blink—not quickly, but with the heavy, deliberate motion of shutters closing against rain. Some of the builders have faces of mud, continually remade by dripping water. Some have faces covered with hammered copper leaves. Some turn and show only a flat plane of pale plaster, marked by a single wet thumbprint.

They make no language that can be heard over the chamber.

Their instructions travel by tapping: three taps on a wedge, two on a jar, a long scrape along the underside of a beam. A rope answers with a creak. A shelf shifts. Somewhere, a hidden worker begins kneading clay. When thunder comes, they pause—not in fear, but in attention. The great metallic bellow moves through the room and all its little vessels answer. In that ringing, perhaps, the builders receive the larger plan.

Or perhaps thunder is one of them.

There are also the small inhabitants, the ones no visitor would think to name.

The baskets breathe in damp weather. Their woven mouths loosen, opening darkly; their bellies fill with warm vapor and seeds and broken pegs. In drier moments they cinch themselves tight, guarding whatever they have gathered. Some baskets have handles like bent backs. Some shuffle a fraction of an inch toward leaks.

The ladders are watchful. Their feet seek level ground, though there is no level ground in the room. At night they settle with tiny wooden complaints, placing themselves more firmly among the crowded layers. When a new wall grows where a ladder needs to pass, it does not resist. By morning it has shortened itself, or acquired a bend, or grown a second set of rungs on its underside.

The tiles have a slow, overlapping intelligence. At the approach of a storm, they press closer together. Their edges lift and resettle. Water is led away from fragile places, toward basins, roots, thirsty mortar. A tile cracked by cold does not die; within the crack, a pale seam of fresh clay swells, and by evening the break has become a channel with a purpose.

The jars are perhaps the oldest creatures there.

They stand mouth-to-base in the walls, warm with stored water. Their bellies bear the ghosts of every hand that has turned them. Some hum faintly when full. Some sweat around their rims. A few have begun to grow small handles where no handles were intended, delicate loops of clay reaching outward to touch neighboring vessels. When one breaks, its pieces are not discarded. They travel. A shard becomes a roof edge, a drain lip, a tiny flashing of red beneath a windowless seam. Nothing in the room loses its membership.

Even the plants are not merely plants.

The ferns keep accounts of moisture in the curled arithmetic of their fronds. The vines are scouts. They enter places the builders cannot reach: the warm cavities behind old bricks, the black centers of wicker bundles, the narrow breathing gaps between a wall and the wall that is forming behind it. When a vine finds space, it reports by growing. When it finds danger, it thickens. Its roots bind loose matter together, and the builders, if there are builders, accept the advice without question.

The tall folded leaf at the center of the room is a resident of special gravity. It is too large, too green, too insistently alive. Its creases hold pools of water where pale insects breed and drown and breed again. It pushes against the ceiling but does not break it. Instead, roof beams curve around it, tiles part, ropes reroute themselves. The leaf has become an axis. All the crowded structures recognize it and make room by becoming more crowded elsewhere.

Within its folds, something sleeps.

Not an animal in the ordinary sense. Sometimes the leaf opens a little after thunder, and a breath moves through the chamber, warmer than the water, smelling of wet stone and new-fired clay. Lamps bow their flames. Moss flattens. The person-shaped shadows stop working for the duration of the breath.

Then the leaf closes again.

The invisible sleeper may be the room’s first builder. It may be the mountain’s buried heart, brought near the surface and given leaves to breathe through. It may be only a concentration of heat, pressure, and patient making. But every new construction seems to lean, however slightly, toward it.

There are children, perhaps.

Not small people exactly, though small people are sometimes heard: a brief cough behind a mat, laughter like water in a narrow pipe, bare feet pattering on the roof where no roof can bear them. They may be the newest parts of the room learning to move. A newly extruded peg rolls off a shelf and stops upright. An unfired brick wriggles into a gap, pleased with itself. A little shutter opens and closes too often, testing the idea of weather.

These young things are curious about visitors.

They steal warmth from gloves. They loosen bootlaces and retie them in better knots. They place a fresh clay step beneath a tired foot. They may offer water in a cup that was not present a moment before. The water is always warm. It tastes of copper leaves, smoke, stone, and something sweetly earthen, like rain falling into a kiln.

But they do not want anyone to stay.

Not because the room is hostile. It is too occupied with care for hostility. Rather, a body that remains begins to acquire a task. First it notices the leaks. Then it begins to see which shelf needs shoring. It becomes aware of the intolerable looseness of a single cord. Its fingers ache to press a thumb into wet clay. Its breath becomes useful to the warm fog. Soon the room recognizes it as a possible wall, a possible brace, a possible pair of hands.

The builders do not command this. They may not even notice it.

They are already halfway transformed themselves. Their elbows have the grain of old wood. Their ankles are ringed with clay. Their ribs make faint ceramic sounds when they inhale. One shadow crouched beneath the central leaf has a small tiled roof growing across its shoulders. Another, reaching into a wall, has fingertips that divide into roots. They are not dying. They are being included.

Perhaps this is what lives at the top of the world: makers who have forgotten the distinction between shelter and body.

They keep making because the sky is cracked and rain enters. They keep making because the mountain is cold and the chamber is warm. They keep making because every finished edge invites another edge beside it. They have not learned how to stop at a wall, or a roof, or a self.

And the room, which may be their child, their ancestor, their god, or their unfinished thought, keeps making them in return.
