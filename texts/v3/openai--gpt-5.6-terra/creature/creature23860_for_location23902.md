They are people of the basin, though they do not think of themselves as belonging to a rare or isolated kind. To them, a city without birds, insects, herd animals, or dogs is simply a city. A field that does not twitch with hidden life is a field. A pond without frogs is still a pond. They have words for rain at different temperatures, for the strength of springwater, for the feel of ash under a heel, and very few words for creatures beyond themselves.

They are not numerous. The city has enough people to keep its drains clear, its gardens tended, its kilns warm, its roofs repaired, and its knowledge from thinning out. It is large enough that no one knows every life in it, but small enough that every household is known to several others by obligation: who can replace a cracked water jar, who has the best dry peat after rain, who knows the old channel lines beneath the eastern houses, whose hands are steady enough to set a broken bone or polish a blade edge.

Their bodies are shaped by walking on wet stone and carrying what no animal carries for them. Their calves are strong, their shoulders broad without grandeur, their palms lined with old cuts from reeds, basalt, fiber, and clay. Children learn balance early. They walk the narrow edges of garden beds, leap the drainage cuts, and carry small jars of water before they are trusted with fire. Older people often move carefully but remain useful for a long time: sitting near workshop doors, sorting seed grain, untangling wet fiber, telling younger hands where a buried spring will rise if a wall is placed badly.

Their clothing is light but layered against dampness. They weave coarse grasses with softer plant fibers from the warmer garden crops, making tunics, wrapped skirts, sleeveless overgarments, belts, leggings for work in wet ground, and broad hooded mantles for morning mist. The colors are mostly those the land permits: clay red, ash gray, leaf green gone dull with washing, the pale yellow of dried reeds, black-brown from mineral mud. Bright dye exists, but only in narrow bands at cuffs, hems, and household cords. A person may be recognized at a distance by the particular pattern of knots at their belt or the colored thread braided into their hair.

They wear little metal because there is little metal to wear. Their adornments are smooth spring-polished stones, pierced clay beads, twisted fiber rings, polished fragments of pale mineral crust gathered from the mountain seeps. Some carry a small token tucked beneath clothing: a bead from the basin spring, a chip from the threshold stone of a childhood house, a bit of fired clay marked with the thumbprint of someone dead. These are not always sacred objects. Often they are simply things that have remained.

The people do not divide the world sharply between labor and ceremony. Every necessary act has its careful form. A new drain is opened with water poured first from an old household jar. The first clay drawn from a fresh bank is left overnight beside the spring basin before it is worked. When a roof is repaired, neighbors bring reeds and stone chips without being asked, then share hot roots and grain afterward on the roof itself, sitting above the mist while the patched clay dries.

Their larger ceremonies are quiet enough that a traveler might mistake them for ordinary gathering. At the turning of the planting season, households bring seed in small covered bowls to the central square. The bowls are arranged around the spring, not as an offering exactly, but as a way of letting every household see what the others have saved. At the end of the day, the seed is taken home again. In years when a new spring appears in the marsh, people do not immediately build around it. They visit it for several days, measuring its warmth, tasting its mineral edge, tracing where its water travels. Only after it has been understood is it given a channel, a marker, and eventually a name.

Names matter, but are not treated as fixed possessions. Most people have a childhood name, usually simple and local: Wet Stone, Pale Root, Third Mist, Ash-Hand, North Channel. Later, another name may settle on them through work, kinship, or temperament. Someone who repeatedly finds lost water lines may become Called-By-Drains. Someone whose house receives travelers may be known as Open Shutter. A person can refuse a name, though doing so is considered a serious declaration: not rebellion, but a statement that they are changing and wish others to notice.

They are patient speakers. Their language has pauses built into it, spaces in which another person may correct, soften, or complete what has been said. Loudness is associated with danger: a wall collapsing, a person slipping into a flooded cut, an argument carried too far. Children are taught not merely to be quiet, but to listen for changes in the ordinary quiet. The altered sound of water beneath paving. The hollow note of a stone that has loosened. The sudden absence of a familiar drip from a roof gutter. The faint cracking beneath a clay floor that means the ground is drying or shifting.

Because there are no animals, they do not keep pets and do not tell many tales of cunning beasts. Their oldest stories are instead full of places that act almost like persons: a ridge that turned rain aside for three generations, a spring that moved its mouth across a field, a patch of ash ground that would not hold a house, a black stone found warm at dawn for no known reason. These things are not gods in the manner of rulers or judges. They are presences with habits. One accommodates them, remembers them, and does not pretend to command them.

They know death intimately but without the animal signs that elsewhere might surround it: no scavengers gathering, no circling birds, no insects returning the body to the world. Their dead are washed in springwater and wrapped in woven grass cloth. Some are placed in stone-lined chambers beneath the low knolls beyond the gardens, where the ground is dry enough to trust. Others are cremated in kiln heat when fuel permits, their ash mixed with clay and made into a small unmarked tile set in a family floor or wall. Thus the dead remain in houses—not as spirits constantly consulted, but as material continuity. A person may lean against a wall made partly of a grandmother’s ash without remarking upon it.

They eat what the basin can give without animal help: grains ground into dense pale cakes, wetland roots roasted in ash, leafy greens, herbs with peppery or bitter tastes, thick soups of tubers and seed meal, fermented pastes kept in cool jars, fruit from a few tended shrubs in sheltered places. Their food is plain but various in texture. They prize sourness, warmth, and the mineral taste of water. A well-made meal is one that can be eaten in silence after work, with steam rising into the damp air.

There are travelers among them, though travel is never casual. Small parties go through the mountain passes to other basins, high shelves, old ash plains, and settlements beyond the ridges. They return with unfamiliar stone, news of weather, new weaving patterns, copper in tiny quantities, and stories difficult to verify. Such stories are listened to politely. A traveler may claim to have seen forests dense enough that sunlight did not reach the ground, rivers full of moving silver bodies, or clouds of small winged things rising from a field. The basin people do not call these lies. They receive them as accounts of lands with stranger rules.

The people themselves are perhaps the only quick life in their country. Their movement gives the city its flicker: a figure crossing mist with a shoulder pole, hands lifting shutters at dawn, children splashing deliberately through a channel despite being warned not to, workers passing bowls of wet clay from one to another, old people sitting in warm squares with their feet on sunlit stone. They are the only voices that answer one another across the water.

And because there is no other animal life to absorb their attention, they attend to one another with unusual precision.

They notice when someone’s gait changes. They remember which household has been drawing too much water. They can tell from a person’s silence whether it is the silence of concentration, grief, anger, illness, or simply rest. Privacy exists, but neglect is difficult. A person may close their shutters; they may not disappear easily. The city holds them through paths, channels, work, and memory.

At night, behind the thick walls, they sleep close to the stored warmth of hearthstones and kiln bricks. Some dream of water. Some dream of the high black ridges opening like doors. Some dream, though they have never seen such things, of small lives moving in grass: bright wings, quick feet, dark eyes at the pond edge. In the morning they wake, step into the mist, and find the grass still undisturbed except by rain and wind.

Then they begin again: lifting shutters, clearing drains, carrying water, tending the narrow green beds, fitting their patient lives into the still-young country that has made room for them.
