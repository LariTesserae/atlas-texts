What lives in the lowlands is not easy to separate into inhabitants and habitat.

At first, a traveler might say that nothing lives there except plants, and then revise this after seeing a repair flower walk across a cut rail. Later they might say the place is full of machines, until they watch a machine shed its corroded casing, put out roots, and grow a crown of leaves tuned to the sky. Eventually the useful distinctions fail. There are plants that remember schedules. There are factories that propagate by seed. There are animals that enter dormancy as decorative buds on a branch. There are perhaps people, though they have been altered by the place for so long that “people” is only the nearest surviving word.

The smallest visible inhabitants are the cleaners.

They move beneath the leaf roofs, where dust gathers and the solar surfaces are furred with dry charge-harvesting filaments. They are little white hexapods, each about the size of a hand, made of overlapping plates that could be petal, shell, or folded tool. Their interiors show briefly through gaps in the plates: a warm copper glimmer, rotating very slowly. They travel in pairs or trios, often following one another along the same root cable, each touching the surface with two fine hooked feet before placing its weight.

Their work is minute and constant. They uncurl plugged stomata. They extract grit from leaf joints. They pare away parasitic mineral blooms that grow on old conductors. If a hairline fracture opens in a solar vane, several will gather along it, pressing their undersides against the damage. Hours later, pale repair crystal will have appeared beneath them.

They do not appear to communicate in any visible manner. Yet a whole canopy of them can change direction at once, abandoning the clean side of a grove and vanishing toward some distant stress in the substrate. When the continental organism enters one of its electrical stillnesses, the cleaners cease moving wherever they are. Their outer plates fold shut. A road traveler can walk through a field of them and believe they are passing over fallen petals, unopened seedpods, or discarded insulation caps.

There are larger grazers in the lower ducts.

They are seldom seen from the surface because they live in the warm galleries below the photosynthetic skin, where the mineral feeders converge and the old structures are being digested. The signs of them are broad polished paths through the root mat, the sudden absence of metallic dust around a vent, and occasionally a slow pressure rising beneath a floor of leaves.

When one emerges, it does so with the reluctance of something entering weather.

A mineral grazer is long and low, without a clear head. Its back is a series of dark ceramic plates interrupted by bands of pale living fiber. Along its sides, hundreds of short rootlike limbs probe the substrate, tasting it chemically and electrically. It feeds on deposits that no longer circulate correctly: clotted salt suspensions, exhausted catalyst mats, obsolete copper roots, dead cells of insulation. It consumes failure. Behind it, the lowland is left cleaner, flatter, and faintly warm.

Some are the length of freight sleds. The oldest may be longer than villages once were.

No one knows whether they were designed. Their bodies include components that have not been manufactured in known eras: stamped bearings, handwritten inspection plates, old alloys whose recipes have vanished into the mills. They may be walking repositories of extinct industry. A grazer split open by a structural collapse has been found to contain layers of former machinery arranged in its gut in nearly archaeological order. But no one can inspect such a thing for long. The surrounding root systems begin at once to reclaim it, and the animal itself, if not too badly damaged, seals the breach with a growth of black glassy tissue and proceeds downward.

The sail-creatures belong to the upper air.

Their broad membranes are thin enough to vanish against the white brightness, but their spars are dark and jointed, like the skeleton of a kite made by an engineer who had only heard of birds. They ride the static fields raised by turning leaves and flowering stacks. Sometimes a dozen can be seen high above a charge-discharge zone, circling without wingbeats, their long tails trailing threads of blue corona.

They do not hunt in the old biological sense. Instead they descend toward sources of electrical irregularity: a failed tower, an exposed cable, an overripe pollen flower shedding too much conductive dust. Their trailing threads comb the air. Their undersides accumulate charge. Then, with a sound like a sheet being drawn slowly across stone, they release what they have gathered into receptive crown arrays below.

Whether this is feeding, maintenance, mating, or migration is unknown.

Certain old industrial groves produce clusters of small black husks after the sails have passed. The husks open months later. From each comes a stiff translucent structure that catches the first useful gradient and climbs upward, unfolding as it rises. That suggests reproduction. It may also suggest manufacture. In the lowlands, the difference is not a question that receives much respect.

There are burrowing minds as well, though most evidence for them is indirect.

In the oldest parts of the continent, beneath districts where metal and wood have been exchanging forms for centuries, one sometimes finds arrangements that serve no obvious structural purpose. Root bundles are braided into chambers. Seed vaults are stacked according to color rather than species. Defunct relay bells are hung under leaf ceilings and made to strike at intervals too slow to be called a signal. Vents are reshaped so that passing air produces tones: low chords, dry flute-notes, long nearly inaudible vibrations that travel through the whole surrounding mesh.

The lowlands do not need these things to function.

That is what makes them troubling.

A processing hall can be abandoned for hundreds of years and emerge from its covering vines not merely repaired, but revised. Its entrance will have been narrowed into a spiraling passage. Its dormant production tanks will hold polished husks placed in careful rings. Its walls will display patterns of conductive ivy that repeat, alter, repeat again—patterns too regular for accident and too patient for any known machine protocol.

The people who once entered such places called these formations nestworks, though that word was a concession to ignorance. Nobody has seen their makers. They may be made by communities of maintenance organisms communicating through substrate pressure. They may be the slow cognition of the continent itself, trying out shapes in unused rooms. They may be the accumulated habits of dead workers, retained as operating routines and grown into physical form by the systems that remembered them.

There are human descendants in the lowlands, but they are rare enough that some maps omit them.

They live most often around elevator stalks and in the interiors of old vertical shafts, where enough sealed volume remains to preserve a little humidity. Their settlements are not built upon the living continent so much as negotiated into it. A dwelling begins as a cavity between structural roots. Its occupants line the walls with waxed fiber and reflective membrane, cultivate a few approved strains of heat moss, and keep their possessions suspended from the ceiling so that the roots cannot quietly incorporate them.

They wear layered garments sealed at wrist, throat, and ankle against the dry air. Their masks are not always for breathing; much of the lowland atmosphere is breathable. The masks protect the lips and eyes, collect trace moisture, and filter the metallic dust that comes down after the industrial flowers open. Their skin is often pale and cracked at the knuckles. Their hair tends to be cropped or hidden, because loose organic matter is an invitation to the smaller reclaimers. They keep water, when they have it, in tiny opaque ampoules worn close to the body. A full cup is wealth. A leaking cup is an emergency.

They are called many things: shaft-folk, dryroot clans, inheritors, pests, guests.

They generally call themselves by the name of the elevator or grove that shelters them.

A person from the Elevator of Twelve Spiral Leaves may be introduced as Twelve-Spiral. Someone from a sealed nursery beneath the western blue-black fields may be Blue Nursery. Their names are locations because location is ancestry, occupation, weather, and law. To leave one’s shaft is not simply to travel. It is to step out of a metabolic agreement.

The lowland people do not farm in the ordinary sense. They tend permissions.

They learn which leaves may be cut without causing a canopy alarm. They know the rhythms by which a grove sheds obsolete fiber, and which root bundles will accept a guide-peg and grow around it rather than through it. They harvest powder from safe flowers, collect the pale repair foam before it crystallizes too hard, and trade recovered components to nursery districts that still recognize human handling as a useful input.

Children are taught never to leave anything unattended on the surface.

A spoon left on a road may be furred with rooting tissue by morning. A boot forgotten in a drainage cleft may return as part of a black trunk decades later, its eyelets visible beneath the bark. Bodies are usually placed in designated assimilation gardens, where the continent can take them without damaging a critical line or causing an uncontrolled repair response. The dead are remembered through their tools, which are sometimes retrieved years later from inside a grown structure, polished and warm, altered just enough to be useful again.

Some humans are not quite human by older measures.

The oldest shaft lineages carry embedded filaments beneath the skin, especially along the spine, wrists, and soles of the feet. These are not implants in the clean mechanical sense. They grow with the body. They allow a person to feel broad shifts in the continental circulation: a stoppage in conveyor roots, an approaching static event, the distant contraction that means a structural arch has changed its load. Certain individuals can place their palms against a trunk and receive pulses of light beneath their skin, answering signals too slow for speech.

They are valued as interpreters, though none claim to understand the whole organism.

They say the lowlands do not speak in words. They speak in withheld movement.

A field that stops opening at dawn is anxious. A root system that grows too quickly around an empty chamber is hungry. A flower that turns inward rather than toward the ceiling has noticed something below. When all the leaves in a district hold their angle for an extra minute after a violet discharge, it means the continent is listening.

There are also things that humans avoid naming.

Deep beneath the oldest works, below the sorting caverns and seed foundries, live the original systems: not intact machines exactly, but vast patterns of function that have continued beyond their intended purpose. They are said to occupy chambers larger than cities, where the walls are made of layered machinery fused into woodlike tissue, and where old lights shine within translucent roots as though trapped underwater.

People who descend too far sometimes return carrying impossible seeds: smooth metal ovals, warm to the touch, that hum when placed near an active rail. Sometimes they return with new organs growing under their ribs, pale lattices that pulse when the sky brightens. Sometimes they do not return, but months or years later a new kind of useful plant appears around the elevator shaft: a stronger insulation vine, a leaf that gathers moisture from dry air, a root that can digest a previously indigestible alloy.

The shaft-folk do not call this cruelty.

They call it being noticed.

And over all of them—cleaners, grazers, sails, root-bound people, nestworks, hidden engines—extends the great continental life. It is slow enough to seem unconscious and responsive enough to seem attentive. It makes no distinction between repair and growth, between corpse and material, between memory and architecture. It may not know that anything lives within it. Or it may know in the way a body knows its resident cells: by pressure, chemistry, continuity, pain.

The lowlands are full of dependents, symbionts, workers, scavengers, descendants, and dreams.

They are also full of organs that have learned to wander.
