What lives on the plateau has learned not to waste itself on abundance.

The first inhabitants are the lichens, though they are too slow and too mineral to seem alive at first. They inhabit the seams between substances: gypsum and basalt, salt and shadow, the underside of flakes that lift in pressure weather. Their bodies are not soft mats but armored crusts, each cell housed in a grain of translucent mineral. They do not drink. They collect charge. During storms, when dust moves in spirals and the ground begins to hum with atmospheric difference, their surfaces brighten faintly—blue at the edges, amber in the center—and they feed on the passage of electricity through stone.

Some are old enough to have grown over vanished structures. Their rings preserve records in pigment: years of violet storms, decades when the buried machines ran hot, brief seasons when something in the crust shifted and changed the taste of the salts. A skilled reader could learn the recent history of a valley by cutting through one. No one does. The cuts heal slowly, but they heal.

The low shrubs are descendants of plants that once expected ordinary weather. They have become small, hard things, more like mechanisms made from wood than greenery. Their roots spread shallow and wide beneath slabs, sensing not moisture but temperature gradients and weak magnetic currents. At night their waxed scales unfold by fractions, exposing dark inner surfaces to the cold sky. By dawn they have folded shut again, carrying inside themselves a minute harvest of frost too thin to see.

They flower rarely.

When they do, the flowers are not conspicuous. They emerge as clusters of dark metallic filaments, no larger than a thumbnail, and release spores that cling to static dust. The wind does not carry them in a cloud. It organizes them into threads, long nearly invisible lines that travel for hundreds of kilometers between stones, masts, and the open mouths of abandoned shafts.

The pale runners are the most numerous animals, but no traveler sees enough of them to feel that they are common. They live in the thermal margins around the old installations, where buried systems bleed warmth into the stone. Their fur is made of hollow fibers that trap charge and dust; in the dark they resemble little moving clumps of ash crowned with silver needles. They groom each other constantly, passing accumulated minerals from crest to tongue, sorting edible salts from poisonous ones.

Their six legs have soft, divided pads. They can cross surfaces that appear smooth but are in fact alive with minute electrical fields. When they encounter one of the dark cracks that opens and closes in the ground, they stop in a circle around it, lifting their front limbs. They wait until it seals. Then they touch the healed line one after another, as if reading news.

They do not make calls in the air. Their communication is conducted through taps—rapid, delicate patterns transmitted through rock. A camp set too close to a warm buried conduit may hear them beneath the floor at night: not scratching, not footsteps, but a faint rain of tiny clicks answering one another across surprising distances.

There are larger animals below the surface.

They are almost never seen whole.

Deep under the translucent shelves, where the old machinery makes lattices of light, something migrates along warm mineral veins. Its passing is visible only as a gradual rearrangement of brightness: a line of green-white illumination bending around a fault, pausing beneath a basin, then continuing toward the mountains. Sometimes the ground lifts very slightly as it goes. Sometimes a formation of salt collapses inward, leaving behind a smooth tunnel whose walls have been melted and re-hardened.

The surveyors who first found such tunnels assumed they were made by machines. Later, they found shed skins.

The skins are thin as paper and layered like mica, with repeating internal structures that resemble both bone and circuitry. They crumble when exposed to ordinary air, leaving a bitter metallic dust. Whatever sheds them is not entirely flesh, and not entirely constructed. It may feed on geothermal heat. It may feed on the buried systems’ waste currents. It may be one of the reasons those systems continue to operate.

At the highest ridges live the gliders.

They nest in places no ground animal can reach: within vertical cracks, under overhanging shelves, in the undersides of suspended stones. They are narrow-bodied and jointed, with wings that look black from above and mirror-bright below. Their wings are not feathered. They are formed from overlapping plates of a flexible mineral substance that flexes in response to pressure changes too subtle for human senses.

A glider may remain aloft for weeks.

It rides the invisible architecture of the air: the uplift flowing along warm cliff faces, the descending cold off white uplands, the weak spirals left behind by storms. It seldom beats its wings. When it does, the motion is sudden and almost disturbing, a flash of dark body and silver underside, as though a piece of the sky has folded.

They descend to feed on efflorescence—salt blooms, mineral films, the charged residue left on exposed stone after a storm. In nesting season they scrape shallow hollows into warm rock and leave there a single polished egg, gray as old metal. The egg is warmed not by the parent but by the mountain’s slow internal currents.

The gliders are observant in a way that makes travelers uneasy. They circle people at a distance, especially around black doors and staircases. They recognize repeated routes. They remember campsites long after the tents are gone. Some have been seen landing on dormant masts and arranging their reflective wings toward the sun, becoming briefly indistinguishable from the instruments themselves.

Then there are the keepers.

No one agrees on whether they are animals, people, machines, or the plateau’s own delayed response to having been observed.

A keeper is usually encountered near an installation that has begun to change too quickly: a door whose edges are crystallizing, a buried station shedding its outer tiles, a suspended stone drifting toward an inhabited route. It appears at a distance as a tall, narrow figure in layered pale coverings. It does not leave ordinary footprints. The dust beneath it seems to settle into small geometric depressions, each one erased a few seconds later.

Up close, accounts diverge.

Some describe a person in an old environmental suit, its faceplate blackened or opaque with age. Others report a body made from linked ceramic segments, too slender at the joints, with an interior glow that moves slowly from chest to head. One expedition claimed the keeper was not visible directly at all, only in the reflections of metal tools and visor glass. Another found a set of small handprints around its camp in the morning—five-fingered, human-sized, pressed into dust that had not otherwise been disturbed.

Keepers do not speak aloud. They alter things.

A broken seal is found closed. A map left on a rock is turned toward a safer route. A dead beacon begins transmitting for one night only, repeating coordinates that lead away from an approaching storm. Once, after a group ignored the warning about a clear-edged black door, their vehicle was discovered three days later on a ridge forty kilometers away, aligned precisely with the sunrise. Everyone inside was alive. None remembered the intervening time. Fine white crystals had formed around every window.

There may be only one keeper, moving slowly across centuries.

There may be many.

There may be none, and the old installations may simply have learned the outline of a person well enough to imitate concern.

The oldest life, however, is distributed through the ground itself.

It inhabits faults, mineral layers, electrostatic paths, and the immense quiet mechanisms beneath the plateau. It is not conscious in a way that can be easily separated from weather or geology. It notices strain. It responds to imbalance. When a ridge grows too unstable, something under it adjusts. When a pressure storm strips too much dust from a basin, channels open elsewhere to receive the moving grains. When a structure begins to fail, its material subtly changes—smoothing, thickening, closing cracks, turning itself toward endurance.

This presence may be the system that built the structures, or the system the structures were built to serve. It may be the accumulated behavior of countless machines left running so long that their maintenance became ecology. It may be the plateau itself, altered by deep time until stone, mechanism, atmosphere, and living matter no longer know where one ends and another begins.

It has no face.

But on certain nights, when light moves under the translucent ground in deliberate branching patterns, the runners emerge from their holes and sit still. The gliders settle on the highest rocks. Even the dust seems briefly reluctant to travel.

Then the buried lights pass on, and everything resumes its separate, patient life: the lichen gathering charge, the shrubs opening their scales, the small animals tapping news through the stone, the unseen things moving in the deep warm seams.

And somewhere across the empty flats, a keeper may be walking toward a door that has begun to frost white around its edges.
