What lives there is not one thing.

At first it seems to be the animals: the lizards on the walls, the red-throated frogs in the drains, the little smooth-faced branch-walkers carrying their fruit with grave, mutual purpose. They occupy the rooms as if the rooms were made for them. They sleep in niches. They drink from the basins. They know which doorways will close and which roots can bear their weight.

But none of them owns the city.

The snakes come nearer to ownership than most. They are old here, old in the manner of roots: not necessarily ancient as individuals, but continuous. Every abandoned bridge has a snake along it. Every warm sill has a coil. They pass through the hanging-root doors without disturbing them, enter hollow trunks, vanish into cracks that breathe warm air. Their skins carry the city’s patterns—moss green, wet-bark brown, the pale gold of filtered noon—and when they shed, the discarded skins remain caught in branches like translucent maps.

Still, the snakes are visitors to something deeper.

The true inhabitants may be the things beneath the streets.

They are not visible in their entirety. A white root at the edge of a lane, a swelling under moss, a pale thread drawn through a rotten beam: these are only surfaces, fingertips. Below the basin lies a mesh vast enough to hold every tree, house, pond, and court in one slow body. It drinks the rain before the rain can become a flood. It feels the weight of animals crossing overhead. It moves minerals from a fallen wall to a new shoot three quarters of the city away. It sends sweetness into one hollow chamber and poison into another. It raises a threshold by an inch each season until a creature that once lived beyond it no longer can.

Perhaps the houses are its organs.

Perhaps the trees are merely its flowering structures.

Perhaps the central white tree, when it appears, is not a tree at all but an aperture: a temporary upright mouth through which the buried city listens to weather.

There are people of a kind, though one would hesitate to call them people until they look back.

They move in the canopy and the middle levels, seldom touching open ground except after rain. Their hands are almost human. Their faces are smooth and dark-eyed, with mouths too small for the amount of fruit they consume. They wear nothing, because fur, leaf-skin, and the shadows of leaves conceal them adequately. The young ride folded under the adults’ bellies. The old grow quiet and remain near the warm cracks beneath the root-plazas, where they are fed mashed fruit and fungal paste by the others.

They do not build.

They prune.

This is the nearest thing they have to craft. With their teeth and blunt black nails, they tear away a vine that has grown across a useful passage. They strip fruit before it becomes too heavy for a branch. They break open seed cases and place the seeds in particular cracks. They pull dead animals from shallow pools and leave them beneath chosen roots. Their actions are small, repetitive, and almost invisible. Yet an avenue remains open for a century because of them. A hall continues to hold rain without flooding because of them. A certain kind of thorn does not spread into the nesting courts because every spring they eat its first buds.

They have no visible language, but they are never silent.

They click their tongues against the roofs of their mouths. They hum through closed teeth. They slap the buttress roots with open palms, listening to the answer that travels through the wood. At dusk, groups gather in empty halls and make a layered sound—low, wet, pulsing—that is hard to separate from the groan of trunks and the work of roots below. When the city answers, they become still.

Whether they understand the answer is impossible to know.

There are other residents, more difficult to see.

In membrane windows, flat pale things unfold at night and feed on the moonlit damp. In the black center of rain pools, small bodies hover upright beneath the surface, their limbs tucked close, their faces turned toward the sky. They rise only when no birds are watching. In the hollow spaces under the oldest roots, something warm rearranges itself whenever a large animal dies nearby. By morning, the bones have been cleaned, polished, and pressed into the soil as if they were seeds.

And in certain chambers—especially those that appear empty—there are presences that may be neither animal nor plant.

A visitor may enter a round room and find only red water-plants around a dark pool. The air is close. Drops fall from the ceiling. Nothing moves.

Then the water plants slowly turn.

Not toward light, not toward warmth, but toward the visitor.

The roots beneath the floor tighten. A leaf opens somewhere in the wall with a sound like a lung filling. The room recognizes weight, breath, salt, heat, perhaps memory. It does not attack. It does not welcome. It simply makes space or refuses to make space, according to a judgment whose scale is too broad to be called intention.

This is what lives in the city most completely: attention.

Attention distributed through roots, wings, wet stone, seed, fungus, and flesh. Attention without a single face. The city notices rain before it falls, notices rot before it begins, notices footsteps long before any creature reaches the center. It is patient enough to let a wall become a tree, a tree become a house, a house become soil, and soil become a different kind of room.

The animals live in it.

The smooth-faced gatherers tend it.

The buried network dreams through it.

But the city itself may be the inhabitant: low to the ground, incapable of rising, endlessly making chambers for lives it has not yet decided whether to keep.
