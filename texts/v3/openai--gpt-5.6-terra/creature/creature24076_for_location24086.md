What lives in the basin is not one species, nor even an ecosystem in the ordinary sense. It is a pressure of adaptations. A wet intelligence distributed among shells, pipes, silt, blind flesh, corroding metal, and the intermittent labor of the pump.

The smallest lives are the plated things in the channels. Up close, their translucence is misleading. Their shells are not truly clear but layered with such thin mineral films that the water shows through them. Each carries a small blue organ, a bead or gland or trapped spark, somewhere behind the foreplates. When they feed, the lights dim. When they are alarmed, they brighten in sequence, not simultaneously: a shiver of cold blue moving through a colony as though one body has remembered pain a fraction of a second before another.

They eat what the city sheds and what water loosens from stone. Grease. Rust. Skin. Spores. Oil molecules thinned to rainbows. The soft black bloom between paving stones. They worry shell fragments clean, leaving them pale and polished. They enter the drowned cables and return with copper dust stippling their joints. In the warmer channels beneath the pump house, they grow large enough to be mistaken for broken pieces of white porcelain until they fold themselves open and go away.

There are generations of them in every puddle. Some are no wider than fingernails; others, old and thickly plated, have bodies like the palms of children. The old ones do not move much. They sit in the mouths of drains where warmer water rises, collecting a mineral crust over their backs. From a distance they look like a scatter of pale stones. When floodwater comes, they wake almost together and unfasten themselves from the ground.

The wall-clingers are older still.

They may once have been soft-bodied animals in the manner of slugs or limpets, but the basin has taught them another form. Their skin takes on the nature of whatever they feed upon. Those that live low on the brick are ridged red-brown and powdery at the edges. Those that cross the iron plates grow almost black, with a dull metallic sheen. A few inhabit the pale seams in the retaining wall and are so white and dry-looking that one only notices them when their hooked feet make a slow, deliberate adjustment.

They do not have faces. Or, if they do, their faces are on their undersides, pressed permanently against the world.

At night they migrate. Not far: a handspan, a brick’s height, perhaps the width of a doorway over several hours. But by morning the wall will have changed its pattern. A dark patch may be gone. A clean wet stripe may have appeared beneath a crack. The chalky bloom will be grazed down to bare masonry, while another section is suddenly crowded with closed, pebble-like forms. The basin’s wall is never still; it only changes more slowly than an eye expects.

Some of them have found their way inside the pump house. They lie along the lower seams of its metal walls, beneath the patched doors and behind the cloudy windows. The motor’s shudder passes through them. With each three-second effort they contract almost imperceptibly, as if listening through their bodies. The oldest clingers gather nearest the machinery, not for warmth exactly, but for vibration. They have worn the bottoms of certain pipes smooth.

The blind swimmers belong to the hidden water rather than the visible pools. They are seen only because they occasionally rise.

They hatch—or assemble—in places no person can reach: in the black throat of dead drains, in pipe bends packed with mineral sediment, in chambers beyond the retaining wall where tidal water may still enter, or where old industrial runoff lies warm beneath newer layers of the city. Their transparent fins are not fins so much as continuous trembling edges. They move by sending a ripple from skull to tail, and the gleam of their inner supports travels with it: white lines, tiny hooked shadows, a milky flicker where a heart should be.

They have whiskers because sight would be wasted. The whiskers taste heat, current, rust, pressure, traces of oil, the electrical pulse leaking from the striped cabinet. A school will circle a submerged bolt for hours if a faint charge runs through it. They gather in pale clouds beneath the working lamp when it flickers, each body turning its transparent flank toward the surface. The light in them is not reflected. For a moment, the water appears full of little interior moons.

They are born hungry and remain hungry. The smallest graze on the residue of the plated scavengers. The larger swallow anything soft enough to fit through their narrow mouths: shed skins, clotted algae-like films, larvae of things that never rise into air. In seasons of heavy rain they enter the higher gutters in great numbers, flowing through the basin at ankle level with the water. A person standing still in such a flood might feel them brush their boots, dozens at first, then hundreds, each contact light as wet string.

The six-footed predators are called nothing, because nobody who works nearby speaks of them much.

They live in low, dry-enough cavities: under steps, inside collapsed conduits, beneath the false floor of the pump house. They are not especially aggressive. Aggression is too expensive in the basin. They wait with a patience that makes them difficult to remember. One may be watched for ten minutes and seem to be a lump of coal washed against a stair. Then a swimmer passes too close, and the coal shifts. A throat opens. The water folds inward.

Their mouths are larger than their bodies should allow. Their jaws are not jaws but soft, radially opening plates, ringed with inward-pointing teeth. When feeding, they make no splash. The only sign is a brief tremor of blue light inside their throats, stolen from the plated scavengers and the transparent fish.

They are warm. This is their most disturbing quality. In cold water, a predator under the surface gives off a faint living heat. Swimmers feel it and veer away, but not quickly enough when the water is crowded or when the pump changes the current. The predators breed during floods. Then smaller black forms can be found wedged into the holes of brickwork, each no bigger than a fist and already motionless, already waiting.

There are other residents that reveal themselves less often.

In the sump beneath the winch lives a colony of ribbon-bodied things that may be worms, roots, cables, or all three in succession. They are dark red in warm water and almost invisible in cold. Their bodies knot around submerged machinery. When the valve coughs and sends a surge through the basin, they recoil into a compact mass. Afterward they extend again, slowly threading themselves into the sediment. They feed on heat more than matter. Where they gather, the water is always a degree warmer.

Inside the electrical cabinet, despite its stilts, there are pale dry creatures that look like flakes of insulation. They have flat bodies and too many tiny legs, and they live in the narrow protected space behind the corroded terminals. They come out only when the cabinet hums. Their appetite appears to be mineral dust and the crystalline residue left by evaporated water. Sometimes one falls into a pool below. The swimmers avoid it. The plated scavengers swarm it. Within minutes there is nothing left but a white outline in the mud.

There are things in the wall that never emerge at all.

The retaining wall is full of damp voids, failed mortar, old conduit, pockets where bricks have settled against one another and made chambers no larger than fists. Something lives in those chambers and periodically exhales through the cracks. That may be the chalky bloom. It may be spores, eggs, salt, or waste. On very quiet days, when the pump has paused and no rain falls, a soft ticking can be heard from within the masonry: not one sound but hundreds, irregular and patient.

The wall-clingers gather beneath these places. The swimmers rise beneath them in the nearest water. Even the coal-black predators avoid the patches directly below the ticking.

Perhaps what is inside is a brood. Perhaps it is merely the wall decaying in an organized way.

The basin’s true inhabitants may be the old machines themselves. The pump, the pipes, the winch, the stranded wiring: they have been inhabited so long that they no longer have clear boundaries. A pipe carries water, but it also carries eggs, mineral deposits, feeding colonies, sleeping bodies. The pump draws water down, but its rhythm signals the walls to climb and the swimmers to change course. Its failures make temporary shorelines; its recoveries erase them. Every creature knows its labor.

When the motor shudders, the basin listens.

The plated things tighten their clusters. The transparent swimmers sink or rise according to the pull. The clingers hold themselves flat against brick and iron. Under the steps, a predator opens one eye—if the bright, wet seam in its hide is an eye—and closes it again.

The pump is not their enemy. It is the weather.

And beneath all of them, in the lowest drain where the water leaves clearer than it arrived, there is a current that never quite stops. Nothing visible lives in that current. Nothing that has been caught, examined, or named.

But sometimes, when the basin is nearly full and the working lamp falters amber above the black water, every blue body beneath the surface lights at once.

Not in alarm.

In answer.
