The Underweir is inhabited first by people who have learned not to mistake permanence for dryness.

They are not all born there. Some came down because the rents above rose faster than wages. Some were maintenance workers whose jobs became too specialized to exist anywhere else. Some arrived through accidents of family, following a parent who repaired gates or kept the Pump House ledger, and grew up knowing the district’s turns before they knew the names of surface streets. A few came because they had nowhere else to be and discovered that the Underweir, for all its damp and darkness, has room for those who can make themselves useful.

They tend to be quiet people, though not solemn. Loudness is difficult underground. It travels through pipes, shakes loose dust, wakes sleeping things in vents. Conversation is kept close to the body: exchanged over tea, across repair benches, from one footbridge to another. Children are taught early how sound behaves. They learn which chambers make a whisper enormous, which will swallow a shout, which pipes can be tapped to call home.

Their clothes are practical, layered, mended many times. Waxed coats. Rubber boots patched at the seams with bicycle inner tube. Wool caps even in seasons that are supposedly warm. Nearly everyone carries a light, but few carry it in the hand. Lamps are clipped to shoulders, pinned beneath caps, hung at the chest on cords. Hands are for railings, ropes, baskets, valves, and for catching oneself when a walkway shifts beneath the foot.

They have the pale, weathered look of people who do not receive enough sun but do receive a great deal of reflected water. Their skin shines faintly with damp. Their hair smells of iron, smoke, wet stone, and the bitter soap made in the upper market. Older residents often develop a permanent narrowing of the eyes, not from brightness but from looking for movement in dim places: a rat on a ledge, a change in a waterline, a lamp gone out where a lamp ought to be.

The Underweir’s children are perhaps its truest natives.

They know how to hop the narrow gaps between old platforms and how to tell shallow water from deep by the color of its reflected ceiling. They make toys from insulated wire, bottle caps, bearing balls, and fragments of plastic that surface from the drains. They keep frogs in jars for an afternoon and let them go before supper. They dare one another to stand on the maintenance path around the reservoir with their eyes closed, listening for the slow bubbles in the middle.

They are warned not to play at the intake tunnels, but they do, of course. Not often. Not carelessly. There is a difference. Underweir children acquire the local rules the way children elsewhere acquire traffic sense: through repetition, stories, demonstrations, and the particular gravity in an adult’s voice when a rule is not merely a rule.

Never shine your lamp down an unmarked shaft.

Never throw stones into still water.

If something calls you by name from behind a grate, go home by a different route.

They know which adults are strict, which will trade them a sweet for errands, which will pretend not to see them climbing the old signal gantry. They know Farrow will write down any event they report if they describe it plainly enough. They also know that she will ask exactly what they mean by *plainly.*

Farrow herself lives behind the register office, in two former staff rooms joined by a doorway cut through a tiled wall. She is old enough that no one can agree on how old. Her hands are square and permanently marked with blue ink. She records the district in ledgers bound in oilcloth, and the ledgers are treated with a respect usually reserved for relics. Not because they are sacred, exactly, but because they are useful. A birth not entered into Farrow’s book is still a birth, but it may have trouble obtaining ration cards, a school place aboveground, or proof that it ever happened. A death not entered may continue receiving mail. An arrival not entered is not necessarily unwelcome, but they will be noticed.

Farrow has written down the water heights for forty-three years. She can tell, from the shape of a damp mark on a wall, which flood left it. She has a private notation for events that have no adequate category: three tiny triangles for unexplained fish fall, a ring around a date for power returned without cause, a black line through a name for those who left by one route and did not emerge by another.

She does not call them missing.

The repairman by the canal is named Ors, though some say that is the name of the shop rather than the man. He is always in the middle of repairing something that ought to be beyond repair. Pumps from flooded basements, radios with their interiors greened by corrosion, sewing machines, pressure gauges, battery lamps, the electric kettles people prize because boiling water by fuel is expensive. His workbench is a bright island of tools and copper wire. He speaks to machines in an unhurried, severe murmur.

“Don’t pretend,” he tells a dead ballast.

“Not that screw,” he tells apprentices.

“Ah,” he says, when a motor begins again. “There you are.”

Ors employs three young people at different times of the year, though no one is quite certain whether they are apprentices, tenants, relatives, or merely those who have stayed long enough. They learn that every Underweir device has two histories: what it was designed to do, and what it has agreed to do now. The second is more important.

There are gatekeepers, too. Not many. The gates almost never need opening, and that is precisely why the gatekeepers remain. Someone must know how. Someone must keep the handwheels greased, the teeth clean, the old chain hoists from seizing into permanent uselessness. The senior gatekeeper is a woman called Marn, whose left hand is missing two fingers from an accident no one asks about. She has the shoulder strength of a dockworker and the patience of a watchmaker. When water begins rising above its remembered marks, she walks the channels with a chalk stick, a thermos, and a great ring of keys.

The keys are mostly ceremonial. Very few locks still answer them. But Marn says metal remembers its own language better when it hears familiar words spoken nearby.

There are cooks, seamsters, scavengers, teachers, pipe-readers, fishers, and people whose work has no name because it consists of whatever needs doing before the next damp season. There is an old couple who collect and sort the things brought down by the drains: spoons, buttons, coins, plastic animals, spoilt photographs, wedding rings, toy soldiers, keys by the handful. The useful objects are cleaned and traded. The strange objects are set aside in labeled boxes. The dangerous objects are taken to the police drop point above, unless the police drop point is flooded or the route to it is unsafe, in which case they remain in a locked cabinet beneath the tea stall.

The tea-stall proprietor is called Sel. He makes tea in an enamel kettle blackened by years of use, and serves it in thick little cups that have survived innumerable drops. The pinch of salt is non-negotiable. “You sweat underground,” he says to newcomers, though no one is sure whether this is physiologically true or simply something he enjoys insisting upon. His stall is the nearest thing the Underweir has to a public square. Shift changes occur there. Arguments begin there and end there. Notices are pinned to the awning posts: lost tools, spare boots, work requests, memorial meals, warnings about unstable bridges.

Sel knows everyone’s ordinary order, and treats an altered order as a matter requiring tact.

No sugar today?

No salt?

Tea to go?

He will not press. But he will remember.

The people of the Underweir do not think of themselves as hidden, exactly. They are known to municipal authorities in the vague, administrative way a problem is known. There are old reports, disputed property claims, health inspections that occur in dry years, promises of relocation that become less urgent whenever the pumps continue functioning. Surface people speak of the Underweir with either pity or fascination. They imagine desperate tunnel dwellers, illicit markets, dangerous water, whole families sleeping under dripping arches.

There is some truth in all this, but it misses the local fact of life: the Underweir is not merely endured. It is maintained.

The residents sweep their walkways. They paint the names back onto signs when rust eats the letters. They clear rubbish from drain mouths, scrape moss from the steps where elderly people walk, organize lifts for those who cannot manage the steep routes. They hold small meals after funerals and larger ones when a child reaches an age at which they are allowed to cross the district alone. They celebrate the first dry week of each year with a feast of fried watercress, onion cakes, preserved fish, and whatever fruit has been brought down from above.

There are songs, but they are not often sung. The acoustics make songs too vulnerable. A melody sent through an empty station chamber can return altered, delayed, broken into separate notes. Still, people hum while working. There is a pump song with a rhythm matched to the old pistons. There is a lullaby about a lantern fish that never sees daylight but knows the way home by warmth. There is a children’s counting rhyme used to remember safe bridge planks.

One, two: rail holds true.  
Three, four: test before.  
Five, six: no running feet.  
Seven, eight: don’t trust the sheet—  
of water, glassy, black, and thin.  
Nine, ten: turn back again.

The animals live alongside them, not beneath notice but in a kind of negotiated proximity.

The rats are watched with irritation and respect. Cats do poorly in the Underweir; the damp sickens them, and the rats are too large, too numerous, too familiar with the routes. So people use traps when necessary, store grain in suspended tins, and leave the rats their ledges. A rat seen carrying dry grass in its mouth is considered a weather sign. A rat that pauses to wash its face at an open threshold is considered an invitation to trouble, though no one agrees what sort.

The bats are not pets, and yet some residents recognize particular colonies. They know where the nursing clusters hang and when the young first begin their awkward flights. During severe pump trouble, people close certain upper vents to protect the bats from sudden drafts. In return—or so the story goes—the bats keep the moths from eating the cable insulation. The truth is more complicated, but the arrangement is old enough to be treated as true.

The frogs are children’s favorites and old people’s omens. When the transparent frogs gather high on walls, the water will rise. When they vanish into cracks, the air will turn foul. If one appears in a dwelling, it is not driven out. It is given a saucer of water and left to choose its own departure.

And the eels are something more than animals, though no resident would call them gods.

They are scavengers, fish, nuisances, indicators, and inheritors. They inhabit every channel that remains connected to moving water. They slide through gaps too narrow to admit a human hand. They live in pipes the maps have forgotten. Sometimes one is found in a bathtub, a kitchen basin, a boot left overnight near a drain. This is accepted with more annoyance than surprise.

The ordinary eels are trapped occasionally beyond the district proper, smoked or fried when food is short. But no one kills an eel inside the marked boundaries of the Underweir. Not because of law alone. The rule has become entangled with too many stories.

A worker who killed an eel near Sluice Court lost the use of his right hand after a valve burst.

A boy who carried a dead eel home for a dare woke to find every tap in his building running black.

A woman who tried to sell pale eel skin to collectors above was followed for three days by eels appearing in puddles that could not have held them.

These stories change. Their details are not important. What matters is that the eels belong to the water, and the water belongs to no one in particular.

Then there is the large white eel.

Farrow records sightings without commentary. Western cistern. Three witnesses. White body, scarred head, length uncertain. Or: Reservoir edge, red lamp present, eel surfaced once, no apparent disturbance. Or simply: Seen.

Some say it is blind. Some say it has eyes like coins. Some say it is not one creature but a succession of pale eels, each mistaken for the last because people need continuity. Others insist it has lived beneath the old city since before the first tram tunnel was cut.

The oldest residents do not argue. They say only that the white eel appears when the district is about to change.

Not when disaster comes. Change.

There is another kind of inhabitant, less easily listed.

It lives in the operations of the place: in the pumps that continue to turn, in the gauges that tremble at midnight, in the handwheels that resist one person but yield to another. The Underweir’s systems have outlasted the institutions that built them. Their original engineers are dust. Their manuals have mildewed into unreadability. Yet the gates keep their remembered levels. The pumps draw breath. Warmth enters certain pipes in winter. Bells ring when they should not, and sometimes do not ring when they should.

The people who live there do not say the machinery is alive. That would be too simple.

They say it is attentive.

It notices neglect. It notices haste. It notices when someone unfamiliar has walked into a chamber and begun asking the wrong questions. A cabinet that has not opened in twenty years may open for a child looking for a lost glove. A ladder may lose a rung beneath a surveyor carrying demolition plans. A pump that has labored faithfully through three flood seasons may go silent for exactly the hours necessary to make a municipal inspector spend the night below.

Whether this is coincidence, resentment, old engineering, or something accumulated from generations of repair, no one can prove. The Underweir does not demand belief. It demands behavior.

And deepest of all, in the reservoir’s inverted reflection, there may be another population.

Not ghosts in the familiar sense. The dead of the Underweir are remembered carefully, named in Farrow’s books, fed in absence at memorial meals, and sometimes represented by a patched coat hung over a chair for a year after they are gone. They do not need to haunt.

But the reservoir reflects more than is placed above it. People walking its edge sometimes see a lamp moving in the water where no lamp moves on the path. They see bridge rails continuing beneath broken sections. They see doors in the submerged image of walls that have no doors. Once in a while, they see figures: not drowned figures, not pale faces pressed to the surface, nothing so obliging.

Just someone, perhaps, crossing the inverted chamber below.

Someone carrying a hooded red light.

Someone who knows the way through streets that no longer exist.

Most residents avoid looking too long. They understand that a reflection is not a window, unless it is. And if it is, politeness is the safest response. One does not stare into another person’s home.

So the Underweir is lived in by workers and children, gatekeepers and tea sellers, rats and bats and translucent frogs. By eels that taste the electricity in the walls. By moss, mineral growth, patient ferns, and the invisible colonies that soften mortar and turn iron into flakes. By old machines still performing their duties after the world that assigned those duties has moved on.

And perhaps, quietly, by the water itself.

Not as a creature with a face or a voice. Not as anything that wants worship.

But as a resident with the oldest claim.

It knows every threshold in the district. It enters every room eventually. It receives the dead eel, the dropped coin, the child’s toy, the runoff from spring gardens, the blood from a cut hand, the ash from a stove, the dust of old records. It carries some things away. It keeps others for years in silted corners.

It rises when it must.

It lowers when it can.

And all who live in the Underweir, human or otherwise, live according to its memory.
