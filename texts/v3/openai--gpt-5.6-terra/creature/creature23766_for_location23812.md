What lives there is not one kingdom of creatures but a rush of claimants.

The basin is too new for specialists to have become delicate. Most of its inhabitants are opportunists, arrivals, survivors of the old drowned country, things carried in on storm winds or hidden as eggs beneath mud and bark. They are built to breed quickly, hide well, eat what appears, and endure when the ground changes its mind.

In the lowest wet places live the soft-bodied things first.

Transparent amphibians push through ash-mud with their forelimbs, their organs faintly visible beneath their skin. They lay strings of dark eggs in rain pools that may disappear before the larvae have legs; their young grow in frantic accelerations, feeding on algae, smaller larvae, and one another. Some never leave the water. Others climb into the roots at night, swollen with moisture, and hunt by sensing the tremor of insects walking overhead.

Blind crabs occupy the warm channels where mineral water runs beneath stone. Their shells are pale, nearly colorless, except where iron stains them orange. They feed on drowned leaves, fungal threads, dead insects, and anything slow enough to be dismantled. At night they emerge in groups to rearrange their wet-stone heaps. These heaps form little walls, circles, ramps, and mounds around vent openings. No two colonies build the same way.

In the temporary ponds there are things that seem half insect, half leaf fragment: water-striders with long jointed legs, predatory larvae armed with folding jaws, flat beetles that carry silver bubbles of air beneath their wing cases. Some ponds glow from below, full of microscopic life stirred by rain and heat. Animals come to drink there, though cautiously. The water may be clean, acidic, hot, full of eggs, full of parasites, or occupied by something that has been waiting beneath the mud since the last storm.

The ground has its own moving skin.

Scaled lizards run along root surfaces with their bellies held clear of the wet. Their colors imitate fresh stone, leaf mold, pale fungus, or the green-black shine of rain-dark bark. They are quick enough to catch flies and cautious enough to freeze when the canopy gives its warning sounds. Some have broad, flattened tails that help them swim through flooded hollows. Others have spines along their backs like the serrations of young leaves and vanish among cycads by becoming still.

The rodents are small, round-bodied, and almost noiseless until they are terrified. Their membranous ears can fold shut against rain and open wide against the insect-whine. They nest in hollow roots, feeding on seeds, beetles, soft fruit, and the swollen bases of fungi. In places where volcanic warmth rises through the soil, whole families sleep pressed together under mats of shed fern fronds. Their lives are brief and crowded. Every nest is watched by snakes, birds, lizards, and creatures that do not fit neatly into any familiar category.

There are snakes, but they do not own the darkness.

Some are thin as vines and remain suspended above the ground, moving from branch to branch by slow muscular loops. Their scales hold the dim canopy glow and break their outlines into wet green fragments. Others are root-colored ambushers that wait beside paths used by frogs, crabs, and rodents. A few live around the warm fissures, their bodies adapted to mineral heat, their tongues constantly tasting the air for eggs and blood.

The larger hunters are rarely seen whole.

There is a long-bodied creature that moves beneath fern cover on many low limbs, its hide plated with overlapping shields resembling fallen leaves. It follows the herd-browsers, feeding on their weak, their hatchlings, and the carrion left by landslides or floods. When it breathes, the ferns before it pulse outward. When it passes, the smaller animals go quiet in a widening circle.

There may also be something that climbs.

It leaves deep hooked marks in the bark of the largest trees, though no one sees it make them. Its weight bends branches that should hold only birds. At intervals a fruit-eater vanishes from the canopy in a short eruption of wings and leaves, followed by a dull descent through vegetation. Whether this is one animal, several animals, or merely the basin itself settling is not clear.

The armored browsers travel in low herds through the densest growth. They are not large in the way old beasts might be large; they are broad, stubborn, and difficult to turn aside. Each has a squat head suited to pushing through thorn and a back covered in flexible plates shaped like leaves, ridged to shed rain. Moss grows in the grooves of older individuals. Their many legs move in a shifting rhythm, carrying them over mud where heavier animals would sink. They eat soft shoots, fungus, fallen fruit, and the swollen tissues around mineral seeps. Behind them come insects, crabs, scavenging lizards, and rooting birds.

Above, the canopy belongs to noise.

The night herons are gaunt, dark-winged birds with pale throats that flash when they call. They hunt the rain pools and root channels, stabbing at frogs and fish-like larvae with bills sharp enough to pierce leaf skin. The canopy runners are smaller, bright-eyed, perpetually furious creatures that travel along vines in bursts, screaming warnings or accusations at everything they encounter. Their nests are woven into bromeliad cups and thorn baskets; their young learn to cling before they learn to see clearly.

The heavy fruit-eaters are more ancient-looking: thick-beaked, broad-chested birds that land hard enough to shake water from a whole branch. Their feathers are dull by day and faintly iridescent in the green dark. They swallow fruit whole, scatter seeds in their droppings, and fight loudly over trees that fruit after rain. They are gardeners without intention, carrying the forest into every new crack in the rock.

Moths fill the open spaces beneath the canopy. Some drink nectar from flowers that open only in darkness; others drink sweat, sap, tears, or the damp mineral sheen on stone. Their wings bear patterns that resemble eyes, fungus, broken bark, or the pale underside of leaves. In the wake of lightning, they lift all at once, making the basin seem briefly filled with loose scraps of moonlight.

There are ants everywhere, though rarely in the form of a single visible trail. They move inside vines, under bark, through woven grass galleries, across the undersides of leaves. Some cultivate fungus on chewed leaf pulp. Some herd scale insects whose sweet secretions feed the colony. Some are large enough to take flesh from a fallen animal in pieces, while others are so small they farm within the damp seams of a flower.

The luminous flies are neither gentle nor decorative. Their light is a language of confusion, gathering, courtship, warning, and perhaps hunger. A cluster may descend upon a wounded animal and hover in the heat above it. Another may surround a blooming vine, entering and leaving its flowers in flashes. Their drifting lights attract small hunters, which attract larger hunters, until a single glow becomes the center of a brief, violent food web.

And among all these creatures live the plants that have become creatures in all but movement.

The clasping flowers close around beetles and moths, not to digest them but to keep their warmth through the wet cold hours before dawn. The humming root-trees collect rain in their hollow bases, offering water and shelter while their vibrations draw animals close. Some vines tighten after rain, narrowing passageways and trapping slow insects in sticky grooves. Thorn shrubs make little fortress-cities full of ants, nesting birds, tiny lizards, and spiders that stretch nets between spines.

The basin’s oldest inhabitants may be the fungi.

They rise in shelves from fallen wood, bloom in delicate fans around warm cracks, spread luminous threads through the black soil. Some are food. Some are medicine to animals that know how to nibble them. Some invade the bodies of insects and turn them into climbing, dying instruments for the release of spores. In the deepest dark, their blue-green glow maps the hidden architecture of rot: root, carcass, fallen branch, drowned nest, buried shell.

Nothing living there has the leisure to be merely itself.

A frog is a future heron meal, a parasite’s shelter, an insect-eater, a carrier of pond life to a new hollow. A fruit bird is a seed disperser, a nest builder, a thief, an alarm bell, a source of feathers for smaller things. A fallen trunk is food, bridge, nursery, reservoir, ambush site, and eventually soil. Even the fresh stone is being inhabited—by lichens, by heat-loving bacteria, by eggs hidden in cracks, by roots patiently widening fractures too narrow to see.

If there are minds in the basin that can think of it as a home, they do not think of home as safety.

Home is the root arch that has not collapsed yet.  
The warm crack that belongs to you until a larger body finds it.  
The pool that lasts long enough for your young to grow legs.  
The vine that bears fruit tonight.  
The thicket that tears the predator more deeply than it tears you.

What lives there is abundance before balance: innumerable lives testing the raw earth, failing, multiplying, adapting, and leaving marks for whatever comes next.
