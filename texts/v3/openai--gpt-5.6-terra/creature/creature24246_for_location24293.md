What lives in the high drowned court is not one people, nor one animal kingdom, but a compact held together by touch.

The smallest lives are the most numerous. They are the pale grazers, the wall-mouths, the little roof-shelled things. They do not appear to fear one another. A crab will settle on the back of a sleeping fish. A fish will rest among the shell colonies, its transparent belly pressed to their roofs. The armored stone-scrapers travel in files, not because they follow a leader but because each finds the softened trace left by the mouth before it. They consume the mineral skin that grows over the old masonry: the faint bacterial bloom, colorless and tasteless to anything with eyes. Without them the court would slowly close beneath its own deposits. Their work keeps the seams open, the carvings legible, the drains breathing.

Some of the shell-creatures are old enough to have become architecture. Their first shells cling to the slabs like small huts. Later, as they grow, they add chambers below and around themselves, dissolving stone with a slow secretion and rebuilding it in folded white layers. A colony can turn a wall crack into a porous cathedral of shell. The water moves through these structures and comes out clearer, warmer, subtly changed. All the court drinks through them.

There are creatures in the copper lines as well.

They are thin, segmented animals, no thicker than threads, their bodies plated in a dark green metal-like mineral. In stillness they cannot be distinguished from corrosion. When the dim grayness descends from above, they wake in every cord at once. They uncoil from the copper, extend feathery foreparts, and pass minute currents from one tie to another. The old ropes hum then—not audibly, but in the teeth and sternum of any body near them. The court's inhabitants know this vibration. The fish turn their translucent heads toward it. The blind eels leave their drains. Even the broad-backed swimmers become attentive.

The cord-worms are perhaps the closest thing the court has to nerves.

At the center live the broad-backed ones, the slow dark swimmers. They are called nothing, now, because names require mouths that use air. Their bodies are warm against the surrounding water. Their skin is not quite skin: it is folded, pitted, inhabited. Shell-creatures lodge in the creases of their shoulders. Fine pale fish shelter beneath the rims of their fan-like forelimbs. The small crabs clean the mineral plaques from their backs. In return, the large swimmers carry entire communities from chamber to chamber.

They do not breed in the court.

At long intervals—perhaps every few generations, perhaps only when the immense thing above has passed a particular number of times—one of them will cease its slow circuits. It settles in the central bowl, folds its many-fingered fans over its face, and grows still. The animals gather on it as they gather on a fallen wall. Its flesh gradually pales. Seams appear along its back. After days, or months, the body opens without violence, and from its interior emerge several smaller, softer swimmers, each no longer than a forearm. They do not resemble their parent at first. They are almost white, with transparent fin-frills and open, helpless eye-pits.

The adults take them into the oldest corridors.

There, in chambers whose entrances have been shuttered with stone and copper mesh, the young are taught the first necessary lesson: how to close the eyes.

Not to shut eyelids—at that age they have none—but to draw the soft sensing organs inward beneath the skull. They learn by pressing their faces against the fan-fingers of the adults. They learn the court by texture: the shallow cut of a chisel mark, the roundness of a repaired seam, the trembling difference between a living cord and a dead one. By the time they return to the open basin, their heads are smooth and blind.

There are other large inhabitants below the court, and the court does not welcome them.

The drains lead down into places where the water turns cold and carries a pressure that makes stone complain. From there come the long listening animals: bodies like strips of wet black cloth, with rings of pale sensory pits down their sides. They are too large to enter most of the corridors. Their front ends linger in the drains, tasting the court through water movement. When stone is struck after the dark hour, when a cord snaps, when a frightened animal thrashes too hard, these listeners rise.

The old rule about vibrations is not superstition. It is a survival instruction.

Sometimes an eel disappears into a drain and does not return. Sometimes the nearest shell colony shuts for a year afterward. Once in a long while, a broad-backed swimmer bears crescent scars across its side, smooth-edged and white, as if part of it had been carefully removed.

But there is one inhabitant stranger than the listeners below and stranger than the vast passage overhead.

It lives in the upper masonry where cold gathers. No one has seen its whole shape. It is known only from effects: a line of fresh clear stone where mineral crust has been taken away; a ladder rung found turned around in its lashing; a copper peg removed from one wall and set neatly into another. Small cages are sometimes opened, then closed again with nothing missing. A shutter that has been jammed for centuries will swing once, very slowly, and settle.

The broad-backed swimmers do not avoid these changes. They approach them. They pass their fingered fans over the moved object, over the altered knot, over the newly bare patch of stone. They remain there for a long time.

Perhaps the thing in the upper walls is one of the builders continued beyond any recognizable body. Perhaps it is a colony of small hands hidden in the cracks. Perhaps the court itself has learned, through copper and shell and pressure, to make repairs when the water demands them.

There are signs that it remembers the old rules.

No ceiling in the court has ever collapsed inward. Drains that become clogged are cleared before the pressure can build. Faces with open eyes, where they survive on sheltered lintels, are gradually rubbed away—not by the grazing animals, whose mouths leave finer marks, but by something broad and deliberate. Closed eyes remain.

So the drowned high place is inhabited not merely by animals living among ruins. The ruin is alive in the manner of a reef, a body, a custom.

The fish preserve its stillness.  
The shell colonies cleanse its water.  
The cord-worms carry its warnings.  
The blind swimmers carry its young and remember its passages.  
The unseen keeper alters what must be altered.  
And beneath it, in the deep routes that must never be blocked, larger hungers wait.

When the gray pressure descends and the immense presence moves far above, every living thing in the court knows its own response. The transparent fish orient. The crabs vanish. The cords tighten. The great blind swimmers press themselves against the ancient walls as though listening through them.

For those few minutes, the court is not a basin, not a ruin, not an enclosure.

It is a held breath inside a much greater animal.
