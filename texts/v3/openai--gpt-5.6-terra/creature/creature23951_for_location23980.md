What lives there is not quite a population. It is an arrangement of continuances.

The first inhabitants are the roots.

Not individual plants, exactly—though there are figs, reeds, horsetails, grasses, lilies, mosses, climbing things, and the immense soft-leaved plants that make rooms of the paths—but the rootwork beneath them. It has grown so densely through peat, pipe, brick, drowned timber, and the softened remains of previous growth that it behaves like a buried animal with no central body. It senses pressure. It remembers dampness. It closes over absence.

A boot placed too heavily on a patch of mat may be felt, in some diluted fashion, at the tower, where a red root tightens around a crack in the concrete. A warm pipe beginning to fail may draw a pale net of fungal threads toward it long before the metal splits. The roots do not think in words, nor even in the quick reflexes of animals. Their attention is patient and distributed. It is made of slow pressure, mineral taste, vibration, moisture, heat.

They are what decide whether a path remains a path.

Among them live the smaller lives: the creatures of folds, undersides, hollow stems, and pockets of drowned air.

There are frogs so dark green they disappear against the wet leaves even when they are sitting in plain sight. Their calls are not loud but oddly ventriloquial. A visitor may hear one chirp from beneath a footbridge, then again from the roof of the pump house, then from inside a pipe several yards away. They travel through the place by channels no person sees: damp clefts under the root mat, flooded pipe runs, the cups formed where leaves overlap. In the dry part of summer they sleep packed together in the cool mineral mouths of the old intake pipes, each one coated in a slick translucent film that smells faintly of iron.

There are pale eels, or something sufficiently eel-like that no one bothers to distinguish it. They are long, silver-gray, and almost blind. They do not inhabit the open pools so much as the hidden water beneath the visible marsh—the old conduits, the clogged drains, the dark compressed seams where water moves through peat. Sometimes one emerges in a clear channel after rain and lies almost still, its body making only a minute correction against the current. It is said that the oldest of them have wires caught in their flesh, not painfully, but grown through: copper strands disappearing beneath smooth skin, bits of insulation trailing like weed.

The heron is perhaps the only creature in the basin that seems to have arrived from elsewhere.

It is not always the same heron, though people speak of it as if it were. A pale bird stands beneath the catwalk in the hours before sunset, in water black enough to turn its legs invisible. It watches the little openings in the root mat. It watches the places where the water inhales. It has learned that the machinery startles tiny things loose: frogs, water beetles, larvae shaken from stems by the pulse of distant pumps. When the conveyor clatters more quickly after storms, the heron comes closer to the shed.

Sometimes it steps onto the conveyor belt and allows itself to be carried into the leaves.

No one has seen whether it comes out.

The insects are many, though they are not readily noticed as insects. There are midges that collect around warm vents in floating gray veils. There are small yellow moths whose wings resemble dead petals until they rise together. There are beetles with lacquered backs, living in the submerged windows of the pump house, where they walk upside down beneath the surface tension of the water. At noon, when the electrical hum becomes strongest, certain gnats gather above the tower in a faint revolving column. They do not circle the signal lamp; they circle an absence just beneath it, as if some invisible shaft continues upward through the broken tank.

And there are things in the machinery.

Not rats, despite the scratching sometimes heard in the corrugated shed. Rats leave evidence: droppings, teeth marks, little dry trails of food. These leave only rearrangements.

A wrench placed on the pump-house floor may be found a week later beneath a different machine, wrapped in a skirt of root hair. A panel which was hanging loose may be bolted back into place with mismatched screws. The conveyor occasionally stops for exactly the time needed for a branch to pass through its rollers without jamming, then resumes. Once, an observer saw a pale hand-shaped cluster of roots withdraw from the motor housing when the casing was opened.

The old workers, when there still were workers, called these presences the fitters.

They were not imagined as ghosts. A ghost would be a person remaining after death. The fitters were something that formed because the work remained after the people did not: habits of repair accumulating in warm metal, damp wire, rotating shafts, and the recurring need for water to go somewhere. They know the machines only as living systems know their own organs. They keep nothing in good condition. Good condition would be foreign to the basin. They preserve function at the edge of collapse.

A belt may be frayed almost entirely through, but it will not break until a seedling has rooted on the other side of the shaft and can take up part of its motion. A pump may grind for years with a bearing worn to red powder, because its vibration has become necessary for a particular ring of moss. A generator may continue to make its exhausted hum long after its fuel source should have vanished, because somewhere below the peat, water is falling through a narrow throat and turning something older than the generator itself.

The fitters are most active at night.

Then there are the lights.

The amber bulbs in the pump house are not merely bulbs. Their glass is cracked, their wiring chewed through and rejoined by corrosion, their housings full of drowned insects and rootlets. Yet each evening they wake in a sequence that resembles recognition. First the lowest lamp, reflected in the submerged window. Then the one above the door. Then a dim glow far inside the building, as though someone has carried a lantern past a room at the rear.

The red lamp on the tower turns because a mechanism inside the tank still turns it. But the beam does not behave entirely as a beam should. It slows when it passes over the central water. It catches in the leaves even where no leaves stand. On damp nights, its red sweep is seen beneath the root mat, moving through black water in long broken bands.

Small creatures follow it.

Not every night. Not in winter. But in the close, thunder-heavy weeks when the basin smells of hot iron and crushed stems, small soft-bodied figures can be seen standing at the margins of the paths after dark. They are not large—perhaps no larger than a child’s hand—and they have the indistinct upright outline of things that have not settled on a shape. Some appear to be woven from wet grass. Some are clothed in strands of pale fungus. Some seem to have faces made only of smooth seed-pod surfaces, without features except a shine where eyes might be.

They do not approach visitors.

They gather where water has changed course, where a pipe has opened, where a plant has been cut. They stand in clusters until dawn, touching the disturbed place with their thin hands. By morning, the cut stem may be bent toward the earth, the spilled water may have disappeared into a new seam, and the open pipe may be packed with a careful plug of moss, clay, and white roots.

If they are seen clearly, they become ordinary things: a knot of vine, a sheaf of reeds, a set of leaves moving in the wake of no wind.

The people who visit—the few who understand that the place is not a ruin—do not call them anything. Naming is a kind of removal. It separates a thing from its surrounding work.

They leave offerings instead, though they would deny that word. A length of copper wire beside a broken junction box. A cup of clean water poured into a dry pocket of moss. A rusted bolt returned to the floor where it was found. Once, a woman who had lost her wedding ring in the basin found it three years later growing inside the hollow stem of a taro leaf, bright and clean as if washed continuously by invisible hands. She put it back.

Most of all, the marsh is inhabited by its old water.

Rain from decades ago remains low in the peat, darkened by tannin and root stain. Water that once filled the tower now rests in the roots of the fig. Water pumped from somewhere beneath the eastern bank returns by seepage through the western moss. It carries traces of every substance it has passed through: clay, rust, pollen, machine oil gone sweet with age, frog skin, root sugar, the sharp green bitterness of crushed leaves.

In the deepest channels, the water may be conscious in the way a mirror is conscious: not by holding a self, but by holding everything that comes near it. It preserves pressures. It repeats distant sounds. It remembers the shapes of pipes that have corroded away.

At certain hours, leaning over one of the brown eyes in the mat, a person may hear the pump house from below: the motor’s slow turning, the belt’s rasp, perhaps even the faint clack of a tool laid down long ago. The sound rises through water that has been listening for a very long time.

So that is who lives there:

the roots that maintain the fullness;

the frogs and silver channel-things moving through its hidden circulation;

the heron, visiting its machinery as another kind of shoreline;

the fitters in the motors and wires;

the small night custodians that gather around every disturbance;

the lights, which have become watchful;

and the old water, which has outlasted every purpose imposed upon it.

None of them own the basin.

Ownership would imply that something could be taken away.

They belong to its ongoing occupation: the long shift in which plant, machine, water, mineral, and memory keep relieving one another without ever leaving.
