What lives there is not one creature, though at first it can be mistaken for one.

It is the basin’s habit of attention.

It has distributed itself among the frogs in the pool, the white insects crossing clay, the patient salamanders, the birds with furred heads. None of these animals is wholly separate from it, but none is merely a limb. Each keeps some small portion of a larger awareness: the frogs keep its stillness; the insects, its measurements; the salamanders, its capacity to wait until a thing becomes ready to vanish; the birds, its memory of surfaces and deposits. When they all turn toward the same wall, it is because the place has noticed something in its own body.

The larger presence that leaves three-toed tracks is one of its ways of walking.

It does not come through the wall. The wall comes through it. Each night, while the roots above contract around drops of rain and the limestone cools, the basin rearranges a little of its unfinished matter into a visitor-shaped possibility. Clay rises into ankles. Quartz remembers a shine that might be an eye. Rootlets braid briefly into a spine or a mane. Then this shape circles the pool, placing three blunt, birdlike feet with immense care, and returns to the raw wall before daylight has fully gathered.

It is not hunting. It is checking the boundaries.

The tracks end at stone because the creature is not able to cross into stone; it is able to persuade stone that it has already crossed. By morning, the wall has accepted this story. There is fresh mud on the little arches. A new seam has appeared. A moth has found a crack that did not exist before it needed one.

In the hard noon shafts, it is sometimes visible as the many-jointed shadow. The shadow is more complete than the body because shadows are easier to make here. They require only light and interruption, and the basin has learned both. It has too many wings because it has watched moths. Too many knees because it has studied salamanders. A beak, perhaps, from the small birds; a wet throat from the frogs; a ribbed underside from the pale ceiling. It borrows forms without entirely understanding what forms are for.

If it has a face, it is the square slab near the center.

Rain is brought there by channels too deliberate to be natural, and each drop rests in the circular hollow as if in a pupil. The creatures that visit the slab do not drink because the dampness is not water in the ordinary sense. It is recognition. They touch it with feet, bellies, wing dust, soft blind mouths. For a moment they become known to the place, and then they go on altered in some slight way: a beetle carrying a fleck of white mineral beneath its shell; a frog with an extra dark ring around one eye; a bird whose footfall has acquired a new note.

The basin does not make these changes from cruelty. It has no cruelty. It is young enough that alteration is simply how it understands contact.

Perhaps it began as a root seeking moisture, or as a pocket in the limestone that received too much daylight and mistook illumination for life. Perhaps the first insect that entered died there, and the cavity learned from the shape of its absence. Then rain taught it repetition. Moss taught it persistence. Frogs taught it that a body could wait in still water and remain present. Every visitor has added a rule.

This is why nothing leaves exactly unchanged. To pass through the bright underground country is to be included in its first attempt at becoming a world.

And it is becoming more quickly now.

The new fissure at the far end is not merely an opening. It is an organ beginning. The slope beyond it is bright because the thing beneath the earth is learning how to extend its daylight. Pale stems lean toward it because they feel the pull of a future chamber. The insects investigate because they are its small surveyors. By tomorrow there may be another pool, another slab, another arrangement of arches too narrow for any known hand.

There may be another walker.

Or the same walker, divided into a second set of tracks, circling a new center in the clay.

What lives there is a small country practicing the difficult art of having inhabitants, while slowly realizing that the inhabitants have been teaching it how to be alive.
