What lives in the basin is not a people who have escaped time, exactly. It is a people who have had to become careful around it.

They are called, by themselves, the **Ishar**, though the word means less “a nation” than “those who keep the places between.” Outsiders who have reached the high passes call them the Stillfolk, the Rain-Holders, the Unhurried. The Ishar accept none of these names with offense or agreement. A name, to them, is a tool for bringing attention to something. If used too often, it begins to wear a groove.

They are neither especially tall nor slight, but their bearing makes them seem deliberate. They stand as if they have been placed rather than having arrived. Their faces tend toward broad cheekbones, dark eyes, and calm mouths; their hair is usually black, brown, or the pale iron-gray that appears in some families without indicating age. Age is difficult among them. An infant becomes a child, a child becomes an adult, adults develop the settled gravity of long practice—but the visible stages are not reliable measures of years.

Some elders have smooth faces and black hair. Some young adults wear silver at their temples. There are women whose children are older in appearance than they are, men who remember teaching skills to ancestors whose names are now carried by living grandchildren, and households where three generations agree that they were all born in the same season, though no one means the same year.

The Ishar do not argue much about this. They do not count age by numbers except for practical records. They speak instead of **turnings**: the number of times one has changed one’s work, one’s household, one’s grief, one’s chosen path through the city. A person may be “five turnings old” even if they have watched the same stars cross the basin for a hundred years. Another, who has never left the court of their birth or altered a habit, may be said to have “not yet entered their first life.”

Their clothing is layered for the cold height and wet air: wool close to the skin, then reed-fiber cloth, hide, or heavy woven capes. They favor colors that can be made from the basin itself—lichen gray, volcanic red, rain-dark blue, goat-brown, the green-black of moss. Bright dyes exist, brought by traders from below, but are reserved for occasions requiring attention: births, joining ceremonies, the opening of a new water jar, the return of a hunting party, the sealing of the dead.

Much of their cloth is patterned with interrupted lines. A path begins at one edge, turns sharply, stops, begins again somewhere else. Rain is shown as a row of beads suspended beneath a cloud. Mountains are triangles with their upper points deliberately unfinished. Children learn the patterns before they learn formal letters, and are told that complete circles are dangerous things to weave unless the circle contains a break.

They do not trust seamless continuance.

Their homes are inhabited not only by families but by accumulations of relationship. A dwelling may contain sisters, former spouses, unrelated apprentices, nursing children, old friends who have simply remained after a shared loss, and travelers who came to trade decades ago and discovered no compelling reason to leave. A household is called a **warmth**, even when the people within it are not affectionate. It is defined by the fire it tends and the cistern court it shares.

No one is expected to live forever in one warmth. Indeed, remaining too long is considered unhealthy unless one has a particular duty: tending an elder, raising a child, keeping a shrine, repairing a wall, making a craft whose pattern requires long familiarity. At intervals no calendar can quite predict, people announce that they have become “too fitted” to a room. They then leave, usually at dawn, taking only a garment, a tool, and perhaps one bowl or bell. Their old sleeping place is not immediately filled. The empty space is left open for a while, so the house may remember that it has changed.

The Ishar have children, though birth is one of the basin’s mysteries rather than its reliable abundance. Some couples wait a long while. Some children are born after no apparent waiting at all. Some are found.

This last fact is spoken of quietly.

A child may be discovered asleep in a reed bed, sitting beneath a condensation gutter, standing barefoot at the edge of a quarry, or crouched in an unused chamber with no sign of how they entered. Such children are called **arrivals**. They are not treated as miraculous in the sense of being holy; miracles, in the Ishar view, are unstable and therefore rude. Arrivals are simply given a warmth, a name, and a set of hands to hold them.

No one asks them too many questions when they begin to speak.

Most resemble the people of the basin. A few do not. There are children with eyes too pale, children who know the names of lower-country fish, children who are frightened of wind though they have never encountered a true storm. The Ishar say that the basin sometimes receives what the world elsewhere has misplaced.

In return, it sometimes keeps more than was intended.

The dead in the red-rock chambers are not thought to sleep. Sleep is temporary and belongs to the living. Nor are the dead believed to be watching. That would be too close a relation, too much attention held without release. They are called the **Set Aside**.

The dead are washed with still water, dressed in clothing without knots, and carried up to the chambers by those whose hands have not touched them often in life. Parents do not bury children; lovers do not bury lovers; the closest family members wait below among the blue reeds or in the shadow of the cliff. This is not cruelty. It is an act of separation.

Before the stone plug is fitted, a small object is placed beside the body: a spoon worn thin by use, a spindle, a goat bell, a broken obsidian blade, a child’s carved animal, a strip of patterned cloth. Never something whole or new. The dead are given evidence that they belonged to the moving, imperfect arrangement of living things.

Then the chamber is sealed.

No one visits. No flowers are left. No names are spoken near the grooves in the cliff.

But in the evenings, especially when the sky is full of stars, people tell stories of the Set Aside around their fires. These stories are not biographies. They are accounts of gestures: the way someone held a cup, the phrase they used when a wall was badly built, the song they always began and never finished. Memory is safer when it does not try to make a person complete again.

The Ishar’s strongest social obligation is maintenance.

A child begins by tending small things: placing fallen roof reeds back in order, clearing moss from a stair edge without removing all of it, carrying a bowl of water without making a ripple, learning which stones may be stepped on and which must be passed by. Adolescents are given public work: goat paths, cistern lips, market awnings, the ropes on tower stairs. Adults take on longer responsibilities—terrace walls, burial chambers, garden boundaries, the old water houses, records of trade and descent.

The greatest honor is not to create a thing admired by all. It is to care for a thing so well that no one notices it has needed care.

Their builders are respected more deeply than their rulers. In fact, the city has no single ruler in the lower-country sense. Each precinct has a **setter**, chosen not for force or inheritance but for an ability to make disputes come to rest. Setters decide where a new house may stand, which garden can become animal-ground, whether a damaged retaining wall must be repaired or left to become a boundary of another kind. They settle trade disagreements by arranging objects on the floor: one stone for each claim, one cord for each promise, one bowl of water for what cannot be resolved.

A good setter does not decide quickly.

The most revered officials are the **watchers of contact**. They inspect new walls, new shrines, repaired doors, joined households, marriages, adoptions, and even friendships that have become too binding. They measure the hand’s-width of air required between old and new stone. They advise people to sleep in separate rooms for a time after an argument has ended. They may tell a family to move a table away from a wall, a craftworker to stop repairing the same cracked pot, or a grieving person to remove a dead loved one’s clothing from the doorway where it has hung too long.

They are not priests, though they attend the central court. They are more like physicians for arrangements.

The priests, if they can be called that, keep the twelve bronze bowls.

They are few, and often chosen from those who have lost their certainty about the basin. This is important. Anyone too convinced that the city is blessed, or too convinced that it is a prison, is considered unfit to look into the water. Bowl-keepers wear plain dark garments and carry polished bronze mirrors facedown. They clean the black paving stones, collect suspended rain from roof gutters in narrow ladles, and speak certain phrases at sunrise and dusk.

They do not claim to know what lies in the bowls.

Some say the bowls show forests rising and falling, seas moving under storms, faces changing, cities crumbling and being rebuilt. Some say they show nothing but the viewer’s own eyes, made old in a moment. Some say that the water does not show another world but a hunger: the part of every living thing that wants to become otherwise.

Children are forbidden to look. Adults are not forbidden. This distinction matters. The Ishar believe a person must be allowed to choose unease once they are old enough to carry it.

There are people in the city who look often.

They are called **the forward-minded**, not as insult but warning. They make small experiments: planting seeds in fresh soil outside the garden boundaries, chipping new channels in the gravel, carrying a bowl of water far up a ridge and watching whether it remains water. They collect scraps of stories from traders about storms, ships, wars, orchards, clocks, and snow that melts. They build wind toys that do not turn. They make wheels and push them along the rare smooth causeways until the wheels stop exactly where they are released.

Some forward-minded people leave through the passes.

Not many return.

Those who do return are changed in ways the basin cannot easily settle. They may speak too loudly. They may flinch when a door closes. Their eyes may follow moving clouds for hours. They bring objects from below: rusted iron nails, paper that yellows, bread that grows stale, seeds that must be planted at the proper time or die. They are welcomed, fed, and listened to, but people give them space. Their belongings are stored in separate rooms. Their stories are heard in short portions.

A returned traveler is never asked, “Why did you come back?”

The answer is understood: even a world that changes can fail to provide a place for a person.

The basin’s animals are not owned in the strictest way. Goats wear cord collars marked for particular warmths, and packs of long-necked grazers are followed, harvested from, and occasionally guided away from gardens. But an animal is regarded as having its own agreement with stillness. A goat that leaves a household is not pursued beyond the third terrace. A raven that begins nesting above a door is given a bowl of water and a few scraps, but no one attempts to tame it. Deer are hunted only when a hunter has first seen the animal on three separate days without seeking it.

This practice is called **being acknowledged**.

Hunters are patient people, often people who have difficulty living in crowded warmths. They travel alone or in pairs, carrying obsidian blades, hide cords, smoke-drying frames, and little clay lamps whose flames do not consume their oil. They do not boast of skill. The best hunter is said to be the one who comes home with meat and no story about themselves.

There are also creatures in the basin that no one puts into useful categories.

In the warm fissures of the upper slopes live black salamanders with bright white throats. They emerge only far enough to place their heads in the cold mist. In certain abandoned cisterns, thin blind fish drift beneath the unmoving surface, though no one knows how they entered. High in the roof gardens, moths with gray wings settle so perfectly on stone that they appear to be flakes of ash until they open and reveal inner markings like gold eyes.

And sometimes, in the old quarry grounds at night, something large walks between the cut walls.

It leaves no tracks. It makes no sound except for the faint click of stone against stone. No two witnesses describe it alike. Some call it a horned animal with a body of wet basalt. Some say it is a person taller than the watchtowers, carrying a ladder over one shoulder. Some say it is only the quarry remembering the mountain from which it was taken.

The Ishar do not seek it.

They leave a clear path through the quarry and never build across it.

Their art is full of unfinishedness. Songs often end before their resolving note. Carved figures have one hand detailed and the other left rough within the stone. Potters deliberately press a thumbprint into the base of a vessel, breaking the perfect circle. Dancers move in slow lines through the courts, pausing at intervals too long for outsiders to bear, then changing direction without signal.

Their favorite instrument is the bell, especially small bronze bells with no clapper. They are struck by hand, once, and allowed to hold their tone until it disappears into the damp air.

They have celebrations, though they do not have anniversaries in the usual sense. They celebrate a completed wall, a recovered child, a first milk goat, the opening of a passage blocked by fallen stone, the return of a traveler, the moment a garden has become too dense and must be thinned. They celebrate a person changing work, a household becoming less crowded, a quarrel reaching its proper ending.

The largest gathering is called the **Making Room**.

On that day—if day is the right word for an event that may happen after an interval of years or weeks—the people of every precinct carry one object from their homes into the broad open spaces of the basin. A broken tool, an old cloak, an unused bowl, a dried herb bundle, a child’s abandoned toy, a stone kept for no reason. They lay these things in long rows across the pale gravel. Then, without speaking, they walk among them until each person finds an object that belongs to someone else and feels able to carry it.

Nothing is traded back.

The old owners do not explain. The new owners do not ask.

By nightfall, the objects have entered other warmths, other uses, other stories. The city has altered by a fraction, which is enough.

For all their gentleness, the Ishar are not without fear. They fear deep cracks opening in the ground, because a crack is an invitation to elsewhere. They fear a rain bead finally falling, and every child is taught what to do if one does: stand still, observe where it lands, do not touch it until a bowl-keeper arrives. They fear a wall that cannot be repaired. They fear a person who cannot let go of a dead body, a ruined house, a former name, a grievance.

Most of all, they fear the possibility that the basin’s mercy is not endless.

Yet another part of them fears that it is.

That is why life in the city, for all its calm, is not empty of courage. To raise a child is courageous when childhood may last an unknown length. To love someone is courageous when separation must be deliberately made. To build a new wall is courageous when every stone risks becoming too fixed. To leave through the pass is courageous. To remain is also courageous.

At dusk, the people come out onto roof gardens, tower platforms, stoops, and the flat black paving around the central pool. They feed goats. They shake condensation from reed mats into jars. They speak softly to one another. They touch the bronze bells and listen to the notes diminish.

Then the stars emerge in their old, innumerable arrangements.

The city below does not darken all at once. Lamps appear one by one beneath eaves. Smoke rises from cooking courts and holds itself in thin vertical threads. Somewhere a child runs across a dusty terrace and leaves footprints behind them. Somewhere an elder smooths one of those prints away with the edge of a sandal, not to erase the child, but to keep the path from deciding it belongs to anyone forever.
