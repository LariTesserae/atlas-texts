What lives there is not a population, exactly. It is a maintenance of presences.

First there is the keeper.

No one sees the keeper arrive. The cable car has not moved in so long that its hanging frame is furred with lichen, and the iron track ends too soon to have brought anyone anywhere. Yet at certain times the water gate is raised by a hand’s breadth. A cracked belt is replaced with one that has been neatly spliced. The brass face of a pressure gauge is wiped clean. Dead lamps are taken from their sockets and new ones, impossibly dry and bright, appear in their place.

The keeper may live in the red-roofed hut. There is a narrow cot inside, a kettle blackened only on one side, an oilskin coat whose shoulders are permanently beaded with water. There are tins of tea, packets of screws sorted by size, a weather log written in a cramped hand that changes every few pages: angular, then round; young, then trembling; hurried, then careful.

The entries do not name a person.

They record reservoir levels. Steam pressure. The depth of the cloud layer. The number of relay strikes heard after midnight.

Some entries are only:

**Wheel holding.**

Or:

**Below active. Kept flow steady.**

Or, after a long gap:

**It asked again in the old way.**

Perhaps the keeper is one person who has survived far beyond the ordinary duration of a human life in the warm wet breath of the mountain. Perhaps the hut receives successive keepers, each instructed to wear the same coat, write in the same book, and never ask what the machinery is for. Perhaps no human keeper remains at all. The gates may be adjusted by a thing that learned hands from watching hands.

There are signs.

The boots on the pipe rack shift position between visits. Mud gathers around them, but never inside. The bench beneath the rain shelter bears the shallow indentation of someone sitting for very long hours, facing the reservoir. On clear nights, a small square of light appears in the hut window. Sometimes a figure crosses it: thin, stooped, carrying a lantern low. Sometimes the silhouette has no head where a head should be, only a pale blur of cloud-brightness. Sometimes it has too many joints.

The keeper is careful not to be encountered.

It leaves food in a sealed tin for strangers: hard bread, dried fruit, mineral tablets, a small folded note reading, in several languages and in a hand increasingly difficult to decipher:

**DO NOT FOLLOW A STOPPED PIPE.**

The pale bird lives there too.

It is older than it looks. Its feathers are not white but the gray-green of stone seen through water, and its long legs have the delicate black joints of a machine’s linkage. It hunts insects in the reservoir and sleeps beneath the wheelhouse, one eye open toward the warm cleft.

It knows the relays.

At each metallic strike it becomes still, not startled but attentive, as though hearing a name spoken from a great distance. If the strike rings clear, it resumes its patient hunting. If the echo returns dull or late, the bird climbs to the reservoir wall and stares into the water until the lamps come on.

There are smaller lives hidden in the wet machinery: moths that lay their eggs in the insulation around warm cables; translucent beetles that feed on the orange exhaust fungus; frogs no larger than thumbnails, their skins black and gold as volcanic glass. They breed in the shallow pools above the engine house. Their calls are nearly inaudible beneath the wheel, a faint rapid ticking that can be mistaken for rain.

In the bamboo reeds lives something with fur.

It is not a fox, though from a distance it borrows the shape of one: low body, narrow muzzle, quick dark movement. It has too many toes, and its tracks circle the pipes rather than crossing them. It eats fallen birds, beetles, and the soft yellow grubs that emerge from the hot ground after tremors. It never approaches the hut while the amber lamps are lit.

When the lamps go out, it comes close enough to press its face against the door.

Sometimes, in the mud outside, there are tracks beside its own: bare human feet, long and narrow, pointed toward the reservoir.

The largest inhabitant is below.

The station was built not to extract anything from the mountain, nor to send power down to the valley. Its machinery is a bridle.

Water is taken from the basin, measured, divided, warmed, cooled, returned. The wheels turn at a particular pace. The belts carry that pace into the engine house. The pistons convert it into a rhythm. The dynamos feed a small but constant current into cables driven deep through boreholes in the black rock.

The purpose is not electricity. Electricity is merely the language the old builders knew for making a pattern endure.

Beneath the shelf, beneath the volcanic walls and the root-tangled soil and the wet chambers where water finds water, something keeps time with the mountain.

It is not a beast curled in a cave. It is too wide for that. It may be a pressure, an intelligence distributed through hot seams and buried reservoirs; a mind made from mineral heat, subterranean water, iron deposits, and the slow rearrangement of stone. The mountain is young because something in it is still deciding what shape to become.

The machinery persuades it to decide slowly.

When the wheel turns, the thing below sleeps in increments. It dreams through the pipes. It dreams the dark bird into its long-legged watchfulness, dreams moss onto the gears, dreams the keeper into whatever form is needed to tighten bolts and read gauges and open gates before pressure rises too far.

The relay is not a signal sent by the station.

It is an answer.

Once, perhaps, the people who built this place tried to speak downward. They found that a regular pulse of water, metal, and current could be felt in the hot chambers under the rock. They learned that the mountain answered after a delay: a click, a resonance, a tremor in a pipe. They made the station larger. They gave it reservoirs, spillways, insulated cables, a cable car for supplies, a hut for keepers.

Then the valley forgot why it had been built.

The station did not.

If the waterwheel stops, the ordinary sounds of the basin become suddenly distinct. Water still drips from the cliff. Steam still sighs from the pipes. Ferns still gather their weight of mist. But beneath these sounds comes another: a slow interior movement, too deep to be called a rumble. It is like a stone door being opened underwater.

The bird leaves the reservoir then.

The furred thing vanishes into the reeds.

The keeper, if there is one, goes at once to the gatehouse, carrying a lantern that burns blue at the center.

And from cracks in the volcanic wall, warm air begins to breathe outward in measured intervals, as though something immense beneath the mountain has remembered that there is a surface above it, and has begun to count the distance.
