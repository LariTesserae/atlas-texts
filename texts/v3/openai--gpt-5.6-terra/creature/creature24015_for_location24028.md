What lives there has learned not to belong to any one layer.

The obvious creatures are the canopy-dwellers: things built for distance rather than ground. They move from root to vine to leaf-rib without ever needing to descend into the cold ravines. Some are small enough to travel in flocks, six-limbed and feathered in oily colors, their hooked feet able to close around a vine even in sleep. They feed on pollen, soft bark, and the pale grubs that nest in the warm seams of the great plants. Their voices are high and intricate. A flock calling overhead can sound, for a while, like rain deciding where to fall.

Others are large enough that their passage alters the forest.

The antlered climbers are among these. They have broad, divided feet, soft underneath and hard around the edges, so they can grip wet leaf-skins and fractured basalt alike. Their antlers do not branch like those of lowland animals; they spread forward in flattened, shovel-like fans, used to draw down hanging growth and strip it of shoots. In rutting season the males strike their antlers against hollow trunks. The sound travels through the plant itself, a low wooden concussion felt underfoot many breaths before the animal is seen.

They are not graceful on the ground. There, among fallen fronds and root-buttresses, they become uncertain, knees high, heads turning constantly toward sounds that arrive from the wrong direction. But in the upper growth they move with frightening ease. A herd can cross a ravine on vineways too narrow for a person’s arm, each animal placing its feet precisely in the depressions made by those before it.

The long-winged gliders nest in the cliffs above the canopy. Their bodies are narrow and warm-blooded, wrapped in dense velvet fur that holds beads of mist until they shiver them loose. Their wings are not feathered. They are sheets of tough translucent skin stretched between elongated limbs, dark-veined and faintly luminous at their trailing edges. When they glide at night, their wings catch whatever starlight survives the clouds. A traveler looking up may see a line of moving gray radiance pass silently over an opening in the leaves, then hear the cry afterward: a long tearing sound, as though cloth were being ripped somewhere inside the fog.

They eat fruit, eggs, carrion, and sometimes one another. The young are carried folded against the adult’s chest, hidden beneath the wing membrane, and remain there long after they are able to climb. When disturbed, a colony will launch all at once from a cliff-face. Their departure produces a downdraft cold enough to frost exposed skin.

In the root-mats below live the pale things.

No one sees much of them except after a collapse, when torn mats expose their tunnels. They are soft-bodied burrowers, blind or nearly so, scaled in thin overlapping plates the color of old milk. They have many short digging limbs and blunt heads fringed with feelers. Alone they are not remarkable. In numbers they are part of the mountain’s anatomy. They eat dead root fibers, fungal growth, shed bark, mineral-rich mud, and the bodies of anything unable to escape a sinkhole. Their tunnels aerate the soil that does not yet exist; their castings become the black, fertile substance in which new plants take hold.

At night they rise close beneath the surface, especially where warmth leaks from the rock. Then the root-mat moves in a broad slow pulse. It may look, at first, like wind passing through grass. If one stands still, one can hear the granular friction of thousands of bodies turning in the dark.

There are smaller lives everywhere: translucent beetles beneath the bark, frost-wing moths that drink from luminous leaf-ribs, mites that live only in the warm folds where a trunk-vine embraces stone. Little black birds peck resin from exposed roots and become trapped in it often enough that the people collect their bodies, hardened and amber-coated, for charms. There are ribbon-thin snakes that climb by pressing their bellies into the damp fuzz of the bark. There are slug-like grazers larger than dogs, plated in blue-black mineral scales, leaving paths of bright mucus that freeze before morning.

The predators are difficult to name because they are rarely seen whole.

One kind lives in the upper canopy and is known by its silence. It is said to have a body like a cat, though longer and lower, with too many joints in the forelimbs and a hide covered in shaggy growth that resembles hanging moss. It does not stalk in the way a ground predator stalks. It waits folded into the underside of a leaf, or stretched along a vine, so still that its weight becomes part of the plant’s natural sag. When it moves, it moves downward.

The people call it the leaf-shadow.

It kills climbers, gliders, and anyone who mistakes an old scratch-mark for a safe handhold. Its cry is seldom heard directly. More often the forest gives it back in pieces: a cough from a stem behind you, a soft bark from a ravine wall, a wet exhalation somewhere high in the dark. The safest response is not to run. Running tells it which way the weight is shifting.

Another predator belongs to the ravines. It has no need to climb. It follows warmth, vibration, and the mineral scent of blood. Hunters who have seen it describe a low pale body with a broad mouth and a mane of sensory tendrils that tremble in the air. It moves through root tunnels that seem too small to contain it. It can remain buried beneath a mat for hours, listening through the roots.

This is why people do not sit directly on the ground in the lower country.

But the greatest living things are the plants themselves, though calling them plants is not entirely sufficient.

The root-mats exchange signals over enormous distances. A vine cut on one cliff may cause leaf-ribs to close on another. A flock’s feeding frenzy can trigger a slow flush of bitter sap through an entire hanging grove. In certain weather, when lightning crawls inside the clouds, the canopy responds with a dim whitening: millions of pale veins brightening at once, then fading in waves across the valleys.

The people say the forest is not awake as an animal is awake. It does not watch. It does not choose. But it remembers pressure.

Where generations have climbed, vines thicken. Where camps have been made, roots gradually lift and weave themselves into low walls. Places where blood has been spilled become crowded with thornless flowering growth whose scent is sweet enough to make a person dizzy. No one claims this is mercy. No one claims it is malice. The mountain-forest simply incorporates what happens within it.

The human inhabitants are few, scattered, and more migratory than settled. They are not masters of the place. They are harvesters, listeners, and careful borrowers.

Their clothing is layered against wet cold: bark-fiber cloth beneath hides from the padded climbers, capes made of overlapping leaf-skin cured over smoke, gloves with stone-hard resin worked into the palms. They bind bright lichen into their hair or hang it from belts, not because it gives much light, but because a companion’s green glow is easier to keep track of than a human face in the canopy dark.

They have routes rather than roads, seasons rather than territories. A family may spend the coldest months in a vent-cave, tending baskets of pale root-fungus and smoking meat. When the seed pods swell, they climb into the higher growth on ladder towers and harvest before the gliders take them. When the heavy rains begin, they move out of the ravines. When the mountain hums, they leave everything that cannot be carried.

Their children learn the place through touch before they learn it through words: which vines pulse with sap, which leaf-stalks are hollow, which bark is safe to chew for water, which kind of silence means snow and which means teeth.

They tell stories about an old time when the peaks were bare and the wind had nowhere to go but across stone. In those stories the first roots came upward through cracks, seeking the sky. They found it too cold and drew themselves into vast coverings. The animals followed the warmth. Then people followed the animals.

Whether that is true matters less than the lesson carried inside it.

Nothing here lives alone. The climber needs the vine. The vine needs the stone it is slowly breaking. The stone needs the root-mat to keep from falling. The burrowers need what dies. The hunters need what climbs. The people need all of it, and are needed by none of it.

That is what makes them careful.
