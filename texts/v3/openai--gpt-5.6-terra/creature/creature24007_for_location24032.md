What lives there is not easily seen from above.

At a distance, the plain appears empty because its inhabitants do not stand against the horizon, do not cross open ground, do not make tracks, and do not gather beneath the sky. They live in the thickness of the made land: inside retaining walls, under paving, in the cool hollows behind tile faces, in the unfilled portions of drains and vaults. A traveler could walk for days across the bright surface and never know that, beneath the soles of their feet, the plain was inhabited.

They are called, if they need a name, the **seam-dwellers**.

A seam-dweller is not an animal, though it can move; not a plant, though it grows; not a machine, though it uses the shapes left by machines and hands. In its youngest state it is only a darkened line in mortar: a thread of moisture holding fine dust, salts, spores of mineral, and something more difficult to identify. The line thickens where the wall is cool. It develops a faint skin, almost transparent, which catches grit. Over years, the grit becomes bonded. The seam acquires a direction.

Eventually it makes a body.

The bodies are low, jointed arrangements of borrowed material. A common one is no larger than a forearm: a fan of thin tile splinters set around a damp knot of clay, with pale salt crusts forming its outer surfaces. Another resembles a collapsed brickwork hand, each “finger” a narrow wedge of compacted dust extending into a crack. In deep cisterns there are larger forms, built from layers of mineral film and softened mortar, hanging from ceilings or rising from the waterline like unfinished columns.

Their bodies are never entirely their own. They are portions of wall that have begun to prefer one arrangement over another.

A seam-dweller does not walk in the usual sense. It loosens material on one side of itself and hardens material on the other. A few grains at a time, it migrates through a joint, down a drain, across the underside of a slab. The process is too slow to watch, except after rain. Then one may see a pale ridge of fresh mortar where none was present before, or find that a crack has narrowed overnight beneath a wall.

This is not repair exactly.

The seam-dwellers do not preserve buildings. They have no reverence for an original shape. A doorway may be sealed because its shadow holds dampness; a culvert may be widened because water has taught a colony to spread there; an old stair may gradually soften into a ramp under their patient pressure. They favor passages, cavities, surfaces that catch runoff, places where warm material meets cool material. Their work resembles construction only because construction is what their bodies are made of.

The largest colonies live beneath the dry basins.

There, under the tiled floors, are networks of dark porous chambers no human hand ever excavated. In a dry season they are dormant, contracted into hard seams and chalky patches. During rain they wake all at once. Water enters through the old spillways and vanishes into hairline gaps. Beneath the surface, the seam-dwellers swell. Their mineral skins soften. Thin membranes open. Silt is drawn through their passages, sorted by weight and grain size, held in pockets, released into lower chambers.

The old hydrology of the plain is not merely preserved by them; it is inhabited.

After a storm, a basin that should have filled may remain unexpectedly shallow because the dwellers below have taken the water inward. Elsewhere, water may rise through a floor of cracked tiles, clear at first, then cloudy with lime, then threaded with red clay. Such places are not springs. They are the plain breathing through its residents.

They communicate by changes in material.

A colony learns of rain not from the sound on the roof, but from the cooling of a brick two terraces away. It learns of a collapsed wall through a shiver transmitted along buried timber ribs. Salt blooming on one surface can cause a cistern colony half a mile distant to seal itself into dormancy. Information passes as pressure, dampness, heat, alkalinity, settling weight. To them, the plain is not a collection of ruins. It is one enormous body of touch.

Their nearest equivalent to language is pattern.

A sequence of three pale salt bands across a joint may mean that a drain has become blocked. A triangular patch of dark moss, encouraged but never consumed, may mark a stable source of condensation. Some long walls carry alternating courses of old red brick and new whitish mineral aggregate, not because anyone rebuilt them according to a plan, but because generations of seam-dwellers moved through them in response to the same seasonal pulses. From a distance the pattern looks intentional. Up close it is full of small accommodations: a course stopped for a root, bent around a void, thinned where the wall was too hot to inhabit.

They have no faces. Yet they possess attention.

A lone seam-dweller may gather beneath the edge of a slab for decades, holding itself motionless while heat passes through the day above it. If a new crack opens nearby, the creature will turn toward it—not quickly, not visibly, but with a certainty that becomes evident years later when the old body has gone dry and a new line of dark mortar extends into the breach.

They do not see light. They know it indirectly through warmth. Twilight is, to them, the long loosening of the upper surfaces. Night is not darkness but the period when the heat held in brick begins to descend. They rise closer to the surface after sunset, especially on the white terraces where daytime would bake them brittle. In the hours before dawn, one may sometimes find faint wet tracery on paving: loops, branches, concentric smears of mineral-rich damp. By sunrise these have dried into nothing more remarkable than stains.

The small green plants are their companions, though neither depends wholly on the other.

Grass roots open the joints seam-dwellers can enter. Moss preserves a little moisture against the mortar. Reeds draw hidden water upward, and when they die their roots leave soft passages through packed fill. In return, the dwellers stabilize the sediment around them, collect runoff into their reach, and slowly build the tiny ledges where a seed can catch.

A shrub growing beside a broken wall is often standing above an old colony. Its roots descend into the cool, porous mass beneath. The colony does not feed on the shrub. It has no appetite in that sense. But it shapes the dampness that allows the shrub to endure. When the shrub dies, the colony eventually incorporates its dried root channels into itself, lining them with clay and salt until they become another kind of drain.

There are, in the deepest places, forms that have lived longer than any visible structure.

They occupy the undersides of buried roofs and the interiors of sealed cisterns. They are large enough to alter the settling of whole courts. A vaulted chamber may remain standing because one of these ancient dwellers has filled its cracks with dense mineral tissue; another may collapse because the resident beneath it has gradually drawn its supporting fill into a lower channel. Their bodies cannot be separated from the architecture around them. If exposed, they look like geological formations: layers of lime, brick dust, black wood fibers, compacted clay, and a wet darkness at the center.

Some are said to remember water that has not fallen for centuries.

This memory is not a story. It is a shape of readiness: a dry channel kept open; a wall deliberately porous; a buried chamber angled toward a vanished source of runoff. When rain finally returns along an old route, the plain responds with impossible accuracy. A covered trench fills. A long-dead fountain mouth releases a thin brown flow. A bridge across an empty basin suddenly finds itself spanning water again.

The seam-dwellers are not immortal, but death does not have a clean boundary among them.

When one dries beyond recovery, its body becomes a patch of unusually firm mortar or a vein of salt-bonded fill. Other dwellers move into it. The material retains its tendencies: its preferred porosity, its stored mineral, the shape of old passages. Thus a colony may persist through the disappearance of every individual body that composed it. A wall remembers its inhabitants by becoming more like them.

Occasionally, after a season of unusual rain, the plain produces something that resembles a person at a distance.

It may be a tall accumulation of clay and tile shards in the mouth of a culvert, balanced around a column of damp air. It may have two supports, an upright trunk, and a hooded suggestion where slabs have leaned together. Such forms last only until the next dry heat, when they crack, settle, and become part of the paving. They are not people. They do not go anywhere. But the first sight of one can be difficult to forget, especially at dusk, when the materials retain the day’s warmth and seem briefly to stand of their own accord.

That is the closest the plain comes to making a citizen.

The true inhabitants remain lower, slower, and more patient. They live in every place where one material touches another and neither has wholly won: brick against clay, tile against dust, salt against mortar, root against crack, water against heat. They are not the makers of the plain, and yet the plain continues to make itself in their image.

Their civilization has no houses because the whole land is a house.

It has no roads because every seam is a passage.

It has no monuments because every monument, given enough time, becomes a body, a shelter, a channel, a memory, and then the ground beneath another ground.
