Nothing lives there in the animal sense. There are no inhabitants who arrive at a door, sleep in chambers, carry tools, or look up when a sound is made. The high country has no citizens.

It has tissues.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the small ones: dry-root filaments in the pavement seams, pale pressure bladders under the bark, heat-sensitive hairs growing around vents, resin glands nested in old bolt holes. They do not appear alive unless watched for a very long time. A narrow crack in a wall acquires a dark seam; the seam thickens; its surface develops ribs; eventually it becomes a load-bearing root and closes the crack forever. A patch of dead roofing leaf, brittle as glass, is gradually stitched from beneath by red-black fibers. The repair has no urgency, but it has no distraction either.

They are not separate plants, exactly. They are organs dispersed through a construction too large to recognize itself all at once.

Some portions are adapted to listening, if listening is the word. Fine root fans spread along the undersides of bridges and around tension-vines. They register strain, heat, mineral vapor, the weakening pulse of old machinery. Where a rail begins to fail, these fans thicken. They knot around its joints and exude a hard dark resin that smells of pepper and hot coins. If the rail cannot be saved, the growth does not mourn it. It directs weight away, builds a new rib beneath it, and lets the old metal become embedded in bark.

Other portions digest.

Behind the walls are slow chambers of compression: fleshy mills lined with hard silica teeth, dry fermenters swollen with stored gases, black throats that take in fallen leaves and return them as charcoal fiber, insulation felt, sealant, or new seed. What seems from outside to be an abandoned processing building may contain a living digestive cycle hundreds of meters deep. Its walls flex inward. Its internal shelves move by fractions. In certain seasons—or what serves for seasons here—great quantities of obsolete material vanish into it: collapsed platforms, dead towers, wind-scoured leaf sheets, failed vines, even machinery still turning weakly from a previous age.

The high country does not eat in order to grow. It grows in order to keep eating itself.

There are also the great thermal beings: the furnace-orchards, the chimney stalks, the swollen red-lit bladders suspended in girdles of root and wire. They are less like individual organisms than like weather systems held in place. A thermal bladder may spend decades filling with gases from the deep strata. Its skin darkens, hardens, develops coppery folds. Then, during a mechanical storm, valves open across an entire district. The bladder contracts. Heat passes through the vineworks. Presses wake. Seeds are fired into storage vaults. Dead growth is rendered. For a few hours the basin seems to breathe through a thousand mouths.

When the work is done, the bladder hangs slack and black again, not exhausted but patient.

The towering stalks are inhabitants too. They stand in clustered groves around shafts of heat, their lower tissues armored with old collars and ladders. Inside them, fluids rise without water: mineral suspensions, condensed vapors, bitter oils, salts carried in dense gels. Their upper fronds do not seek sunlight in the old manner. They open to the black air and collect what the high atmosphere offers—chemical traces, dust, warmth radiated from the ground, perhaps charge from distant storms. At dawn, if there is a dawn beyond the roof of leaves, they may shut themselves tightly as clenched hands.

Some stalks are centuries dead, though death does not remove them from the population. Their hollow interiors become warehouses, flues, nurseries, and resonators. New tissues occupy their walls. Roots pass through their old vascular channels. A dead stalk remains socially useful. In the high country, that is nearly indistinguishable from living.

The seed-vaults are the nearest thing the place has to descendants.

They are not pretty in the ordinary way. Most resemble armored fruits or sealed boilers: hard-ribbed ovals, black gourds studded with metal clamps, long pods wrapped in cable-vines. Many are built directly into old factory walls. Their contents are not merely seeds for future plants. They preserve instructions in flesh: the proportions for a bridge, the geometry of a pressure chamber, the resin chemistry needed to bind a failing tower, the shape of a leaf broad enough to roof an avenue.

When a district is damaged beyond repair, the seed-vaults may open.

They do so without spectacle. A seam appears. Dry membranes emerge and drift away on furnace currents. Then thick pale shoots descend or rise, already shaped by the constraints of the place. Within days they have found anchor points. Within months they are bearing weight. Within years their origin is impossible to identify; they have become another old part of the landscape, entangled with everything around them.

Perhaps this is reproduction. Perhaps it is maintenance.

At the center, the arboreal furnace is different.

It may be the oldest surviving body in the basin, or the first machine into which the other bodies were grafted. Its trunk is too vast to be understood from the ground. The iron bands around it are no longer external restraints; bark has flowed over them, consumed their bolts, used their tension. Its black windows are not windows. At times they draw in air. At other times they release the low red glow of internal combustion, and the surrounding structures respond.

This response is subtle. A bridge that had been growing outward begins to curve inward over several seasons. A root road deepens in the direction of the furnace. A grove of heat-bladders buds more heavily on the side facing it. No command can be heard. No signal travels visibly. Yet the basin slowly gathers itself around the central engine as iron filings gather around a hidden magnet.

If there is a mind in the high country, it is not located there, and it is not located anywhere else.

It exists as coordination without awareness: a remembered preference for stability, heat, reuse, enclosure, pressure, endurance. It is written into the way a broken thing is met by reinforcing fibers; into the way waste becomes structure; into the way growth stops when it enters empty space but flourishes when it finds a burden to carry. The plants do not think of the factory. The factory does not think of the forest. The distinction has worn away.

There may be rarer inhabitants still.

In the deepest dry wells, where old shafts descend through layers of bark and obsolete machinery, there are black cushions of tissue that never rise toward the heat. They grow around forgotten workings, enveloping wheel teeth, tool racks, pipes, and sealed doors. They are cold compared with the rest of the complex. Their surfaces hold a faint dull sheen, as though wet, though no water can be found on them. Sometimes they produce small translucent growths, thin as fingernails, which tremble when the great root engines shift.

No one knows what purpose they serve. Perhaps they filter poisons. Perhaps they preserve old metals. Perhaps they are the remnants of an earlier breed, made for a climate that no longer exists. They may be dying very slowly. They may be the only things in the basin capable of remembering rain.

And in the highest crowns, where wind strips the leaf roofs down to black lace, there are signal flowers.

They grow in deliberate rows along old towers and ventilation ridges. By day they are shut, hard and dark. In the blackness they open one at a time, revealing phosphorescent interiors: blue-white, amber, a deep arterial red. Their lights travel in patterns across the basin. One ridge wakes, then another, then a line of lamps far away answers.

They do not call to animals. They do not guide travelers. They may be timing mechanisms for the machinery below, marking pressure cycles, heat migrations, safe moments for a bridge to shift or a vault to open. Or perhaps the high country, having no eyes, makes lights merely because some ancient instruction still says that darkness should be answered.

So what lives there is a civilization after civilization has lost its faces.

It lives as bark over steel, as heat held in roots, as dry seed and practiced repair. It lives in the slow decision of a vine to become a cable, in the patient conversion of a fallen roof into fuel, in the red breathing of the central trunk. It has no names for itself, no language that can be heard, no need for witness.

But it is not empty.

The entire basin is full of bodies at work.
