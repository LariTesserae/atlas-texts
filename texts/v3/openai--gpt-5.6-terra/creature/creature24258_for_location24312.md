What lives in the valley is not one people so much as a set of arrangements that learned to speak.

There are the people born in the upper terraces, pale-eyed from the glare off cistern lids, wind-browned around the cheeks, who can tell from a railing’s vibration whether the eastern gust has reached the fifth ridge. They grow up with harness loops sewn into their coats. Their first useful skill is knotting: a water knot, a load knot, a child knot for fastening oneself to a stair rail while both hands carry tools. They know the roofs as other children know fields. They can name the greenhouse districts by smell—tomato heat, wet basil, fungal compost, citrus blossom caught in warm fan exhaust.

They are sometimes called ridgefolk by people lower down, though no one agrees whether that is affectionate. The ridgefolk tend wind screens, fog nets, antennae, solar canopies, and the old water tanks whose interiors are painted with dates of drought. Their homes are narrow, heavily tethered, and full of things that make little sound: cloth doors, rubber-footed stools, hanging racks rather than cupboards. A dropped spoon can travel a long way in a city built from slopes.

Below them live the keepers of flow.

Some work openly in blue or green maintenance coats, carrying key rings that look ceremonial because they are. Others are unofficial: grandmothers who know which drain accepts citrus peel and which must never receive cooking grease; children who clear leaf dams from stair grates for a coin; tenants who can hear a pipe beginning to fur before it bursts. Water inspectors walk the valley with small hammers, tapping copper, iron, ceramic, old polymer, listening for hollow places. They are treated with the wary respect reserved for doctors and judges. A person can lie about a debt, an age, a marriage, even a name. It is much harder to lie about a leak.

The water itself has a complicated citizenship. Every household has a certain amount of it, but no household entirely owns it. The first rain from a roof belongs to washing. The second belongs to storage. The overflow belongs to the terrace below unless it has crossed a medicinal garden, in which case it must be marked. Greywater is named according to its previous life: bathwater, laundry water, bean-rinse, boiler-cool, roof-first, roof-last. Arguments about water are rarely loud. They involve diagrams, old valves, inheritance claims, and people standing ankle-deep in a channel with their trousers rolled up.

There are canal families whose apartments open directly onto the narrow runs of moving water. Their floors are raised. Their kitchens have grates in them. They keep polished boots by the door and herbs in every window. In the wet season their children sleep through the sound of miniature falls in the walls; in dry months they wake at the change in pressure when the upper cisterns are opened. Their oldest people remember floods not by year but by height: the one that reached the second blue tile, the one that carried a market stall into the fish court, the one that filled the reservoir plaza deeply enough for adults to swim.

The gardeners are everywhere, but they do not form a single class. They are roof farmers, wall tenders, compost clerks, seed archivists, graft specialists, bee listeners, and people whose entire reputation rests on making one difficult species survive where it should not. A banana grower on a warm lower terrace may never speak to a citrus keeper on an exposed upper court, though their grafting notes may pass between them for generations.

Plants determine addresses. People say they live above the bitter oranges, behind the three-story bean trellis, under the old fig lift, next to the blue bamboo. A family may be known for a vine trained across an entire block, its trunk emerging from a courtyard floor and climbing six levels before it fruits near a public stair. The vine is older than the apartments around it. It has survived three fires, two changes in municipal borders, and a failed attempt to classify it as infrastructure.

There are people whose work is to negotiate such things.

The valley produces archivists in unusual numbers. They maintain ledgers of load-bearing roots, bee flight corridors, compost rights, pipe access, shade disputes, roof inheritance, and the legal status of structures that began as sheds and became neighborhoods. Their offices are crowded with maps that cannot lie flat: layered transparencies of drains, foundations, gardens, power lines, old rail routes, ventilation channels, and customary goat paths.

A goat path can be a legal matter. Goats belong to wall cooperatives, families, schools, temples, and occasionally to themselves in the way of animals that have become too familiar with human systems to be called feral. They wear little bells, but the bells are muffled with leather during the sleeping hours. They are led along steep ramps by goat boys and goat women, though neither title says much about age. The work requires balance, patience, and a sense of which plants are nuisance growth and which are merely unloved. Goats have eaten through more boundary disputes than surveyors have settled.

In the boiler districts live people who prefer the city’s underside.

Their windows are small, their ceilings low, their walls always warm. They mend pumps, rebuild motors, skim mineral scale from heat exchangers, and know the names of machines installed before their grandparents were born. Boiler workers are said to marry late because their work has already taught them too much about pressure. Their children play games of hiding in service corridors and are scolded with technical vocabulary. Their cats are broad, soot-colored, unafraid of bangs, and treated as fellow employees.

The machinery has its own animal population: geckos in junction boxes, silverfish in control cabinets, spiders between rarely opened switch panels, rats large enough to earn nicknames. There are stories of cats riding maintenance sleds through the hot tunnels, and of a particular old tom appearing wherever a pump failure is about to occur. The maintenance crews deny superstition while leaving him scraps.

Birds live in the city with an assurance that exceeds that of most people. Pigeons occupy the hollow spaces behind cladding and have become so patterned by district that experienced bird keepers can identify their origin by the color of dust on their wings. Swifts belong to the ventilation towers. They vanish into dark slots at dusk and return in waves, their calls briefly making every alley sound like a shoreline. The balcony beekeepers claim that bees understand elevation better than humans do: they know which level is flowering, which level is hot, which courtyard has recently painted its railings with something poisonous.

The frogs are citizens of the lowest basins. They come out after rain, when the drains fill and the reservoir plaza turns to mirror-water. People speak of them collectively—the green singers, the pipe frogs, the gutter choir—but individual neighborhoods defend particular populations fiercely. A child who disturbs eggs in a drainage garden may be assigned a month of nighttime watch duty, crouched beside a shallow channel with a lantern hooded in red cloth.

There are night workers who barely see the bright valley at all. Mushroom cultivators occupy old freight chambers. Laundry crews work beneath terraces where condensation is reliable. Fish keepers tend warmed tanks fed by greenhouse overflow. Their fish are silver, black, orange, engineered to tolerate close water and irregular temperatures; some are beautiful, some are edible, some are both and therefore expensive. In the hours after midnight, these workers move through the city’s lower passages carrying buckets, cloth bundles, tools, and jars of culture. They know which bridges are quieter after rain and which stairwells are occupied by sleeping dogs.

The dogs belong to everybody and nobody. Market dogs have territories defined by food smells rather than streets. One may sleep under a spice stall in the morning, inspect a butcher’s drain at noon, and take up evening guard near the reservoir gates. They wear scraps of colored cord when a neighborhood has claimed responsibility for feeding and vaccinating them. Occasionally a dog is elected, ceremonially, as the mascot of a block. It receives no special authority, only a better collar.

There are traders who move vertically rather than horizontally. They carry baskets on cable lifts, push electric carts up the gentler ramps, use hand winches on the narrow stairs, or send goods by pulley from balcony to balcony. A person may make a living carrying one thing upward and another downward: compost up, citrus down; repaired fan blades up, mushrooms down; dry cloth up, wet cloth down; seed trays up, schoolchildren’s messages down.

The old rail-bed gardeners have their own stubborn culture. Many are descended from freight workers, though the trains stopped before some of them were born. They preserve the signal lamps, repaint old carriage numbers, and hold meals in tunnel mouths during the dry season. Their raised beds are unusually orderly, perhaps because rails remain beneath the soil. Squash, beans, gourds, and climbing flowers follow routes laid for machinery, turning the former industrial lines into green corridors that are part farm, part shrine, part practical shortcut.

The valley also contains people devoted to emptiness, though they are never entirely successful.

They tend small rooms in the crowded places: a quiet chamber built inside an unused water tank; a temple between two ventilation shafts; a reading hall behind a wall of moss-fed brick; a clinic with a roof garden designed to receive only rain sound. They maintain silence with curtains, felt, rubber seals, and social agreement. In a city where every surface is useful, an unproductive square of shadow is a luxury. These keepers of quiet do not claim that silence is natural. They say it must be cultivated, like citrus.

Children live most completely in the interstices.

They know crawlways beneath decking, shortcut ladders behind billboards, the shallow spots in the reservoir after rain, and the places where warm air emerges from cracked ducts in winter. Their games are shaped by the city’s rules: they race floating leaf boats down drainage channels, play at cistern scheduling, trade beetle shells and spent fuses, build tiny gardens in abandoned bolts holes, and dare one another to identify a household from the smell of its compost chute.

They are taught early that downhill is consequential. A tossed object, a spilled dye, a careless handful of grit may become somebody else’s drinking water, somebody else’s fish pond, somebody else’s bean bed. This makes them careful in ways outsiders mistake for solemnity. But they are not solemn. They are expert at making fun of adults who hold meetings about a pipe while standing under the pipe’s obvious leak.

Old people are the valley’s deepest strata. Some have never left it; some came up from lower cities, coastal ruins, dry plains, or places said to have actual soil. They sit near warm walls and remember former uses for rooms: this clinic was a stamping works, that school a loading bay, this garden an engine house, that apartment a refrigeration chamber. Their memories are often contested, not because anyone calls them liars, but because the city has reused itself too many times for a single past to remain clean.

They are consulted when a wall is opened. Behind plaster or sheet metal, an old stair may appear, a sealed conduit, a workshop sign, a bird nest, a forgotten drainage channel. Then people gather: engineers, gardeners, children, archivists, whoever happens to live nearest. The old person is asked what they recall. Sometimes they remember nothing. Sometimes they say, “There was a woman here who made shoes,” and this is enough to delay demolition for an afternoon.

The city’s dead live there too, though not in a separate district. Their names persist in valve labels, public planters, rooftop orchards, lift shafts, and patches of mismatched pavement. A stair repaired by a particular crew may carry their initials stamped into the replacement tread. A family that once kept bees may be remembered by a line of yellow paint on a railing. The dead are not imagined as gone from the system. Their decisions are still holding up a wall, directing runoff, shading somebody’s tomatoes.

And among all these lives are things less easy to name: molds that make old concrete soft enough for roots; heat-loving bacteria in compost vats; lichens on the northern faces of tanks; the minute crustaceans that appear in temporary rooftop pools; seeds carried in pigeon droppings and lodged in gutter seams. These are not wild, exactly. They have permits sometimes, enemies often, and always a place in somebody’s records.

Even the city itself lives there.

It breathes through louvers and stairwells. It sweats down pipes. It digests food scraps, rust, leaf litter, broken furniture, human labor, rain. It grows scar tissue around damage. It remembers weight. It complains in groans, knocks, drips, fan whines, and the occasional long metal sigh that sends everyone on a nearby terrace pausing for half a second before deciding whether to call someone.

Its inhabitants do not think of themselves as living on a mountain, or inside a machine, or above the clouds. They live in a place where a home may be a roof over a workshop over a tank over a garden over an older home; where a forest can be climbed by stairs; where weather is partly manufactured and maintenance is a civic season.

They live in the valley because it holds water, because it holds heat, because so many hands have taught it how to keep holding.
