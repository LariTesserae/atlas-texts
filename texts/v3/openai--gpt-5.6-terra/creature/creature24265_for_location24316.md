What lives in the low country is not numerous, but it is difficult to count, because much of it spends its life pretending to be stone.

The smallest things live first.

They inhabit the wet margins where water touches mineral and neither has yet decided what it will become. They are threads, skins, and dark granular veils; they spread over submerged rock in stains so thin that they vanish when looked at from above. In the cracks beneath the ridges they form the black filaments that recoil from touch. There, away from open air, they knit themselves through waterlogged pores and feed on the metals leached from the new ground: iron, sulfur, copper, salts with bitter names. They do not need light. Light is an inconvenience to them. When exposed, they collapse into grey froth or oily dust, and the rock keeps their taste.

Other colonies live inside the pale mineral itself. They make no visible bodies. Their presence is known by the slow appearance of strange colors beneath a crust—an amber cloud in a white shelf, a green dimness growing within clear stone, a pulse of purple spreading through a ridge after several days of cold. They breathe through the land’s wetness. They alter the water as it passes. Some pools are black because of depth; others are black because something in them has been patiently digesting the mountain.

The jointed things beneath the stone lips are built around these colonies.

At first they seem to be deposits: little fans of chalky segments, hanging like badly formed stalactites; knuckled strips of grey material folded into the shadow. But when the cold deepens, they detach one limb at a time and rearrange themselves. Their bodies are narrow, many-hinged, and almost flat, with each segment plated in the same salts that coat the ridges. Their undersides are soft and dark. They cling to stone using rows of minute hooked feet, each foot tasting the surface before it takes hold.

They have no eyes because there is little use for eyes beneath a lip of rock, in mist, in water, in the permanent weak half-light. Instead, their bodies are tuned to pressure. A drop falling several arm-lengths away can travel through the shelf and reach them as a small bright disturbance. They know the approach of splitting stone long before the crack opens. They know when a pool is filling, when a channel is emptying beneath them, when something large turns over in the dark water.

Their clicking is not speech in any simple sense. It is a kind of collective testing. In cold weather, when the ridges tighten and the crusts become brittle, a cluster will click one by one. The sound moves through the stone. Other clusters reply from ledges across the channel. After a time the whole basin may be making those tiny hard sounds beneath the greater booming of water. Then, having established where the safe thickness lies and where a fracture is beginning, they become still.

They feed by scraping mineral bloom from the stone and by grazing on the dark films in sheltered seams. Their mouths are hidden beneath the first body segment. Their waste hardens quickly into pale nodules, and over many years their colonies create some of the smaller terraces around the pools. A traveler might mistake these formations for natural ornament. The creatures themselves would not make the distinction.

In the deeper water live the long pale forms.

They are not fish, though from above they may briefly appear fishlike: a white curve below black water, a soft turning shape, a narrow body passing where there should be only reflected cloud. They have no scales. Their skin is translucent in places and opaque in others, layered like soaked parchment. In the largest individuals, the internal channels can be seen carrying dark fluid along the length of the body, slowly enough to watch.

They rarely rise to the surface. The surface is too unstable, too exposed to cold air and falling mineral grit. They move instead through the buried architecture of the country: flooded fissures, old channels sealed beneath new crust, caverns made by water dissolving what water has only recently deposited. A single animal may occupy several pools at once, its body threaded through passages no wider than a wrist. When it turns, pools half a valley apart may shiver.

They eat what the water brings down: dead jointed things, mineral-fed colonies, drowned insects blown in from places beyond the basin, and one another when the channels narrow. Their mouths are circular and soft-rimmed, opening along the underside of the headless-looking body. They fasten to rock and draw nourishment from the film that coats it. In old, deep pools, they grow very slowly, adding translucent layers each year until their bodies become as much deposit as flesh.

The wet slap sometimes heard in a pool is often one of these creatures surfacing to discharge a skin.

It does this without urgency. A pale body rises. The water bulges. Something broad and boneless folds once against the surface, leaving a sheet of clear membrane caught on the mineral edge. By morning the membrane has either dissolved, been consumed by the smaller life in the cracks, or hardened into a glassy flap that joins the shore.

There are also things that live in the hollow ground.

These are the reason for the rules about still pools.

They are called nothing by the country itself, but the old structures suggest that people once had a name for them. There are markings on the inner walls of the slab hut: shallow repeated arcs, each crossed by a vertical line. The same pattern appears beneath the accretion on some of the yellow stone posts. It may be writing. It may be warning. It resembles, uncomfortably, the outline of a body seen from above in dark water.

The hollow-dwellers are seldom visible as whole creatures. Most of them never emerge into open pools. They occupy flooded chambers under the crust, where warm mineral water rises from depths and meets the cold descending seepage from above. They are large enough to alter the behavior of water but diffuse enough that no single outline can be trusted. A person standing on a thin shelf might feel a pressure below, as though a great animal had leaned its weight upward against the stone. The shelf might lift by a fraction. The water around it might draw inward. Then the pressure would leave, and the country would settle.

Some say they are masses of blind flesh, enormous and folded, with rows of soft feelers that explore the roof of their chambers. Others say they are not animals at all but colonies grown beyond the point where a colony can be separated from its surroundings: living organs of the basin, pumping and filtering and shifting buried water as lungs shift air.

They may be both.

When a pool becomes perfectly still, one of them may have opened a channel beneath it.

The water does not move because it has become suspended between motions. It is being measured. Pressure is accumulating along a lower seam. The creature—or the colony, or the underground arrangement of bodies—has extended itself into a chamber that cannot contain it. A moment later, perhaps, nothing happens. Or a quiet whirlpool forms without wind. Or the pool empties down through its own reflected sky, leaving a basin of steaming black mud and the slow, wet sound of something withdrawing.

The old buildings are inhabited too, but not by people.

The square hut shelters pale crust-moths in its joints. They have thick folded wings like flakes of mica, and they remain closed against the walls for weeks at a time. When the basin hums, they awaken. They crawl upward, gathering in the highest corners, where they form trembling patches of silver. If the humming persists, they release themselves all at once and disappear into cracks in the ceiling stones. No one has seen them fly in open air.

Inside the abandoned structures live thin, transparent scavengers that move only during the hours before dawn. They are shaped roughly like small animals with too many elbows, but they have no fixed number of limbs. Their bodies can flatten into a seam or gather themselves into a wet, trembling knot. They feed on the mineral slime that forms in mortar lines, on moth skins, on the pale membranes shed from the pools. If surprised, they turn rigid and cloudy, becoming indistinguishable from damp plaster.

The columns attract another inhabitant: a slow shell-builder that resembles a low, many-legged slug when uncovered, though it is almost never uncovered. It begins at the base of a post as a dark wet bead. Over years it crawls upward, laying down a yellow-white sheath behind itself. Its shell is porous, irregular, and absorbs the colors of whatever water has passed over it. The bowed-headed shapes around the columns are sometimes merely mineral deposit. Sometimes they contain one of these builders, curled within its accumulated house, asleep through a season of pressure and cold.

Nothing here lives independently for long.

The long pale swimmers depend on the buried seams. The jointed stone-clingers depend on the films made by the smallest dark life. The smallest life depends on the minerals dragged upward from below. The hollow-dwellers, if they are creatures, depend on all of it: on the channels remaining wet, on the crust remaining unbroken but not too unbroken, on a country continuing to form around them.

Even the ruins have entered the arrangement. Their floors alter the flow of seepage. Their walls gather condensation. Their foundations make pockets where particular colonies flourish. A fallen arch can dam a channel just enough to create a pool; a pool can feed a black film; the film can feed the jointed grazers; their nodules can build a terrace; the terrace can redirect water beneath a slab; the slab can crack; and through that crack something patient and pale can enter a place where it has never been before.

Perhaps people lived here once, before the channels wandered away from their causeways and returned beneath them.

If they did, the low country did not drive them out in any sudden disaster. It simply continued becoming. It wetted their floors. It softened their foundations. It filled their wells from below instead of above. It taught the stone to hum, taught the pools to hold still, taught their shelters to remember the shapes of older watercourses.

Then the people became another brief texture on the ridges: a little warmth, a few straight lines, some fitted slabs.

The things that live there now have longer patience.
