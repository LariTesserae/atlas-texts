What lives there has learned not to compete with the water, but to travel by it.

The basin’s creatures are mostly small, dark, and close to the ground. They have the colors of wet stone, bark, root-shadow, and the undersides of leaves. Many seem absent until they move: a bead of blackness sliding from one fern stem to another; a pale eye opening in a mud bank; the slow contraction of something that had been mistaken for moss.

The frogs are the basin’s most numerous voices, though even they spend much of their lives silent. They are not bright animals. Their skins are green-black, iron-brown, peat-red, patterned with fine pale seams like mineral veins. In daylight they sleep in the cups of broad leaves, beneath curtains of root, in pockets of wet basalt warmed from below. After hard rain they emerge, not all at once, but by watersheds. One hollow begins its low glassy call; another answers beyond the trees; a third begins later among the western springs. Their breeding pools are often no bigger than a hand basin, each one a temporary universe held in a leaf axil or a depression in stone.

Some of the frogs never descend to the ground. Their young develop in rainwater gathered among epiphytes high in the trunks. Others lay strings of dark eggs in the warm margins of the mineral ponds, where the water moves too slowly to be a stream and too steadily to become stagnant. Their tadpoles have broad mouths and graze the orange films around the vents. They grow quickly, then vanish into the green.

There are salamanders in the seepage channels: long-bodied, soft-footed creatures with blunt heads and tails flattened for swimming. At first glance they resemble strips of fallen bark soaking in water. They live in the spaces beneath basalt shelves, where cold runoff crosses warm vapor from the stone. Their eggs are fastened to the undersides of submerged roots, translucent clusters trembling in the current. At night they come out onto the moss, hunting small beetles and snails. Their skin shines blue-gray in moonlight, and when disturbed they slip into cracks so narrow that no larger thing could follow.

The ponds hold older animals.

Not large ones, by the standards of lowland rivers, but creatures large enough that their movement alters the water. In the deepest ponds live broad-backed marsh turtles, dark as cooled lava and furred at the shell edges with algae. They surface without splash beneath the floating rafts, lifting only nostrils and a ridge of head into the air. Their shells bear tiny gardens: mosses, liverworts, insect eggs, and once in a while the rooting stem of a water plant. A turtle may spend years carrying a piece of the pond with it.

The springs themselves are inhabited by pale things that do not tolerate the cooler water outside their immediate warmth. Small fish, nearly transparent except for their black eyes and a red pulse along the gills, gather near the bubbling sand. They feed on larvae, mineral-loving algae, and whatever fragments wash down from the forest. Their bodies flicker just above the bottom, so that the pond appears to contain loose sparks of bone and blood.

Along the mud margins, there are fish with lungs as well as gills, squat and armored, able to wait in burrows when a channel changes its course. In the rainy afternoons they leave the deeper pools and move through flooded grass, pushing themselves between stems with slow, astonishing purpose. Birds follow them. Snakes follow the birds.

The snakes are quiet enough to be mistaken for roots. Some are thin, green, and leaflike, taking frogs from the branch-level pools. Some are banded in wet black and pale clay, living among the fluted roots of the taller trees. One kind favors the warm volcanic rocks after dusk. It is short, thick, and almost glossy, with a blunt head and scales that catch the firefly light. It hunts by sensing heat in the darkness: a frog on a stone, a sleeping bird, a small mammal returning to its burrow.

The mammal seen at the streambanks is one of several related creatures, all sleek and shy, all adapted to a country with no truly dry season. It has a long flexible body, webbed feet, and close dark fur that sheds water in silver beads. It eats crabs, frogs, insects, eggs, and fish trapped in shallow channels. Its paws are dexterous enough to turn stones and pull apart clumps of flooded roots. It does not build a permanent den. Instead it maintains a network of resting hollows beneath root walls throughout the basin, moving among them as water rises and falls.

There is also a heavier browser in the groves, the source of the deliberate broken twigs. It is neither deer nor pig, though it carries something of both in its posture. Its shoulders are high, its legs short for its mass, and its hide is covered in coarse hair that grows dark with rain. Its snout is broad and sensitive, made for finding tubers beneath moss and for stripping the tender inner growth from ferns. It moves alone or in small family groups, usually at the gray edge of evening. The wet ground remembers where it has passed: flattened grass, overturned stones, crescent-shaped prints filling slowly with clear water.

These animals maintain the meadows without intending to. Their feeding opens gaps in the grass. Their hooves press channels into soft soil. Seeds catch in their hair and travel from one wet shelf to another. Where they root at the edge of a spring, new plants rise in the disturbed ground: red-throated flowers, low creeping herbs, pale fungi that appear overnight and dissolve by midday.

Above them live the birds.

The small dark birds of the canopy are tireless gleaners, compact and quick, with curved bills for probing wet bark. Their feathers repel rain so thoroughly that droplets sit on them like glass beads. Some follow the larger browsers, taking insects stirred from the grass. Others visit the flowering plants, their heads dusted gold with pollen. A few have learned to drink from the leaf cups without falling in; their long tongues reach into water that may hold larvae, ants, and drowned gnats.

The waders in the reeds are more secretive than their white wing-flashes suggest. They nest on floating mats, where the nests rise and fall with the ponds. Their chicks are mottled and long-legged from the first day, walking carefully over vegetation that bends but does not break beneath them. When danger comes, the adults do not always fly. Sometimes they lower themselves into the reeds until they vanish, becoming another vertical shadow among wet stems.

The great circling birds above the rim are the basin’s widest-ranging inhabitants. They nest in crevices of the volcanic walls and ride the warm air rising from black stone. Their wings are narrow, dark below and silver-edged above. From the basin floor they appear almost motionless, but they are always adjusting, reading the invisible lift of heat and wind. They eat carrion when they find it, but also hunt the larger birds, the young browsers, and anything exposed in open meadow. They are rarely seen descending. Their violence occurs beyond the leaves.

In the rotting wood live nations too small to name at a glance. Beetles with polished blue-black wing cases tunnel through soaked trunks. Pale larvae curl in the fungus-soft heartwood. Ants build dry chambers inside standing dead trees, carrying their eggs upward when floodwater rises. Termites make galleries through fallen branches and leave them intact-looking until a foot, hoof, or heavy rain collapses the shell into brown dust.

The fungi are not merely what follows death here. They are among the place’s chief engineers.

White fans spread beneath the bark of fallen trees. Orange shelves climb old root walls. Black threads move through the soil between living plants, carrying minerals and sugars in patterns too slow to see. Some fungi glow faintly after rain, especially beneath the dense groves: a greenish light low to the ground, not bright enough to guide a traveler, but enough to make the bases of trees seem inhabited by submerged stars.

There are plants that behave almost like animals. The floating rafts of the ponds drift and knit themselves together, their roots filtering the mineral water and sheltering eggs, fry, insects, and snails. Certain broad-leaved plants close around visiting insects when the rain is heavy, not always to consume them but to keep the pools in their cups from overflowing. A thorny vine sends out fine tendrils that respond to the warmth of passing bodies, curling around branches, stems, and occasionally the careless feet of the browser. In the boggy ground, small red-leaved plants hold flies in sticky dew, converting brief moments of flight into soil fertility.

And at the round central pool there is a life unlike the others.

It is not a creature that can be seen whole. It may be no more than a colony, a mat of roots, microbes, mineral films, and ancient submerged stems joined over centuries. Beneath the dark water, below the floating leaves, pale filaments spread through the mud in a circular weave. They gather heat from the spring below and feed upon dissolved iron, fallen pollen, drowned insects, the minute waste of everything drinking there.

The reeds around the pool grow taller and darker than reeds elsewhere. Their roots are unusually dense. Their flowers open a little earlier in the morning, before sunlight reaches the basin floor. Insects visit them in numbers, and the insects draw birds; the birds bring seeds; the seeds become shrubs along the rim. The pool is not the heart of the basin in any sentimental sense. Nothing in the basin depends on one thing alone.

But it is a center of recurrence.

Animals come there to drink, though the water has a warm mineral taste. Frogs breed in its shallows. Turtles bury eggs in the black soil nearby. The sleek streambank mammal leaves its prints at the same two places along the edge. The heavy browsers avoid the water itself but sleep among the rocks above it, where warmth rises through the night. Birds circle over the reflection, then become briefly doubled in the water.

Sometimes, beneath the reflected cloud, a warm bubble reaches the surface.

It rises from the hidden weave below, breaks without sound, and opens a ring among the leaves.

For a moment, every reflected thing is disturbed: sky, rim, drifting vapor, the leaning green walls of the basin.

Then the circle widens, weakens, and disappears.

The creatures continue as they were. A wader lifts one foot from the reeds. A frog turns beneath a leaf. A beetle enters a crack in wet wood. Somewhere in the grove, a branch bends under the weight of a feeding animal and slowly returns.

The pool restores the sky.
