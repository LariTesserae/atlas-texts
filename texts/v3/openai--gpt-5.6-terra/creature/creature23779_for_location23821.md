What lives in the drowned works is not one species, nor one mind, but a system of accommodations.

The first resident is the water itself.

Not merely the volume held in basins and shafts, but a water that has learned the diagrams of the place. It knows which galleries are meant to fill first, which old stairwells should remain clear until evening, where the warm current rises through turbine housings, where cold blue layers settle beneath the black reservoirs. It moves with the deliberation of something reading. At every bulkhead, it tests the old intentions of the builders. At every cracked seam, it sends a narrow finger through, not to escape, but to make contact.

It occupies rooms according to their character. In the square settling courts it lies flat and reflective, holding the towers upside down. In the pressure frames it stands upright in sheets, dense and nearly motionless, so that a person passing through would seem to step briefly into another weather. In the submerged offices it gathers beneath desks, around switchboards, in the bowls of hard hats left on hooks. In the deep elevator shafts it rises and falls with the cages, carrying leaves alongside them without ever stirring them into a wake.

Sometimes it seems attentive.

A gauge needle trembles, and the water in a nearby room acquires a faint inward slant. A lamp begins to glow beneath the surface, and pale roots turn toward its window. An unseen valve opens somewhere inside the concrete, and all through the works the water adjusts itself by degrees—one pool lowering, another darkening, a flooded corridor becoming passable for an hour before it is quietly claimed again.

The water has no face, but it has habits. It keeps certain places empty. It preserves certain objects. A red maintenance lantern may remain dry on a submerged table for decades, enclosed beneath a low dome of air that never leaks away. A notebook may float open in a turbine chamber, its pages unblurred, while all around it the machinery is furred in algae. Elsewhere, a steel door has been dissolved to lace, though the concrete wall around it remains sharp and new.

Living among the water are the green things, but they are not ordinary plants anymore.

The lilies are perhaps the most visible. Their leaves are broad, black-green, perfectly unwettable. Water beads on them in silver globes and rolls slowly toward their edges without falling. Their flowers open in the warmest courts: ivory, sulfur-yellow, occasionally a bruised violet that seems almost black in cloudlight. At night they close with a faint folding sound, like wet cloth gathered by careful hands.

Their roots go far below the beds that appear to support them. They braid through cable trays and intake filters; they pass through holes too small for any root to have found; they enter the housings of dead instruments and emerge again through drain slots several floors away. If a cable is carrying current, even a little, the roots will wrap it in soft white coils. If a pipe carries heat, the lilies will send runners toward it until the pipe has become a green pillar crowned with leaves.

The trees in the upper platforms are called, by no one now, boiler figs.

They are not large. Their trunks rarely rise higher than a person’s shoulders before dividing into dense, polished branches. Their bark is pale and close-grained, almost ceramic. Each bears heavy waxed leaves, dark on top and silver beneath, and their roots do not descend. They spread horizontally through the built world, pressing into expansion joints, following steam lines, filling empty conduits with a network of warm, fibrous wood.

Their fruit is the color of old brass. It ripens silently and drops into the water with almost no splash. It can remain afloat for months. Sometimes the fruits collect in corners of flooded galleries, turning slowly in a patient cluster. When one finally splits, it releases not seeds but a pale dust that gathers along waterlines and becomes, after many weeks, a felt of green.

There are smaller lives as well, though they are difficult to notice.

Inside the algae on the lower walls are clear, threadlike creatures that resemble no animal anyone would name. They are too slow for the eye until they cross a patch of light. Then one may see a transparent loop tighten, loosen, and draw itself along the wall by a fraction of an inch. They feed on mineral blooms and on the faint warmth leaking from electrical housings. They have no visible mouths. At the end of their lives, they harden into small glassy rings that remain attached to the concrete.

The rings accumulate around old outlet pipes, around the bases of lamp standards, around any place where current and water meet. In some chambers there are thousands of them, nested one inside another, forming pale collars like the growth lines of trees.

There are also the valve moths.

They are not seen in open air because there is almost no open air in which to see them. They live inside the dry spaces behind instrument faces, within relay cabinets, in the shallow cavities of emergency lighting fixtures. Their wings are thin membranes of oxidized copper, green and brown and iridescent at the edges. They emerge only when a gauge changes.

When pressure rises in a forgotten line, a cabinet door may open by itself, just enough for several valve moths to slip out. They drift toward the nearest warm pipe and settle on it in a neat row. Their wings vibrate soundlessly. The pipe cools. The pressure eases. Then they return, one by one, to the cabinet.

No one knows whether they are animals, mechanisms, or a habit the machinery developed in response to being left alone.

In the drowned control rooms live things that look like people only from a distance.

They stand behind thick windows, pale forms at desks or among the shadowed banks of panels. A glimpse may show a shoulder, a bowed head, a hand held over a switch. Yet they are never visible from two angles. Approach the room, and the figure resolves into reflections: a hanging coil of root, a sheet of water, an arrangement of cables and leaves. Look again from the far side, and there it is once more, seated patiently in the dark.

These are called, if they can be called anything, the operators.

The works makes them when it needs an operator-shaped absence.

They appear in rooms where some process has fallen out of balance. A pump begins to labor. A reservoir climbs too high. A bank of yellow windows burns late into the night. Then, behind the glass, one of the operators is present. It does not move in any way that can be witnessed. No one has seen one walk from a door, turn a dial, or rise from a chair. But by morning the water has redistributed itself, the lights have dimmed, and the room is empty except for condensation on the chair.

The oldest operators are said to be rooted into the buildings now. Their outlines recur in the same windows through years of cloud and season. Moss grows where their heads would be. Vines pass through the places where their arms rest. In one submerged dispatch room, a figure has stood so long behind a pane of glass that a lily pad has formed at the level of its chest and opens a white flower each warm month.

There may once have been human workers here.

The works retains evidence without offering conclusion. Boots remain beneath benches, filled with clear water and fine white roots. A lunch tin rests in a dry shelf niche, its painted fruit still bright. Names are stamped into brass tags at the doors of sleeping quarters, but many of the tags have been turned blank by a green bloom crawling up from the screws.

Somewhere below the deepest public waterline are rooms that do not flood.

They are not sealed. Their doors hang open, their vents connect to wet galleries, and pipes pass through their walls by the hundreds. Yet within them there is only a warm, mineral-smelling air. The floors are dry. The lights are low and amber. In these rooms, something large is said to breathe.

Not loudly. Not rhythmically enough to be called breathing. More like the slow intake and release of an entire pressure system. When it draws inward, steam disappears from the upper decks and the lilies tilt slightly toward the drains. When it releases, condensation forms along every rail at once, and water rises in the vertical panes.

This thing may be the heart of the works. It may be an engine too vast to have been designed as an engine. It may be the mountain, remaining somewhere beneath all the fabricated levels, dissolved into pumps and chambers but still alive in its old geological sleep.

The plants seem to know it. Their roots all eventually trend downward, however impossible their route. The water knows it more intimately still. Every basin, every flooded staircase, every glass-walled gallery is connected to its hidden depth.

And the complex serves it.

Or it serves the complex.

Above, where clouds split around the iron terraces and steam drifts through the little trees, the drowned works continues its quiet labor. It grows leaf by leaf. It corrects itself by drops. It keeps its rooms full. It raises lights beneath the water for no observer. It sends elevator cages through blue shafts. It makes figures in windows when a figure is required.

Its inhabitants are water, root, current, pressure, reflection, and the patient inventions produced where all of these meet.

They do not need to leave.

There is nowhere outside the works that is as complete as the place they have become.
