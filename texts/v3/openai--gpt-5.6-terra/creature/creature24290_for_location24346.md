What lives there is not a set of species so much as a pressure: life adapted to the fact that there is nowhere empty, nowhere warm, nowhere quiet.

At the base of everything live the stone-eaters.

They begin as films inside hairline fractures, clear or black or rust-red depending on what the rock gives them. Some feed on iron sweating from basalt. Some use sulfur breathed from warm seams. Some sleep frozen for most of the year, then wake during an hour of melt and multiply so quickly that a cliff acquires a dark nap. They are the first tenants of new ground. Their bodies trap dust, hold moisture, and split mineral grain by grain. The mountains rise sharp, but nothing stays bare.

Above them spread the hum-plants: not one family but a treaty of forms.

The blue-green mats are woven colonies, each soft surface hiding a dense architecture of tubes, nodules, air bladders, and mineral fibers. Their outer tissues freeze readily. This is useful. Frost pulls moisture from the air; sunlight melts it inward; the plant drinks the thaw before the wind can take it. Many have dark undersides, nearly black, which collect warmth from the rock. Others grow reflective hairs that protect them from noon glare. Some are rooted; some can loosen themselves in a storm, roll downhill like damp bundles of wire, and establish wherever they lodge.

The tallest of them are the vapor columns of the fog forests. A single column may appear to be a trunk, but is actually a city of plants: structural stalks at the center, moisture-combing fronds around them, parasitic thread-vines beneath the skin, fungal cushions in the warm interior chambers. Small animals live inside these columns without ever seeing daylight. They eat pale internal growth, drink condensed water, breed in the warm pulse of rising vapor, and emerge only when the colony flowers or dies.

The grazers are the obvious citizens.

They are called, in another world’s language, herds; here they might better be called moving weather. Most are six- or eight-limbed, because the slopes do not forgive imbalance. Their feet divide into hooked pads, bristled fans, and hard mineral nails, each suited to a different kind of stone. Their blood—or whatever performs its work—is cloudy with antifreeze proteins and green compounds borrowed from their food. In strong sun, their pale coats transmit light into the skin. Along their flanks, one can sometimes see the dim internal green of cultivated symbionts.

The great cliff-grazers never truly stand alone. A young one is born into the pressure of the herd and may spend its first months supported on three sides by adults. When they sleep, they lock their limbs together. When storms come, they turn outward in layers: large bodies taking the wind, smaller bodies breathing in the warmed folds beneath them. Their migrations leave patterns visible from high above—broad cropped swaths, darkened by dung and crushed vegetation, advancing through the valleys with the patience of glaciers.

There are lesser grazers that live in the coats of the great ones: thumb-sized comb-beasts with translucent jaws, soft-footed creatures that harvest frost from hair, flat-bodied mites large enough to carry their young in rows along the belly. Some are parasites, but few are merely parasites. Their waste feeds coat-mosses; the coat-mosses feed insects; the insects feed the tiny frost-feathered birds that ride the herd’s backs.

Those birds are not birds in the clean lowland sense. They have feathers, but every feather is waxed, filamented, and edged with gripping hooks. They nest in shed coats, carcass ribs, and the warm hollows under sleeping grazers. Their calls are short, metallic bursts, made to carry through wind without wasting breath. At dawn whole flocks descend on rime-covered plants, pecking out the minute animals that rise with the thaw. Their eggs are dark and heat-hungry; they are laid against black stone, vent-warmed soil, or the bodies of host animals.

The creatures that resemble roots are among the oldest-looking. They spend most of their lives buried in vegetation mats, with only pale branching tips exposed. When a grazer’s hoof compresses the ground, or when meltwater passes overhead, they uncoil. Some are scavengers, some filter feeders, some ambush hunters whose bodies open like flowers around anything small enough to be held. In cold weather they knot themselves into hard balls and wait. A colony may wait for years beneath a new lava shelf, waking only when vapor returns.

The pools make their own animals.

Transparent larvae fill them first, their internal organs bright as ink strokes. They graze algae from the undersides of ice and grow so slowly that a single individual may survive dozens of freeze-thaw cycles. There are black fan-worms that anchor in silty margins and strain spores from the water. There are red thread colonies that look vegetal until disturbed, when they recoil together and reveal hundreds of feeding mouths. There are glass-shelled swimmers whose bodies crystallize in winter, becoming almost indistinguishable from the ice until spring releases them.

At night, some pool life signals to itself with blue light.

Not communication exactly, at least not in any simple sense. The pulses begin when water chemistry changes, when a shadow passes, when a predator feeds, when an entire basin reaches a certain temperature. One patch lights, then another, and then another. The pattern travels through connected melt channels under the ice. Animals follow it. The smallest grazers rise toward it. Predators learn to wait at its edges. The pools become maps of activity, blinking beneath their frozen roofs.

The scavengers of the high air are perhaps the closest thing the country has to sovereigns.

They are vast, thin-bodied things with leaf-rib wings stretched over hollow spars. Their bones are light but not fragile; their joints lock in soaring positions; their respiratory passages run through much of the body, so that wind itself seems to inhabit them. They feed on carrion, certainly, but also on the afterlife of carrion: marrow, hide colonies, bone fungi, the dense knot of organisms that gathers around a fallen grazer. Their hooked beaks are narrow enough to work between ribs. Their tongues are rough with mineral teeth.

They do not descend alone when food is plentiful. A single circling shape is often followed by dozens hidden in the glare. Their arrival changes the sound of a valley. Small feeders scatter into cracks. Frost-feather birds lift in glittering clouds. The grazing herd tightens, not from fear of attack but from habit: exposed warmth is always claimed by something.

There are hunters too, though they are difficult to see.

Some lie beneath snow membranes, sensing vibration through the ice. Some cling to cliff faces in colors matching wet basalt and unfold only when prey passes beneath. Some are long, many-jointed pursuit animals that move through the undersides of plant mats, following the warmth and electrical activity of clustered bodies. The largest may be responsible for the deep calls heard beneath the glaciers: vent-dwelling bulk, perhaps, or immense blind grazers moving through subglacial caverns where heat and mineral water meet.

No one on the surface sees them clearly. Now and then a fissure opens after a thaw, and something vast has passed below: a smooth track pressed into sediment, a shed translucent plate larger than a door, a chamber filled with warm breath that smells of iron and green rot. Then the opening freezes, the humming plants creep over it, and the mountain keeps its lower inhabitants.

Even death lives there.

A carcass does not simply feed the scavengers. Its warmth creates weather. Snow melts around it. Steam gathers beneath the hide. Insects hatch from places that appeared inert. Fungal cords arrive through the ground. Bone-borers perforate the skeleton; grease-moss drinks the seepage; small grazers return later to eat the growth nourished by their own kind. Eventually the carcass becomes a patch of unusually rich vegetation, visible from far away as a green-black bruise on the slope.

And among all these lives are things too small or too slow to notice: mineral lichens that record each summer in colored rings; wingless spore-herders that carry fungal blooms between columns; heat-loving bacteria living in the footprints of large animals; pale mites surviving in the folds of ice; tiny predators in every drop of condensation.

The place is inhabited by specialists, but no specialist is independent.

The wing requires the herd. The herd requires the humming mats. The mats require the fractures opened by ice, roots, hooves, heat, and microbial acid. The pools depend on the fog columns; the fog columns depend on the valleys; the valleys depend on mountains still being made. Beneath all of it, the warm black stone keeps breathing.

So what lives in the high country?

A crowded machine made of hunger, frost, vapor, and mutual use. A continent-sized body whose organs have learned to walk, fly, root, burrow, freeze, thaw, and sing.
