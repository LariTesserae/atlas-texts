What lives on the plateau is not a people in the lowland sense. There are no crowds, no market paths, no smoke rising from a cluster of roofs. Its inhabitants have learned that proximity is a kind of deafness here: too many bodies, too many shelters, too many tools sounding against one another would disappear into the plateau’s continual chord.

They live separately because the land does.

A plateau-dweller may know the positions of thirty other dwellings and visit only three of them in an entire life. They recognize one another not by faces at first, but by resonance. Each person carries a pattern of small suspended objects: copper reeds, teeth of glass, polished stone disks, hollow segments of plant stalk. These tremble differently in the wind and underfoot. At a distance, one person’s approach can be identified by the little dissonance that precedes them—their particular arrangement of answering noises.

They call this a voice, though it is not speech.

Their bodies are spare and heavily wrapped, not primarily against cold but against abrasion. The air desiccates exposed skin; the mineral dust finds every crease; sound enters through bone. They wear long mantles woven from the silver hairs of the columnar groves, darkened with sootless pigment until they resemble folds of the landscape moving on their own. Under the outer fiber are close layers of soft hide, felted moss fiber, and thin beaten copper at the throat, sternum, and temples. The copper is not armor. It is tuning. A properly shaped plate can take some part of a groan from the rock and distribute it through the body before it shatters a tooth or loosens the blood behind the eyes.

Their faces are often veiled, but not hidden. The veils are porous membranes cut from shed frond skin, stiff as paper until warmed by breath. Through them, the wearer smells the mineral state of the air. They can tell when a seam beneath the ground is opening, when a dust current is carrying salts from a distant basin, when the root-warmth under a grove is turning sour or rich.

Their eyes are adapted less to darkness than to uncertainty. The doubled horizon and inverted mountains have made ordinary sight unreliable. Plateau-dwellers do not trust the first outline of anything. They stand still often. They blink in patterned intervals. They look at an object, then at its shadow, then at the trembling in a copper charm, then at the nearest vegetation. Only when several things agree do they accept that a ridge is a ridge, a person is a person, and a path has not become a wall.

Children are taught this before they are taught names.

A child begins with listening lessons. They are seated inside one of the round roofless enclosures, among stones rubbed smooth by generations of hands. An elder—or simply an older inhabitant, since rank is weak here—strikes nothing. They wait. The child learns the layers of the world’s sound: wind on glass, glass under strain, deep stone, root-flex, distant frond chord, salt ticking, copper responding. For years they are not allowed near the central mountain. They hear it through the ground, through their beds, through bowls of dust trembling on shelves. Its vast note becomes part of their sense of weather.

When a child is old enough, they are taken to a place where two groves face one another across a ravine and make a pocket of silence. There, for perhaps the first time, they hear their own breathing without the plateau around it.

Some are frightened by this. Some weep. Some never wish to leave that silence and become its keepers.

The inhabitants cultivate not plants exactly, but agreements with plants. They do not believe the dark groves belong to them. A grove is older than a dwelling but younger than a cliff; it may travel away, split itself, collapse into mineral husks, or rise overnight through a crack in a shelter floor. A person who builds too close to one accepts that the house will eventually be taken apart from beneath.

Gardens are negotiations. Stones are placed to soften a plant’s chosen sound, to catch warmth from a breathing seam, to redirect the dry wind, to make a root turn a little away from a sleeping place. Fronds are tied down only briefly, with braided fiber meant to fail when the plant’s growth becomes insistent. Nothing is pruned without apology. Nothing is harvested without leaving an answering object behind: a bead of copper, a polished shard, a little bundle of hair-fiber returned to the roots.

From the groves they take food that is neither fruit nor leaf. In the cool interior of certain stalks, a pale green tissue forms in layers around mineral vapor. It is scraped out with bone or glass and dried into thin curls. It tastes bitter, metallic, and faintly sweet afterward. They grind luminous lichens with mineral dust for medicine. They steep moss in sealed copper vessels until it gives up a dark resin used to seal wounds and quiet the ringing in the ears.

They are careful with fire.

Open flame is possible, but unwelcome. It produces a sound too sharp for the plateau, a crackling that attracts attention—not necessarily from creatures, since there are none known in the open country, but from the land itself. Fire near an unfinished cavity can cause a whole shelf of stone to answer, splitting in clean geometric planes. Instead, they make lamps from hollow stalks whose interior gases glow when exposed to certain salts. The lamps burn cold and green. Their light does not travel far, which suits a people accustomed to knowing only what is near.

Their dead are not buried. The ground is too active, and graves do not remain where they are made.

A body may be set upright in a grove after being wrapped in frond skin and copper thread. Over months or years the grove’s silver hairs work through the wrapping. The body dries, darkens, becomes light. Eventually there remains a standing shape among the stalks: a gray pillar, narrower than a trunk, often bent in the direction of the prevailing wind. Families visit these pillars not to speak, but to hang small resonant pieces from them. As the dead hollow and weather, they become part of the grove’s chord.

Those who die near the central elevation are treated differently. Their remains are broken into many small parcels and carried outward to distant shelters. No one wishes to give the aperture a whole person. It is said that the mountain already knows too well how to rearrange a body.

Whether there are other inhabitants is unclear.

There are things seen in the mineral days: tall forms moving along a far ridge with too many joints; pale heads rising from geometric wells; figures that seem to cross the salt flats upside down, their feet attached to the violet sky. The plateau-dwellers do not call these animals. An animal, to them, is something that leaves a repeating trace—tracks, droppings, nesting scars, a recognizable hunger. These forms do not. They may be errors in distance. They may be the mountain’s notes lingering in the eye. They may be people seen from another arrangement of the land.

Still, travelers hang bells of soft copper outside their shelters at night. Not bells meant to ring loudly. Bells meant to change pitch if something passes nearby.

There are also the listeners.

Not every person can remain in one place. Some become devoted to the central aperture. They walk toward it over many years, stopping at each grove and shelter to learn its tonal pattern. They repair marker towers, replace snapped cords, collect the old copper plates from abandoned windbreaks. Their bodies become increasingly laden with instruments: strings stretched between shoulder and hip, crystal prisms sewn into mantle hems, hollow bones of plants mounted behind the ears.

By the time a listener reaches the lower slopes of the impossible mountain, they can no longer speak in ordinary sounds. The continuous pressure has reshaped their breath and hearing. They communicate by touching stone, by placing their hands against another person’s copper plates, by arranging objects in the dust.

Some listeners return.

When they do, they bring no maps and no clear account. They may carry a new kind of crystal, a strip of black material that remains warm for years, or a fragment of stone that hums only when held against the chest of a sleeping child. They may build a shelter far from all others and live in it without emerging. Sometimes, after a deep note from the aperture, a returned listener is found standing miles from where they slept, facing the wrong direction, with their mantle full of white dust and no memory of crossing the ground.

The plateau’s people do not consider this madness. They consider it weather.

What lives there, then, is a scattered human endurance, altered by constant listening: gardeners of unstable stone, keepers of silences, makers of small answers to immense sounds. Around them live the groves, the mosses, the grasses that bend as one, and perhaps the unfinished appetites of the land itself.

They do not imagine they have conquered the plateau.

They have only learned how to remain during the intervals when it agrees to be a world.
