They are people, though the dry-world stories disagree about what that means.

Their oldest bones, when the deep wells have done their work, are recognizably human: narrow hands, upright spines, skulls with familiar sockets and teeth. But nobody in the basin has seen an unaltered human body, and few would find one entirely comforting. The city has lived under water too long for that.

Its people are broad through the chest and soft through the middle, built for holding warmth and breath. Their ribs have become more flexible over generations, their lungs generous but not miraculous; they must still breathe, and the need to rise is one of the facts around which all life is arranged. Some households keep chambers near the upper water, where old domes have trapped air beneath their ceilings. Others use breathing gourds: great bladders cut from buoyant weed, sealed in treated skin, carried on long journeys and shared carefully. Children are taught early not to empty a gourd in fear. They learn how to make one breath carry through a difficult passage, how to lie still in an air-pocket while their heartbeat settles, how to recognize the ache that means *turn back now*.

Many have webbing between fingers and toes, not the neat webbing of a duck but pale, irregular folds of skin, different from family to family. Some are born with hands almost ordinary; some with fingers that spread wide as fans. Their feet are strong and flexible, useful on mud, stone, and sloping walls. Their legs have not vanished, because streets still require walking, because ladders and ledges remain, because there are times when swimming would stir too much silt or attract the wrong attention. Yet even the oldest people move in water with a grace that makes standing still seem like an unusual choice.

Their skin tends toward muted colors: brown, gray, olive, silt-gold, the blue-black sheen of wet stone. In childhood it is smooth, but adulthood brings fine pale lines around joints and throat, a slight thickening over shoulders and shins, scars that heal shiny. Most shave their heads or wear their hair cut close, because long hair tangles in ropework, eelgrass, nets, and the hands of frightened children. Those who wear it long braid it tightly with fishbone pins and weighted shells. In certain families, hair is cultivated rather than cut, kept in dense ropes that serve as signs of mourning, kinship, or debt.

Their eyes are large by surface standards, often dark enough to disappear in the face. Some catch what little light exists with a green or amber gleam. They see better in dimness than a visitor could, but not well enough to trust sight alone. The city has made tactile people of them. They know one another by palm, wrist, scar, shoulder, the structure of a jaw beneath a hood. Greetings are often made with the backs of fingers against the throat, feeling the small pulse there. Lovers learn each other’s breathing before they learn each other’s faces. A child separated in a crowd calls with a low throat-note and holds one hand out, not expecting to be seen.

Their language is not silent, but much of it is quiet enough to be mistaken for silence.

They speak in close voices: humming phrases, clicks inside the cheek, soft vowels released into another person’s listening hand. Distance speech is slower and more formal, shaped through pipes of hollow shell or sent as patterned strikes along walls and rails. A family can pass a message from one end of a dwelling to another by tapping a certain rhythm into reefstone. Market sellers use strings of clacking shells, each sequence meaning something practical: fresh catch, spoiled catch, current turning, child missing, deep animal nearby.

But the language has a second body made of touch.

A hand pressed flat to the breast means *I am here.* Two fingers at the wrist mean *listen beneath what is said.* A thumb passed across the knuckles can mean apology, warning, affection, or mockery depending on pressure and timing. People conducting serious business often hold a shared cord, each feeling the other’s movements through its braided length. Public law is sung, yes, but it is also signed into the water with arms and hands, so that a crowded hall can understand even when currents muddle the words.

They have no single appearance because the city is not one bloodline. It is a basin where generations of survivors gathered: people from drowned coasts, quarry villages, old shore settlements, wandering raft-families, perhaps places much farther away. Their descendants keep those differences in fragments. Some have round, seal-soft faces and heavy lids. Some have sharp noses and narrow mouths better suited to dry air but inherited nevertheless. Some are compact, dense-boned, and powerful in the current. Some are long-limbed and nearly weightless in motion. There are households whose children are usually born with a faint stripe of silver along the spine, and households in which everyone develops a dark patch beneath the left eye.

Nobody believes these traits make a person better. The city has too many practical measures. Can you hold a rope in a flood? Can you judge a changing current? Can you keep another body warm? Can you remember the path home after black water has moved the stones?

That is what matters.

They wear little in the warm districts: belts, soft wraps, netted pouches, harnesses for tools and children. Clothing is made from cured ray skin, woven grass, shed seal-hide, the tough translucent membranes of large fish. In cooler passages, people add layered capes of kelp fiber and fur-like moss. Decoration is common, even lavish by the city’s standards. A person may wear polished vertebrae at the throat, a circlet of snail shells, a pebble drilled with a hole and passed down for twelve generations. Some stain their skin with mineral reds and blacks that survive immersion. Others scar delicate maps into their arms: household channels, remembered roads, the branching route to a particular warm spring.

The elderly are especially ornamented. Not because age is automatically revered—there are selfish old people, foolish old people, and old people whom nobody lets near the communal stores—but because surviving long enough to become old beneath the sea is an accomplishment that accumulates visible evidence. An elder may carry six generations of repairs in the cords around their waist: a daughter’s shell knife, a dead companion’s breathing clasp, a grandchild’s first carved bead, a sliver of white stone from the year the northern wall broke.

Their children are less solemn than outsiders would expect.

They play in the canals, racing along routes that adults consider too narrow for dignity. They dare one another to touch rays in the public squares. They keep tiny crabs in clayless shell-cups, naming them after relatives. They learn to whistle to fish, although the fish rarely obey as reliably as adults pretend. In the upper chambers, where air hangs under ceilings, children make forbidden games of shouting and listening to the strange violence of a voice traveling through empty space.

Some children are born unable to swim strongly. Some cannot hold breath for long. Some have weak joints, clouded eyes, or minds that cannot endure the sensory confusion of the deep streets. The city has made room for them because it has had to. There are ropeways through difficult passages, shared carriers shaped like broad baskets, guide-fish trained to follow a certain person’s hand-sign, quiet chambers far from market noise. Competence is prized, but independence is not worshipped. No one remains independent in a city that can turn black overnight.

Their work follows the water.

There are current-readers, who sit for long periods with bare feet or palms against stone and can tell whether a distant gate has failed. There are shellwrights, coral-tenders, eel-keepers, bone-cutters, net-menders, mud-divers, and people whose entire trade is finding objects lost in silt. There are air-tenders who inspect the trapped chambers, scrape away foul growth, patch leaks, and judge whether an air pocket has gone bad before anyone sleeps inside it. These people are treated with a mixture of respect and the mild suspicion reserved for those who understand something too necessary.

There are singers of names, who maintain the dead-well records. They do not write, because ink runs and paper dissolves and carved stone is too slow for ordinary lives. Instead they memorize the chains: who was given to which well, who returned bones to a family niche, who disappeared in a black tide and was never recovered. Their songs are long, plain, and exact. A singer may know the dead of every household within three districts, and when one dies, the city considers it a loss comparable to a collapsed roof.

There are also surface-goers.

They are not a separate caste, exactly, but they are set apart by temperament and practice. They rise through shafts, flooded towers, and old wells toward the world above. They gather rainwater, floating wood, bird eggs, washed wreckage, and occasionally news from places beyond the basin. Many are afraid of the surface even after a lifetime of visiting it. They describe it as too large, too exposed, too loud. The sky unsettles them: a brightness with no walls around it, an emptiness that does not press back.

Surface-goers return with impossible things.

Dry cloth. Rusted bells. Fragments of printed pictures whose colors have bled into ghosts. Jars. Knives of real metal. Once, generations ago, someone brought back a door with a square hole of glass still set in it. The glass was not useful underwater, but it was kept in a communal hall because it showed a person’s face with an unnerving clarity. Children were forbidden to look into it alone.

Not all inhabitants are people.

The web-footed dogs are household creatures, work animals, scavengers, companions, and occasionally a nuisance of such talent that entire neighborhoods have songs insulting particular bloodlines of dog. They are long-bodied, deep-chested, sleek-furred or nearly hairless, with ears that fold flat in water. They can smell a returning person from astonishing distances. They sleep in warm piles near doors, patrol eelgrass boundaries, retrieve dropped tools, and know when the black tide is coming before the current-readers do. Their barking underwater is more vibration than sound, a blunt pulse felt in the teeth.

The eels are not pets, exactly. Some families feed particular eels and claim they recognize voices. Some eels live for decades in the same household basins, becoming as familiar as irritable relatives. They are valued as cleaners and omens. An eel that leaves its accustomed drain is noticed. An eel that refuses food can empty a room of conversation.

Crabs belong to themselves, but the city bargains with them. In the oldest districts, certain species have become large and bold enough to take scraps directly from offered hands. They clean bone, eat rot, and remove the soft intrusions that gather in cracks. Children are warned never to harm a red-knuckled mortar crab, because it will remember the shape of your ankle. Whether this is literally true is not important; nobody tests it.

And then there are the things that might be people, or might only be city-shaped habits of animal life.

In the drowned foundations, pale figures are sometimes glimpsed beyond the last doorways: long arms folding through cracks, faces too smooth to identify, eyes reflecting algae-light from impossible angles. They are said to live in the deep walls, where old chambers have filled completely and no air has remained for centuries. Some say they are descendants of those who chose not to surface. Some say they are scavengers wearing the memory of people. They leave gifts on occasion: an intact shell, a drowned child’s bracelet, a fish arranged in a spiral. No one follows them.

The inhabitants call them the Near Ones.

They are not worshipped. The city does not have much use for worship that cannot mend a wall or feed a child. But when a Near One appears, people lower their hands and wait for it to pass. If it touches a threshold, the family inside does not leave by that door until the next change of current.

The city’s people are not resigned to their life. They are attached to it.

They complain about silt in the bedding, about eel bites, about cousins who borrow shell cups and return them chipped. They fear the black tide, the upper shadow, the warm wells, the sicknesses that come when a dead fish goes unnoticed in a sealed room. They grieve ferociously. They laugh with their whole bodies because laughter is easier to feel than to hear. They court, quarrel, steal, reconcile, and raise children who believe the drowned roads have always been roads and that stone has always had water moving over it.

When they speak of land, they do not speak of returning.

They speak of it as one might speak of a grandmother’s house burned down before one was born: a place preserved in stories, full of sharp corners, open flames, weather that touched the skin directly, and distances crossed without needing to know what moved beneath you.

It is real to them. It is ancestral. It is also, unmistakably, elsewhere.

Home is the basin: the pressure against the ears, the warm mineral breath of the floor, the familiar drag of current along a particular wall; home is the cord in another person’s hand when darkness comes and the city disappears around them.
