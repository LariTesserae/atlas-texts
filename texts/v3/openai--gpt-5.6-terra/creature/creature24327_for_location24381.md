What lives there is not a single species, though the basin has taught all its inhabitants to behave as if they are parts of one body.

The first life belongs to the wetness itself.

It clings to the dark wall in a skin too thin to notice until light catches it. In that skin live threads of algae, pale bacteria, fungal filaments, invisible grazers that feed on mineral salts released from the stone. They make the wall slick, green-black, faintly iridescent. At night, in the deepest folds where no daylight reaches, some of them give off a tired blue glow. Not enough to see by. Enough to reveal the outline of a drip, the wet track of a snail-sized crawler, the underside of a fern leaf trembling when something passes beneath it.

The creatures that drink from the wall have translucent mouths. They are small, soft-bodied things, almost slugs, though their backs carry overlapping plates of pale mineral. They spend the hot hours flattened in crevices, indistinguishable from damp stone. At dusk they emerge and move upward rather than down, grazing the algae film in slow, deliberate paths. Their young are borne in clusters beneath the parents’ plates. When the basin is especially dry, the adults gather in heaps around the dripping cups and seal themselves together with mucus that hardens into a communal shell. Inside, they wait for water.

The moss is inhabited more densely than soil.

Blue-black beetles make tunnels under its cushions, not excavated tunnels exactly, but routes pressed and polished by generations of use. Their polished shells are not merely armor. Fine ridges along them catch condensation. Before dawn, beetles climb the wet wall, turn their bodies toward the incoming warm air, and collect tiny beads of moisture across their wing-cases. They return to the moss burdened with water, where smaller animals lick the droplets from them: mites, larvae, narrow-headed insects with mouths like needles, and the pale, blind juveniles of the wall-grazers.

The beetles tolerate this. Some appear to seek it. A beetle that has been cleaned by enough small mouths gleams more brightly than the others and is allowed into certain moss chambers where eggs are laid.

The lizards know these chambers. They know the beetles’ paths, the bird nests, the warm spots along the half-buried pylon. But the lizards are not simply hunters in the basin. Their bright throat-fans have changed under the conditions of the hollow. Their colors are rich in ways that would be useless on the plateau: violet, oily green, a sudden white flash like sun on water. Their calls are built for stone. A lizard on one ledge can cry into a seam and hear, through the altered return of the sound, whether another lizard has entered the basin, whether a bird has disturbed its nest, whether a larger body is moving in the sand.

Some of the oldest males have scars across their heads from fighting. Some have no visible scars at all and are not challenged. These old ones choose ledges close to the standing pylons. They sleep with their bellies against the dark material. In the mornings, before any direct light reaches them, they are warmer than the other lizards.

No one knows whether the pylons warm them, or whether the stone remembers warmth from elsewhere.

The birds are the basin’s quickest lives. They make their nests in holes that look too shallow to contain them, weaving dry grass, vine fibers, beetle cases, and strips of translucent shed skin. Their rusty backs are the color of the plateau above. Their bellies are the color of the mineral crust left after water evaporates. In flight, they appear and disappear between shafts of light so rapidly that the eye begins to mistake them for flashes from the blue lattices.

They carry more than insects to their nests. They carry seeds.

Seeds from the flowering creepers collect in the birds’ throat feathers and under their claws. Seeds from outside occasionally arrive too: seeds of plants that cannot survive on the exposed plain but may live for a season in the basin’s damp margin. A bird brings them in by accident, and the hollow decides whether to keep them. Most sprout brightly, extravagantly, then fail. A few find a crack, alter the local shade, feed a new kind of caterpillar, and become part of the place.

The birds do not sing in the manner of open-country birds. Their voices are too sharp, too patterned. Their calls bounce from wall to wall and return altered. A pair may hold a conversation with echoes between them, each answering not the other bird directly but the hollow’s answer to the other bird.

At dusk the ceiling awakens.

The pale bats emerge first, their wings soft as scraps of skin held against the dark. They do not fly in wide loops. There is no room. They fold and unfold through the air in abrupt turns, finding invisible corridors among vines, ledges, birds, and falling water. Behind them come the gliders: long-bodied creatures with fine membrane sails between their limbs and fluted mouths that produce thin, almost inaudible whistles.

The gliders have eyes that reflect the pylons’ blue light in a dull silver. They cling in rows to the ceiling during the day, but at dusk they descend upon the flowering creepers. Their mouths fit deep into the flowers. Their bodies become dusted with yellow pollen. When they rise again, the flowers close behind them with a small, wet sound.

Some gliders do not leave the basin at all. They are born there, mate there, die there. Others vanish through cracks in the ceiling and return months later with torn membranes, new parasites, unfamiliar scents in their fur. Those returning animals are treated differently. The others make space for them along the ceiling, but do not cluster close until they have hung through several nights.

The pool contains its own age.

It is not a pond in the sense of open water. It is a dark lens, shallow at the edges and unexpectedly deep beneath the overhang. Its floor is packed with soft mineral mud, old leaf matter, insect shells, the fine bones of creatures that fell in and were not recovered. The filter feeders crowd its surface in shifting chains, their tails beating beneath them. They are not tadpoles, though they resemble them. Their round mouths open on the water film and draw in whatever arrives there: spores, pollen, dissolved stone, the oils from insect wings, the microscopic debris of every animal that drinks.

When mature, some leave the pool.

They climb out on humid nights and become squat, four-limbed animals with blunt heads and loose silver skin. They do not travel far. They hide beneath wet leaves and feed on the tiny creatures living there. In the deepest part of the night they gather around the smallest of the dripping cups and make their low booming call.

The sound is felt through the sand.

It is a sound the birds pause for. The lizards flatten themselves when it begins. Even the pool’s insects alter their tapping pattern. The call may be a warning, a courtship, a way of measuring water, or a vibration that tells the hidden animals beneath the mud that it is time to rise.

For there are things under the basin as well.

They are rarely seen. They live in the sand below the drip-lines and in the cracks where the old stone has been softened by centuries of moisture. Their bodies are long and segmented, pale where they remain underground and reddish where they surface. They have too many legs to count quickly and heads protected by shovel-like plates. They eat rot, roots, fallen wings, dead lizards when they can find them, and the soft material accumulating around the pylons.

They are the basin’s buriers. Without them, the pool would choke, the fern wall would harden under its own dead growth, and every open patch of sand would become littered with the remains of life. They remove almost everything eventually. But not the pylons.

Around the pylons, their tunnels curve away.

This is true even of the youngest ones, hatched in darkness and never yet exposed to the blue lattice. They can cross the warm sand beside a pylon; they can feed on moss growing against it; but when they begin a tunnel, they turn aside. Their bodies make a clean crescent around the black material, as if responding to a pressure too gradual for any living animal to notice.

There may be another inhabitant, or there may only be a pattern mistaken for one.

It can be heard most clearly in the hours before sunrise, when the plateau is cooling and the basin has not yet begun to receive light. The drops fall. The beetles climb. The gliders settle. A lizard shifts on a ledge. The blue lattices within the pylons brighten and fade, not in synchrony with one another but with intervals that seem almost responsive.

Then the basin’s sounds organize themselves.

The clicking of beetles falls into bands. Bird calls arrive in pairs. The pool creatures tap a repeated shape into the water. The booming animals answer from under leaves. Even the drips seem to hesitate and resume at particular moments.

It does not last long. A shaft of sunlight comes down through the cracked roof, touches the silver-green leaves, and the whole place becomes merely busy again: hungry, territorial, breeding, growing, decaying.

Still, the feeling remains that something lives in the arrangement.

Not a hidden person. Not an animal large enough to find. Not an ancient machine waiting behind a sealed door.

Perhaps it is the garden itself: the damp stone, the roots, the insects, the nesting birds, the buried circuits, the old mineral water, all learning one another so thoroughly that together they have become a creature. A creature without a face, without a central mind, without any need to leave the hollow that keeps it alive.

Above it, the plateau continues to weather itself into silence.

Below, in the unseen room, the living thing listens to drops of water falling into its small, dark cups.
