They are called, by people below, the Saddlefolk.

They do not call themselves that. Most have no use for a name that means “those who live high,” because to them there is no ordinary world beneath the mountains against which height must be measured. They name themselves by water, by stone, by a route kept open, by a line of ancestors who first put a retaining wall where a slope had wanted to fall.

“I am of the Second Basin,” someone might say.

Or: “My mother’s people kept the Red Gate spring.”

Or simply: “I am here.”

They are human, though lowland visitors sometimes insist otherwise after a few days among them. They are not taller or stronger than other people, not in any simple way. If anything, many are compact, broad in the chest, narrow at the hips, with hands made thick by cold and work. Their faces are weathered early: darkened around the eyes by reflected snowlight, cut by wind, creased by smoke. Children are carried beneath wool and hide until they can walk; after that they learn stairways almost before level ground. A toddler of the city may stumble in a flat market square but can cross a tilted roof in soft snow with astonishing calm.

Their balance is famous. So is their patience.

They stand differently from people of the lowlands. Feet turned slightly outward for uneven stone. Knees relaxed. Weight ready to move. They do not hurry unless there is fire, flood, or falling rock. Even then, their urgency is precise rather than wild. To run blindly in the city is considered dangerous, almost rude. A person who moves too quickly is one who has not listened.

They have a word for the kind of attention needed to survive there. It does not translate cleanly. It means, roughly, *to keep company with what may change*. A person keeps company with the snow, the wall, the goat, the child, the illness, the sky. One does not control these things. One watches them, remembers them, and makes oneself useful when they move.

That is the foundation of their manners.

A guest is not first asked where they have come from. They are asked what weather they crossed.

A marriage is not primarily the joining of two people but the joining of obligations: whose roof will be repaired, whose goats may winter in which shed, which grandmother will teach the next child the old routes, who will tend a spring if one household must leave for the lower valleys.

An elder is not merely old. An elder is someone whose memory has become civic infrastructure.

There are people in the city who can recite the changes of a single retaining wall over fifty years: the first outward lean, the winter it shed stones, the spring when the lane beneath it had to be narrowed. There are women who know every birth in a neighborhood because they have delivered most of them, and can tell, from a child’s breathing, whether it is mountain cough, smoke fever, or grief. There are goat-keepers who recognize their animals not by horn shape or coat color but by the sound each makes when slipping on ice.

The city’s people are never entirely alone. Solitude exists, but it is sought carefully: an hour on a high path, a turn tending a distant herd, a night in a watch shelter above an avalanche chute. Most life occurs within earshot of others. Houses share heat, walls, chimneys, food storage, arguments, songs, and sometimes illness. Privacy is made not by distance but by custom. A lowered curtain, a certain bowl left outside a door, a pair of boots placed crossed at a threshold: these are understood.

Their clothing is layered, repaired, inherited, and difficult to date. Outer garments are wool felted almost hard as bark, patched with hide at shoulder and knee. Gloves are often mismatched because a good glove is not discarded when its partner is lost. Children wear hoods padded with fleece and scraps of old cloth. Adults wind long woven bands around calves and ankles for warmth and stability on loose stone. The wealthy are not distinguished by silk or bright metal, which would be impractical and rare, but by dryness: better roof tiles, thicker felt, a private smoke vent, a storage room where grain has never frozen.

Color survives in surprising places. A red thread around a sleeve cuff. Blue beads traded from far away and worn in the hair of a bride. Yellow mineral paint on a shrine wall. Stripes woven into belts whose patterns identify a household’s old obligations. In the long white season, people become fiercely attentive to small colors. A green sprig in a market basket can draw a crowd.

They are practical, but not humorless.

Their jokes are usually about weather, goats, and people who came from warmer places with large opinions. They enjoy exaggeration. A lazy person is said to “wait for the mountain to come down and carry them.” A boastful person “has never met a slope they did not claim as their uncle.” Children dare one another to touch the ice on communal basins with their tongues, despite every adult knowing exactly what will happen. Old people tell frightening stories with deliberately absurd endings, and nobody admits to being frightened.

They sing, though visitors often mistake their songs for work calls.

The songs are low and narrow in range, shaped to carry through wind without exhausting the throat. Some are sung while grinding grain or hauling stone, where rhythm matters more than melody. Others are sung in pairs across a ravine: one voice begins in a doorway, another answers from a high terrace. In winter, when the smoke ceiling holds sound close, the city can become full of these distant exchanges. Not a chorus, exactly. More like many households reminding one another that they remain.

There are also those who belong less clearly to households.

The route-keepers spend much of the year beyond the dense city, checking cairns, replacing prayer cloths, cutting away ice, and looking for the first signs of slides. They are trusted and slightly feared, because they return carrying news of the outer mountain: a glacier crack, a dead traveler, a wolf track where there should not be wolves, a pasture newly exposed by retreating snow.

The wall-readers are masons, but more than masons. They inspect the stone courses of old districts, lay their palms against cracks, listen when a hammer strikes a suspicious block. Their judgments can empty whole streets. When a wall-reader says a house must come down, even its owners usually help dismantle it.

The smoke-wives and smoke-men tend the communal chimneys, which are among the city’s most important structures. A blocked flue can kill a family in a night. They climb roofs during storms, clear vents, judge fuel stores, and know every hidden passage by which smoke travels through joined buildings. Their faces are permanently darkened around the nose and mouth, and children sometimes make little clay figures of them with black paint rubbed on the cheeks.

There are bone-setters, midwives, goat healers, stone carvers, channel cleaners, hide curers, bell makers, and people whose entire skill is knowing how to pack a burden so it will not shift on a cliff path.

There are also the dead.

The Saddlefolk do not place all their dead in one cemetery. The mountain offers too little soft ground, and frozen earth resists digging. Some are laid in stone chambers beneath family houses. Some are given to high ledges where the great birds find them. Some, especially those lost to avalanche or ravine, receive no body at all. For them, families carve a notch into a memory board and add a small stone to a communal cairn. The dead remain active in conversation. Not as ghosts in the simple sense, but as remembered judgment.

“Your grandfather would have widened that drain.”

“Your aunt knew the western roof would fail.”

“Do not step there. That is where your brother fell, and the stone has not improved since.”

The city is inhabited by animals almost as intimately as by people.

Every household knows the sound of its goats moving on the roof. Goats are scolded, praised, blamed, mourned, and negotiated with in nearly human terms. Children receive a kid before they receive many other possessions; caring for it teaches them the mathematics of winter. Dogs sleep in doorways and under carts, their thick paws splayed for ice. Pack animals are treated with solemn respect. A mule that has carried stone for twenty years may be named in local records alongside people.

Ravens are regarded as neither sacred nor ordinary. They are said to be the mountain’s gossip. They watch butcher yards, follow caravans, steal wool, and appear before storms. Some neighborhoods leave scraps for particular ravens and claim they recognize individual voices. A raven that speaks from a chimney is not necessarily an omen, but it is always discussed.

The city also contains travelers, though fewer than it imagines. Traders come from below with fruit, wood, news, and manners that seem extravagantly wasteful. Pilgrims arrive to see the high shrines. Debtors come seeking work. Fugitives come because a city spread across mountains offers many places to disappear, provided one can endure the cold and earn a place by labor.

Not all stay.

Those who do are changed. Within a few years they begin to speak more quietly. They learn to pause at doorways and look upward before stepping outside. They stop wasting water. They stop treating a wall as scenery. They learn that every object has a winter use and that a broken spoon may become a hinge, a marker, a child’s toy, a splint, a ritual token, or the last piece of metal in a house.

The people of the saddle are sometimes thought severe because they do not make much display of grief, generosity, affection, or faith. But these things are everywhere, embedded in maintenance.

Love is a roof repaired before the first snow.

Faith is clearing the channel even when the spring is low.

Grief is carrying a dead person’s tools to the household that needs them.

Generosity is giving away dung, dry wood, warm space, or a place near the stove.

Their gods, if they have gods, are not imagined as masters above the peaks. The highest peaks are too remote, too indifferent, too clean. Their holy beings live nearer: in springs that return after freezing, in old stones that do not move, in the first goat born after a hard winter, in the sound a wall makes when it settles safely into place.

The most revered figures are often not gods at all but remembered people: the woman who diverted a flood into an abandoned quarry; the boy who crossed a snow bridge to warn the western quarter; the mason who designed a stair that has outlasted three neighborhoods; the nameless family who kept a communal fire burning through the winter when the pass stayed closed.

And beneath all this—beneath the people, animals, roofs, smoke, water channels, and carved boards—there is another inhabitant.

The mountain itself lives there.

Not metaphorically. Not as a deity exactly. But as a presence with weight, mood, memory, and appetite. It gives stone, water, shelter, pasture, and warning. It takes walls, paths, goats, children, entire rooms in the night. The Saddlefolk do not think of themselves as owners of their city. They think of themselves as the temporary warm part of a much older body.

They keep their fires in its folds.

They stack their homes in its cracks.

They raise children in the small intervals of mercy it permits.

And when they speak of endurance, they do not mean surviving unchanged.

They mean learning, generation after generation, how to become something the mountain does not yet know how to shake loose.
