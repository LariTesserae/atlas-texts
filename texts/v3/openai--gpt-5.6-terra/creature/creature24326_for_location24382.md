What lives in the garden depends on how long one remains there.

At first, it seems to be occupied only by the small creatures: the lizards with velvet or chalky skins; the birds that walk as if they are measuring the terraces for faults; moths that fold themselves into the shapes of pale petals; beetles that carry grains of copper dust on their backs. There are mice, or things close enough to mice, with long narrow faces and paws that can untie a knot if it has been badly made. There are blind, warm-bodied animals that sleep in the hot seams beneath the paving and surface at night with ash in their fur. Their young are born already accustomed to constructed things. They nest in cups, handles, sleeves, hollows in bricks. A fallen sandal, if left unused, will become an animal’s den before dawn.

But these are not the gardeners, not entirely.

The gardeners are called, among themselves, the Below-Folk, though none of them claim to have come from below. They are a small people, spare and brown-skinned from dust and sunlight, whose ages are difficult to guess. The oldest may look newly made; the youngest sometimes have the watchful stillness of old tools. They wear soft wrapped garments of reed cloth, old sailcloth, beaten fiber, and strips of something leaflike that does not rot. Their clothing is patched so often that a sleeve may include material from several generations of wearers. They favor pockets, loops, belts, and cords. Every one of them carries something: pruning shears, a spool of wire, a clay knife, a waxed packet of seeds, a little brush for cleaning dust from joints in the paving.

They do not own the garden. They are careful not to say that they built it, either.

They say they were invited into it when the walls were still low enough to see across. Some insist their grandparents came from one of the changing scenes in the wall: from the white-powder ocean, perhaps, or the forest moving across the desert. Others believe they arrived as animals first, and were taught their present shapes by the garden over many slow seasons. They do not argue hard about this. In the garden, origins are considered less reliable than maintenance.

Their work is nearly constant and almost invisible.

They tie a stem before it can choose the wrong direction. They file the edge of a copper leaf so it will not cut a bird’s foot. They turn ceramic plates to expose their warm sides when a certain root requires heat. They collect the dust that falls from the walls and sort it by color, texture, taste, and the way it clings to the fingertips. Some dust is fed to plants. Some is pressed into cracks. Some is saved in tiny stoppered tubes and buried in the uppermost layer of paving, where it may eventually become useful.

They know which plants are hungry for warmth and which are hungry for attention. A silver-haired stalk may need to be stroked each morning with a bone comb. A bladder-vine may thrive only if spoken to through a clay funnel. There is a low blue plant, common around the warmest terraces, that closes permanently if it hears a human voice used in anger. The Below-Folk avoid shouting. When urgency is required, they ring spoons against the teeth of old tools.

They sleep in the garden but not in houses. Their sleeping places are woven into it: hammocks beneath vinework, chambers behind clay screens, padded niches built into the inner walls. They change beds often. A place safe in one season may be claimed by a flowering root in the next; a wall socket may become warm with the beginning of something beneath it. They regard this inconvenience as a courtesy. The garden tells them when it needs space.

There are also those who cannot be seen until they choose to move.

A bundle of dry reed at the foot of a wall may be a person wearing a rainless cloak. A carved stone beside the well may lift its head on a thin neck and blink. Some inhabitants are too old or too altered to be easily named. They have allowed the garden to make use of them. One woman has stood for so long beside a copper channel that fine green roots have grown through the seams of her fingers. She no longer travels far, but she can tell, by touch, where the warmth beneath the paving is flowing. An old man sleeps inside an overturned clay vessel with a slit cut for breathing; he says the vessel is remembering the kiln, and he listens to its dreams.

There are children, though they are not numerous. They are raised communally, and each is given a small unfinished object rather than a name at first: a handle, a ring, a tile, a button, a spoon. The object changes with them. It is added to, repaired, engraved, carried, lost, found, and sometimes incorporated into the garden when the child is old enough to choose a proper name.

Children are taught the prohibitions before they are taught songs.

Do not pull at a root that enters the ground.

Do not pry open a seam beneath the paving.

Do not ask the well what it is winding up.

Do not follow a scene in the wall if it begins to show you yourself.

And, most important: when something new appears, do not decide too quickly whether it is an object, an animal, a room, or a guest.

The garden has produced all four.

Once, a small black ladder appeared beside a terrace, no taller than a child’s knee. For three days it led only to the underside of a stone. On the fourth day, a man climbed it and did not return until evening. He emerged older by several years, carrying a basket of seeds that had never been catalogued. Another time, a glazed bowl appeared filled with pale vapor. The gardeners left it untouched, placing a ring of tiles around it as a sign of respect. By morning the vapor had condensed into six sleeping animals, each no larger than a thumb and each possessing a humanlike hand.

Not every inhabitant is welcome, but few are expelled. The garden has no true exterior into which something may be cast. Harmful things are redirected, contained, fed differently, or given a task. There is a thorned vine that once strangled birds; now it is trained around the well, where its sharpness keeps curious animals from chewing the rope. There are pale beetles that eat resin and weaken the reed mats; the Below-Folk keep them in a warmed clay trough and give them broken combs, old lacquer, and the shed skins of certain lizards.

Even the dead remain inhabitants for a while.

The Below-Folk do not bury bodies below the paving. They lay their dead in high wall niches, wrapped in plant fiber and thin sheets of copper. Over time, the garden takes what it can use: heat, minerals, hair, memory of hands. The niches become warm. Roots gather around them but do not break the wrappings. Sometimes a dead person’s tools disappear from beside them and later emerge elsewhere in the garden, polished, altered, or bearing new marks.

The inhabitants say this is not resurrection. It is continuation by other means.

And beneath all of these lives the largest resident: the garden itself.

It is not merely a place, though it has no face, no single voice, no intention that can be trusted. It notices pressure. It remembers repair. It prefers certain patterns of care and resists others. It makes room, but never freely. It grows downward because there is nowhere else for it to grow, and because something beneath it is either calling it, feeding it, or waiting to be reached.

The Below-Folk tend it with the grave patience one gives a child, an animal, a wound, and a god that has not yet learned its own name.
