What lives in the high country is not a collection of species so much as a collection of ways to survive being unfinished.

Nothing there expects a stable home. The creatures of the basins, cliffs, vapor forests, and new screes are born into a world that may alter its angle before they reach maturity. Their first instinct is not territoriality. It is attachment: to warmth, to moisture, to a mineral taste in the air, to a vibration in the ground that says the stone beneath them will remain stone for another few hours.

There are animals that have never slept twice beneath the same horizon.

The immense shelled kneaders are among the oldest-looking things, though no one could say whether they are old. They move in low, deliberate files through the warm basins, their plated backs dark as forged iron and streaked with green mineral bloom. From a distance they resemble boulders pushed by some buried tide. Up close, each shell is made of overlapping plates, newly secreted at the rear and worn away at the front, so that an individual is continuously traveling through its own body.

Their undersides are soft, pale, and crowded with mouths.

They do not feed as animals elsewhere feed. They ingest terrain. Their lower surfaces loosen mud, roots, dead organisms, hot water, ore-bearing gravel, and pieces of whatever has fallen from the slopes. The material passes through chambers where fungi, bacteria, and heat-loving worms break it down. What emerges behind them is sorted: black metallic plates, veins of glassy slag, soft mounds of fertile sediment, occasionally a clutch of translucent young embedded in a brick of warm mineral pulp.

Other beings depend on their passage. Mosses settle in the shallow grooves left by their shells. Reed beds root in their discarded sediment. Small burrowers follow them to eat the pale mineral crust that gathers around their joint seams. When one dies, it does not rot quickly. Its shell opens along old growth-lines, and the basin begins using it as architecture. Ferns rise through it. Hot water collects in its cavities. Whole colonies of smaller animals live for generations within the dead furnace-body, while the shell gradually becomes indistinguishable from the terraced land.

The white crack-larvae are stranger still. They live where the earth is most feverish: in vent mouths, fissures, and the red-lit undersides of recently lifted shelves. They are blind and soft as peeled roots, yet their digestive systems contain a difficult kind of precision. They take in silicates, ash, acidic water, and the mineral leavings of other creatures. They exhale ceramic.

A cliff threaded with their work can look webbed in old bone. Fine white cords emerge from hairline cracks and sag through empty air. Thick cables fuse loose slopes into lattices. Some curtains of filament are so dense that moss and vine root within them, making hanging gardens that sway above ravines. The larvae do not appear to know what they build. Their creations are merely hardened breath, waste shaped by the demands of their bodies.

But other lives know.

The pale climbing animals make nests inside the ceramic curtains. They have long forelimbs split into many hooked digits, and folding facial frills that conceal their eyes, mouths, and heat-sensing pits. When still, they resemble pale knots in the hanging stone. When disturbed, they unfold in a sequence too quick to follow and vanish upward, hand over hand, into mist.

Their young are born without bones. For the first weeks they drift in warm vapor pockets beneath the parents’ bodies, feeding on condensate and spores. Only after they have learned the motion of the cliff do their internal supports harden. If the cliff falls before then, the young can flatten themselves against a new surface and remain there until their bodies decide which way gravity has become.

There are creatures for whom gravity is only a seasonal condition.

During a mountain’s inversion, when water lifts from pools and stones pause on slopes, the high country’s smallest animals become briefly visible in their true abundance. The moss opens. The reeds uncoil. Whole swarms rise from soil that had seemed empty. Some are no larger than ash flakes, with transparent sails stretched between their limbs. Some are dense, beadlike things that normally cling beneath rocks but ascend during the uncertainty, drifting toward electrical fields. Some have no fixed shape at all: gelatinous colonies that spend ordinary days as damp stains in cracks, then gather themselves into floating nets when the world’s downward pull weakens.

These lives are eaten by the great winged creatures above the cloud line.

The winged creatures are perhaps the nearest the high country has to sovereign animals, though they do not rule anything. Their bodies are narrow and warm, plated in dark membrane and reddish translucent tissue. Their wings unfold in several pairs along the body, each joint able to flex independently. In dim weather they are seen only as changes in the cloud: a long dark shape moving against the bronze light, an inner glow passing beneath vapor, a pattern of slow wings reflected in a mineral pool.

They feed not only on drifting life but on conditions. Spores collect in comb-like structures along their throats. Fine dust clings to their wing membranes. Electrical charge moves through their blood in visible blue pulses. In the charged cloud layer, a flock may wheel for hours, gathering the tension of the sky. Then they descend through a valley in a long spiral, their wingbeats drawing charge down toward the silver-backed reeds.

The reeds receive it.

Their roots form black conductive mats beneath the mud, connecting pool to pool, basin to basin. When struck by the winged current, their seed-heads flash in patterned sequences. The flashes are not merely light. Burrowing animals wake. Grazers change direction. The enormous shelled kneaders alter their lines and begin moving toward a different warm basin. Across distances too great for sight, other reed beds answer.

It may be communication. It may be a nervous system spread through wetlands. It may be weather remembering where it has been.

The small garden-backed grazers seem to understand it best. They travel in soft waves through the amber moss, each animal no larger than a hand, each carrying on its back a living mound of fungus, algae, and tiny heat-licking flowers. Their legs are numerous but move so gently that a passing herd barely disturbs the cloudwater gathered on the moss. They eat mineral film, root fuzz, and the newly soft edges of young stone. Their gardens feed from the waste they produce; their gardens also feed them when the ground becomes barren.

A grazer separated from its garden dies quickly. A garden separated from its grazer may slowly grow legs of its own.

In the sheltered vine mats live the frilled climbers, but also warm-bodied things that seem more plant than animal. They root their rear limbs into wet crevices and extend broad, veined sails into the steam. When rain comes in its brief vertical threads, they close around it. When the air is dry, they release stored water as a fragrant fog. Smaller animals gather beneath them, and their broad surfaces become miniature landscapes of mites, pollen-eaters, sleeping larvae, and tiny predators that hunt by sensing changes in temperature.

Some of these rooted creatures eventually detach. Their rear limbs thicken; their sails fold into muscular flanks. Then, often during an earth-lurch or following a long change in underground heat, they pull themselves free and migrate downslope in slow, deliberate groups. The place where they had grown is left with a smooth, moist scar full of roots. Within days, other things colonize it.

The high country produces predators, but not always in recognizable forms.

The long-bodied hunters of the hot pools are patient enough to be mistaken for geological features. Their skin is ridged and root-colored, coated with a film of algae and mineral sediment. Only their eyes remain clean: dark, faceted, set beneath the waterline. They can lie for months in a shallow warm pool while new reeds grow through the mud along their backs.

They do not strike at every movement. They wait for an animal already confused by changing ground: a grazer whose herd has turned, a frilled climber forced down from a broken cliff, a burrower fleeing steam. Then the pool folds around them. The hunter’s body is mostly muscle, but its bones are flexible chains of black glass. It can knot itself through channels too narrow for its prey to imagine entering.

The angular pack-things of the high screes are more active. They are built from planes and hinges: narrow torsos, long rear limbs, wedge-shaped heads, and padded feet that cling to obsidian without sound. They hunt in newly fallen rubble, where everything is sharp and unstable. Their black feet are not keratin, not rubber, but a living glass that grows and wears like skin. When they leap, the pads briefly soften, molding themselves to a landing stone before becoming hard again.

They hunt soft-bodied burrowers, especially the ones that live beneath freshly warmed rock. These burrowers are blind cylinders of muscle and glandular tissue, with mineral plates embedded beneath their transparent skins. They eat heat-loving fungi and the dense microbial mats that grow around vents. In turn, their bodies concentrate rare salts and metals. The scree-hunters can smell these minerals through a foot of rubble.

Yet the packs do not merely kill. When a slope begins to shift, they gather at its edge and call—not audibly, but by stamping their glass feet in rhythms that travel through the talus. The burrowers hear it first. Then the grazers. Then the trees bend. Sometimes an entire living slope begins moving before the rock itself has decided to fall.

There are more obscure inhabitants beneath all of these.

The soil, where soil exists, is crowded with hot-rooted networks that may be fungi, may be organs of the land, may be the juvenile state of some immense buried animal. They extend through ash, stone, shell, ceramic filament, and old root systems. In some valleys, a white thread found in the mud can be followed for miles before it vanishes into a steaming crack. In others, black cords rise from the ground during electrical weather and braid themselves around reeds, trees, or sleeping animals.

The animals avoid cutting these cords. Even predators step around them.

When the roots of the world-thick networks are exposed during an inversion, they do not look dead. They pulse. Great layers of pale tissue shift under translucent membranes. Lights drift inside them as though carried by slow blood. Sometimes an opening mountain reveals an entire hanging root mass, hundreds of feet deep, and creatures emerge from it that are never seen afterward: wet, colorless forms with too many limbs, or floating sacs that rise into the upward wind, or things that fold themselves around newly exposed stone and become part of the cliff.

Perhaps these are not separate animals at all. Perhaps the high country has organs that occasionally mistake themselves for fauna.

And there are the dream-spores.

They are carried in mist, caught in fur, lodged in the warm folds of shells and wing membranes. Most are harmless, or useful: they help an animal remember a migration route after the route has been destroyed; they cause a herd to wake together before a fissure opens; they lead a grove of soft-trunked trees toward a hotter seam. But in dense valleys, where vapor cannot escape and the bronze cloud presses low, the spores accumulate.

Then the dreams become larger than the sleepers.

A group of animals resting together may dream of a basin that no longer exists, its pools glowing under a mountain that has long folded inward. They wake and walk toward it. They may travel for days across new terrain, following a memory inherited from stone, fungus, weather, and the bodies of the dead. Sometimes they arrive at nothing.

Sometimes, by arriving, they make the basin happen: kneaders compact the earth, reed roots bind the mud, spores seed the air, and condensation gathers where a hundred warm bodies have changed the temperature of the ground.

The high country does not contain animals in the usual sense. It contains participants.

Every creature is an instrument for moving heat, water, minerals, charge, seed, hunger, and memory across an unstable surface. The shelled kneaders make ground. The larvae stitch it. The reeds conduct its storms. The winged things harvest the sky. Grazers carry gardens over bare stone. Predators prune the panicked and the stranded. Rooted creatures become walkers. Walkers become habitat. The dead become terraces, pools, nests, cliffs, and soil.

Even the things that seem solitary are rarely alone. A hot-pool hunter contains algae, glass-boned parasites, and sleeping spores. A winged giant carries weather in its membranes. A moving tree shelters insects inside its vapor-soft trunk. A stone may be newly exposed but already inhabited by films of life waiting in the cloud.

What lives there, finally, is a vast appetite for continuation.

Not survival exactly. Survival implies preserving a shape.

The lives of the high country do not preserve shape. They learn the new shape quickly. They soften, root, molt, climb, burrow, drift, transmit, harden, open. When the mountain turns inside out, they do not ask why. They become the creatures of the inside.
