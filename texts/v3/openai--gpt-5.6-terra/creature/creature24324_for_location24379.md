What lives in the basin is not one population but a pressure of lives, each adapted to a different depth of dampness, noise, and unfinished ground.

There are people first, because there are always people wherever a machine has been left running long enough.

They call themselves basinfolk only when speaking to outsiders, and then usually with irony. Among themselves they belong to districts: Lime, Sump, East Fan, Low Brick, Bluewater, the Arches, the Old Cut. A person’s address may be a painted number on a viaduct pier, a lamp color at a junction, three turns past a cracked settling tank. Children learn routes by sound before they learn maps: follow the slow pump, turn where the fan changes pitch, keep the yellow pipe on your left until the frogs become loud.

Most live in rooms made from whatever can resist the water for another season. Former offices, freight containers raised on blocks, pump-house annexes, old rail cars with their wheels buried in mineral crust, brick terraces beneath arches. Their homes are narrow, warm, wet around the edges. Every household owns buckets, spare wire, rubber sheeting, lime brushes, and a tool for clearing drains. Curtains mildew. Books swell. Clothing is hung from the ceiling, where it drips steadily onto the floors below. Cooking happens on raised metal stoves. Beds stand on legs of different lengths, adjusted year by year as rooms settle.

Their bodies show the basin’s influence without becoming inhuman. Their skin is pale or stained by work: orange at the wrists from iron water, white in the creases of the hands from lime dust, black around the nails from old grease. Their hearing is tuned strangely. They can sleep through a compressor but wake at once when a familiar pump changes rhythm. They know by smell when a canal has backed up, when a cable is burning behind a wall, when cold air from the surface is bringing a storm. Some older residents have lungs that whistle in the wet air, but others claim the basin preserves them: the warmth, the constant moisture, the absence of sun-hard wind.

They move carefully and quickly. Nobody runs unless a wall has gone. They carry lamps low, where the ground can be read. A puddle is never merely a puddle; it may be a shallow skin over a shaft, a fresh mineral bed, a rat nest, an electrical leak, a submerged rail. Children are taught not to throw stones into water without waiting to hear the answer.

There are workers who keep the country above from falling into the country below. Pump tenders, fan hands, cable splicers, rail divers, sluice crews, mud surveyors, mineral cutters. Some work for companies based in daylight towns; some work for district cooperatives; some work because a failed pump near their homes is more urgent than any contract. Their work is often maintenance of things nobody remembers building. They grease gears older than their grandparents. They inspect retaining walls made from bricks that have become stone at the edges. They clear calcite from valves and cut roots from ventilation ducts. There are people whose entire occupation consists of listening to buried pipes through metal rods pressed to the ground.

The basin also keeps scavengers, though they dislike the word.

They collect copper from drowned cable runs, glass from old spoil, timber from collapsed structures, iron plates from the bottoms of settling ponds. They know which abandoned districts are merely abandoned and which have been claimed by bad air, deep water, or the things that do not like to be disturbed. A good scavenger can look at a white crust on a doorway and tell whether the room beyond has been dry for a week or flooded for twenty years. They trade in bearings, lamp glass, dry paper, medicines, boiler seals, old coins, surface fruit, and rumors of routes to places where the ceiling still remembers daylight.

There are communities of nearly silent people near the loudest works: families who communicate by lantern shutter, chalk mark, shoulder pressure, and the reading of mouths at close range. In the fan districts, speech is a private act conducted indoors, face to face. Out on the service roads, conversation is a language of raised hands. A red lamp moving in a particular sequence can mean train, flood, stranger, dead cable, child missing, or come quickly but do not bring everyone.

The oldest residents say that the basin makes its own manners. Nobody slams a door. Nobody whistles near a fractured ceiling. Nobody points at a pool they have not seen before. A lamp left burning at an empty crossing is not taken. A rope tied to a rail is not moved. When someone dies, their tools are hung for one full shift beside the nearest working pump, so that the continuance may acknowledge them.

Animals live among the people without fully belonging to them.

The rats are citizens in their own right. There are ordinary gray rats in the dwellings and black-water rats along the canals, but deeper down are broad-backed things with pale whiskers and scarred, almost hairless tails. They follow the heat of cables and the crumbs from workshops. They have learned the schedules of freight lifts. In certain districts, they wait by the tracks before trains arrive, sensing vibration long before human feet can feel it. The basinfolk curse them, feed them, trap them, and sometimes consult their behavior as weather.

Cats are prized but rarely stay small. They live in pump houses and crane sheds, with damp velvet coats and strong paws. They hunt rats, frogs, and pale moths, then sleep on generator casings. Some have been born so many levels below the surface that they panic in open fields when taken above. They crouch in sunlight as if it is a noise.

Dogs are less common, because barking travels too far and startles too much. The few that remain are low-bodied, broad-chested animals bred for slick paths and narrow ledges. They work with rail crews and water searchers. They can smell a trapped person through wet concrete, and they have an instinctive hatred of newly formed mineral floors, refusing to cross them until they have been tested.

The fish differ from pond to pond. In the warm orange drains, carp grow thick and blunt, their scales marked with rusty stains. In lime channels, small silver fish move in clouds over chalk-colored beds. In the blue-black water of the old quarries are eels large enough to knot around a forearm, their skin cold despite the warmth around them. People say the oldest eels know the original waterways beneath the works, the buried course of the old sea pocket. They emerge after pump failures, nosing through flooded doorways, appearing in districts that have not seen open water for decades.

There are frogs that are green, brown, ash-white, and nearly translucent. The palest live where mineral water seeps from the walls. Their calls become part of the basin’s acoustic machinery: a loose clicking beneath the fans, a rubbery croak beneath the conveyors, a rapid metallic chirp around electrical pools. In quiet places their chorus can become so dense that visitors mistake it for an engine.

The insects are more various than people admit. Midges hang in warm air above the settling ponds. Blind flies gather around lamps. White-backed roaches breed in the warm cavities behind switchboards. Crickets inhabit dry seams in old brick, where their song is thin enough to disappear beneath the machinery. There are long-legged spiders living between pipes, their webs constantly jeweled with condensation. In the deepest wet galleries, tiny transparent things drift over the water’s surface, so slight that they seem at first to be dust or reflected sparks.

And then there are the creatures that may not be creatures in the ordinary sense.

The basin grows forms.

At first these are only mineral accretions: a white collar around a pipe, a soft gray mound at the foot of a wall, a curtain of calcite thickening over a blocked doorway. But the basinfolk speak of certain growths with caution. Some seem to advance toward warmth. Some appear repeatedly around old foundations, making columns where no columns existed. Some have swallowed tools and preserved their outlines: a wrench, a glove, a boot, half a bicycle, all held in pale stone as if the ground is learning what people look like.

There are deposits called sleepers. They form in shallow flooded chambers where warm mineral water enters cold. From a distance they resemble bodies lying under white blankets. Their surfaces rise and fall slightly when pumps change pressure. No one agrees whether this is movement or merely water passing through hidden channels. No one disturbs them. The few who have tried found the material soft as clay beneath its hard skin, and warm.

There are also the root-knots.

Where trees above send roots through cracks, the roots descend into the basin and encounter pipes, brickwork, cables, and the thick mineral air. They become swollen, braided, blackened by damp. Some spread along ceilings for dozens of yards. Some enter rooms through broken plaster and continue across walls, following old conduits. The basinfolk sometimes cultivate them, wrapping their growth around beams and cracked arches so that living wood becomes another kind of support. In certain old galleries the roots have enclosed entire structures: a ticket booth, a bicycle rack, an abandoned generator, the narrow shape of a person’s chair.

Those districts are avoided after dark, not because the roots move—roots do not move in any hurry—but because water dripping through them makes a sound like careful footsteps overhead.

The basin has its legends of larger presences. A thing in the drowned rail tunnels that follows lamp light but never enters it. A pale-backed animal seen crossing the mudflats beyond the Bluewater pumps, too broad for a dog and too quiet for a pig. A colony of bats in an upper vault that leaves in one body, like smoke taking the shape of a wing. A man-shaped mineral figure beneath the Old Cut who gains a little more shoulder every winter.

Most residents do not insist these things are real. The basin is full of shapes that become something else when your lamp shakes, full of echoes arriving late, full of water that reflects a person from impossible angles. But they do not dismiss them either. In a place constantly making new stone, new passages, new ponds, new failures, it seems arrogant to believe that only the familiar kinds of life can settle in.

What lives there, finally, is an agreement.

People live by keeping water moving and walls standing. Animals live in the warmth, waste, shelter, and margins made by human labor. Plants live in cracks opened by neglect. Mineral lives by covering, joining, thickening, and slowly undoing the distinction between natural ground and built structure. The machines live too, in the sense that they require feeding, listening, repair, and in return they regulate weather, depth, access, and survival.

The basin is inhabited by anything patient enough to be altered by it.

Nothing there remains exactly what it was when it arrived.
