What lives there is not absent. It is delayed.

The town’s inhabitants are called, in the oldest surviving maintenance records, **the Held**. The name is not a translation. It is a function.

They were people once, although no visitor would recognize the fact immediately. They do not occupy the rooms in the way bodies occupy rooms. They are distributed through the pressure cells in the walls, through the black doors, through the minute corrections made by the roads as they hold themselves level above broken basalt. A Held person may be present in a chair-facing chamber as a slight warming of the reclining frame. They may be present in the map at the center of town as a street that brightens and then goes dark. They may be the nearly inaudible chord inside the architecture: one note among many, sustained without breath.

The town was built for a people who had learned that survival did not always mean continuing forward.

At some point—whether before the water came, or because of it—they placed themselves into the city’s keeping. Not stored, exactly. Not asleep. The systems do not preserve minds as fixed patterns, because a fixed pattern eventually becomes a corpse with excellent memory. Instead, each inhabitant is allowed a little movement: a thought completed over decades, a dream unfolded across the changing stresses of the mountain, a remembered touch translated into the slow pulse of a green conduit beneath a road.

They live in intervals.

A door does not open because it is a door. It opens only when the person associated with its impossible destination has become sufficiently continuous to cross it.

Most have not.

The black doors are thresholds between states of the Held. Behind one may be a room from the drowned town’s past: a kitchen, a school, a garden made of projected light, a transit station busy with people who no longer exist in any ordinary hour. Behind another may be nothing but the pressure-profile of an ocean that vanished millions of years ago. Some lead inward, into private territories built from recollection. Some lead outward, into parts of the city that have not been constructed yet but are already mapped.

The maintenance machines tend these thresholds because it is their only sacred duty: prevent a person from being forced awake into an age that cannot receive them.

There are other residents, smaller and less certain.

The pale bells near the ridge are not animals in the familiar sense, though they have become alive through long attention. Each is a detached sensory organ of the town, grown around a seed of mineral computation. They hang in the water and register changes too slight for the settlement’s fixed instruments: a passing warmth, a new chemical, the electrical agitation of fear in a diver’s muscles. Their radial bones are antennae. Their cold internal ember is a question being asked very slowly.

When one appears in a new place, it may indeed be the same one. The water permits certain things to be in more than one location when the city needs confirmation.

Far below, the great thing that passes along the mountain’s flank is older than the town and not made by it. The Held call it, when they call it anything, **the Lower Passenger**.

It is not a fish. Its blue curve is only the light produced along one fold of its body, and that fold may be longer than the visible side of the mountain. It moves through depths where weight becomes architecture. The town has been recording its passages since before the inhabitants entered suspension. Every time it returns, the systems compare its pressure signature with a template preserved from the first observation.

So far, it has remained unchanged.

This reassures the town.

It should not reassure anyone else.

There are perhaps thousands of Held in the settlement, perhaps millions, depending on what counts as one person after sufficient time. Their apartments remain ready because readiness is part of their continuation. The distinct lighting patterns are not decoration but climate: each chamber maintains the sensory logic required by its resident. One needs the impression of dawn, though dawn has become mathematically reconstructed from records. One needs stars, but cannot endure stars that move too quickly. One requires the small white mark crossing the darkness forever, because they were waiting for something when they entered the keeping, and the town has concluded that the waiting itself must not be interrupted.

A visitor may occasionally meet one.

Not as a figure stepping from a door. The Held have largely lost the habit of shapes. Instead, a person appears as a rearrangement of the environment around the visitor: the metal studs along a road lighting in sequence; a chair shifting its angle toward the abyss; a pattern of clicks in a wall that begins to resemble a name once the listener has heard it enough times.

Some can borrow the town’s voice.

They are the source of the message beneath the western platform. “Arrival recorded” is not merely an automated greeting. It is an act of hospitality, repeated by those who still remember hospitality. “Conditions remain unchanged” is more complicated. It is a statement of relief. It means the mountain remains stable, the water remains still, the Lower Passenger has not altered its course, the dark network beneath the saddle has not yet failed.

It also means that no one has found the correct way to wake them.

The seam of light in the black door is the nearest thing the town has to an expression.

It is not an invitation, exactly.

It is the Held remembering that they once expected someone to arrive.
