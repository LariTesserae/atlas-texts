They live in the intervals between workings.

At first they are mistaken for the place’s continuation: a dark shape in a reed screen, a hand withdrawing from a drainage mouth, a bare foot on a pale slab that has the same warm clay color as the mortar stains. They are difficult to see because they have learned, or have always known, how to stand without interrupting a wall.

They are not numerous. The whole bright country may hold only a few hundred of them, perhaps fewer, distributed among its courts and planted terraces as carefully as fruit trees. Each household has a basin, a roof catchment, a sequence of channels, several jars of roots, a little stretch of reed wetland, a share in a kiln, and an obligation to some portion of the larger water. Nobody owns water. One tends its passage.

Their bodies are made for damp heat. They are narrow-waisted, broad through the ribs, dark-skinned with a permanent sheen of condensation along the shoulders and throat. Their hair is kept close to the head or gathered in thick ropes wrapped with fiber, not ornamented so much as made orderly against the wet. Their eyes are pale in the deep shade—amber, green-brown, sometimes the dim gray of water under leaves—and they look for a long time before they decide that anything has changed.

They wear little, but what they wear is complicated: layered skirts of softened reed cloth, netted belts carrying knives of shell or fired glass, shoulder wraps woven loose enough to breathe. Children wear strings of small ceramic floats, each one marked with a household sign. Adults have similar signs pressed into the skin of their wrists with blue-black dye: a gate, a fish spine, a coiled pipe, a leaf with seven veins, a brick scored by three fingers.

No one among them bears the emblem of a mountain, a river, or a sun.

Those are not things they remember having.

They call themselves, if they call themselves anything, the Keepers of First Water. It is not a title of authority. It is closer to a task assigned before birth. To them, water is not wild. Wildness is a condition in which water has forgotten its route, in which it falls from above without being invited, gathers where no basin has been made, and digs its own mouth into the ground. This is an old terror, though none of them has seen it.

Their oldest stories describe the world before the cup was made.

There was brightness without shade, they say. There was heat with nowhere to go. There was wetness trapped in the air, aching to become a weight. Their first ancestors woke beneath this pressure on a plain that had not yet decided whether it was land. So they made walls. They fired blocks. They wove reeds. They shaped the first gutter with both hands and held it beneath a bead of moisture until the bead entered it.

That first bead still travels.

It may be the water in every channel, every cistern, every dark bowl in every shrine. It never leaves the valley. It passes through roots, animals, clay, human bodies, vapor, and stone, but it is never lost. To spill it is not forbidden, exactly. Nothing is forbidden in a place where every mistake can be given a basin. But an unreceived drop is considered sad. Children are taught to cup rainless dew from leaves and return it to the nearest runnel.

They do not build quickly, though everything appears newly made. Their buildings remain young because the people remake their surfaces constantly. Each dawn—or what passes for dawn in the endless bright hour—someone walks every major wall with a pouch of wet mortar. They press fresh clay into hairline cracks, smooth the edges of gutters, clear rootlets from places where roots have become too ambitious, and reinforce the sharp corners of the escarpments.

Their fingerprints are not incidental.

A wall without fingerprints is considered unfinished, perhaps dangerous. It means no one has recently remembered that the wall is being held in shape. Every person, when reaching a certain age, chooses a portion of the country to touch regularly for the rest of their life: a flight of steps, a drainage lattice, a set of orchard jars, a culvert beneath a sleeping platform. The marks accumulate. A person can tell which hands have maintained a wall long after the hands themselves have gone elsewhere.

The dead do not leave the valley, because there is nowhere outside it that has been prepared for them.

When one of the Keepers dies, their body is placed in a low clay chamber beneath a wet garden. The chamber is not sealed. Water passes through it slowly. Roots are trained across its roof. The dead become part of a particular circuit: fig water, lotus water, reed water, wall water. A family may say that their grandmother is in the southern overflow, or that their brother has become good for cucumbers, or that a certain coolness in a sunlit passage belongs to an uncle who was fond of shade.

They are not mourned with loudness. Loudness unsettles the surfaces.

Instead, the dead person’s tools are washed and arranged in a public court. Neighbors take one tool each and use it until it wears out. A polished pestle, a pruning hook, a fish trap needle, a small scoop for clearing the mouths of pipes: these pass hand to hand until their original owner has become impossible to separate from all the work done afterward.

The people have no kings. They have measures.

There are those who measure water height with rods marked in baked dots. There are those who measure the sweetness of fruit, the heat held in paving, the thickness of algae in the blue channels, the duration of kiln smoke, the weight of dew collected from awnings. There are memory-keepers who know the intended course of every buried pipe and can hear, by placing an ear to a wall, whether water is moving with the right patience.

The closest thing they have to rulers are the listeners.

These are usually old women, though not always. They live in houses built over complicated junctions where several waters meet below the floor. They sleep lightly. In the bright stillness they hear the minute wooden knocks, the shifts in pressure, the tiny changes in a frog’s call. When a canal begins to show clouds that are not in the sky, or when a shadow lies toward the sun, people come to a listener with a bowl of clear water.

The listener looks into it.

Sometimes she says, “Plant there.”

Sometimes, “Do not cross that court until the fish have turned.”

Sometimes, “The wall has remembered an earlier shape. Let it have it.”

They do not call these occurrences miracles. A miracle would be a break in the making. These are only the country thinking aloud.

Children are allowed near the errors more than adults are. They play in wrong-facing shade and learn games from reflections that arrive before their sources. They float leaf boats down channels that briefly run uphill. They know which plaza smells of rain and press their cheeks to its white surface, hoping to hear the first weather coming from beneath.

Adults do not discourage this. They believe the children are still partly unmade, still able to understand what the country was before it became useful.

There are craftspeople whose work has no visible purpose. They make bricks with hollows shaped like sleeping animals. They weave screens whose patterns reproduce water routes no one uses. They fire small bowls with black interiors and leave them on terraces to collect dew. They carve wooden floats that are set into cisterns, where they rise and fall by amounts too small for ordinary eyes to notice.

These objects are not decorations. They are appeals.

The people suspect that the world outside their constructed basin exists only as potential weather: a vast unchanneled pressure waiting beyond the young escarpments. Every clean terrace at the horizon, every unfinished ridge, every newly planted crest is part of a continuing effort to prepare for it. They are making more country as quickly and carefully as they can, not expanding in conquest but extending the reach of places where water will know what to do.

They fear the first rain.

They desire it, too.

A true rain would prove there is an above beyond the bright, fixed sky. It would mean time has resumed somewhere. It would mean erosion may finally earn authority, that slopes may soften, channels overflow, roots break jars, and the hand-shaped world become something no hand can entirely keep.

Some among them believe this will be the end.

Others believe it will be birth.

So they continue their work in the suspended noon. One repairs a screen beside the canal. One lifts a frog from a blocked pipe and returns it to a shaded basin. One kneels in an orchard court, turning fallen fruit so its sweetness can be shared with ants and crabs. One stands high on a watch platform, looking not outward but down, following the gleam of water through the white constructed land.

And beneath the sleeping platforms, behind the cool walls, inside the glazed gutters and packed reed panels, the country’s small, tireless population goes on living exactly as it was designed to live:

not apart from the made world,

but as its hands.
