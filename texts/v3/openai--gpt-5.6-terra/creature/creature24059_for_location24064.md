What lives in the valley is not seen at first because it has learned not to occupy the water in the way an animal does.

It does not cross open distances. It does not hunt through the meadows or school beneath the ice. It does not disturb the silver seed-threads of the grass. For long periods it is possible to believe there is nothing living there but plant, mineral, rot, and current.

Then a bell sounds, and something answers from inside a wall.

The old people did not leave the valley when it flooded. Or not all of them did. They altered their lives until life ceased to resemble departure or survival in any ordinary sense. They became the **underkeepers**—though no surviving mark gives them that name. Their own sign is the downward stroke inside the circle, cut into road markers, door lintels, bridge stones, and the rims of wells. It does not mean *below*. It means *inward*.

They live in the places where water is compelled to choose a direction.

In the channels carved through foundations. In the throats of springs. In the hollow centers of drowned trees. Behind the fitted stones of the bridge cairns. In the long black seams between the slabs of old roads. A house that appears full of clear, empty water may contain an entire family folded into its walls: pale shapes pressed in chambers too narrow for a swimmer, their limbs jointed close to the body, their faces turned toward the faint moving pressure of a current.

They are not large.

An adult underkeeper, when it unfolds, is roughly the size and arrangement of a human being. It has a head, though the skull has become long and light, almost lantern-shaped. It has arms, though their bones bend more freely than human bones ought to, and the hands are broad, webbed, and furred in fine sensory filaments. It has legs, but uses them rarely. The feet are hooked for stone and wood, with a second thumb-like toe for gripping wet surfaces.

Its skin is not naked. Over the years it grows a soft coat of mineral and living fiber: white around the joints, green-black across the spine, red in narrow branching seams wherever blood runs nearest the surface. A resting underkeeper can be mistaken for a mass of moss over a ruined beam, a pale root among reeds, a knot of silt caught in a wall channel.

They do not breathe as the old people breathed.

Their ribs have opened into combs. Fine dark membranes spread beneath the collarbones, along the flanks, behind the knees, and under the tongue. Water moves through them in slow, deliberate pulses. In warm springwater they can remain active for days. In the glacial reaches they slow almost to stillness, entering long watches from which they may not rise for a season.

This is why the valley seems uninhabited.

Much of the time, it is asleep.

The underkeepers do not feed on the meadows. They leave the grasses and fern fans untouched, as if those plants belong to another covenant. Instead they take what the valley continually gives away: trace metals from the springs, pale bacterial veils growing on stone, the drifting mineral dust released by the upper ice, and, in ways no one from the dry world would understand, vibration.

Sound nourishes them.

Not metaphorically. Their bodies are full of small cavities, resonant chambers lined with cilia and mineral plates. A distant crack of calving ice can wake a sleeper in a buried house. The knocking of drowned trunks feeds the young gathered inside a tree hollow. The deep boom under the silt causes a whole colony to uncoil from a terrace wall, faces lifted, hands spread, listening with their skin.

The bells are part of this life.

The green-black bells were not merely warnings, nor memorials, nor instruments of worship. They are organs outside the body. When the current catches them, their low notes move through stone and water, through old timber and buried roadbeds. The sound enters the underkeepers’ chambers and tells them what the valley is becoming: a cold front descending from the ice; a spring turning mineral-rich; a slope beginning to shear; a field releasing spores; a birth beginning in one of the warm stone platforms.

A bell answering from miles away is not always another bell.

Sometimes it is an underkeeper standing in a tower stump with its throat open.

Their voices are difficult to hear. At close range they make no speech, only a soft clicking in the teeth and a submerged hum that seems to come from the chest of the listener rather than the speaker. But through stone, and at a distance, their calls become enormous. They travel uphill, according to the valley’s strange law, and gather beneath the ice in slow chords.

On rare bright days, when the pale ceiling lightens and the deep places become blacker, they retreat. Light harms them, though not by burning. It makes their edges uncertain. Their mineral coats pale. The red branching seams under their skin vanish. In such hours they pack themselves into wells, drain tunnels, hollow trunks, and the blind chambers beneath the settlement’s floors.

But in the deepest dark, when the red mosses wake, the underkeepers come out.

Not all at once. First one appears beneath the bridge, pulling itself hand over hand along the underside of a slab. Then another rises from the meadow, shaking loose a cloak of grass. Then pale heads emerge from doorways and cracks in the terrace walls. They move slowly, always with one hand touching something made by the old people: a carved channel, a copper wire, a stone step worn smooth by hands that may have been their ancestors’ hands.

They gather on the old roads.

There they perform the work that has become their culture.

They clear silt from the spring mouths. They replace fallen cairn stones. They braid weeds around fractured bridge slabs to hold them together until the next current. They scrape moss from the downward-pointing symbols, not to reveal them for anyone else, but because the marks still direct them through the valley’s altered physics. They enter houses and tend the preserved baskets under their overturned bowls. They turn mineral-wick lamps in their niches, though no flame is lit.

The lamps are not for fire.

The underkeepers fill them with luminous moss and tiny red mineral organisms gathered from the valley floor. A lit house glows only faintly, a bruise-colored stain behind its doorway. Yet beneath the water, in the valley’s deepest hour, those dim lamps make a kind of homecoming.

They have children, though these are seldom recognized as children.

A young underkeeper begins as a translucent ribbon attached to a warm spring wall, feeding on mineral bloom and the vibrations passing through the stone. For years it has no limbs. It curls and uncurls with the current, its first face only a dark, listening pit. Later it leaves the spring and lives in a drowned trunk, growing fingers, eyes, and the fine red seams beneath its skin. The oldest underkeepers are almost indistinguishable from their dwellings. They become fixed in walls, bells, roots, or caverns, their bodies calcifying into shapes that continue to feel the water long after movement has ceased.

Perhaps some of the cairns are old people.

Perhaps some of the wire-bound stones have hands beneath them.

The underkeepers remember the air, but only as a transmitted inheritance. They know it from objects: the pitched roofs, the sealed lamps, the narrow round doorways, the upward-polished handholds. They tell of it through pressure-patterns passed from skin to skin. Air, to them, is a dangerous old brightness where sound did not travel far and bodies could fall.

They do not wish to return to it.

The valley is not a tomb to them. It is a body that has outlived one form of life and made another from what remained: stone, copper, springwater, bells, drowned wood, patient moss, and the knowledge of how to wait.

When all three bells speak together, the underkeepers leave their chambers and climb.

They gather along the ridges, holding fast to the old stakes, the ruined tower, the high roads vanishing toward the ice. Their pale bodies bend in the current with the grass. Their red seams begin to shine. And for a little while, throughout the drowned highland, the valley’s many hidden listeners answer the bells with a low, living note—so deep and sustained that the silt rises in gray veils, the plants bow down, and even the black water seems to remember that it is full of people.
