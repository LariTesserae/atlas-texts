What lives there is not merely a population, though there are people.

The first inhabitants are the things that can endure dampness without complaint: mosses, sedges, liverworts, small ferns with dark polished stems, fungi that make their shelves beneath staircases and their pale folded cups in the warm seams of boiler rooms. They live with an old municipal confidence. They understand drains, brick pores, the undersides of bridges, the quiet backs of cupboards. They occupy every place that has been neglected only briefly, and every place that has been deliberately offered to them.

Some are useful plants and know it. The reed-grains lift their narrow heads in the greenhouse beds. The bitter greens return after cutting. Root mats hold the banks of the channels together and clean what passes through them. Vines have been taught to open shutters, but they are not servants exactly. They pull because pulling is what they do; the town has simply arranged its windows in places where a vine’s desire can become a useful gesture.

Others have no apparent task. They live in the margins of utility: tiny blue flowers in a crack beside a pressure gauge; a silver moss on the lip of an unused loading dock; a dark-leaved creeper threading itself through the keyhole of a door that still locks. The residents leave these alone unless they block a bearing, smother a signal lamp, or begin to test the mortar around a water main. The line between nuisance and neighbor is carefully maintained.

The human inhabitants are few enough that one can spend a whole afternoon walking the principal streets without seeing one. Yet their presence is everywhere in the shape of passage.

A strip of paving has been scrubbed clear to the dark wet shine of old stone. A railing has been wrapped in a fresh layer of braided fiber, soft and resilient beneath the hand. A wooden wedge has been placed beneath a door swollen by rain. The dead leaves have been swept from the pump-house threshold but not from the yard beyond it, where they are meant to settle and become soil. Someone has adjusted a mirror at a blind corner. Someone has repaired the small bell that rings whenever the cistern level falls below a painted line.

They are a people of rounds.

In the morning, before the mist has left the square, one or two figures may emerge from the former hotel, wearing waxed coats or patched overalls, boots with broad soft soles, gloves knitted from coarse plant fiber. They carry long-handled hooks, oil tins, pruning knives, bundles of wire, sampling jars, keys. Their work is not divided cleanly into gardening, engineering, sanitation, farming, or caretaking. Such distinctions matter less here.

A person who opens a blocked culvert may afterwards harvest watercress from its outflow. A person checking a generator may stop to turn a young vine away from the ventilation fan. Someone who makes bread may also maintain the warm fermentation room, inspect the fungal trays, and record the first flowering of a medicinal climber. Children, if there are children in the town, learn the routes of water before they learn the names of streets. They are sent with messages between the market hall and the river wall, with small baskets of cuttings, with instructions to listen to a pump and report whether its beat has changed.

The residents are not hidden, exactly. They are simply not interested in being conspicuous.

They have inherited a settlement in which display is expensive. Bright paint flakes. Fabric mildews. Unprotected paper becomes pulp. So they keep their important words in metal, etched glass, fired tile, stamped lead, or in the minds of people who repeat them while working. Their homes have few large windows opening directly onto the streets; they favor inner courts, upper rooms, insulated alcoves behind greenhouse walls. Much of domestic life occurs near warmth and water: around cookstoves, beneath drying racks, beside the softly ticking pipes that distribute filtered river water.

Their clothing tends toward dark greens, browns, rust-reds, and the yellow-gray of old canvas. This is not camouflage in any martial sense. It is practical. The town marks bright things quickly with pollen, damp, soot, leaf stain, and the fine mineral residue left by evaporated water. Their garments are repaired visibly. Patches are not signs of poverty but records of use: a knee mended with sailcloth, a cuff reinforced in woven root fiber, an elbow marked by an old manufacturer’s stamp from a reclaimed machine belt.

They may have names for themselves that outsiders would misunderstand. Basin people. Channel keepers. The low-town. Perhaps no collective name at all, because the town is so complete a fact that saying one belongs to it would be redundant.

There are older residents who remember when some of the systems required more force. They can recall the years when the river rose above the retaining wall, when the pumps ran hard and the square held a sheet of water for three days. They remember fungal blights in the seed archive, roots breaking into the western transformer room, the winter when every roof gutter froze and the town had to carry water in buckets through the tram sheds.

They are valued not only for their memories but for their ability to recognize deviation.

One old worker can put a palm against a pipe and tell whether the flow below it has become too warm. Another knows, from the smell of the market hall at dawn, whether the upper beds need airing. A third can hear a turbine belt slipping through two walls and across a rainy courtyard. The town’s machines are old enough to have personalities, but not so old that they are indulged. Their sounds are listened to with the attention one gives animals: affection without sentimentality, concern without surprise.

The machines themselves live here in the way old machines sometimes do when generations of people have depended on them.

They have routines. They wake under load. They complain in weather. They maintain temperatures, lift water, turn compost, light corridors, keep small reserves of charge in rooms whose walls are wrapped in roots. Their bodies are made of iron, brass, rubber, enamel, ceramic insulators, belts repaired with layered material, housings painted many times and worn back down to their older colors.

Some have names, but these names are local and changeable: the South Pump, Old Sable, the Blue Fan, Number Four, the Singer. A machine that fails beyond repair is not simply discarded. Its useful parts are removed, its casing may become a planter or a bench, its gears may be cleaned and stored in the market hall’s tool room. Some defunct motors rest in the walls of greenhouses, where their heavy housings stabilize trellises. The town does not confuse life with operation, but it does not consider usefulness the only reason to preserve a thing.

There are other inhabitants that no census would include.

The damp has its colonies. Thin translucent worms move through compost beds. Mites inhabit the root mesh. Beetles shelter beneath trays of cut plant matter and emerge after dark to cross the clear pavements, their black shells briefly catching the tram lights. Moths gather at the turbine-house lamps in small solemn numbers. Spiders make low webs between railings and leave them strung with droplets by morning.

A few birds nest in high places where the vegetation is thinner: under the eaves of the old administrative building, in the girder cavities of the market hall, behind the loose letters of a rusted sign. They are not numerous. The town has little appetite for chatter. Their calls are short, dry, and separate, heard more often from a distance than close at hand.

The foxlike thing in the roof gardens may be a true fox, a descendant of some animal that once came down from the embankment and found the basin warmer, quieter, and more generous than the fields. Or it may be several animals seen at different hours and assembled by habit into one creature. It knows the paths between roofs, the gaps beneath the depots, the doors that are sometimes left unlatched. It takes eggs, beetles, scraps from the fermentation rooms, perhaps rats when rats become bold enough to appear. The residents do not try to domesticate it. They notice its passage in the same way they notice a change in wind direction.

In the channels beneath the streets, there are lives even the residents encounter only indirectly. Root systems make slow decisions there. Water molds spread and withdraw. Blind pale insects move through the sediment. Fine fish may enter from the river during high water and remain in the colder cisterns until they grow thin and silver, nearly invisible against the walls. The old channels are not quite sewers, not quite canals, not quite foundations. They are the town’s buried memory, and things live in memory differently: without being seen, but with consequence.

Perhaps the deepest inhabitants are the systems themselves.

The agreement between root and wall.  
The agreement between wet brick and dry floor.  
The agreement that allows a roof to become soil without becoming a collapse.  
The agreement that says a corridor must remain clear, a pump intake must remain open, a handrail must not be swallowed, a window must still admit light.

This agreement is maintained by people, plants, tools, weather, repetition, and attention. No single resident possesses it. It exists in the timing of pruning, in the choice to leave one patch of moss and remove another, in the placement of a drain tile, in the habit of listening before changing a valve.

So what lives in the town is a small, dispersed civilization of caretakers and cultivated growth, of persistent machines and patient water. Its citizens include the person carrying a tray of seedlings through the tram shed, the vine tightening around a shutter wheel, the beetle beneath the wet board, the pump drawing brown river water through roots, the fungus whitening the edge of a compost bin, the bird on the wall, the old worker who knows which pipe is wrong by touch.

None of them rules the place.

The town is alive because each has learned the limits of its claim.
