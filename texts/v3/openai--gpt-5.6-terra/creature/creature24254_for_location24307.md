There is something in the hollow that does not use the shelter, though the shelter belongs to it in the loose way that a fallen branch belongs to the tree that dropped it.

At first there seems to be only the ground. The bare ground has its crust and its cracks, the channel its black strip of mud, the shrub its thin hooked branches. But the dark seam where the water disappears is never quite merely dark. In the hours after rain, when the clay has softened underneath without yielding at the surface, a slow pressure rises there. Not enough to make a mound. Not enough to disturb the grit except in a circle no wider than a hand.

Then, sometimes, a pale knob appears in the mud.

It may be the back of a skull-sized stone, except that it is smooth where the other stones are rough, and colored like old wax or the underside of a mushroom. It remains exposed for a long while. Water gathers against it. A reed bends nearby. Nothing otherwise changes. Then the knob is gone again, and the mud has closed around the place with no visible hole.

The thing beneath the hollow is not large. It could not be, not with the banks so close and the clay packed as hard as it is. Yet it has lived there long enough that the place has acquired its shape around its movements. The shallow channel runs where it runs because, deeper down, there is a passage that accepts water. The darker seam is not entirely a seam. The occasional slip and hollow sound underfoot come from chambers pressed into the clay by a body that does not dig quickly, but lies against the earth year after year until the earth gives way.

It is warm-blooded, perhaps, but only barely. In the cool shade below the northern bank it may become so still that the damp on its skin condenses and gathers in beads. It drinks little. Rainwater finds it. It feeds on what comes down through the soil: drowned roots, pale grubs that arrive from somewhere beyond the hollow, the softened bodies of small things that fall into cracks and are not recovered. There are no insects on the mud because the ones that reach the mud are not there for long.

There may be more than one.

In the hottest hours, when the cloud cover thins and a weak yellow light lies over the plain, the thorny shrub sometimes trembles from within. Not in the wind. Its branches lift and settle in a sequence too measured to be weather. Beneath its roots, where the bank is held together by the old woody grip of the plant, there is another opening. A narrow one, lined with packed black soil. Something rests there with its face turned inward.

Their faces, if they are faces, are not made for looking across open space. They are blunt, low, and unfinished-looking, with sealed or clouded eyes. They know the hollow through pressure: the drumming of rain on the upper banks, the minute collapse of a grain of sand, the vibration of a reed stem as it dries. The leaning shelter speaks to them differently from the earth. Its boards contract at evening. Drops fall from its roof in a repeating pattern. The old rope, when damp, makes a faint fiber-deep creak. These sounds have become part of their weather.

At night one of them may emerge.

It does not come fully into the open. The hollow is too broad, the sky too exposed even under cloud. It brings itself out in portions: first a rounded pale forepart at the channel mouth, then a pair of short limbs placed carefully in the wet clay, then the long heavy remainder dragged without urgency behind. Its skin is the color of the bank after rain, grey and yellowed, marked with fine grains that never entirely wash away. It leaves no clear trail. The crust reforms. The mud shines, then dulls.

It crosses only as far as the shelter.

There, beneath the sloping roof, it pauses beside the flat stone. The stone holds the day’s warmth after everything else has cooled. The creature presses its underside to it and remains motionless until the warmth is gone. Perhaps this is why the shelter still stands. It has been braced from below more than once by a weight that does not understand building, only the persistence of a useful shape.

The rope is sometimes found shifted by an inch or two.

No hand has tied it. No deliberate knot has changed. It has simply been nudged, nosed, gathered under a slow body and left in a slightly different curve. In damp weather, a line of clear mucus may dry along its fibers, so thin that it resembles a sheen of rain.

It is not lonely in the way a person would be lonely. The hollow provides enough variation: wet and dry, warmth and shade, the yearly loosening of the bank, the different smells that arrive with rain. A single fallen leaf is an event. A new crack in the clay is a rearrangement of the world. The creature, or creatures, endure these changes with an attention too deep to be called waiting.

The place is quiet because nothing there needs to call.

Beneath the crust, beneath the old clay and the thin leaking water, there are bodies curled in the earth’s retained warmth. They breathe slowly enough that the hollow can seem empty for days. Then the ground darkens near the channel. A small pale shape presses once against the mud. The reeds lean. Somewhere under the bank, water slips into a chamber and is received.
