What lives there is not one people but a negotiated multitude: creatures that have learned the city’s temperatures, timings, leaks, and permissions.

The human inhabitants are basin people, even when their families came from elsewhere. They have the look of those who live among stone, vapor, and work that cannot be postponed by rain. Their coats are waxed, patched, and dark at the cuffs. Their boots are made for slick stairs and shallow water. Children learn routes not by street names but by sounds and smells: the pigeon bridge, the hot-pipe alley, the tannery stair, the goat wall, the tunnel that tastes of iron. Most can tell which district they are entering by the rain on their faces—clean cold water from the eastern shelves, warm metallic drizzle near the works, soot-dark drops below the western stacks.

They do not think of themselves as living *beside* animals. They live among them, and much of civic life is arranged around that fact.

There are goat-keepers who know the upper wards as shepherds know hillsides. They do not own every goat they guide; ownership in the old quarry bands is complicated, inherited, disputed, and occasionally irrelevant. A keeper may be responsible for thirty animals on paper and three hundred in practice. They carry salt bricks, hooked staffs, small tins of ointment for hoof cracks, and bells with distinct tones. In the morning they encourage the bands upward toward runoff channels and loading yards; in the evening they coax them away from tram lines, market awnings, and the warm mouths of furnace buildings. Goats have right-of-way on certain staircases, written into municipal law so old that no one remembers its passage.

There are buffalo handlers in the perimeter claylands, broad-shouldered people in waterproof coats stiff with dried mud. They live in concrete stations raised slightly above the flood levels, with sleeping rooms upstairs and feed stores below. Their work is slow, close, and dangerous in the untheatrical way of large animals: checking a hoof, turning a frightened bullock before it reaches a culvert, walking knee-deep through brown water to free a harness. They paint horns for holidays, yes, but also for identification in fog. Some buffalo carry family marks older than the rail lines. Their handlers speak to them in low repetitive phrases that sound less like commands than reminders.

The freight-yard dogs live with crews, not owners. A dog may sleep in one signal cabin, eat at another, and ride three districts on a brake car before returning to the same coal bunker at dusk. They are shaggy, clever, usually damp, and excellent judges of character. They know when a cable engine is about to start, when a night crew has dropped food, when a train has brought strangers who do not understand where their feet belong. A yard dog will follow a worker for years without ever entering that worker’s home.

The drainage dogs are different: narrower, short-coated, gray or brindled, with paws worn smooth by brick channels. They belong to the underlevels, to pump crews and rat-catchers and people who repair gates beneath streets. They disappear through openings no larger than a dinner plate and emerge days later from another ward, wet to the shoulders and pleased with themselves. Children leave bowls for them near grates. Adults claim they are vermin. Everyone knows their names.

Cats occupy the city’s warmth. They are found in boiler rooms, bakery cellars, transformer stations, tram depots, hospital laundries, and beneath the lifted corners of market stalls. Their coats are often smoke-colored, rust-colored, or the pale mineral white of the upper terraces. They have little interest in rain, but a profound interest in steam. The oldest cats grow round and solemn beside public vents, attended by stallholders who pretend not to feed them. Some are said to have ridden up from the lowlands generations ago in grain cars. Others, people insist, came out of the quarry depths before the first buildings were raised. Both stories are told with equal seriousness.

The pigeons are almost a second weather system. They are heavy-bodied birds, cliff-bred and unafraid of height, their wings darkening the mist beneath viaducts. They nest in clock towers, bridge trusses, chimney mouths, unused ventilation shafts, and the old ceremonial facades of government buildings. Their feathers are harvested when they molt; their droppings are collected, cursed, processed, and traded; their movements are read by people who work outdoors. A sudden tightening of the flock can mean a cable engine has failed, a storm is coming through a high cut, or a hawk has entered the basin from the north.

The bats belong to those who work below. Drainage inspectors know their colonies by season and sound. Old tunnel families speak of particular vaults as if they were neighborhoods: the Narrow Mouth, the Blue Arch, the Seven Pipes. In summer, when the bats come out in their evening sheets, stallholders pull canvas over fruit crates, children stand under awnings and cheer, and the city’s insect problem visibly thins across the wet streets. No one calls them beautiful exactly. They are too useful for that. But people watch them.

There are also those less welcome residents that no city can eliminate: rats in the feed warehouses, silverfish in records offices, pale eel-like things in the warmest drains, feral hens in the back courts of the old market. The rats are enormous in some districts, fat on grain and fish scraps, and have prompted generations of inventive traps. The hens are descendants of shipments meant for kitchens; they have no soil in which to scratch, so they peck at mortar, dung heaps, and dropped feed. Their eggs, when found, are considered lucky.

Among the human residents are rail families, whose apartments shake gently whenever the night freight crosses nearby. There are pump families, who understand pressure, gauges, floodgates, and the moods of old machinery. There are market families, living above narrow stalls where vegetables from the lowlands arrive wet with packing ice and are sold before they can spoil. There are quarry-descended households in rooms cut far into the slope, their rear walls bare stone and their floors always slightly cool. There are chemical-workers’ districts where every balcony has a drying rack for protective clothing, and reservoir villages whose children grow up knowing that a bad valve, miles away, can change the whole basin’s night.

Many residents have work that would seem minor elsewhere but is indispensable here. Grate-clearers keep leaves from being a problem despite the absence of trees; what blocks drains is feathers, packaging, feed husks, hair, soot, and the strange fibrous residue of industry. Roof-channel tenders climb the stacked neighborhoods checking filters and shared tanks. Manure collectors run carts behind goat bands and buffalo convoys, gathering fuel, fertilizer for distant farms, tanning material, and methane stock for neighborhood digesters. Feather-sorters work in warm sheds. Bone boilers render scraps into glue, stock, ash, and industrial additives. Cable listeners walk beneath the climbing lines and know from a change in hum when a carriage is overloaded or a wheel is beginning to wear.

The city has people whose whole vocation is mediation between species. Animal clerks issue herd permits and feed allotments. Stair marshals redirect goats during rush hour. Veterinary teams travel by small rail carts, carrying slings, sedatives, hoof tools, and portable lamps. Children sometimes apprentice as pigeon counters, sitting under eaves in rain and recording the movement of flocks for the weather office. There are public arguments about buffalo access, dog taxes, feed subsidies, pigeon nesting rights, goat damage to municipal masonry, and whether the bats should be protected in the old capital drains even when their guano corrodes the brick.

The old people are especially important. In a place where infrastructure survives by memory as much as by maintenance, they remember which wall sweats before a collapse, which stair turns to ice first, which culvert backs up during a week of ordinary rain. They remember the last time the basin flooded high enough to float rail ties in the central market. They remember districts before the viaducts, before the new floodworks, before the goats were regulated, before the eastern reservoirs were sealed. Their apartments often contain jars of screws, carefully folded maps, spare valve handles, old cable tags, and photographs of relatives standing beside animals or machines that now seem impossibly large.

What lives there, then, is a civilization of damp endurance.

It is made of workers, children, cooks, mechanics, clerks, herders, scavengers, train crews, bath attendants, pipe-fitters, night watchmen, animal medics, roof walkers, and people who simply keep going because the basin requires it. It is made of goats with salt-whitened horns; dogs who understand train schedules; cats sleeping in steam; pigeons filling the air beneath rain; bats emptying from the dark below; buffalo moving through clay with the solemnity of barges.

Nothing has much privacy in that city. Heat is shared. Water is shared. Noise passes through walls. Animals sleep against doors. A household may wake to the sound of a goat coughing on the landing, a dog shaking rain from its coat in the hall, pipes knocking under the floor, pigeons muttering in the vent, and the first cable carriage beginning its climb through cloud.

And because nothing roots in the ground, life roots in habit: in routes, warm surfaces, feeding times, old agreements, familiar noises, and the repeated decision to remain.
