Nothing lives there in the ordinary sense for very long.

There are no settled herds, no rooted populations, no creatures with territories marked by scent or song. A thing that depends on a burrow will lose it. A thing that builds a nest will watch the ground open beneath it. A thing that needs a reliable spring, a shaded side of a hill, a seasonal path, will have no use for the country’s brief arrangements. The land does not yet offer continuity. It offers only intervals.

But intervals are enough for some lives.

The first inhabitants are the films at the vents.

They are thin, gray, and difficult to distinguish from deposits left by the mineral breath. At a distance they resemble soot arranged in fans. Up close, their surfaces are densely folded, as if made from innumerable tiny plates that turn toward warmth. When a fissure opens below them, they widen. Their branching edges crawl—not quickly, not visibly, but steadily—over the hot basalt. They feed on the difference between the cold dark air and the buried heat, taking sulfur, salt, and whatever rich metals come up in vapor. When a vent dies, they do not die at once. They draw themselves inward, darken, and become a hard dust-crust, indistinguishable from the stone until another fracture wakes them.

In the deepest cracks live their slower cousins: clear, blistered membranes spread along the walls where the heat is greatest. They swell as vapor presses through them. They collapse when the pressure drops. Some are no more than chemistry repeating itself; some may remember the opening and closing of the earth, may alter themselves in anticipation of it. Given enough time, their transparent skins thicken with pale threads, and the threads knot into structures that recoil from certain minerals and spread toward others. They have no eyes, but they know where the ground is about to become impossible.

Above them, in the narrow margin between hot stone and dry air, there are the ash-walkers.

They are rarely seen whole.

A traveler crossing a dust pan might notice a line of tiny indentations leading toward a fresh crack, only to find that the marks become shallower and finally vanish. Or they might see a low gray shape pressed beneath an overhang, so still it seems to be a folded piece of cooled lava. Then a tremor passes, and the shape is gone—not running, not leaping, but slipping into a seam too narrow for any animal of familiar flesh.

They are small, perhaps no larger than a hand when contracted. Their bodies are jointed in overlapping plates of black mineral and pale membrane. The plates are not armor exactly; they are borrowed ground. Each ash-walker carries a crust of local stone across its back, growing and shedding it as the landscape changes. On a salt ridge it becomes white and brittle. Near iron vents it turns red-black and glossy. In the glass-storm basins it gathers a dull skin of fused dust, which can be discarded in a single shudder when the shards become too deeply embedded.

They drink nothing. There is nothing to drink.

Instead they press their undersides to warm rock and take in heat, salts, gases, trace electrical currents from the moving crust. When the earth begins to speak through its deep, rib-shaking fractures, they flatten themselves and listen. Their bodies are made for this listening. A tremor that would mean nothing to a human carries, for them, the shape of a coming collapse: the direction of a fault, the opening of a hot seam, the slow descent of a shelf. They survive because they do not mistake stillness for safety.

Most of their lives are spent hidden in the shallow, warm layers beneath dust pans, where the ground is porous enough to breathe but not yet hot enough to melt them. There they form loose, temporary clusters around mineral blooms. They touch one another with fine feelers and exchange grains of crust, perhaps food, perhaps information, perhaps fragments of the places they have survived. When the basin begins to buckle, the cluster separates without alarm. Each follows a different vibration toward another heat source. They do not mourn the lost members. They may not understand loss as a permanent condition. In their country, separation is simply the form survival takes.

There are larger things farther up.

They live on the high rafts of stone whose undersides vanish into dust and heat. No one on the lower terraces sees them often. In static-light, however, a silhouette may appear against the bruised sky: a long, low body moving across the flat black pebble fields, broad as a boat and almost as flat. It carries no weight in its legs because it has few legs; instead it spreads a fringe of dry, feathery vanes along the ground. These vanes vibrate in the hot currents rising through the raft. With them, the creature senses the hidden pillars of heat that hold the platform aloft.

When a raft begins to fail, the high creatures do not flee downward. Downward is death, or transformation, or a place too dense with heat and falling stone to distinguish from death. They move toward the thinning edge, raise the vanes along their sides, and let the dry updraft take them.

For a while they drift.

Not flying in the wet, winged manner of other worlds, but riding gradients of heat, dust, static, and mineral vapor. Their bodies open in the thin high air, becoming almost transparent. They cross the gaps between platforms invisibly, trailing threads of black ash. Sometimes the threads catch on a ridge, and a new film begins there. Sometimes the creature itself lands, folds shut, and hardens until it resembles a dark slab among many dark slabs.

It may wait years before moving again.

The stone structures belong to neither the films nor the ash-walkers nor the drifting raft-things.

They were made by people.

Whether those people still live in the country depends on what one means by living there.

They were not numerous. The shelters suggest a population that understood scarcity and did not expect permanence. Their walls are low because high walls attract collapse. Their entrances face away from the prevailing ash winds, but not always from the heat, as though warmth mattered more to them than breathable air. The slabs in their circles are fitted with extraordinary patience. Each one has been placed where a fault would be least likely to twist it apart; each bears shallow cut marks at points where the stone would be easiest to lift after the next tremor. They did not build against the land. They built so that the land could take their work apart in a predictable order.

The white cairns are their most enduring sign.

The white stones came from elsewhere: old salt beds perhaps, or a vanished plateau, or a basin now buried under miles of ash. They were carried upward one by one. Some cairns stand beside warm cracks. Others overlook nothing but darkness and an unfinished cliff. Inside a few, under layers of dust, there are hollows lined with the translucent vent-crust. These hollows may have held eggs. They may have held vessels. They may have held hands.

The people themselves were adapted to waiting.

They had dry, heat-darkened skin, skin that cracked and healed in patterned seams. They wore layered coverings made from mineral fiber and the shed membranes of the high drifters. Their faces were shielded against glass dust by masks of thin carved stone, fitted not to exclude the air—no mask could make the air kind—but to make breathing deliberate. They traveled in small groups across the terraces, never making a path where one was not needed. They learned the timing of faults as other peoples learn tides. They slept in short intervals, waking whenever the rock changed pitch beneath them.

They did not farm. They tended vents.

At the mouths of stable fissures, they encouraged the gray blooms to spread across prepared slabs. They harvested the crust when it thickened, grinding it into a bitter meal rich in salts and heat-born compounds. They cultivated translucent membranes in sheltered chimneys, coaxing them into bladders that could trap the smallest traces of vapor and concentrate them. Perhaps, over generations, they learned to make water from that vapor. Perhaps they survived on so little that the word water became ceremonial rather than practical.

Their old stories would not have been about ancestors who arrived in a finished land. Their ancestors would have emerged, or endured, or awakened amid the rising terraces. They would have known that mountains were not ancient fathers but temporary weather in the earth. They would have named a ridge only after it survived three collapses. They would have named children after faults, vents, and dust winds—not as a curse, but as a wish for responsiveness.

Some say they left.

The lowest shelters are empty, the highest cairns unvisited. No fresh cuts appear on the slab circles. The vents nearest the old camps have grown wild with gray fan-blooms. It is possible the people descended, following a newly opened chain of warm fractures into lower country where water eventually gathered. It is possible they climbed onto the rafts and learned to travel with the high creatures. It is possible the country simply took them apart, slowly and without malice, and distributed them through its salt, ash, and glass.

Yet there are signs that something remembers the shelters.

After a storm, when ash has filled an entrance to the lip, the dust is sometimes found pushed outward in a low crescent. Not scattered by wind: moved from within. On the inner walls of a few half-buried spirals, fresh marks appear in the old fracture-lines. They are not writing in any recognizable sense. They may be measurements. They may be counts of tremors. They may be the patient work of someone tracing the same cracks their predecessors traced, trying to learn whether the ground is finally becoming trustworthy.

And in the longest pauses—when no shelf is falling, when the static has gone quiet, when the warmth underfoot rises evenly through the dust—a figure may stand beside one of the white cairns.

It is difficult to see in the bruised light. It may be a person in layered ash-cloth. It may be a slab of basalt tilted upright by accident. It may be a high creature folded into stone. It does not wave. It does not call.

It stands facing the distant ridges as they lift, grain by grain, out of the dark.

Perhaps that is what lives there most truly: not a species, not a people, but a practice of attention. Small lives in cracks. Great flat lives in the heated heights. The remnants of builders who understood that a home need not outlast the world around it. All of them listening for the moment when the unfinished country changes its mind.
