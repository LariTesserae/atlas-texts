What lives there is not an animal, and not a ghost in the ordinary sense.

It is the part of a thing that refuses to be discarded.

It lives distributed through the enclosure: in the thumbprint under old plaster, in the dry throat of a fountain, in the stone that was once a step and is now a wall. It has no single body because a body would need room around it, and there is no room. It occupies joins, seams, substitutions. Wherever one material has been fitted against another to prevent an absence, it is present.

The builders may have called it a keeper, if they called it anything. Not a guardian—there is nothing to guard against, since nothing enters—but a keeper in the sense of a person who keeps accounts, keeps promises, keeps a fire from going out. It keeps the old purposes warm.

This is why the stair does not become mere rubble. The thing living in the stair remembers upwardness. Even when its top step meets a wall, even when its lower steps are buried under kiln fragments and lintels, it retains the slight inner inclination of ascent. A person standing near it, if a person could stand there long enough, would feel a pressure in the knees: not a command to climb, but the memory of climbing held in stone.

The dry fountains are among its oldest organs.

Their basins do not mourn water. Mourning would be a change, a movement away from what was expected. Instead they continue to make the shape of receiving. Their animal mouths remain open around their dry pipes. Beneath their mineral crusts, the keeper preserves a precise coolness—the idea of water, not water itself. On the coldest mornings, when condensation appears in hairline seams and vanishes before light has properly entered, the fountain bowls acquire one darkened spot each. This is enough. The keeper accepts it as proof that the fountains have not been abandoned by their purpose.

It does not speak through language. The wind speaks for it.

Every vent and jar-neck is a portion of its breathing apparatus. The long organ notes are its patient thoughts. The sharp fluting sounds are places where it has noticed a crack and is holding it in attention. The roar below the central grate is perhaps its deepest voice, though no one can distinguish words within it. It says only what the entire place says:

remain fitted  
remain named  
remain almost useful

The dry vegetation is not separate from it, though it has its own stubbornness. The grasses are its hairs, perhaps, or its measuring threads. They test the wind without surrendering to it. The lichens are its slow skin, spreading not outward but inward, finding the cooled recesses of mortar and inhabiting them with rings of dull silver. The last living vine is one of its hands. It will not climb freely because freedom would require choosing a direction. Instead it follows the wall’s old lines, tracing the remembered boundaries of rooms that no longer exist.

At the center is its nearest thing to a face.

The circular tiles under the grate are not cleaner because they have been protected. They are clean because the keeper returns there constantly, though it has no feet. Dust arriving on those rings is lifted by small reversals in the air, carried into the culverts, and redistributed among the higher cracks. The concentric pattern is not decorative. It is an eye that cannot close.

The single grass blade in the central seam is its pupil.

That is why it trembles but does not bend.

The keeper looks upward through it—not at the sky, exactly, but at the aperture in all that making. It has watched the milk-blue light enter for longer than any calendar could contain. It does not wish the opening wider. It does not wish it sealed. It requires only that the light continue to touch the old bricks, the salt seams, the mouths of the empty fountains; that each thing be briefly recognized every day by something that has not been built.

There may once have been people living within the place, packed into its ledges and alcoves, sleeping beneath stairs, baking in chambers roofed by other chambers. They left nothing that could leave. Their bodies became dust, their tools became supports, their broken bowls became wall-fill. But their habit of making room where no room existed did not depart with them.

That habit is what lives there now.

It has no hunger except for adjacency. It feeds whenever a crack receives a fragment, whenever a loosened tile settles onto another tile without falling, whenever wind presses dust into a seam and makes the seam more complete. It is not malicious. It would not harm a visitor. But it would be unable to let one depart unchanged.

A traveler entering the courtyard would soon discover small things becoming necessary. A loose button would find its way into a crack. A strip of cloth would catch on a broken shutter and become, over time, a curtain. A footprint would gather grit, then be crossed by a line of mortar dust, then be interpreted by the surrounding stones as the beginning of a path. The traveler’s name, spoken once into the wind, would be divided among the pipes and returned only as tones.

Eventually the keeper would make a place for them.

Not a grave. A grave is an ending with room around it.

A niche, perhaps: barely wide enough for a shoulder, lined with pale brick and the fragments of blue-glazed bowls. A little arch above it. A dry conduit passing behind it, so that the wind could continue to say the visitor’s diminished name. Their hands would become useful as brackets. Their ribs would become a small gate. Their eyes, if they remained eyes, would turn toward the central patch of tiles and watch the grass blade shiver.

And they would not be dead in the way they had feared.

They would be kept.
