What lives in the basin is mostly not separate from it.

There are things with bodies, certainly. There are things that hatch, feed, mate, migrate, and die. But the basin has had too long to work on them. It has worn away the sharp distinction between animal and shelter, plant and tool, person and place. Anything that persists here does so by becoming difficult to distinguish from rain-dark mud, from floating peat, from the black inward motion of water.

The smallest lives are the most numerous and perhaps the oldest.

Under every skin of water are colonies of clear-bodied filterers, no larger than grains of rice, joined in lattices that tremble when the ground-thunder passes. They live in the silt itself, opening and closing minute hard mouths to strain rot, mineral dust, spores, and the dissolved remains of all that has gone under. In a dry season—which is not dry, only briefly less flooded—their colonies harden into gray-brown plates across the flats. Travelers mistake them for cracked clay until rain returns and the plates loosen, flex, and begin to crawl toward lower ground.

There are mud-clickers, the unseen creatures heard at the edges of pools. They are not insects exactly, though they have jointed limbs and glossy shells. Their bodies are built low and wide, like a handful of wet seeds pressed together. They live in networks beneath the surface, in tunnels that continually collapse behind them. A mud-clicker can feel the pressure of rain striking water a mile away. Whole colonies move before a flood, not fleeing it but assembling under its future path, feeding on the rich disturbed layers it will uncover. Their clicking is both speech and excavation. When enough of them gather beneath a bank, their combined work can make the earth seem to breathe.

In the deeper mud live the bellows-worms. They are pale, blind, and vast by the standards of anything that should be able to exist without being seen. A person may stand on one without knowing it. Their passage creates the boils that rise in still water and the sudden softening of apparently solid ground. They feed on buried peat and old roots, on drowned carcasses, on seams of warm chemical richness far below the ordinary soil. Some are said to be older than the huts, older than the black pillars. Whether this is true cannot be proved, because when one dies its body becomes indistinguishable from the bank around it. A dead bellows-worm is a place where reeds might have grown, if reeds could survive here.

The pale things that turn in flooded channels are called variously eels, roots, drowned snakes, and the basin’s fingers. They are long-bodied amphibious grazers with a translucent skin through which a dark central cord can sometimes be seen pulsing. They burrow into peat-islands and carry pieces of them away in their flesh. They eat algae, carrion, mineral film, and perhaps electricity: after lightning, they rise close to the surface and coil in the charged water, shining faintly from within. Their young are no larger than thread and drift up into the storm clouds with the evaporating water. No one knows how they return.

The birds are more visible but less comprehensible.

They do not flap because their wings are not used chiefly for flight. Their wings are broad, rigid membranes, black above and pearl-colored beneath, held open to catch differences in pressure. They live inside the lower weather. A basin glider may travel for days without landing, its body tilted against the wind, reading storms as fish read currents. When one is forced down by calm—which is rare and dangerous—it folds itself flat on the mud and becomes nearly impossible to see. Their calls, if they have calls, are below hearing; people sometimes report a pressure in the chest just before one passes overhead.

There are smaller storm-birds that nest inside the hollow crowns of the black columns. They make nests from salt fibers, shed skin, and the thin woody remains of flowering stems. Their eggs are soft and dark, and the parents keep them warm by pressing them against the mineral-wet stone. When lightning roots through the clouds, the birds launch all at once. For a few moments they make a cloud of black fragments around the flash, and afterward the low plants bloom.

The low-backed herd animals are called levee-beasts by those who claim to have seen them closely. They have many legs, though not always the same number from one sighting to another. Their backs are broad and ridged with layers of shell, peat, and old mud, so that sedge and moss can grow upon them. A herd crossing a wet plain may look like a line of moving islands. They feed by pushing their blunt faces into soft ground and drawing up roots, shell-creatures, and wet soil. Behind them they leave channels. In front of them they seem to find ridges that have not yet formed.

They do not flee floods. They enter them in groups, shoulder to shoulder, and become rafts. It is possible that some of the floating islands are herds that have stopped moving for many years. It is possible that some distant levees are their descendants, asleep beneath their own accumulated coverings. The people of the basin do not hunt them. To kill one would be to make an island angry.

There are also the pillar-dwellers.

The black columns are not dead trees, though they may have begun as trees in an age when the basin had a clearer idea of land. Their interiors contain wet chambers, mineral veins, fungal growths, and narrow vertical pools. They grow slowly downward as much as upward. Their curtains of falling water are part of them: the columns draw rain through their porous crowns, sift it through old wood and stone, and release it at their bases enriched with salts. Around these bases grow the stubborn plant-knots that hold the flats together.

Within the pillars live white crabs with transparent claws, red-throated frogs that never emerge, and soft-bodied lichen-eaters that resemble strips of folded skin. Sometimes a column gives a deep cracking sound and leans several degrees over the course of a day. The creatures inside do not evacuate. They rearrange themselves. In the basin, a home is not a thing that stands; it is a thing that continues while changing its angle.

And there are people.

Not many, perhaps. Not in villages as an outsider would recognize them. They live in the low huts when the huts remain huts, and under overturned boats when boats can be found, and inside the broad dry throats of the old columns. Some live on travelling peat-islands for whole seasons, fastening their roofs to mats of root and shell, following no route except the routes that persist beneath their feet.

They are often called the Listening People, though this is not their name for themselves. A name, in the basin, is a dangerous fixed thing. They wear layered wraps made from water-resistant grass fiber, fish-skin, and sheets of cured algae. Their skin is darkened by peat stain and weather; their hair is kept close to the head or bound with shell pins, because loose hair collects the basin’s grit and small biting larvae. Many have thin scars around their ankles and wrists from old waterline cuts, and their feet are wide-soled, with toes that spread naturally in mud.

They do not build for permanence. A hut may be begun in the morning and abandoned by evening, not from carelessness but because the basin has made another decision. Their walls are woven to unravel. Their roofs are layered so they can be stripped away, carried, or allowed to become peat again. They make vessels from fired mud only when they must; more often they use folded bark, shell bowls, braided water-grass, or living bladder-plants whose hollow stems can be sealed and carried. Metal is avoided. It draws too much weather into itself, and its rust is considered a kind of slow scream.

Their tools are mostly soft: digging paddles of dense rootwood, cutting edges made from shell or sharpened bone, nets knotted from hair-fine marsh fiber, poles with notched ends for testing ground. They use sound more than sight. A practiced listener can strike a pole against mud, put an ear to its length, and tell whether the next few steps contain buried stone, floating peat, hollow worm-tunnel, firm shell pavement, or water lying under a thin mineral skin.

They do not navigate by landmarks, because landmarks are unreliable. Instead they learn sequences: the order in which certain pools begin to foam, the taste of rain after it passes the northern salt pans, the pitch of wind through a particular kind of grass, the timing between underground thunder and surface tremor. A journey is remembered not as a line across a map but as a chain of conditions.

*Walk until the water tastes of copper. Wait for the second ground-pulse. Keep the cold rain on your left cheek. Cross when the mud-clicking stops.*

Children are taught this before they are taught counting.

Their dead are not buried, because burial is too close to ordinary weather. Instead, bodies are given to particular forms of return. Some are weighted with shell and lowered into the slow black channels, where the pale channel-creatures will take them. Some are set within the hollows of the pillars, wrapped in flowering stems, so the column can drink from them. Some are left on moving peat, with a cup of rainwater at the head and a handful of salt at the feet, to travel until the island breaks apart.

No one asks where the dead have gone. The basin answers too many questions at once.

There are stories among these people of older inhabitants: the Ones Who Tried to Stand Still. They built roads, they say, and straight foundations, and houses with corners intended to remain corners. They marked land with posts and believed the posts made the land theirs. The basin did not punish them out of anger. It merely continued. Their roads softened. Their foundations filled. Their posts became stakes leading nowhere. In time, the people became part of the pressure beneath the flats, another buried arrangement the water occasionally remembers.

The Listening People tell this story gently, almost fondly. Standing still is not a sin. It is a grief.

There may be other people deeper in the basin, where the water rises from below in warm streams and lightning hangs low enough to illuminate faces. Occasionally a drifting island carries signs of them: a braid of blue reed-fiber unlike any made nearby, a small clay whistle with no finger holes, a fish bone carved into the shape of an ear, a child’s handprint preserved in salt crust. The locals do not follow these signs. To follow is to assume the sign was meant for you.

What truly lives there, beneath birds and worms and people, is recurrence.

The rain lives there, though it is not merely rain. It knows the hollows by their former depths. It finds old channels under new mud. It rises from one pool and falls in another carrying traces of everything it has touched: salt from vanished seas, soot from fires no one remembers, the loosened skin of animals, pollen from plants that bloom only once in ten years of lightning.

The mud lives there. It stores weight, warmth, footsteps, seeds, bones, and pressure. It is never still. Even where it appears dry and split, it is considering water.

The basin itself may be alive in the only way anything can be alive after so much time: not as a creature with a boundary, but as a habit that has become vast. It feeds by breaking forms down. It reproduces by making new surfaces. It remembers without preserving. It speaks through flooded channels, through the vibration of pillars, through the deep submerged thunder that the Listening People press their palms against.

And all the things within it—glider, worm, peat-beast, pillar-crab, hut-maker, drowned ancestor—live by learning the same lesson.

Nothing here survives by refusing to change shape.
