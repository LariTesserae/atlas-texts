What lives in the drowned town is not one people, though the people at its center often speak as if they are.

They call themselves the **Held**.

It is an old name. Older than the flood, perhaps. In their own language it means several things at once: held by roots, held beneath the sky, held in the memory of the dead, held in the mouth of the water. Outsiders—if there were any outsiders capable of reaching the basin—might call them amphibious. The Held would find this insufficient. A frog is amphibious. An otter is amphibious. The Held are people who made an agreement with the lake and have spent centuries honoring its terms.

They are not fish, though children sometimes ask whether the first ancestors had tails.

They are small-boned, broad-chested, and pale in the way of things that live beneath leaves. Their hair grows thick and dark, often worn in long ropes tied close to the skull so it cannot drift into the face or catch in rootwork. Their eyes are large, with clear inner lids that draw sideways over the eye while swimming. In bright surface light their pupils contract to pinpricks and their faces twist with discomfort. In the blue avenues below, they see well enough to recognize one another at a considerable distance: a hand raised at the end of a canal, a familiar gait of the legs, the particular trailing of a cloak.

Their hands are webbed lightly between the fingers. Not enough to make them look inhuman from a distance, but enough to turn a careful sweep into a strong stroke. Their feet are long, the toes flexible and often bare. Elders’ feet are mapped with old cuts, thorn scars, and pale seams from coral scrapes. Children learn to read these scars as they learn family histories.

Their bodies are neither wholly dependent on air nor free from it.

Along the sides of their ribs, beneath the arms and down toward the waist, they bear branching folds of living tissue—soft red fans that open in cold water and close against the body in warmth or exertion. These are not quite gills in the manner of fish. They are more like inherited organs of exchange, grown over generations from a partnership between human flesh, lake microbes, and the strange mineral richness of the basin. When a Held is resting, or moving slowly through clear cold water, the fans do much of the work. When they swim hard, fight, give birth, grieve, laugh too long, or climb into an air chamber to sing, they still breathe with lungs.

This is why air cannot be trusted.

It is necessary in moments. It is precious. It is intoxicating. But it is also unreliable, finite, and capable of vanishing without warning. A person may live by water for a long time. A person who stakes their life on an air pocket may die when a roof shifts, when heat shrinks a chamber, when another frightened person breathes too greedily.

The Held teach their children to take air only when it is offered, never when it is promised.

A baby is born underwater.

For the first few days it is carried against the mother’s chest inside a sling of fine netting, its small side-fans fluttering almost constantly. The infant does not swim at first; it curls around the parent’s body, blinking solemnly through the green light. Grandmothers and older siblings hang nearby in the root-chamber, ready to coax milkfish closer, to chase away curious carp, to hold warming stones taken from a seep. The naming happens only after the child has made its first voluntary dive into darkness and returned by itself to the family leaf-house.

Most names are not words in the surface sense. They are rhythms tapped against reed stalks, patterns of shell-clicks, or short sequences of throat tones that travel through water. A person may have a name that sounds like three low knocks and a rising hum. Another may be named for the tremor made by a fish turning under a lily pad. In the largest air chamber, these names can be spoken aloud, but that is considered intimate, almost embarrassing. A spoken name is naked. It belongs to the dry world of mouths and breath.

The Held’s houses contain more than people.

Every leaf-house has its residents: threadfish that eat parasites from the walls, small blue snails that graze algae from windows, blind white crabs in the lower root cavities, and at least one family of frogs if there is an air pocket nearby. Frogs are respected but not beloved. They are noisy, territorial, and prone to squatting in the best chambers. Yet they are also weather-readers. When frogs abandon a roof-pool, the household checks its tethers. When frogs begin calling at the wrong hour, someone swims to inspect the surface mats.

The Held keep animals, though they do not think of ownership in the same way as people above water might.

They maintain turtle gardens in the old market basin. The broad-backed river turtles are fed lilies, root pulp, and the soft bodies of invasive vine-moss. In return, they crop the growth without damaging the root foundations, carry bundles between districts when they are in the mood, and provide shell plates for tools after natural deaths. A turtle shell is never taken from a living turtle. To do so would be regarded not merely as cruelty but as architectural sabotage: turtles are living stones, slow workers in the town’s balance.

There are eel lineages that live in particular neighborhoods and are known individually. Some are sleek black scavengers that patrol drains and take scraps offered at household thresholds. Others are bred for their ability to sense changes in current. These “listening eels” are carried by messengers in woven tubes. If the eel begins to knot itself, the messenger knows that a current has altered ahead—perhaps from a slide, a burst root chamber, a predator, or an opening in the basin wall.

The most feared animals in the town are not the large ones.

It is the little things that can undo a district: the red-mouth fungus that enters a cut and turns the flesh numb; the laceweed that grows over young roots and seals them from light; the glass leech, nearly invisible until it has taken enough blood to shine pink; the burrowing mussel that can turn a carefully tended reed pillar hollow from the inside. Children are taught the names of these creatures before they learn the names of distant mountain peaks. Survival is taxonomy.

There are predators, certainly.

In the deeper avenues, where old streets descend toward the thermal vents, long pale pike move between the ruins. They are not monsters, but adults do not let children swim alone there. Some grow longer than a person and carry old hooks in their mouths or scars from failed attempts at capture. The Held call them **gate-keepers**, because they have a habit of lingering beside collapsed arches and stairwells. They are watched, bargained with in the only way possible—through predictable scraps and respectful distance—and occasionally driven away with drums sounded through the stone.

More dangerous are the surface hunters: diving birds with spear-beaks, osprey and herons altered by the thin high-country air into watchful giants. Their shadows pass over the floating gardens. The Held can tell one species from another by the shape of shadow alone. A child seeing a broad-winged darkness cross the roof of a leaf-house knows to press close to the root wall and wait. The town has lost people to beaks, talons, and panic near the surface. The Held do not tell heroic stories about those deaths. They tell practical ones.

There are also the dead.

They are not buried in ground, because there is little ground and because the Held dislike the idea of sealing anyone away from the water that sustained them. When someone dies, the household tends the body in a warm seep chamber. The hair is combed, wounds are covered with flower petals, and the side-fans are folded closed beneath a wrapping of reed cloth. Then the dead are carried to one of the ancient stone towers at the basin’s edge.

These towers are full of openings where windows once were.

The body is placed within the tower, weighted lightly so it will not rise, and left among the slow current, freshwater coral, and pale fungi. Over months, fish, snails, crabs, and water will take what they need. The bones remain. The larger bones are returned to the family house as needles, handles, flute-pieces, or frames for ceremonial nets. The smallest bones are ground with clay and used to seal cracks in the roots.

No person wholly leaves the town.

This makes the civic hall’s air chamber especially important. It is not merely a place for festivals. It is the one place where the Held willingly enter the old condition of their ancestors: wet-skinned, shivering, breathing hard, exposed to sound that does not soften.

The blackened ceiling is sacred because it remembers fire.

Children are brought there rarely. At their first visit they are made to lie on the stone floor and look upward at the soot. Someone tells them that long ago people commanded a small bright animal to eat wood and give warmth. The children are usually frightened by this story. Fire is difficult to imagine: a thing that requires air, destroys what it touches, and cannot swim.

Some elders insist that fire was real. Others say it is a teaching creature, invented to make children respect water. A few families preserve tiny flakes of charcoal in shell pendants, passed down through the generations. They do not know what the flakes are for. They only know they are dry, black, and forbidden to dissolve.

Beyond the Held, the town belongs to beings that are less easily named.

The roots beneath the settlement have become one great woven body. Each household tends its portion, but no one can say where one reed lineage ends and another begins. The oldest roots are dark as old blood and dense as carved wood. They carry water, nutrients, vibration, and perhaps something else. When a district suffers damage, nearby stalks sometimes thicken toward it within days, bending their growth around broken walls or empty chambers. The Held call this “the town remembering its shape.”

They do not claim that the roots think.

They also do not claim that they do not.

In the deepest foundation, where silt has gathered around the drowned cellars and the old mountain soil lies compacted under generations of shell and bone, there are chambers no person enters. The water is too warm there, the darkness too complete, and the mineral taste burns the tongue. Blind fish live in those places, translucent as peeled fruit. So do black salamanders with crown-like frills around their heads. The Held leave offerings at the upper mouths of these chambers: broken tools, first fruits of lily harvests, the tiny teeth shed by children.

Sometimes an offering disappears quickly.

Sometimes, from below, comes a response: three slow impacts against stone, felt through the soles of the feet and the roots of the teeth.

No one agrees what makes the sound.

The practical people say it is settling rock. The devout say the mountain has a heart. The old say it is the drowned town before it learned how to live, still knocking from its buried rooms.

And perhaps all of these are true.

The Held live among this uncertainty without needing to solve it. They prune the channels. They mend cloaks. They raise children in green chambers. They cultivate lilies in the schoolhouse and catfish beneath the shrine. They remember which streets were once streets and which are now eel ponds. They mark every rockslide not as a disaster alone, but as a change in the town’s body.

They are held by the water, but not passively.

They hold it back from clouding. They guide it through roots. They give it waste, song, labor, bone, blood, pollen, and the heat of their small careful lives. The lake has drowned their ancestors’ town many times over.

The people who live there now are what the town became when it refused to be finished.
