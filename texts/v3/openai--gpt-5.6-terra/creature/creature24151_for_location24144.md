The people of the Dry Garden are not a single kind of person in the way outsiders mean it.

They share blood in tangled, unrecorded ways, certainly. Most can point to some aunt, debt, marriage, grievance, or inherited tool connecting them to any other household in the basin. But the hollow has made room for differences and preserved them. A family that has tended the high ledges for eight generations walks differently from one that lives under the roots. Kiln people smell faintly of ash even after bathing. Goat people have a particular patience in their faces and a talent for sleeping through noise. The seed-keeping families speak more quietly than anyone else, perhaps because they are accustomed to working in rooms where every spilled grain is noticed.

There are probably eight hundred people in the Dry Garden, though no count is ever exact. Births are entered on bark strips, deaths are marked on the council-posts, but people leave for trade and return with spouses; children live for years in one aunt’s household and then another; old people who no longer climb are sometimes said to have “gone into the roots” long before they are dead. At any given time, someone is absent on the mountain path, someone is being born in a curtained roof room, someone has been found sleeping in a storehouse, someone has decided to become a different person than their kin had expected.

They are generally small-boned, not from frailty but from generations of climbing, rationing, and working in narrow spaces. Their hands are broad and extraordinarily articulate. A Dry Gardener can tie a load to a goat in the dark, tell good rope from rotten rope with two fingers, and seal a cracked vessel with resin without looking away from a conversation. Their feet are hard-soled and often bare indoors. The children run along roots and roof edges with a confidence that terrifies visitors, though even they are taught early that falling is not an accident but a mistake with a name.

The mountain shows on their bodies.

Most adults have a pale dust settled permanently in the creases around their eyes and mouths. Their hair is kept close or braided up under cloth caps, not out of fashion exactly, but because loose hair catches burrs, smoke, beetle shells, and the hooked fibers of the vine mats. Their skin tends toward dark gold, umber, red-brown, or a weathered grayness under dust. The oldest residents often have lips stained greenish or purple from sap and pulp drinks. Sap-tappers bear fine scars across the inner wrists, from childhood training accidents and from the sharp spines used to make incisions. Potters have burn marks. Goat keepers have horns, bells, and medicinal smells worked into their clothes. Compost tenders have blackened nails that never quite come clean.

No one in the Dry Garden has soft hands unless they are very young, very ill, or very well protected.

They dress in layers, though the basin is warm. The clothes are made to keep off dust, scratches, insect bites, hot stone, and the sudden cold of high ledges. Most wear wrapped tunics, short trousers, skirts divided for climbing, sleeveless work vests, and broad shoulder cloths that can become sacks, baby slings, shade hoods, or bandages. The dominant colors are not chosen so much as inherited from available dyes: smoke-black, clay-red, faded yellow, fungus gray, the dull silver-green of crushed leaves. Bright colors are rare enough to mean something. A strip of blue cloth may have been traded for over a century. Red thread tied around a wrist can indicate a household in mourning, a person newly apprenticed, or someone who owes labor to the compost beds.

Jewelry is practical until it is not. People wear bead strings that count garden shares; little bone tabs identifying climbing rights; charms made from goat vertebrae, beetle wings sealed in resin, polished rootwood, and old metal fragments too precious to reshape. A few wear river stones drilled through with astonishing care. These are touched during arguments, births, and weather-watchings. A river pebble is proof that somewhere in the world water lies openly under the sky.

The Dry Gardeners are not solemn, though outsiders sometimes mistake their caution for solemnity.

They laugh constantly, and often at the wrong moment by valley standards. They laugh when a goat escapes onto a roof, when a marriage negotiation becomes too pompous, when someone claims their grandfather once saw rain. They enjoy mimicry. Children imitate the council elders, the bats, traders from below, and the exact clipped clicking sound of offended beetles. There are people in the settlement whose principal social value is their ability to retell old disasters in a way that makes everyone laugh before they remember to be afraid.

Their humor is dry, layered, and merciless toward wastefulness. To call someone “a cup with no bottom” is an insult. To call them “a fine wet-season farmer” means they have made a plan for conditions that do not exist. A person who boasts too much may be told, “Ask the hollow to become a valley, then.” That phrase can be a rebuke, a joke, a warning, or, among lovers, a dangerous kind of invitation.

They speak a language shaped by enclosed space. It has many words for echoes, for the degree of shade beneath a particular kind of leaf, for rope tension, for sap that is drinkable but not pleasant, and for the particular silence of animals before a rockfall. Direction is rarely north or south. It is rootward, lipward, ovenward, monkeyward, toward the old crack, beyond the goats, under the seed room. A person may say, “Meet me three ladder-shadows below the bell,” and be understood.

Their songs do not travel far. This is intentional.

Long songs are sung in courtyards, root chambers, and beneath awnings where the sound will not climb too high. The people believe the cliffs listen, though not as gods listen. Stone has no interest in justice, they say. Stone remembers pressure. It repeats what is given to it in altered form. There are songs for packing compost, for tapping pillars, for keeping a child quiet during an animal birth, for climbing in fog, for mourning someone whose body cannot be recovered from a fall. The oldest songs have no words for rain except metaphorical ones: sky-sap, the great spilling, the unroofing.

They do not worship the pillar-plants exactly. Worship would imply distance, reverence, perhaps a hope of being answered.

The pillar-plants are kin, infrastructure, inheritance, food, weather, and law. Children are introduced to the plants of their household before they are formally named. They sleep against their warm bases during the cold week. They are taught to recognize a sick pillar by smell: the sourness beneath the wax, the faint yeast odor of rot, the terrible sweetness of an internal wound. When a mature pillar dies, its household mourns it with a seriousness usually reserved for a grandparent. Its wood is apportioned publicly. No one is allowed to claim too much from its body, because too much grief can become greed if left unwatched.

The people who tend the oldest pillars are called variously the Scar-Keepers, the Green Hands, or, less respectfully, the Sap Misers. They are not priests, although they can become powerful. They decide when a plant may be cut, when a diseased branch must be removed, when a household has taken too much. Their knowledge is practical and mysterious in equal parts: they listen to trunks with hollow bone tubes, taste the edge of a wound, inspect the wax bloom for patterns no one else can see. A Scar-Keeper’s greatest achievement is not saving a pillar. It is persuading a desperate family to wait.

Children are everywhere in the Dry Garden, though fewer than the settlement’s noise suggests. The place makes their voices multiply. They are carried on backs, tucked into root hollows, sent up ladders with messages, assigned the work of chasing moths from drying racks, sorting beetle shells by color, and collecting dropped seeds from pathways. By six, they know which roofs can bear running feet. By nine, they can prepare compost without gagging. By twelve, most have chosen—or been claimed by—some line of learning: goats, pots, seed, rope, smoke pipes, stonework, healing, climbing, trade, story-keeping.

But children are not hurried toward adulthood in all things. The Garden permits a long middle time, especially for those who show unusual talents. A girl who can calm bats may spend years tending eaves and studying wing membranes. A boy who remembers every household’s seed debts may become a public counter. A child who insists they hear voices in the cliff cracks might be given work with the weather-watchers rather than mocked. The people have learned that eccentricity is another stored resource. In a place with so little spare material, no useful perception is thrown away.

The old are not removed from the work of the settlement; they are moved toward work that cannot be climbed away from.

They sit at thresholds, on the council porch, under the shade of thick roots. They mend netting, clean seeds, teach knots, settle arguments, scrape hides, tend grubs, and remember where a wall has been patched badly beneath its newest plaster. They are the ones who know that the floor beneath a particular house is hollow, that a family once promised three jars of pulp to another family’s dead, that the west bridge should never carry a panicked goat. Some have become nearly blind from smoke and glare, but can identify people by footsteps on stone. Some no longer speak in full sentences and are nonetheless consulted because the fragments they retain are old fragments.

Death does not make a person absent immediately. For a time they remain in the division of labor.

Their tools are redistributed slowly. Their garden cuts are held in trust. Their sleeping space may remain empty for a season, especially if the dead person was a child or someone lost outside the basin. At funerals, the dull copper bell is struck only once. It is said that the bell has become dull because it has learned not to call too loudly for those who cannot return.

There are also strangers in the Dry Garden, though no stranger is permitted to remain entirely strange.

Traders come from lower settlements perhaps twice a year, sometimes less. They arrive thin with sun, carrying salt, cloth, fish bone, dried river reeds, bits of iron too corroded to be useful except as talismans, and news that is often treated as a suspect substance. The Garden gives them sap concentrate, medicinal resins, mountain goat skins, rare fungus powders, rope, seeds, carved stone, and occasionally a young adult who has chosen to leave.

Travelers are housed close to the council porch, where they can be watched without making the watching impolite. They are fed cautiously at first. They are asked about paths before they are asked about nations. Anyone who speaks carelessly of water, promising hidden springs or easy irrigation, is regarded with a particular mixture of hunger and contempt. The Dry Garden has survived too many beautiful claims.

A few outsiders stay.

They marry in, or are taken in after an injury, or simply fail to leave before the mountain paths close. The settlement changes them. Within a few years they too begin saving string. They too stop using the word “empty” for a place where no people stand, because they have learned how many things occupy an apparently empty ledge. They begin to listen before moving a basket. They learn the etiquette of passing beneath a sap cut. They develop, almost without knowing it, the Gardeners’ wary affection for limits.

And among all these people live the other citizens of the hollow.

The dogs are perhaps the most obvious. Each belongs partly to a household and partly to the settlement. They sleep where they wish, patrol the garden paths, warn of monkeys, find lost children, and lie beside the sick. They are never fully trained; obedience would make them less useful. A good Dry Garden dog knows which goat theft to ignore, which stranger to distrust, and when to refuse a command because the path ahead smells wrong.

The cliff monkeys are not pets, despite periodic attempts by children. They are thieves, omens, rivals, and sometimes witnesses. When a monkey watches a council argument from a ledge, people become careful about lying. No one can explain why. The monkeys have no respect for ownership, but they understand consequences. They avoid the oldest pillar-plants, steal only ripe fruit from certain households, and have been known to throw stones at anyone who harms a young one.

The root-mice live in a more intimate compact with humans. Some households feed them deliberately with peelings and crushed seed, allowing them to nest in warm wall cavities. In return, the mice are watched for signs of spoilage, sickness in the roots, or changes in underground temperature. A sudden migration of root-mice can empty half a street before a collapse. Other households trap and eat them. There is no contradiction in this. To live with something is not always to spare it.

Beetles are gathered, bred, cursed, traded, and sometimes honored. Their shells are used for ornament and pigment; their larvae turn refuse; their clicking becomes a weather sign. The glowing varieties kept in pierced clay jars are treated tenderly, though not sentimentally. A child may be given a beetle lantern for a birthday, then taught immediately how many dead leaves it takes to keep the insects alive.

Even the fungi have their keepers. They are pale, secretive people who work in compost-dark rooms and speak of rot as if discussing inheritance. They cultivate shelves that feed on waste, producing chewy food, medicine, dyes, and materials for sealing vessels. They are considered a little uncanny because they spend their lives close to decay, but everyone knows that without them the Garden would choke on its own diligence.

Perhaps the truest inhabitants of the Dry Garden are not individuals at all, but agreements.

The agreement between a child and a ladder.

Between a goat and a ledge.

Between a dead pillar and the households that will divide its wood without fighting.

Between compost and fruit, bats and moths, monkeys and roofs, smoke and cracks in the cliff.

Between the people and the mountain, which has never loved them, never forgiven them, and never once made their survival easy.

The Dry Garden is full of beings who have learned that scarcity does not make life smaller. It makes every life legible to the others. A missing beetle, a cracked root, a silent dog, a child taking too long on a ladder—these things are noticed. In the amber dusk, amid the hiss of sap cuts and the old mechanical singing of cicadas, the settlement seems to breathe through a thousand separate mouths.

No one there is alone enough to disappear unnoticed.

And no one is free enough to live without leaving a mark on the whole hollow.
