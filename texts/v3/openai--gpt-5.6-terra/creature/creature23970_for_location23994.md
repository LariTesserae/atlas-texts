What lives there is not a single people, though from below it might seem so.

The plateau is inhabited by continuance: by things compacted, weathered, and adapted until they no longer appear separate from the ground that supports them. Every creature has learned the same lesson in a different form—keep low, keep warm, spend nothing foolishly, know the stone before the weather changes.

The pale animals on the ridgelines are the oldest-looking of them. They are goatlike, though less graceful than goats imagined at pasture: deep-chested, narrow-flanked, their coats faded almost to the color of old snow and weathered limestone. Their faces are dark around the eyes, as if marked with soot. Their horns sweep backward and outward in heavy ridges, recording years of abrasion, fighting, and mineral dust. They travel in lines not because they are led, but because the mountain permits only certain paths. An old female goes first, her muzzle lowered to the rock. Behind her come the younger animals, then the broad-horned males at a distance, appearing unconcerned but always watching the upper slopes.

They know the snow by its sound underhoof. They know which crust conceals water, which conceals air, which will take the weight of a wolf and which will not. In storms they vanish into folds of ground no traveler would notice: a cut in a moraine, the lee side of a slab, a notch beneath an overhang. When they stand still among pale stone, they become almost impossible to count. Only the shifting of an ear, the blink of a black eye, betrays them.

Below them, in the loose stone, live the whistlers.

They are thick-bodied burrowers, gray-brown and sun-warmed, with blunt claws built for gravel rather than soil. Their colonies honeycomb the talus fields and the grassy shelves between boulders. They are never entirely quiet. One gives a high warning call, sharp as a whistle through teeth; another answers from an impossible distance; then the whole slope begins speaking in fragments. Their voices carry farther than their bodies do. A person could hear a warning circle the basin and imagine dozens of unseen sentries passing news from rock to rock.

They have their own architecture, if it can be called that: not structures, but rearrangements. Flat stones dragged over tunnel mouths. Pebbles ranked in bands outside sunning places. Dry grass packed beneath slabs. Bones and feather shafts incorporated into bedding, perhaps by accident, perhaps not. In late summer they sit upright on warm rocks with their paws held close to their chests, fattening themselves against the coming cold. Their stillness is deceptive. At the shadow of a wing, a falling stone, the unfamiliar click of metal, every one of them vanishes at once, and the slope becomes merely stone again.

The shaggy grazers in the basins are larger and darker, built less for cliffs than for endurance. They have broad backs under rain-dark wool, ribs that show through lean seasons, and horns that rise in long crescent pairs. Their breath smells of moss, wet earth, and old grass. They graze with their heads low, tearing at the short turf so close to the ground that their mouths seem to be polishing the plateau itself.

These animals gather around the mineral seep places. There, the stone bears a dark shine from generations of tongues, horns, shoulders, and flanks. They kneel in mud that is barely mud, lick red-brown salts from cracks, and rub their horns against the same outcrops until the rock is grooved. Calves are born in sheltered basins when the snow is retreating. For a few weeks the high ground contains awkward, long-legged bodies running in small circles while their mothers watch with grave patience. Then the calves learn the paths and become part of the scattered movement of the herd.

Above all of these live the birds.

Some are great cliff birds: black-winged, pale-headed, ugly in the intimate way of scavengers and magnificent from afar. They rise from ledges in slow, deliberate launches, then find the warm air climbing the cliffs and let it bear them. Their wings do not beat often. When they do, the sound is dry and hollow, like someone shaking a sheet of stiff bark. They know every carcass before it has cooled. They know where a goat slipped, where a wolf fed, where a snowstorm has covered a wounded animal that will be uncovered at thaw.

They clean the mountain with terrible efficiency. Bones do not remain whole for long. Hide becomes strips caught on thorn shrubs. Horn cores whiten beside stones. What cannot be eaten is carried away in pieces: tendon, feather, hair, splinters of rib. The birds leave no monument except circles of droppings on ledges and the occasional polished bone dropped from the sky.

Other birds are smaller, but more numerous and more argumentative: ground-nesters with mottled backs, snow-colored birds that seem to disappear whenever clouds pass, swift dark shapes that nest in cracks and scream at anything that approaches. Their calls are woven into the wind so completely that at first one cannot tell whether the cliffs themselves are complaining. In the brief flowering season they move through the low plants with startling delicacy, taking insects from petals no larger than a fingernail.

The insects are easy to overlook until the sun reaches its highest point. Then the sheltered moss hollows hum. Tiny flies rise in dark, shimmering knots above meltwater; beetles move beneath flat stones; thick-bodied bees force themselves through the wind to find the low flowers. They are the quickest lives on the plateau, born into a season so short that every hour seems urgent. Their existence makes the bright cushions of bloom possible, and the blooms in turn feed the grazers, the birds, the burrowers. Even in the most exposed country, there are small economies of nectar, pollen, larvae, and rot.

At the margins of night come the hunters.

They are not seen often. The mountain has no need to display them. Sometimes there is only a line of tracks crossing a snowfield: four soft impressions, close together, too round for a dog and too deliberate for any wandering animal. Sometimes a herd abruptly turns its heads toward a dark gully. Sometimes the whistlers fall silent in a way that is more complete than silence.

The hunters are lean, ash-colored creatures with long tails and high shoulders, something between wolves and cats in the way they move. Their fur takes on the colors around them: slate beside shadowed rock, pale brown on gravel, blue-gray at the snow edge. They travel alone more often than together. They do not chase over long distances. They wait above trails, behind ridges, beneath the sound of water. A misplaced hoof, an old animal separated from its herd, a calf delayed by loose scree—these are enough.

They leave little behind. A few hairs caught on stone. A dark mark in snow. The abrupt work of scavengers afterward. Yet all the animals know their presence. It is part of the plateau’s grammar, as fixed as windward and leeward, thaw and freeze.

There are other residents that do not move in ways a traveler would readily recognize.

Lichen lives there with a patience beyond animal hunger. It spreads over black stone in maps of green, silver, orange, and ash-white, entering tiny fractures with acid and rootless insistence. It survives sun that scorches and cold that splits metal. Some patches may be older than the trails crossing beside them. They are eaten, stepped on, buried under snow, uncovered, and continue.

Moss inhabits the wet hollows like a soft conspiracy. It holds water against the dry air, gathers blown dust, makes soil grain by grain. In one basin it may be nearly black and slick; in another, bright as a jewel. Beneath it live minute creatures that freeze solid through winter and wake when meltwater enters their hidden chambers. The moss does not claim the hollow. It simply keeps it from becoming bare stone.

And there are the stones themselves, which have become inhabitants by remaining.

Some hold warmth long after sunset. Some gather ice in their shade and release it slowly through the day. Some make a particular sound when wind enters their cracks. The animals use them as landmarks: the split boulder above the seep, the black slab where the herd turns, the leaning tooth of rock where birds gather before weather. A horn is rubbed against one. A burrow is dug beneath another. A predator waits behind a third. Over centuries, use gives each stone a role.

Nothing names these places, but they are known.

The plateau is therefore crowded, though never busy in the way lowland country is busy. Its lives do not gather in streets or fields. They are distributed through cracks, slopes, air currents, snow margins, and hidden watercourses. Each creature occupies not a territory so much as a set of conditions: a certain warmth of stone, a particular route through scree, a safe depth of burrow, a wind direction, a patch of mineral-rich earth.

Even the dead remain briefly active there. Their bones feed scavengers. Their hides line nests. Their abandoned dens collapse into the soil. Their paths become routes for those born after them. A herd may walk a ledge first opened by hooves centuries before; a bird may nest in a crack smoothed by generations of wings; a burrower may inherit tunnels whose oldest chambers were dug by bodies no longer distinguishable from the mountain’s dust.

So what lives in the high country is a congregation without a center.

Horns, claws, roots, wings, fur, snow, water, stone. Alarm cries and hidden teeth. The small warm pulse inside a burrow. The great slow circle of a bird on rising air. The invisible movement beneath moss where meltwater passes through gravel. All of it held beneath the fierce, unwavering light.

And none of it is lonely.
