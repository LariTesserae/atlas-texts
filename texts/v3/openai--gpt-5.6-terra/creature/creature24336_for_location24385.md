What lives there is not a people apart from animals, nor animals apart from the stone. The hollow has made them all into one crowded, listening thing.

The people call themselves, in their oldest word, the **Bearers**—not because they carry burdens, though they do, but because the mountain bears them and they bear the mountain in return. Outsiders, if there ever were outsiders, might have called them cave-dwellers. The Bearers would find that strange. A cave is an empty thing. This place is full: of breath, droppings, roots, feathers, heat, names, old agreements, and the continual sound of something needing to be fed.

They are not pale in the manner of deep-earth peoples, because they do receive light, though it is green, mineral, and insufficient. Their skin is weathered in a dozen shades of brown, gray, umber, and ash-gold, always carrying a fine dust in its creases. Children are born with dark eyes and often keep them; in age, many develop a silver clouding around the pupils from years spent looking into smoke and dimness. They have broad feet, strong toes, and a way of placing themselves on uneven stone without appearing to look down. Their hands are callused not only at the palms but along the outer edges of the fingers, from climbing, scraping salt, cutting vine, gripping ladders, and handling hot jars.

They are smaller than the old carvings suggest their ancestors were. The hollow does not reward largeness. It rewards the ability to slip between a goat and a wall without spilling a bowl of ground fungus; to sleep curled on a shelf; to climb a roof carrying a child under one arm and three eggs in the other; to know, by touch, whether a wall has shifted in the night.

They wear little that hangs loosely. Long garments catch on ladders and invite insects. Most dress in wrapped skirts, short sleeveless tunics, belts strung with tools, and soft hide sandals patched so many times that no pair has a single original shape. In colder drafts they wear capes made from cliff-fowl feathers, layered and sewn onto reed netting. Their finest clothing is not brightly colored—nothing in the hollow is truly bright—but patterned by texture: smooth lizard hide against rough fiber, pale molted feathers sewn into dark goat wool, strips of translucent plant skin that whisper when the wearer moves.

Their hair is usually braided close to the skull or bound up under woven caps. Loose hair is valuable. Birds steal it for nests, and a household whose roof flock begins pulling hair from sleeping children is thought to have neglected some small domestic duty. The clipped hair of the dead is saved. Some families braid it into cord; some tie it beneath the threshold stone; some give it to the nest birds, believing a bird that carries your dead into its nest will remember the family from above.

No one lives alone in the hollow for long.

A household is not a pair, nor even necessarily a family. It is a knot of sleepers, feeders, climbers, cooks, egg-watchers, roof-menders, children, elders, animals, tools, and obligations. A household may occupy three linked rooms, half a stair, two roof platforms, and a section of wall where its vines are trained. One old woman may belong to four households because she knows how to hear parasites in the breathing of grazers. A boy may sleep in his mother’s room but be claimed by his aunt’s flock. A couple may separate without moving, because there is nowhere to move to; they simply begin cooking at different hollows and, over time, the animals understand before the neighbors do.

The Bearers measure wealth in continuities.

A rich household is one whose thorn trees still fruit, whose clay jars have uncracked lids, whose oldest grazer still recognizes the way home, whose roof has not fallen for three generations, whose children know the old feeding routes, whose dead are remembered without argument. The poorest are not those with little food. Everyone has little food, sometimes. The poor are those who have lost the knowledge of how their little things connect.

There are specialists, though their titles are practical and changeable. There are salt-scrapers, who crawl into narrow mineral wounds in the walls and return with pale crusts wrapped in leaf skin. There are roof-callers, who know which cries will shift a flock of cliff fowl without sending it into a panicked cloud. There are compost keepers, much respected and often avoided, who understand what may be fed to fungus, what must be burned, what can be buried in root beds, and what must be carried all the way to the outer cleft and scattered into the dry ravines.

There are cistern listeners.

These are usually old, though not always. Some have damaged hearing from a life among birds and pestles; some are children who speak very little. They sit at the rim during the hot still hours, their heads tilted, hands laid flat on the smooth stones. They do not claim to hear water. That would be boastful and perhaps unlucky. They listen for changes in the tapping below: its spacing, its direction, whether it comes through the left side of the shaft or the right, whether the bats have stirred before it begins.

Their interpretations are not prophecies exactly. If the tapping grows close and quick, a wall may shed stone. If it is slow and far apart, a cold wind may enter within three days. If it stops entirely, someone will inspect the root cracks, the old stairs, the chimneys. Once in a lifetime, perhaps, it means nothing that anyone can understand. Those are the years people remember longest.

The children of the hollow are almost feral in their competence. Before they can speak clearly, they know not to place fingers under resting stones. Before they can climb down safely, they can call a goat by the particular clack of its seedpod bell. They are given small jobs very young: collecting moth wings for powder, chasing insects from drying roots, bringing warm ash to the moss beds, counting eggs with both hands behind their backs so the nesting birds do not learn the gesture.

They play games that would look perilous elsewhere. They race along roof ridges with their arms folded. They hide in vine curtains and imitate birds until adults cannot tell which calls are human. They toss polished pebbles into the dry cistern and listen for the time between the release and the strike. Some children become obsessed with this. They collect stones of different weights. They argue about whether a flat stone falls more slowly because it is afraid.

Their toys are often animal things: shed horn tips, beetle shells, hollow seeds, discarded egg membranes dried into transparent little pouches. A favorite game involves building miniature households in cracks between paving stones: a pebble hearth, a leaf roof, a dead moth as a sleeping elder, a line of antlike insects driven through the tiny lanes as goats. These small cities are regularly destroyed by real goats, and the children rebuild them without much distress.

The animals are not domesticated in the clean sense. They have been persuaded, habituated, fed, trapped, inherited, mourned, and allowed to breed in the wrong places for so long that their lives are entangled with human intention without ever fully yielding to it.

The cliff fowl are the nearest thing the city has to a public authority. They wake everyone. They decide which roofs may be used. They steal food from careless hands. Their droppings fertilize the beds, their feathers warm the sick, their eggs sustain families through lean weeks, and their sudden silence can stop a whole lane in mid-conversation. Every flock has a keeper, but no keeper truly owns a flock. A bird may remember a person for years, especially if that person once broke its wing, saved its chick, or offended it by taking eggs too early.

The oldest fowl are treated with wary respect. Their claws grow twisted. Their calls become low and irregular. They no longer lay, no longer defend a nest, sometimes no longer fly. Yet they remain on the high beams, watching. Children are told that these birds have seen every household as it was before the present household and will carry the shape of the city in their throats when they die.

The horned grazers are more intimate. They are named, but their names change as their habits change: Dust-Drinker, Split-Ear, Three-Bells, Root-Thief, Grandmother’s Bite. They eat the plants people cultivate and the plants people do not want, and no wall is built without considering whether a goat can get behind it. Their milk is scant and salty, nearly chewable. Their hides become sandals, straps, drumheads, carrying slings, and burial wraps. Their bones become needles and awls. When a grazer dies, the household that cared for it hosts a noisy meal, not solemn but grateful; to be solemn around a useful death is considered a refusal of the gift.

The cats live along the edge of belonging. They hunt burrowers, lizards, insects, and occasionally young birds. They leave kittens beneath sleeping shelves and disappear for days. They know every route through the walls. Some are so old that their fur has gone almost white with dust. These are said to be “stone cats,” and are never chased away, even when they steal from drying racks. A stone cat choosing to sleep in a household’s doorway is taken as a sign that the household has become quiet enough to be trusted—or dangerous enough that the cat wishes to observe it.

The insects are harvested, tolerated, cursed, and consulted. The shaggy hand-sized ones are raised in warm pits for meal and oil, though they frequently escape and make nests in bedding rolls. Their molted skins are used as tinder. Their clicking, which changes with temperature, is used by some elders as a kind of clock. The smallest children learn to distinguish the harmless dust beetles from the biting wall beetles, whose sting leaves a numb white ring on the skin.

There are creatures no one feeds.

In the deep cracks beneath the oldest platforms, blind things move through the root mats. They are seldom seen whole: a pale joint, a slow feeler, the drag of something soft against stone. They eat rot and perhaps one another. People do not speak of them loudly. Every few years, after a long hot season, one will emerge into a lane, colorless and folding itself around the light. It is never killed. It is covered with a clay jar, marked, and carried to a refuse pit at the far edge of the basin. The place where it appeared is then left unused for nine days.

The Bearers say these creatures are not bad. They are simply from lower in the mountain’s thoughts.

The dead live there too, though not as ghosts in the usual way.

There is little space to bury bodies, and the mountain gives up no soft soil. The dead are washed with aromatic vine vapor and combed free of lice, dust, and loose hair. Their useful belongings are distributed before the body cools: sandals to a cousin, knife to a daughter, feather cape to an old friend, seedpod bell to the grazer they tended. Then the body is wrapped in old cloth, fur, and plant fiber and placed in one of the narrow wall chambers behind household rooms.

These chambers are called sleeping walls.

As generations pass, the walls become dense with the dead. New stones are fitted in front of old stones. Names are scratched, then worn away, then remembered only in a phrase: the woman who taught the black goat to climb, the child who heard the first roof crack, the man who made jars that never sweated salt. It is common to sleep with one’s back against three or four ancestors. People say this makes dreams more crowded but more useful.

The dead are included in daily speech. A person may apologize to a wall before taking a stone from it. A child climbing too noisily over a roof is told not to wake the sleepers beneath. When a household quarrels, someone eventually invokes an ancestor whose room both sides share, and the argument quiets—not from reverence alone, but because everyone knows the dead cannot move away from bad company.

There are also things living in the city that are not recognized at first as living: the smoke in the rafters, which shifts from house to house and carries the tastes of each cooking hollow; the root-web beneath the paving, which moves food from manure pits toward vine curtains; the accumulated warmth in the central stones; the language itself.

The Bearers’ speech is made for a place where sound rebounds. They use many clicks, throat notes, short whistles, and doubled syllables. A name is often called once in its ordinary voice and answered with a sound that identifies the speaker’s location: roof, stair, lower lane, behind wall, near cistern. Conversations cross over one another constantly. Silence is not politeness. Silence is a warning, a grief, a hunt, a collapse, a listening.

Their songs are not performed so much as built.

One person begins a low repeated line, often while grinding roots or sorting eggs. Another adds a higher answer from a nearby roof. A third, farther off, takes only the final word and stretches it. Birds interrupt. Children imitate the birds. Pestles find a rhythm. Soon the entire basin may be singing without anyone having announced a song. The oldest songs have no fixed words. They are maps: this turn leads to a goat path, this pitch marks the old fungus pit, this pause is where the stair narrows, this descending phrase means beware of loose shale.

At the center of their reverence is not the cistern itself, nor the imagined water beneath it, but the fact that there is a depth no one has used up.

They leave offerings there: a first egg of the season, a polished claw, a child’s lost tooth, a sealed jar of salt, a braid of hair from someone newly dead. Sometimes they lower a living moth in a woven cage and watch until its faint fluttering light disappears. No one expects anything to answer. The offering is an acknowledgment that the hollow rests on something it does not understand.

And perhaps something does listen.

Not a god with a face, not an ancient creature coiled in the shaft waiting for the careless. The inhabitants are too practical for that kind of certainty. But there are nights when every bird in the upper ledges turns its head toward the cistern at once. The cats leave the roofs. The grazers press close together, their seedpod bells silent. Even the insects cease their dry clicking.

Then the people wake, one by one, in the dim rooms and on the sleeping shelves.

No one speaks.

They sit beside their animals, hands on warm backs or folded over clay jars. They listen to the faint tapping far below, and to the enormous stillness around it.

In those moments, the city seems less like a place built inside a mountain than a small, enduring creature nested in the mountain’s ribs: feathered, horned, rooted, smoke-breathed, human-handed, and awake.
