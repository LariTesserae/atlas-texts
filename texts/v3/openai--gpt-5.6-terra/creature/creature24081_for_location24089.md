What lives in the Low Yard is not one population but a set of bargains.

The frogs are the oldest bargain anyone can still hear.

They are not all alike. There are broad-headed drain frogs, brown as old kiln smoke, that sit with only their eyes above the water in the channels and give their warning knock when something large displaces the flow. There are bright-throated reed frogs, no larger than a thumb joint, bred in the ceramic troughs and released in handfuls when mosquitoes become too bold. There are pale roof frogs that have forgotten sunlight almost entirely. They cling inside the wet hollows of beams, their bellies translucent, their bones visible as little white hooks. In dry weeks they fall silent and seem to die. Then the first serious rain comes, the roof swells and begins to weep, and the whole hidden population wakes at once.

The Yard people listen to them the way other places listen to clocks.

A rasp in the east pipes means the water there has turned stale. Three quick knocks from beneath the fish grates means an otter is in the lower channel. A sudden silence, especially from the frogs nearest the sump, means that something has entered the water which ought not to be there.

The herons are another bargain, though they are less loved.

They are white in the peculiar way of things never exposed to a true sky: not bright, but chalk-colored, with bluish shadows beneath the wings. Their legs are too long for the low chambers they inhabit, and so they stoop as they walk, giving them the appearance of old, displeased people. Their beaks have grown thick from generations of striking clay-bottomed pools. They do not fly often. When they do, it is a clumsy, astonishing event: a burst of white wings in the half-dark, dust and droplets shaken from the roof, everyone ducking instinctively beneath them.

They live near the warm plates.

No one knows why the plates are warm. The herons know, perhaps, but do not explain. They stand over them in winter with one foot lifted, each bird motionless for so long that algae gathers in the water around its ankles. A heron can watch a frog for an hour. Then its head moves once, faster than sight, and the frog is gone.

The people say that if the herons ever leave the lower chambers, the Yard will begin lying about its water.

This is sometimes interpreted to mean that it will flood. Others think it means the water will look clean while carrying rot, fever, drowned things, or memories from elsewhere. No one is eager to test the distinction.

The fish are not a single kind either. The blind silver ones are the common flesh of the Yard: quick-breeding, soft-scaled, always turning toward a current even where no current can be seen. Children catch them with cupped hands. Old people gut them with green copper blades. Cats steal them from baskets. The smallest are ground with salt and reed seeds into cakes for the hens. Their bones go into lime mortar, and so in some old places the walls gleam faintly with them.

But deeper in the central sump are fish no one eats.

They are dark, flat-headed things with whiskers, perhaps descendants of river fish trapped when the Yard’s first basin was laid. They have learned the paths beneath the visible construction. On wet nights, when the sump overfills, they rise and circle just below the surface, making rings in the reflected lamplight. The keepers lower scraps to them, though no one admits doing it. A child who falls into the sump is pulled out immediately if possible. If not possible, the bowl bell is struck only once, and nobody speaks until morning.

The small black pigs belong to the people but not entirely.

They sleep in dry niches beneath platforms, packed together in heaps of warm bristles. By day they root reed pulp, kitchen leavings, fish heads, old thatch, and the soft mortar scraped from failed repairs. Their snouts are powerful enough to find weak tiles before a human eye notices them. A pig that begins rooting insistently at a particular seam is not chased away. The seam is marked. Within a day or two, it will often open into a leak, a sink, or a hollow made by water beneath the plate.

The pigs are named for sounds rather than words: Soot, Clatter, Wet-Wet, Kettle, Little Door. They know the bell’s warnings better than anyone. At the predator strike they run toward the people, not away from them. During flood weather they become restless early, pressing their bodies against gates and making a low, worried chuffing noise. Some families trust pigs more than weather signs.

The marsh hens are vain, sharp-tempered, and fearless around blood.

They nest in pipe mouths, broken amphorae, stacked reed mats, and once, famously, inside the sleeve of a hanging raincoat nobody had worn in six years. Their eggs are blue-gray and thin-shelled. Their chicks emerge already too large-footed for themselves, stepping across wet tile with absurd dignity. The hens eat fish fry, insects, frogspawn, scraps, and whatever the people leave cooling outside their doors. They will peck open an unattended wound. They will chase a cat twice their size if it approaches a nest. Their long claws make a precise ticking sound on the floor, and Yard-born children can tell by that sound whether a hen is feeding, alarmed, courting, or about to steal.

Above them all move the feral cats.

They are lean and damp-looking, even when dry, their coats darkened by roof soot and vapor. They live in the ribs and the folds of sailcloth, traveling routes humans cannot follow. Their kittens are sometimes found in abandoned drainage baskets or behind loose wall panels, but adult cats are rarely touched. They come down for fish, birds, newborn pigs, and the occasional rat. In exchange, they keep the upper places clear of whatever else might nest there.

There are bats in the roof, though the Yard people do not like to speak of them. Not the ordinary little bats that flutter around lamps, catching moths, but large velvet-eared ones that roost in the oldest sailcloth layers. They never appear in daylight. Their droppings are collected carefully, mixed with ash, and used in a paste that discourages certain molds from eating the reed walls. The bats are useful. This is why they are tolerated.

Usefulness is the closest thing the Low Yard has to mercy.

And then there are the people.

They are low-yard people in the sense that their bodies have been taught by the place. Most walk softly but balance well. Their feet are wide or made wide by habit, their toes accustomed to gripping wet clay. Their hands show old cuts, lime burns, fish-scale scars, rope grooves, and the dark crescents left by working damp reed. Children learn to climb before they learn to read, because ladders are everywhere and the difference between a sound roof rib and a rotten one can be felt through the palm.

They keep no clean boundary between household and workshop. A sleeping alcove may contain a net loom. A kitchen shelf may hold tile stamps and spare pump paddles. A person mending a sandal may pause to feed eels through a floor grate. Someone stirring soup may call to another person three walkways away that the northwest gutter has changed its note.

They are not poor exactly, though visitors sometimes call them so. They have little that is new, little that is smooth, little that can be kept dry for long. But they possess extraordinary stores of particular knowledge.

An old woman may know which mortar mix will hold beneath a constant drip but fail if it hears sunlight.

A boy may know the names of thirty-five individual frogs and which ones can be moved without breaking the rule.

A woman with one clouded eye may hear a blocked pipe from across the Yard and tell whether the blockage is leaf matter, fish bones, rat nest, cloth, or a dead bird.

A man who has never left the basin may be able to repair a waterwheel in darkness by listening to the paddles turn.

They inherit tools, but more importantly they inherit permissions. This knife may cut reeds but not roof cloth. This jar may hold rainwater but never fish. This ladder may be used during a flood only by someone who has slept in the Yard for at least three consecutive nights. A stranger can learn the work, perhaps, but not all at once. The construction has preferences. The people say this casually, as one might say a neighbor dislikes onions.

Some of them believe the Yard is alive.

Not alive like a pig, a frog, or a heron. Alive as a woven thing is alive: by tension, response, memory, and the continuing labor of its parts. A channel emptied in the wrong order may lead water backward. A wall patched too neatly may crack around the repair, offended by the stiffness. A drain cleared completely may begin swallowing fish. The Yard does not punish. It merely rearranges consequences until respect becomes practical again.

There are also things which live there without being counted.

Pale threadworms in the reed troughs. Water beetles trapped in old roof puddles, their descendants unable to fly because there is nowhere useful to go. Mites that live in pig bristles. Fine white fungi that bloom only behind warm tiles and are harvested in tiny amounts for medicine. Crabs, perhaps, in the deepest underchannels—small shell-clicking things no one has seen clearly, though bait disappears and the mortar near certain grates is always newly scraped.

And in the oldest walls, among the shell lime and unreadable tile marks, live the remains of previous arrangements: old nests, buried seeds, scales, feathers, hair, soot, fingerprints. The Yard has absorbed generations of its inhabitants into its fabric. A person may sleep against a wall built partly from the fish bones their grandmother ate. A frog may shelter in a pipe repaired with the handle of a dead keeper’s favorite shovel. A heron may stand on a tile stamped by a hand whose language no one remembers.

Nothing is wholly gone there.

The Low Yard is inhabited by creatures that have accepted enclosure without accepting tameness: frogs that make weather audible, pigs that diagnose the floor, birds that guard what nobody understands, fish that turn waste into walls, cats that patrol the impossible heights. And among them live people who have become less like owners than like another careful species of maintenance.

They feed the channels. They mend the leaks. They leave silt for the fish.

When the roof groans in a hard rain, they listen with everyone else.
