What lives in the basin is not a people who chose darkness. They would object to that description. They have sky—more sky than the burrowers of the old passages, more sky than the upland villages whose houses crouch under broad roofs and tree shade. Their sky is simply vertical. It is a strip, a basin-shaped opening, a moving pale thing seen between rooflines and raw walls. Children learn weather by the color at the lip of it. A clean white means cold; yellow-gray means rain traveling in from the west; an abrupt dimming means a crow, or several crows, crossing overhead.

They are called cutfolk by some of the neighboring farms, and ditchers by others. Neither name is quite an insult, though both are used as one when trade goes poorly. Among themselves they tend not to use a single name for the whole settlement. They say the East Ponds, the Lower Way, Three Stakes, Underchalk, the West Mouth. A person is from a stretch of lane, a wall, a household cluster, a particular runnel of water. They belong in relation to a hazard and the people who share it.

“From below the blue seam,” someone might say.

“From the rooms past the cracked lintel.”

“From the willow end.”

That is enough.

Most of them came from elsewhere within the memory of living adults. There are families who had little upland farms pinched between larger holdings; charcoal burners driven out when the woods were enclosed; laborers from the old road; a handful of quarry hands who recognized the usefulness of exposed marl and chalk before anyone else did; widows, debtors, unmarried brothers, people with too many children and too little roof. The first arrivals came because the collapse had made shelter without asking anyone’s permission. The next came because the first had made walls around that shelter. By now there are children who cannot remember living anywhere else, and these children regard the open fields with wary fascination.

They dislike wind.

Wind has no edges in the uplands. It catches clothes, steals words, enters sleeves and mouths. Basin-born children go up to the rim and stand very still at first, as if the air has become a river around them. Some cry. Some laugh too loudly. Some spread their arms and let themselves be pushed.

The adults are less impressed. They keep one hand on a child’s collar near the edge. They look down at the houses, which from above do resemble crates, scraps, patched roofs, smoke caught among the beams. Then they look away quickly. The view makes some of them uneasy. It reveals how little holds the town together: pegs, braces, walls that are also neighbors’ walls, agreements remembered by tired people.

The basin people are practical without being unsentimental. They save strings. They straighten bent nails. They know exactly how much soot can be scraped from a chimney throat before the mortar begins to loosen, and they will mourn a broken clay bowl if its shape was especially useful. A cracked pot is not merely cracked: it is a future seed tray, a scoop, a drain cover, a weight for holding down reed matting, a place to keep ash dry. Children are taught this before they are taught letters, where letters are taught at all.

They have a particular reverence for containers.

Barrels, jars, boxes, baskets, skins, lined pits, folded cloth, rooms with good doors: all are discussed with seriousness. A container means something can be kept against damp, rats, smoke, theft, waste, or sudden weather. A household with three sound jars is regarded differently from one with none. The finest gifts at weddings are not jewelry but a lidded grain crock, a watertight chest, a new cooking pot, a set of shelves made from seasoned wood.

Wood itself is never just wood. It has a prior history. People know where each beam came from, who hauled it down, whether it was salvaged from a storm-felled alder or bought at painful cost from an upland carpenter. A ceiling beam may be named after the person who obtained it. When one finally splits, the household speaks of it as though an old worker has failed.

“Darra’s beam has opened.”

“Then don’t sleep under Darra tonight.”

Their homes are full of these intimate inheritances. A door made from a cart side. A shelf that used to be a bier. Window frames from a shepherd’s hut abandoned on the ridge. Floorboards darkened by three households’ worth of spilled broth, boot mud, candle fat, and rain. The town is new, but its materials are old in scattered ways. That is how it begins to acquire a past.

The people themselves are often marked by the place. Their boots are always filmed with pale dust or brown clay, even after washing. Their hands show tiny cuts from reed, splinters, shale, wire, and rough lime. Many have a cough that comes and goes with the damp. Many wear wool caps indoors in winter, not from cold exactly but because the ceiling seems to breathe chill onto the scalp. Their clothes are patched at knees, elbows, cuffs, and shoulders. The patches become a household language: striped flour sacks on one family’s children, faded blue sleeve cloth on another’s, neat triangular repairs characteristic of the old woman near the common oven, who refuses to waste a straight scrap on a curved tear.

They do not have much privacy, but they have developed subtle forms of it.

A hanging cloth across a bed niche means do not enter. A spoon placed upside down on a windowsill means someone is ill or grieving and cannot be disturbed except for necessity. A narrow chalk mark beside a door means the household is away at the ponds, the market, or the rim. A lamp with its shutter closed but still lit says there are people inside who wish to be left alone, though the walls may be thin enough for everyone nearby to hear the argument, the prayer, the labor pains, or the quiet that follows bad news.

The basin has made them good listeners, even when they would prefer not to be.

They can distinguish between the sound of rain striking turf and rain striking bare clay. They know the difference between a roof drip and a wall seep, between a goat kicking its tether and a beam settling under too much weight. An experienced person can hear a small slide in the slope by the way the dirt arrives: dry clicking grit, then softer grains, then the muffled slap of wet clay. They can tell whether a child is crying from hunger, anger, boredom, or the shock of being briefly lost in the mist.

They know each other’s footsteps.

This is not a poetic claim among them. It is useful knowledge. A rapid heel-first tread: the woman who runs the eastern water gate. A dragging left foot: old Kelm, who is always carrying more than anyone believes he should. A two-step pause outside a door: the boy who sells kindling and hopes not to be noticed asking for payment. A soft, almost apologetic tread: one of the night soil collectors, returning before dawn.

There are people in the town whose work is formally acknowledged, and people whose work is so constant that it disappears into the structure of life.

There is a keeper of the clapper, though everyone says the clapper belongs to the basin, not to any keeper. The current keeper is a broad-shouldered woman named Iven, formerly a potter’s apprentice, who lost two fingers when the first western retaining wall failed. She strikes the hardwood slab without hesitation. Her missing fingers have become part of the authority of the sound. When the clapper speaks, people do not ask whether it is truly necessary. They move first and argue afterward.

There are wall readers: not officials exactly, but men and women whose eyes notice damp shifts, hairline cracks, the unexpected bulge beneath a lime mark. They are consulted over new rooms and cursed when they forbid one. They carry little hammers and often stop in the middle of conversation to tap at a surface. Some say they can hear hollows through clay. Most cannot, but the belief makes people listen to them.

There are ditch workers, who are never idle for long and never thanked enough. There are lime burners when fuel permits it, brick makers, reed weavers, mushroom tenders, goat keepers, clay cutters, salvagers, cooks for hire, two people who can set broken bones tolerably well, and one old man who writes contracts in a hard upright hand for those who need agreements to exist beyond memory.

There are also the children, who are not merely smaller inhabitants but a distinct force of the place.

They know every passage too narrow for adults, every roof that can be crossed without tearing the turf, every abandoned storage cut where someone has left a cache of marbles, bones, polished shale, buttons, and stolen onions. They play games of collapse, flood, rescue, and wall-building with frightening seriousness. They make little towns out of broken bricks and then destroy them with cups of water. They keep frogs in jars after rain and are required to let them go by morning. They dare one another to stand at the mouths of the dark passages and call into them.

Sometimes something calls back: only an echo, only water, only another child hidden farther in. But no child is allowed to investigate alone.

The people of the basin are not especially brave. This is one of the reasons they survive. They do not mistake fear for shame. A person who says, “I will not sleep there,” after hearing a wall shift in the night is treated as sensible. A person who refuses to enter a new fissure is not mocked. Cowardice is running when others require help; caution is knowing when the ground has become stronger than a human being.

Their courage is communal and practical. It looks like six neighbors bracing a roof in the rain. It looks like someone giving up their dry sleeping platform to a family whose room has flooded. It looks like carrying a feverish child through the western passage to find the bone-setter, though the passage has begun to shed chalk flakes. It looks like returning to a collapsed pantry to retrieve a dead woman’s winter stores before they spoil.

They have rituals, though they would not always call them rituals.

When a new room is cut and judged safe enough to use, the first thing brought inside is water, then salt, then a lit lamp. Water proves the floor’s slope. Salt shows dampness if it clumps. The lamp tests the air and gives the darkness a boundary. Only after these is a bed brought in.

When a wall falls but no one dies, people leave a small portion of it untouched for three days. Not out of superstition alone, though superstition is present, but because digging too eagerly after a slide can cause another. During those days children are forbidden to take stones from the heap. Adults may place a cup, a scrap of bread, or a handful of lime nearby. By the third morning, all such offerings are gone—taken by rats, rain, or someone who needs them more than the gesture.

When someone dies, the body is carried above the rim if weather allows. The basin does not keep graves. The ground below has too many unknown hollows, too much seepage, too much instability. The dead are taken to a small rise in the scrubland, where the soil is thin but steady. This ascent is difficult. The mourners climb from the compressed town into exposed weather, carrying the weight of a person who once lived beneath the earth. For many, it is the only time they see the whole basin from above with their neighbors gathered beside them.

Afterward, they return downward together.

There are people who leave and come back. They go to upland markets, to work road gangs, to serve in kitchens or farms, to marry into places with real streets and proper foundations. Some return because they miss the closeness. Some return because the uplands are no kinder than the basin, only less honest about their dangers. They bring back news: new taxes, a fever in another town, the price of lamp oil, a rumor of war so far away it seems almost theatrical.

Their news is heard, considered, and folded into daily work.

The basin does not have room for grandness. It converts everything into a question of material: Will it affect the price of grain? Will there be more travelers? Will men come asking for timber? Will the road be safe? Will rain be worse this year?

And yet there are dreamers among them.

A young mason sketches plans for a stepped cistern carved into the eastern slope, with overflow channels and a covered wash place. Everyone says it is too ambitious. He keeps drawing it anyway.

An old woman grows a thorn bush in a broken chimney pot because she remembers a hedge full of birds from the farm where she was born. The bush barely survives, but each spring it flowers with a few white stars, and people pause to look at it as though it were evidence from another world.

A girl at the west end collects crow feathers. She binds them into fans, brushes, ornaments, and little black wings she sells to upland children. She claims the crows know her. Perhaps they do. They gather near the rim when she comes up with scraps.

A man who has never traveled farther than the eastern valley carves ships into every spare piece of wood. His ships have tall sails and deep keels. He has seen rivers, so he knows such things are possible. The children call him Captain, though he has never touched the sea.

And deeper in the old passages, where the original ground still presses overhead, there may be other inhabitants.

Not monsters in the way upland stories like to imagine. Nothing with eyes shining in the dark, nothing waiting politely for a lantern. There are pale insects in the wet seams; blind, soft-bodied things beneath stones; colonies of tiny roots seeking the moisture that gathers in the cracks. There are rats, always rats, clever enough to learn the timing of traps. There are bats in one high fissure near the western throat, though few people see them leave at dusk because their flight is lost against the basin’s strip of sky.

There is also the earth itself, which the residents do not think of as alive in the same way they are alive. They do not pray to it. They do not trust it. But they address it sometimes.

“Easy,” says a person driving a wedge into stubborn clay.

“Not today,” says another, hearing a small crack in the wall.

“Take what you need, then,” an old resident may mutter after a slide has swallowed a shed, provided no one was hurt.

The hillside is not their enemy. An enemy can be persuaded, defeated, avoided, or punished. The hillside is nearer to weather: intimate, necessary, indifferent, changing its mind through slow pressure and sudden release.

What lives there, finally, is a town of people who have learned not to ask permanence from their homes.

They ask only that the roof last through winter, that the ditch hold through the next rain, that the beam endure another year, that the child come back from the rim, that the wall stay where it is until morning.

And in the mornings, when the mist lifts and the gray light reaches the basin floor, they emerge from their packed rooms carrying tools, bowls, babies, baskets, lime, willow stakes, and all the small objects required to continue. They inspect the new cracks. They clear the new mud. They argue over whose wall is leaning on whose. They mend what can be mended.

Then, by noon, the place is inhabited again so thoroughly that it seems it must have always been there: every raw bank named, every dripping stone assigned a use, every narrow space made warm by breath, labor, and the stubborn decision to remain.
