What lives in the basin is not numerous, and it is not easily seen.

At first a visitor might say that nothing lives there but the pale beetles and the minute snails. This would not be wholly wrong. The beetles belong to the warm water: flat-bodied, almost colorless, with legs so thin they seem scratched into the surface beneath them. They can remain in one place for days, tucked under the lip of a stone or against the smooth inner wall of the circular pool. When disturbed, they do not flee quickly. They fold themselves close and drift a short distance, as though the water has reconsidered where to keep them.

The snails are older-looking than they are. Their shells are not truly shells in the ordinary sense, but tight spirals of limestone, built around a soft dark center. They feed, perhaps, on the translucent films that do not appear to grow. Or perhaps they feed on the water itself. Their movement is too gradual to observe, though a shell found in one crack may be discovered in another a season later.

There are also thread-worms in the black seams, white as soaked hair. They gather beneath loose stones where the warmth rises strongest from below. If a slab is lifted—and it should not be—they draw themselves backward into the wet clay with a single slow contraction. They leave no holes behind. The clay closes after them.

But these are only the basin's small and visible residents. They are its surface life.

The true inhabitants are the keepers of still water.

They do not live in the shelter, though the shelter is theirs. They do not sleep in the central enclosure, though the water there is warm enough to hold a body through the coldest night. They do not own the stone walls or the boards or the copper bell. Ownership is too sharp a relation for them. Those things have simply remained in their vicinity for a very long time.

They are called, in the few accounts that mention them, the Low Ones, the Basin Folk, the Unrising. None of these names is likely theirs.

A Low One is difficult to recognize because it has no fixed outline when seen directly. It is usually first noticed as a discrepancy in wetness: a patch of stone that seems darker than it should be; a reflection in a pool that lags slightly behind the cloud above; a human-sized dimness beneath the surface of brown channel water where the channel is far too shallow to contain anything. If one looks away and then back, the discrepancy may have shifted by the width of a hand.

In deeper water they resemble people more closely.

There may be a bowed head below the surface, pale hair moving without current around it. There may be knees drawn up in the darkness of an old pit. There may be fingers, long and jointed like wet roots, curled around the submerged edge of a stone step. But they are not drowned people, nor ghosts of people drowned here. Their stillness is not the stillness of death. It is attention.

They attend to the water.

The basin's water does not run because the Low Ones do not permit it to forget itself. Every drop that condenses under a ledge, every bead that gathers in a black seam, every faint seep beneath a slab is received. Nothing is wasted in movement. The water falls, enters, settles, warms, and remains. Where elsewhere water has a desire for lower ground, in this basin it has already arrived.

The Low Ones are the reason.

They are said to be made from the first water that failed to leave.

Long ago—though long ago has little purchase here—the basin may have been a spring yard, a wash-place, a sanctuary, or simply an inconvenient wet hollow beside a settlement. People laid boards over its channels, built the circular pool, raised the shelter, placed the bell. They came to rinse clay from their hands, wash wounds, soak stiff joints, draw warm water in coarse bowls. Perhaps they believed the basin cured fevers. Perhaps it did.

Among those people were caretakers: not priests exactly, but those willing to remain in dampness longer than others. They cleared channels with scrapers. They lifted fallen stones. They tested the warmth of the water with their wrists. They listened for changes. Over generations, they became accustomed to the basin's peculiar mercy. It kept them from thirst. It kept their dead from drying too quickly. It softened their tools, their skins, their voices, their grief.

Then the settlement went away.

No catastrophe need be imagined. The larger world may simply have shifted its road, found a drier ridge, followed a more reliable river. One household left, then another. The last caretakers stayed because someone had to know if the warm water changed. They stayed through the silting of the channels and the collapse of the outer paths. They became quiet. Their bodies learned the basin's rules: do not hurry, do not dry, do not demand a future different from the present.

Eventually, they ceased to be distinguishable from the conditions that had held them.

The Low Ones are what remained of that accommodation.

They have no need for food in the usual sense. The mineral water feeds them by contact. Iron gives them memory; limestone gives them form; the faint sourness from below gives them warmth. A Low One may spend years beneath a single slab, absorbing the slow chemical exchange between stone and water. In that time it may grow more definite, or less. It may develop a face, lose a face, become only the sensation of being watched by a puddle.

They are not lonely, though they are rarely together.

Their companionship travels through contact. A falling drop touches one pool; that pool seeps into a seam; the seam wets the underside of a slab; the slab rests against another stone. Through this uninterrupted dampness, a message can take months or centuries to cross the basin. The message need not be elaborate. Most are not.

*Warm beneath the east wall.*

*The old board has lowered.*

*There is a new crack beside the shelter.*

*Someone has stepped here.*

The arrival of a visitor is significant to them, but not alarming. A Low One may follow a visitor's footprints after they have gone, entering each print as it slowly fills. Another may wait beneath the central pool's surface while the visitor leans over to see their reflection. The reflection will usually be accurate. Occasionally it will show the visitor standing with someone beside them: a dark, bowed figure, water-bright at the shoulders.

No harm is intended by this.

The Low Ones are careful creatures. They understand that dry-bodied beings are fragile. They do not touch skin unless invited by exhaustion, fever, or grief. A person who kneels at the circular pool and places both hands in the warm water may feel, after a time, another pair of hands resting beneath theirs—not gripping, not pulling, simply matching the shape. The warmth may pass deeper into the bones than warmth should. Old aches may ease. A remembered sorrow may become less sharp at its edges.

In exchange, something small is retained.

Not a soul. Not a name. The basin is not greedy.

It keeps a little of the visitor's unused motion: the urge to leave immediately, perhaps; the quick turning of the head toward distant things; one small impatience. That motion settles into the water and becomes part of the basin's stillness. The visitor leaves lighter, though they may later find themselves pausing before puddles or listening for drips in quiet rooms.

The copper bell is for the Low Ones, but they do not ring it with wind.

On rare occasions, when the balance of the basin is disturbed—when a stone shifts, when a pit begins to cool, when a dry crack opens where none should be—the bell gives a single dull note. No hand is seen. The sound is not loud, but it enters the water and travels through all of it. The beetles unfold their legs. The snails draw inward. Beneath the slabs, the Low Ones become briefly visible as many dim shapes turning toward the same need.

Then they work.

Their work is almost indistinguishable from waiting. A channel that had begun to choke with silt clears over several nights. A fallen shale plate settles into a new position that shelters the wet ground beneath. Moisture appears in a place that had grown chalky. A board that should have rotted remains firm for another decade. They do not rebuild. They do not improve. They return things to the condition in which they can continue.

This is why little decays there.

Decay requires departure: warmth leaving flesh, water leaving wood, structure leaving stone. The Low Ones slow departures. They make every substance stay with itself a little longer. Wood silvering but not collapsing. Copper darkening but not flaking away. Reeds becoming fiber but not dust. A heel print holding its shape in clay after the heel that made it has vanished from every road beyond the rim.

If one remained in the basin too long, the Low Ones would not imprison them. There are no bars, no malice, no hidden depths waiting to close.

But the place would begin to teach them.

They would stop measuring time by daylight. They would recognize, without looking, which drops were nearly ready to fall. They would learn the distinct temperatures of each channel. They would come to prefer the basin's shadowless light, in which nothing is required to stand out from anything else. Their clothes would remain damp but cease to feel uncomfortable. Their footprints would seem more truthful than their face.

Eventually, they might take up the chipped scraper and clear a little silt from a channel. They might replace a stone in a knee-high wall. They might rest their palm against the central pool and feel an answering stillness.

And, after enough years, someone returning to the basin might notice an additional dark patch beneath the water where no dark patch had been before: a person-shaped quiet, patient under the reflected gray sky, maintaining the world by refusing to let it quite go.
