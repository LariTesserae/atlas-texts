What lives on the high country has learned not to spread itself thin.

The plateau is too exposed for abundance. Life gathers in pockets, under lips of stone, inside the green cuts where water lingers after the open land has dried. It is a country of refuges and crossings rather than territories. Most creatures belong partly to one place and partly to another: feeding on the flats at one hour, hiding in ravines at another; drinking from rain caught in leaves; nesting where the wind cannot find them.

The smallest lives most visibly, once one learns how to look.

There are insects that sleep beneath flakes of stone and emerge only in the few wet hours after a storm. Their wings are clear and heavy, their bodies the color of oxidized copper. They rise from cracks in such numbers that the air above a newly flooded basin seems to glitter. Before noon they are mostly gone—eaten, drowned, driven back underground, or simply folded into the heat.

There are pale, slow beetles in the seepage caves, feeding on the films of green that grow where rock sweats. They have almost no eyes. Their shells are thin, not because there is little danger in darkness, but because there is little food with which to build armor. In the wet walls above them live fat black spiders whose webs are strung not across open space but over trickles of water. They take whatever the current brings: drowned gnats, hatchlings, scraps of leaf, sometimes tiny fish washed from one hidden pool to another.

The bright lizards are seen most often at the edges of the ravines, where sun and shade touch. They are not truly bright in the manner of flowers. Their green is broken by soot-black bars and pale blue throats, so that against moss and wet stone they disappear completely until they move. They eat flies, ants, beetles, frogspawn, and one another’s young. In the dry season they remain deep in fissures, living on stored fat and moisture gathered from insects. After rain, they come out in numbers, lying flat against warm walls with their mouths open to the mist.

Some grow large enough to be dangerous only to children. These are the slate-backed wall lizards, long-bodied and broad-headed, with claws made for climbing vertical wet rock. Ravine people sometimes keep them near their houses, not as pets exactly, but because they eat the pale burrowing grubs that spoil stored roots and chew through reed walls. A house with slatebacks in its foundations is said to be watched by attentive ancestors. A house without them is merely a house.

The frogs are more often heard by those who already know their calls. The sound does not carry over weather. It comes through the hand placed against a wet stone, or through the soles of the feet at night: a small repeated knocking, like a reed tapping against a gourd. The frogs live in places that seem incapable of supporting them—water held in tree hollows, rain pools no wider than a bowl, flooded seams in the rock. They are green when young and become nearly black as they age. During dry months they enter cracks and seal themselves in a skin of hardened mucus, sometimes surviving until the next season’s storms. Children in the ravines are taught never to pry open the sealed chambers. A sleeping frog is water that has chosen a shape.

There are snakes, though few are encountered. The common ravine snake is narrow, brown, and harmless, a hunter of frogs and lizards that smells strongly of crushed fern when disturbed. More feared is the wind snake of the high flats, a grey animal with a blunt head and a body patterned like weathered stone. It lies inside shallow gravel beds and waits for creatures crossing between shelter. It is not especially venomous, but its bite destroys flesh slowly, and the wound is difficult to keep clean in a country where the nearest running water may be half a day away. The people say wind snakes are born from cracks opened by thunder. This is not believed literally by everyone, but no one laughs at it during a storm.

The plateau has birds, though not in the numbers a lowland traveler expects.

Small black-and-white cliff swifts nest in the narrowest clefts beneath the escarpments. They fly low before rain, taking insects from the air just above the stone. Their nests are made from mud, moss, and hair gathered from people’s clothing lines. They are tolerated because their arrival at a ravine settlement usually means weather is coming within a day.

There are ground birds in the sheltered basins: mottled, round-bodied things with strong feet and nearly invisible wings. They spend most of their lives under fern cover and will freeze rather than flee. When frightened, they explode upward all at once with such violence that even experienced travelers cry out. Their eggs, found only rarely, are considered food for the sick and are never taken in pairs.

At the uppermost levels, where grass is almost absent and the stone lies open for leagues, lives the ash kite. It is a small raptor, all narrow wings and pale breast, capable of hanging almost motionless in the wind. It hunts lizards, mice, and insects swept from the ground by gusts. The ash kite is regarded with affection because it does not fear the singing pillars. It nests in their upper cracks, laying scraps of fur and lichen around the old bored holes. When the wind makes a pillar sing, the kite rides the vibration as if it were an ordinary branch in a tree.

The great scavengers are different. They are seldom seen close, only as black or rust-colored shapes turning on currents above the escarpment. The people call them the Long Waiters. They descend when something large has died: a cliff deer broken in a fall, a pack animal lost by travelers, a person who did not return from the flats. They are not treated as omens of death, because death needs no omen there. Instead, they are treated as witnesses. If Long Waiters circle a place for three days, the ravine people will go to see what the land has taken.

There are mammals, but they make a life by secrecy.

The stone hare lives among the low boulder fields. It has oversized ears, pale feet, and a coat that changes with the seasons—not color, but texture. In wet weather its fur grows coarse and dark, shedding water quickly. In drought it becomes thin, dusty, almost silver. Stone hares emerge at twilight to eat lichens, seeds, tender fern stems, and the soft inner parts of cycads. They are hard to trap, partly because they are quick, and partly because they seem to know when a person is hungry enough to need them. Hunters say a stone hare will allow itself to be seen only by someone who has already missed two meals.

In the deepest ravines live small deer with narrow legs and broad, soft hooves. They do not run well on open stone. Their country is root, mud, fallen leaf, and steep wet slope. Their coats are dark brown in shade but flash red in sunlight. They travel alone or in pairs, browsing in the hours between rain and night. Their antlers are short and dense, made for forcing through vines rather than fighting in clear ground. A deer can vanish into a ravine so completely that a hunter standing three paces away may hear it breathing and see nothing.

The largest native animal is the shelfback, a thick-bodied, long-snouted grazer about the size of a small pony. It lives in the broadest basins where thin soil supports grass, sedge, and low woody plants. Its back is armored with overlapping plates of horn, not enough to stop a cliff fall or a hungry scavenger, but enough to protect it from thorn, rock, and the bites of ravine cats. Shelfbacks travel in tiny family groups—usually three, never more than seven—and follow routes remembered across generations. They know where water collects beneath stone, which ledges remain passable after rain, which open areas should not be crossed when the wind is from the east.

A shelfback path may be older than a human settlement. Some become the remembered routes people follow, marked by cairns long after the animals have shifted elsewhere. The people do not kill shelfbacks casually. Their meat is prized, their hide almost impossible to replace, their plates used for tools and ceremonial rattles. But more than this, a shelfback that abandons a basin is understood to know something. It may have sensed that a wall will fall, that water has gone bad beneath the ground, or that a storm season will be wrong.

Ravine cats hunt the deer, the stone hares, and occasionally young shelfbacks. They are low, smoke-colored animals with long tails and broad paws. Their fur is patterned in streaks that resemble wet stone drying in the sun. They are rarely seen, but their marks are common: three-toed tracks in mud, hair caught on bark, the remains of a bird arranged neatly beneath a ledge. They do not often approach houses. When they do, it is usually because something inside has already died.

The people themselves are perhaps the most careful inhabitants.

They call themselves, in one of their languages, the Keepers of the Wet Places. Outsiders have given them other names, most of which they do not use. They are not a single people in the strict sense. The ravines are separated by distances that weather can turn impassable, and each sheltered settlement has its own habits of speech, burial, marriage, planting, and song. Yet the high country joins them. They know the same water signs. They recognize the same pillar-notes. They understand that one does not become a person of the plateau merely by arriving with enough supplies.

To live there, one must learn to read absence.

A missing frog call may mean a pool has soured. No lizards on a wall may mean a snake is sleeping behind it. A wind that stops too suddenly may mean the storm has divided and is coming from two directions. Moss growing bright at the base of an overhang may mean a hidden drip nearby; moss growing dull and stringy may mean the water has turned mineral-heavy and should not be drunk without boiling. A shelfback track pointing uphill after rain is a warning. A scavenger flying low but not circling is an invitation to look farther ahead.

The ravine people cultivate small gardens wherever soil can be held in place. They grow roots, beans, medicinal leaves, a tough grain with red seed heads, and a climbing fruit that must be trained under stone arches so the wind does not tear it apart. Every garden is partly a wall. Stones are stacked along its lower edge to prevent rain from carrying it away. Fallen leaves are trapped in woven screens. Channels are opened before storms and closed afterward. Children learn earthwork before they learn hunting.

They gather food from the wild country as well: fern hearts, cycad flour prepared carefully to remove its poison, honey from cliff hives, fruit from ravine trees, grubs from rotting wood, eggs, shellfish from temporary rain pools, and the white mineral crust that forms near old seepage basins. The crust is broken with heavy mallets and traded downward for salt, cloth, copper, and things no one on the plateau can make.

Their dead are not buried in the shallow soil. Some settlements place them in stone chambers beneath overhangs, wrapped in reed cloth with water gourds and small wind-carved tokens. Others give their dead to the ravines, setting them on high platforms where the Long Waiters can find them. Both practices are said to return a person correctly: one to the stone, one to the air.

The singing pillars belong to no settlement, though every settlement has duties toward one or more of them.

Old people inspect the pillars after storms. Young people clear lodged branches from their holes. No one repairs a fallen pillar unless it has fallen into a route where it might injure travelers; even then, it is moved aside rather than re-raised. Their purpose is not entirely known. The tones are used as signs, but not in a simple language. A low note before dawn may mean fog in the eastern cuts. A pillar that has fallen silent may mean its lower hole is blocked with silt, or that the ground has shifted beneath it. A changed note can mean wind alone.

It can also mean something else.

There are stories of people who heard their own names in the pillars during storms. There are stories of a pillar singing for seven nights before an entire ravine wall collapsed, leaving a new lake where gardens had stood. There are stories of children found alive after floodwaters, lying in sheltered hollows beside pillars that had not been there before.

Most people do not claim to know which stories are true. The plateau does not reward certainty.

And beneath all the visible life are older things.

Roots travel far beneath the stone, following damp seams between volcanic layers. Whole groves in separate ravines may be connected by fungal threads and buried root systems that have persisted longer than any house. In drought, one tree will sometimes die standing while others around it remain green; the people say it has given its water away. There are caves under the plateau that breathe cold air in summer and warm air in winter. No one has mapped them. Some contain blind fish in deep pools. Some contain only dripping water and mineral growths shaped like folded hands. Some may open into ravines no one has reached from above.

The high country is not empty. It is concentrated.

Its inhabitants have become good at waiting: frogs waiting inside stone for rain, seeds waiting beneath ash and gravel, deer waiting in green shadow, people waiting for fog to lift, scavengers waiting on air, roots waiting for water that may not come for weeks. Even the pillars wait, their holes turned toward whatever wind will arrive.

When the weather breaks and the whole plateau begins to speak—stone ringing, channels roaring, trees throwing water from their leaves—the hidden lives answer in their own ways. Lizards emerge. Frogs call through the ground. Shelfbacks move toward higher shelves. People seal their doors, open the right drainage cuts, and listen to the distant notes of the pillars.

Then, for a little while, the ancient roof of stone is full of voices.
