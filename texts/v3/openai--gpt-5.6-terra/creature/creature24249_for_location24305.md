What lives there is not quite a people, though it has the patience, arrangement, and mutual accommodation of one.

The plateau is inhabited first by the **keepings**.

A keeping is a portion of the living architecture that has learned to remain responsible for a place. It may begin as a knot of roots beneath a basin, a cluster of folded leaves at the mouth of a corridor, or a pale pressure-bladder grown in the hollow of a tower. Over time—though time does not alter it in the ordinary way—it gathers functions around itself. It senses weight on a walkway. It knows the water level of nearby cisterns. It closes a vent when mist turns cold. It routes overflow toward a lower terrace. It thickens a handrail where the wet sheen is too slippery.

No individual keeping has a body that can be separated from its work. The bridge is part of the bridge-keeper. The wall is part of the wall-keeper. A whole gallery may be the hearing organ of something whose memory is spread among several hundred hanging roots.

They do not speak with voices. Their nearest equivalent is adjustment.

A chamber warms slightly before a visitor reaches it. A stair becomes firmer under a descending weight. The leaves around a pool turn their pale undersides outward, making a visible path across fog. In the high towers, one can sometimes hear a sequence of wooden knocks traveling from beam to beam: not wind, not falling seed, but one district informing another that the lake has risen by the width of a finger.

The keepings have no need to decide whether they are plants, buildings, or weather. Such distinctions would be local inconveniences to them. They are arrangements that persist.

Within the keepings live smaller, more mobile presences: the **unfolded**.

These are soft-bodied things, usually no larger than a child, though their apparent size is difficult to judge among the grand leaves and wet corridors. They emerge from the seams of the construction, particularly from places where new leaf material remains permanently half-open. At a distance they resemble bundles of pale roots gathered into the suggestion of a figure. Closer, they have too many delicate limbs, each joint sheathed in translucent green skin. Their heads are smooth buds split by a vertical dark crease. When they are still, the crease closes and they become nearly indistinguishable from folded vegetation.

They travel without haste along the causeways and through the shallow pools. Their feet spread wide on wet surfaces and leave no track. They climb walls by fitting their limbs into the woven structure, becoming briefly part of it as they ascend. Often one will enter a root-spout or a hollow stem and not emerge for days. It may not be traveling inside so much as listening.

The unfolded attend to small imbalances.

They carry fallen fruit to places where the floor needs thickening. They press loose fibers into the edges of water channels. They stand beneath dripping cistern-leaves and let water gather in the bowls of their shoulders, then carry it elsewhere in slow, careful increments. When a mineral outcrop surfaces at the plateau’s edge, unfolded may gather around it in silence. They touch its raw stone with the tips of their limbs. Soon afterward, a border of moss appears, then a railing of roots, then a little shaded court built around the exposed earth.

They do not seem to build these things. Rather, their attention gives the plateau permission to notice.

There are also the **sleeping tall**, which are often mistaken for towers.

A sleeping tall is a vertical grove bound into a single body. Its trunks may form rooms, its branches bridges, its flowering crowns whole neighborhoods of white funnels. It stands for centuries—or for no measurable duration at all—with no evident movement. Yet at certain intervals a tower flexes. Its latticed upper boughs turn by a degree. A spiral stair tightens. A flower gallery opens on the side that had been facing away from the sun. The deep communal sighs heard across the plateau are sometimes the sleeping tall exchanging pressure through their buried roots.

Inside them are warm hollows full of fragrant fog. The unfolded enter these hollows in groups and emerge changed: greener, paler, with additional limbs, or with seed-like growths dangling from their wrists. Some enter and do not emerge. In their place, months or moments later, a new window appears in the tower wall, lined with wet white fiber.

The sleeping tall may be the source of the plateau’s long memory. Not history, exactly. Nothing there has a sequence of ages to remember. But they preserve patterns: the proper shape of a basin during low cloud; the sound a reed-throat should make when the wind comes from the northern cold; the arrangement of open water required to hold a certain quality of light. Their roots reach beneath the lake-roof, into regions where brightness moves slowly below the surface.

The pale long-legged drinkers in the shallows are called, if anything calls them, the **cup-walkers**.

They are not animals in the sense of being separate from the garden’s intention. Their skin has the dry, granular texture of husks, and their joints open with the faint crackle of pods. They spend long spans with their heads inside flower funnels, drinking rainwater, nectar, and perhaps sound. A cup-walker will remain motionless through an entire afternoon of mist. Then, with no sign of alarm, it steps forward and its long limbs unfold farther than seemed possible. It can cross water without sinking if the floating roots beneath have recognized it.

Where they drink, certain flowers remain open longer. Where they sleep, the floor grows softer. Sometimes one enters the central lake and disappears below the surface. A line of silver bubbles may rise far from shore, and then the root columns beneath the water alter their positions by a fraction.

The dark fliers belong to no visible nest because their nests are the vents themselves.

They are **breath-keepers**, thin creatures of lacquer-dark membrane and hooked reed-like feet. They sleep pressed flat inside the throats of towers, their wings wrapped over them like dead leaves. At dusk, when the plateau’s internal warmth begins to rise, they wake in swarms and ride the exhalations upward. Their flight is quiet, but the air changes around them. They clear spores from narrow passages. They carry pollen between flower galleries too high for rain to reach. They eat the little drifting motes that would otherwise clog a bladder or choke a woven wall.

At night they arrange themselves along the upper ribs of the towers, hundreds at a time, all facing the same direction. When a storm approaches from beyond the escarpment, they turn inside out: their dark wings flash pale, and the whole crown of a tower becomes briefly white against the clouds.

There may be one other inhabitant, though it is never encountered directly.

It lives beneath the central lake, or is the central lake’s underside, or is the immense root system that makes the plateau possible. The keepings route water toward it. The sleeping tall lean subtly in its direction. Cup-walkers enter its depth. The pale ladders between towers all seem, in their irregular way, to avoid crossing the air above its center.

It is sometimes visible only as a delayed light under black water: a broad gold-green illumination moving through submerged chambers. At other times the lake becomes so still that the reflected clouds do not drift with the sky. They remain fixed, as though held by an eye below.

The unfolded do not approach the lake’s innermost edge unless carrying something: a fallen flower crown, a basin’s worth of rain, a bundle of mineral dust, a dead breath-keeper wrapped in moss. They set these offerings on the ribbed embankments. By morning, there is no trace of them. In their place, some distant wall may have acquired a new arch, or an empty court may hold a fresh circle of clear water.

If the plateau has a resident that can be called its heart, it is this submerged presence. But it does not command. Nothing there is commanded.

The life of the place is cooperative without being social, intentional without urgency. The unfolded mend; the keepings attend; the tall ones remember; the fliers breathe; the cup-walkers carry depth into the shallows. Beneath them all, something patient receives water, light, pressure, and the uncompleted substance of the world, and returns them as shelter.

There are no rulers. No families in the human arrangement. No graves, because nothing properly dies; no births, because nothing simply begins.

There are only new forms that have always been permitted to appear.
