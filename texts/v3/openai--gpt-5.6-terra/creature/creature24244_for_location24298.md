What lives in the Lower Gardens is not a population in the ordinary sense. It is a working arrangement between people, plants, water, and old machinery—each of them having outlasted the purposes for which the others were first brought below.

The people call themselves gardeners when speaking to outsiders, though this is only partly true. They are growers, drain-clearers, mechanics, cooks, electricians, compost tenders, carpenters, lamp-menders, pump-watchers, fermenters, root-readers, and wardens. Most have hands altered by the place: pale, damp-softened palms; black crescents beneath the nails; small white scars from sheet metal, pruning hooks, and cracked ceramic tiles. Their boots are repaired again and again rather than replaced. Their clothing is layered for wetness, not warmth—rubberized capes, wool shirts, waxed sleeves, trousers patched at the knee and seat. Everybody carries a light, though seldom the same kind. Some use hood lamps. Some carry square work lanterns with wire guards. Some prefer narrow inspection torches whose beams can follow a pipe into foliage without blinding everyone nearby.

They are not silent people, but the Gardens have taught them an economy of speech. A shouted warning is swallowed by fans and falling water. Names do not always carry. So they tap railings with tools, flash lamps against walls, wave colored gloves above leaves, or know one another by routines: the person who crosses the west gantry before the six-o’clock pump cycle; the pair who bring hot tea to the filter rooms; the old woman whose yellow hood appears wherever a root has broken masonry; the boy from the market tunnel who can distinguish a failing bearing from a clogged impeller by touch through the floor.

Children live there too, though they are watched closely around water and machinery. They grow up understanding that a green wall can be structural, that a puddle can be deeper than it looks, and that a humming cable should never be touched. Their games are conducted on the safest ribs of concrete and in empty packing rooms: chalk diagrams on tiled walls, contests of balance on old rails, trading polished valve handles and labels from dead control panels. They learn the routes before they learn the city above. For them, rain is not something that falls from a sky. It is the sound in the ceiling, the cold bead on a pipe, the changing weight of a pump’s work.

The oldest residents are often those who remember the Gardens in versions no longer visible: before the north overflow was widened, before the banana vault collapsed and became the banana hollow, before the freight tunnel market had lamps, when one had to shop by touch and smell. They retain maps not on paper but in stories and habits. They know which walls are actually walls and which are roots wearing mortar. They know where the old city’s foundations hang overhead like buried ships. When a path changes, it is usually one of them who says what used to be there, and whether it is safe to build on what remains.

The wardens are the closest thing the district has to an authority. They do not wear uniforms, though most carry root knives, chalk, heavy keys, and bundles of colored cord. Their work is part botany, part engineering, part memory. A warden may spend a morning examining a fig root that has entered a ventilation shaft, an afternoon deciding whether a cracked brick arch can tolerate another season, and an evening walking the marked paths to ensure that no one has left a lamp too close to foliage. They settle disputes over space because space is never merely space below: a family’s kitchen expansion may obstruct a drainage route; a new shelf may shade seedlings; a proposed bedroom wall may imprison the access to a pump valve.

They are feared a little, respected considerably, and relied upon absolutely. The wardens’ greatest skill is refusing to call anything trivial.

A drip is not a drip until someone has listened to it.

A vine is not a vine until someone knows what it is climbing through.

A smell is not a smell until someone decides whether it is yeast, rot, overheated insulation, or the beginning of a flood.

The plant life is more various than most surface residents would imagine. It is not wilderness, though it can look like it to an untrained eye. Nearly every leaf has a purpose, a name, a history of being cut back, divided, redirected, traded, or saved from blight. The taro and colocasia are water workers, their roots taking what the channels offer. Ferns soften the brick and hold the dampness where it is wanted. Mosses mark the slow movement of water, making green maps across stone. Vines are trained to hide ugly surfaces, to bind unstable ones, to shade reservoirs, to carry fruit, or to reveal faults: a vine that suddenly yellows may be telling the gardeners that something beneath it has changed.

The trees are treated with particular seriousness. A mulberry may support a ceiling brace with one branch and feed silkwormless kitchens with another, though no animal enters the Gardens. A guava may have roots wrapped around pipes installed before anyone alive was born. A citrus tree growing from an old tank may be older than the room around it. Their trunks are marked with painted rings, copper tags, ceramic names. People apologize before cutting them. They tie branches upward with cloth slings. They build rooms around roots. A tree that dies is not simply removed; it leaves behind a rearrangement of light, water, pathways, and work that can take years to understand.

There are other lives below that no one calls residents, but everyone depends upon them: the molds blooming quietly in warm corners, the pale threads of fungi holding mulch together, the yeasts in sealed jars and fermentation crocks, the algae in filters and channels, the bacteria in compost bays doing the invisible labor of turning waste back into fertility. The gardeners do not romanticize these lives. They measure them, heat them, feed them, scrape them off the wrong surfaces, encourage them in the right beds. Yet there is an affection in the way they speak of a good compost heap or a healthy water film. In the Lower Gardens, decay is not failure. It is one of the trades.

The machines live there too, in their fashion.

The oldest pumps have names, usually inherited from former workers or stamped manufacturers’ marks distorted by decades of retelling. One is called Auntie Vale because its pulse is slow and dependable. Another is the Red King, despite no longer being red, because it serves the deepest basin and must not be allowed to stop. The pump tenders know their machinery intimately: the temperature of a housing, the rhythm of a belt, the minor quarrels of old valves. They speak of a motor being tired, a fan being sulky, a filter being hungry. This is not naïveté. It is the language of people who know that machinery must be attended to as carefully as a crop.

Some residents are born above and come below by choice, by inheritance, by debt, or because they cannot afford rooms in the dry city. Others have never spent more than a day at a time in open weather. The surface makes them uneasy. They dislike wind because it comes from nowhere and cannot be heard approaching. They find sunlight too direct. They squint in ordinary rooms. But they know things the upper city has forgotten: how to keep food growing under a street; how to hear a flood before it arrives; how to build a home without demanding that every root, leak, and crack be conquered.

Their homes are close, warm, and perpetually damp. Shoes wait on racks above the floor. Pots of herbs crowd windowless shelves under lamps. Laundry hangs wherever warm air passes. Walls bear painted route symbols instead of framed paper. Meals are heavy with greens, roots, fermented fruit, mushrooms cultivated in guarded rooms, grain milled near the old machinery, and whatever trade comes down from above. People eat sitting near pumps, at market counters, on benches beside the Reservoir Court. They share news in fragments: a failed seal in the east channel, a shipment of clean filter cloth, a child’s fever, a tree fruiting unexpectedly, a sealed door upstairs found standing open.

And underneath all this is the discipline of animal absence.

No one says that the Gardens are lonely. They are too full for loneliness. But visitors often feel the missing flutter, skitter, chirp, buzz, and breath of other creatures. The residents scarcely notice it until they go above. Then a pigeon’s sudden wingbeat can startle them. A dog’s barking seems almost indecently loud. Flies around an outdoor market look like a failure of boundaries.

Below, the Gardens belong to what can root, spread, filter, ferment, repair, and endure.

The people are part of that ecology without pretending to be its masters. They prune what would crush them, shore up what would drown them, guide water away from their beds and toward their reservoirs, and make room where room can be made. In return, the green holds the brick together, the roots cool the air, the beds feed the kitchens, the wet growth swallows waste, and the old buried basin remains habitable.

So what lives there is a city of keepers: human beings made patient by dripping ceilings; plants made architectural by necessity; machines made almost familial by constant care; and the vast, uncelebrated life of rot and renewal binding everything together in the dark.
