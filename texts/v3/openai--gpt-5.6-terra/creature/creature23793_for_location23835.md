What lives there is not quite a population. It is a pressure-balanced congregation of things that have learned, separately and together, to tolerate being made.

The first resident is the water.

Not water in the broad mountain sense—snowmelt, storm runoff, ice cracking under sun—but water with an assigned route. It rises when summoned. It waits in dark cisterns under the plates. It travels through insulated pipes, across filter beds, through gravel chambers, past impellers furred with green threads. It comes out warm and mineral-tasting, carrying a faint dust of iron, lime, old root, and whatever the compost drums have lately produced. It knows every false ledge by impact. It has polished the ceramic lips, darkened the bolt heads, made black inscriptions beneath the gratings.

Some of it has been circulating for years.

It has entered leaves and returned through transpiration. It has gathered in the hollow ribs of taro stems, in the cups of bromeliads, beneath curled fern fronds. It has become mist, condensation, runoff, bead, drip, basin, pumpwater. It falls from one manufactured cliff and is lifted behind another. The ravine has no river leaving it in the ordinary way. Its water is a resident because it cannot depart; it survives by becoming other forms of itself.

The moss lives there more completely than any animal could.

It occupies the intended softness of the place, the visual lie for which every rough-faced wall and wet seam was constructed. At first it was probably introduced in discreet torn pads, pressed into mortar with gloved thumbs, watered on a schedule, inspected for color. Now it has preferences. It avoids certain hot pipes. It thickens around slow leaks. It makes a special velvet of itself where vapor condenses beneath the north catwalk. It grows pale and fine on ceramic, dark and wiry on slag, rusty-green on painted steel.

In the oldest places, it has become less like a covering than an archive. Beneath its layers are obsolete labels, old repair welds, the square outlines of panels removed long ago. A pipe may vanish beneath it so gradually that no one notices until the pipe is needed. Then a maintenance hand parts the moss and finds, alive beneath the green mat, a valve wheel with its manufacturer’s name still stamped around the hub.

Liverwort lives there too, flatter and more suspicious. It takes the surfaces the moss cannot quite monopolize: the damp roofs of planter-boulders, the shaded seams between cast plates, the undersides of handrails. It spreads in tiny lobed territories, green-brown and slick, mapping the ravine’s humidity more accurately than any gauge. If a circulation fan fails, liverwort knows first. If a pipe begins sweating behind a wall, it sends a dim stain toward it. It is one of the place’s quiet instruments, though nobody installed it as one.

The ferns are citizens of a more visible kind. They have fronds like elaborate equipment: hinged, ribbed, receptive. Their roots occupy every available pocket of wet medium and then proceed beyond it. They taste mortar. They enter cable trays. They wrap hair-fine roots around warm conduits and make use of the condensation there. When cut back from a drain, they return from a darker route. When an old frond dies, it does not leave so much as become material for the next layer of life.

Some have been there long enough to develop private geographies. A fern beside the western spillway is always bent in one direction, shaped by the draft that arrives whenever the upper shutters open. Another grows low and dense around the basin tower, its oldest stems blackened by mineral spray. Behind the southern wall, in a service cavity no visitor sees, there may be a colony that has never received daylight at all, living on the reflected glow of inspection lamps and the warmth of the pipes.

The vines live with ambition.

They do not understand that a cable ladder is not a tree, that an inspection hatch is meant to open, that a ventilation grille needs space around it. They are not malicious. They are simply committed to connection. Given a wire, they cross it. Given a gap, they bridge it. Given a shaft breathing warm air, they enter and explore. Their leaves make a second architecture over the first, one that has no respect for access routes or maintenance diagrams.

The workers know them individually, though they may deny it.

There is the vine that always closes the hatch above Pump Room Three. There is the heavy-stemmed climber on the east wall that has to be unhooked twice a year from a pressure-release lever. There is a flowering creeper near the old green steel face whose roots have found a crack in the insulation and now drink some hidden condensate supply. Cutting it away would be easy. Cutting it away entirely would be difficult. Nobody seems to have managed it.

The small tree in the reservoir sleeve lives there as a compromise that became a monument.

It was planted to give scale, perhaps, or shade, or the appearance of a valley old enough to have forgotten its builders. Its roots were meant to descend through prepared layers into a contained depth of water and mineral substrate. It did that. Then it continued. Its trunk thickened. Its crown reached the roof. The roof did not move, so the tree did.

Now it grows sideways beneath beams and pipes, branches pressed flat into a green canopy. One limb bends around a steam line and has bark polished smooth by years of intermittent vibration. Another has grown through the rectangle of a redundant frame and incorporated it, so that metal and wood can no longer be cleanly separated. The tree has become a resident engineer by accident, holding moisture, casting shade, sheltering ferns, catching the mist before it can reach certain sensors.

It has likely outlived the people who planted it.

Then there are the things that live only because nothing has excluded them successfully.

The snails are nearly colorless, their shells thin as wet fingernails. They move through the moss in the hours when the lamps are lowest and the warm floor has stopped radiating quite so fiercely. Their paths are visible only as briefly bright lines on painted railings or blackened plates. They consume softness: algae, films, spores, the first green fuzz that comes after a cleaning. In this place of regulated excess, they are among the few beings with no assigned function.

The beetles inhabit the margins of water. They walk the slick handrails, disappear into drainage slots, emerge from beneath planters in pairs or alone. Their bodies are too narrow for easy notice. They have learned to stop when footsteps approach, becoming dark flecks among rivets and droplets. What they eat is uncertain. Dead moss, fungal growth, tiny drowned insects carried in on cloud drafts—there is enough hidden food in a ravine that is always wet.

The frog, if it is the same frog, lives in the sound before it lives in sight.

It gives one low note during the wettest weeks: not a song exactly, more a hollow pulse passing through pipes. The sound may come from the basin edge, from behind the intake tower, from a drain that leads nowhere obvious. Workers have tried to locate it and failed. Once, someone saw a pale throat expand beneath the roots of the tree. Once, someone found a small print in a film of silt beside an open channel. The frog has made itself rare enough to become part of the ravine’s machinery of belief. It is proof that the place is alive, or proof that people wish it to be.

The moths live near the eastern lamp, though perhaps only briefly. They collect in the light’s warm halo, powdery and hesitant, then vanish into the upper framework. Their wings are the color of substrate dust. In the morning, a few may be found resting on damp gauges, on the pale undersides of orchid leaves, on the numbered tags tied around new plantings. They seem made of the same fine matter as the ravine’s beginnings: compost fiber, mineral powder, loose scale from old paint.

Fungi live everywhere but do not announce themselves.

They lace the compost trays. They occupy old root channels. They thread the insulation inside planter walls and bloom as pale shelves behind access panels. Some are welcome, or tolerated, or too useful to remove. Others are scraped away from seals and cable glands, where their softness becomes dangerous. At night, in places where the light is poor and moisture does not fluctuate, they may make faint cream-colored fans across a surface that was bare the week before.

There are bacteria in the basin, in the warm seams, in the filter beds, in the rust-colored deltas of root matter and sediment. They do much of the ravine’s hidden work. They turn waste into availability. They soften leaffall. They make the water smell green rather than stagnant. They live in a world of gradients: oxygen at one edge, darkness at another; warmth from a pipe; a sudden richness when a shutter releases more substrate; the periodic violence of a pump cycle. To them, the ravine is immense and eventful.

And beneath all of this, the machinery lives in its own way.

The pumps have rhythms. The chains have a patient gait. The valves have moods determined by mineral buildup, gasket age, pressure differences, the almost imperceptible settling of the mountain around them. The intake tower opens its grille like a sleeping thing taking breath. The heat system exhales through the floor. Sensors blink behind leaves. Motors wake, strain, settle, sleep again.

They are not alive, but neither are they merely dead objects in this place. They have become participants in a long accommodation. Plants lean toward their heat. Moss hides their seams. Water depends on their insistence. The workers listen for changes in their voices the way one listens for a cough in another room. A pump that begins with too sharp a clatter, a chain that hesitates before catching, a drain that draws more slowly than it should: these are symptoms, but they are also expressions.

The people who maintain the ravine live there only partially.

They come in waterproof boots, with key rings, sampling bottles, pruning knives, test strips, grease pencils, replacement nozzles, bags of pellets, coils of wire. They know which catwalk drips down the collar of a jacket, which rail is loose beneath the moss, which orchid is not permitted to spread but is allowed to remain. Some speak to the plants. Some resent the plants. Most eventually do both.

They remove leaves from strainers and leave a little silt in place because clearing it entirely makes the water look wrong. They cut back roots from critical equipment, then tuck the cut stems into some harmless crevice where they may begin again. They shovel the mechanical deltas flat and watch new ones form. They repair the ravine’s illusion without fully restoring it, because a completely clean artificial valley would fail at being a valley.

Perhaps that is what lives there most distinctly: maintenance.

Not maintenance as a task, but as a creature with many hands. It moves through schedules and drip lines, through pruning and replacement, through the decision to let one patch of moss cover a flaw and the decision to expose another flaw before it becomes a failure. It feeds the place mineral dust. It warms the floor. It measures the basin’s breath. It permits disorder in controlled quantities.

The ravine contains a wilderness, but the wilderness is young at every moment. It does not age toward independence. It ages toward revision.

So its inhabitants are water that cannot leave, plants that cannot stop taking hold, small animals that have slipped through the system’s attention, invisible decomposers, persistent machines, and the intermittent human keepers who preserve the conditions under which all the others can continue pretending they arrived on their own.
