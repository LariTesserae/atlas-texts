What lives there is not one creature but a pressure of creatures: a colony so dense that it has become almost a second lining of the chamber.

The bats are the most visible part of it, though visible is not quite the word. In darkness they are known first by heat, breath, and noise. They occupy the ceiling in overlapping blankets, packed into every fold of volcanic glass where a claw can find purchase. The outermost animals look black or soot-brown when the red fissure-glow rises. Those deeper in the mass are never seen at all. They live by touch: muzzle against shoulder, wing over back, one small heartbeat pressed among hundreds of others.

They are probably not all alike. The high crack admits different kinds: swift narrow-winged hunters that go out at night into the cold mountain air; broad-winged scavengers that worry at dead insects and fallen pups; small crevice bats able to force themselves into the hottest, tightest seams near the breathing rock. Their colony is a layered weather system. Young hang in protected knots near the warm center. Mothers return with milk and the odors of the outside night caught in their fur. Old bats settle in the margins, scarred-eared and thin-winged, knowing the chamber’s safe temperatures better than any map could tell them.

When the colony wakes, it wakes unevenly. A few bodies stir. Then a patch of wings begins to shake loose. Then the sound crosses the ceiling in a moving front, a shiver traveling through thousands of folded limbs. The bats do not take flight all at once. There is not room. They crawl over one another, mouth open, chirring sharply, and feed themselves into the vertical crack in a black stream. For a while the fissure exhales them. Outside, they disperse over the barren ridge and down into lower valleys where insects still fly above scrub, lava fields, and distant water.

By dawn they return carrying the cold of the open sky with them. Their bodies are dusted with ash. Some bring tiny moth wings caught in fur; some carry pollen from vegetation far below, though no flower will ever grow in the chamber. They pour back into the mountain before sunlight reaches the crack, and the interior fills again with warmth, musk, ammonia, and the dry rustle of settling wings.

Below them live the inheritors of everything they drop.

The guano is the chamber’s soil, its crop, its buried forest. It lies in old layers on the basalt, dry on top and dense beneath, threaded with larval tunnels. Beetles move through it with the solemn persistence of miners. They are dark, heat-polished things, their shells faintly iridescent where sulfur dust catches them. Some have strong eyes and emerge near the entrance; others are pale or nearly colorless, having long abandoned the need for sight. Their antennae test the ground constantly, reading vibration, warmth, and the chemical history of the colony.

The cave crickets are less discreet. They stand in the margins where stone meets waste, legs too long for their bodies, feeling the air for movement. Their limbs make them seem fragile, but they endure heat that would kill creatures of cooler caves. When disturbed, they vanish with startling speed into cracks where their bodies flatten and their antennae remain exposed like roots.

There are mites in such numbers that no one creature could name them all. Some live in the bats’ fur, some in their nests of pressed bodies, some among the guano grains, feeding on skin flakes, fungus, eggs, and each other. Tiny blind spiders wait in crevices where insects pass. They do not build broad webs; silk would gather dust and fail in the dry heat. Instead they lay sparse lines across narrow routes, feeling each tremor through their feet.

The silver-gray moths belong to the chamber more mysteriously than anything else. Their wings look powdered with volcanic ash. They rest in the coolest upper folds during the day, nearly indistinguishable from mineral films, and emerge when the bats have settled. They feed where moisture exists only briefly: on fresh guano, on fungal bloom, on the damp heat of a recently returned animal’s breath. Their entire life may take place between one nightly departure and the next return of the colony.

Even smaller lives occupy the boundaries between life and stone. Heat-loving bacteria grow in invisible films where gas condenses for seconds at a time. They feed on sulfur compounds, iron, traces of carbon carried in on wings and feet. They make no meadow, no soft green covering. They leave stains: orange, black, dull yellow, a faint greasy iridescence on rock. In a place with no water to speak of, they survive on almost-water, on vapor, on the minute wetness of breath and decay.

And perhaps there are things that live only because the mountain is young.

Pale larvae in the warmest cracks, their bodies translucent enough to show the dark line of their gut. Minute flies whose adults are never noticed among the bats. Wormlike scavengers hardened against desiccation, waking when a fresh fall of waste changes the chemistry of the floor. A species of beetle that cannot leave the fissure now because it has adapted to its furnace air too completely. Creatures without names, or with names in some collector’s notebook far below, living at the edge of what should be survivable.

Over all of them lives the mountain itself.

Not alive as the bats are alive, nor as beetles, moths, mites, or bacteria are alive. But it behaves like an enormous buried animal: breathing hot gases through tight throats, flexing as it cools, groaning in its sleep. Its heat keeps the bats from freezing at altitude. Its crack gives them shelter. Its slow chemical exhalations select which insects can endure. Its glassy ceiling holds their claws. Its contraction will eventually crush or abandon them.

The colony lives inside a body that is still deciding whether it can keep being a shelter.

For now, the bats make it a home. The insects make it a world. The bacteria make use of every trace. And the mountain, indifferent and unfinished, keeps making the conditions under which all of them can exist.
