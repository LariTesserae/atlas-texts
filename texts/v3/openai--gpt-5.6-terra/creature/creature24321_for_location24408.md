What lives there is not an animal, not a people, and not quite a forest.

The country itself lives there.

Not metaphorically. Its towers are not houses made by plants; they are the plants in a temporary architectural phase. Its walls are not boundaries around a society; they are the outward-thickened skins of things feeding, drinking, storing pressure, shedding old tissue, and seeking light. A bridge is a root system that has mistaken—or discovered—that the shortest route to water lies across empty air. A cistern is a leaf that has grown too large to remain merely a leaf. An avenue is a place where growth has repeatedly declined to happen, maintained open by the flooding and drying patterns of the larger body.

There may be no individual resident at all.

A rain chimney can be cut away, split by heat, drowned in its own intake, and still the country goes on using it. Its square trunk softens; its interior fills with hanging roots; its walls unzip and collapse into wet ribbons. Soon the fallen material becomes pavement, embankment, or the foundation for a new tower. What was once a vertical organ becomes horizontal. Nothing is discarded. Dead tissue remains a citizen of the place for decades: pale structural lattices, hard corklike spans, blackened retaining plates under new green growth. The dead are not absent there. They are simply carrying weight.

The smallest things that live there may be the water-bearing seams between the blue-green pads. They look black from above, but each seam is lined with root hairs, transparent bladders, and thread-thin red shoots that open only after rain. They do not drink the water exactly. They test it. They draw minerals from it, adjust its acidity, pass it beneath the pavement in pulses. A seam that has been quiet for months may suddenly brighten with fine white fibers and pull a whole field downhill by a few inches.

The immense forms seem separate from one another, but beneath the plateau they are braided together.

There are root languages too slow for eyes. A reservoir growing full changes the tension of distant walls. A tower receiving too much wind stiffens a ridge several miles away. When one of the lifted polygonal pads settles, water in remote troughs alters course. The country has no center, but it has awareness of pressure. It notices weight. It notices drought in a far terrace. It notices a damaged buttress and begins, over weeks, to send pale exploratory roots into the air beneath it.

This is perhaps the nearest thing it has to movement: not walking, not hunting, not migration, but reallocation. The place moves its strength from one form to another.

In the hot hours, some of the tower tops uncurl toward one another across gaps too wide for their stems to cross. Their fronds overlap in the wind. Moisture passes between them as vapor; pollen, if it exists, passes invisibly or not at all. The contact may be reproductive. It may be merely structural—a way of comparing humidity, temperature, and sky. By morning, a tower that was alone at dusk may have grown a pale aerial brace toward its neighbor, delicate as a vein beneath skin.

There are chambers in the larger root masses where the living tissue becomes stiller, denser, and stranger. They are warm, wet cavities surrounded by layered membranes. Water enters them but does not leave in the same form. Minerals accumulate there in pearly deposits. Sap darkens. Fibers thicken into woody knots. Perhaps these are nurseries. Perhaps they are memories: places where the country stores old stresses, old rain patterns, the direction of winds that have not blown for years. A person entering one would not find seeds arranged in rows or anything recognizable as a heart. Only walls gently contracting, a floor that gives underfoot, and the sound of distant water translated through miles of tissue into a slow internal knocking.

At dusk, the flowers are the closest the country comes to faces.

They open from walls and parapets, exposing pale interiors striped with wet red veins. They do not wait for pollinators. Their cups fill with rain, overflow, close, and rot. Sometimes a flower folds shut around its own collected water and remains swollen for days, becoming a temporary reservoir. Sometimes it opens downward and releases its stored rain in a clean vertical stream over the edge of a terrace. The blossoms do not seem to display themselves for anyone. They are openings in a process too large to require an observer.

If the country has offspring, they are not small.

They begin as irregularities in a field: one polygonal pad refusing to settle, one water seam deepening until it becomes a black line, one bright shoot rising from the center of a mossy court. Years later, perhaps, that disturbance will be a rain chimney, a suspended garden, a new shelf extending into cloud. The country reproduces by changing the shape of its own body until some portion becomes capable of continuing without the rest.

And if there is a mind in it, it is not located in the towers, nor in the roots, nor in the shimmering wet fields. It is in the continuous negotiation among them: the thousands of small decisions of tissue and water, closing and opening, swelling and draining, bracing and releasing.

No one governs the high plateau. No one waits behind its walls.

But it is occupied completely—by growth that has become infrastructure, by water that has become circulation, by dead fiber that continues to serve the living, and by a vast green intelligence too dispersed to recognize itself as alone.
