What lives in the basin is not one kingdom of creatures but a single immense habit of living.

Nothing there is solitary in the way creatures elsewhere are solitary. Every body is a corridor, a shelter, a moving feeding ground, a nursery for something smaller. The broad-backed reed grazers carry gardens in their hides: green films of algae in the folds of their necks, pale ticklike insects under the plates along their spines, little sucker-mouthed fish in the wet creases behind their forelegs. When they lie down in the shallows, beetles climb onto them. When they stand, threads of water and larvae spill from their bellies. Their slow migrations through the mud are not migrations so much as the circulation of a living landscape.

The basin’s large animals are all unfinished-looking, as though their forms, like the land’s, have not quite made their final decisions.

There are low, wide grazers with six or eight soft-jointed legs, their backs armored in overlapping leathery pads. Their mouths are not mouths so much as broad, rasping shelves beneath their heads, used to comb reeds, moss, and the tender skins from floating plants. They feed without hurry, accompanied by clouds of tiny winged things that drink the moisture from their eyes. Their young are born nearly transparent and spend their first weeks submerged among root mats, breathing through fringed gills before the heavier adult lungs develop.

There are pool-dwellers with blunt skulls and luminous throats, each as large as a canoe. They remain almost entirely hidden beneath the green water, but their eyes are jeweled and lidless above the surface, and their throat-sacs expand at dusk—or what passes for dusk—in slow colored pulses. Their voices are less calls than pressure. A person standing close to one feels the note in the teeth, the ribs, the mud beneath the feet. The smaller animals seem to understand these sounds. Insects still. Birds reposition themselves. The reeds bend in the direction of the vibration, though there is no wind.

Some of these pool-dwellers are old enough to have entire ecologies living on them. Shells grow on their flanks. Fine grass sprouts in the hollows of their backs. A small, long-beaked bird follows each one, plucking parasites from the folds around its eyes. When one rises from a pool and crosses a mudflat, it carries water with it in sheets. Fish wriggle briefly in its footprints. Tadpoles gather where its belly has pressed the mud smooth.

The striped hunters in the branches are not truly catlike, though from a distance they seem to be: long bodies, heavy shoulders, silent padded feet, dark striping broken by soft bands of moss-green. Up close, their faces are too flat and their eyes too numerous. They have a pair of large forward-facing eyes and several smaller ones embedded farther back along the skull, each protected by a transparent membrane that clouds and clears. They do not stalk with the taut hunger of a predator from a harsher place. They wait. Their patience is not discipline but abundance. So many things pass beneath them, and so few places are empty, that eventually something suitable will simply enter the space between their paws.

They feed gently, almost ceremonially. A snake, a wader, a climbing grazer-pup: each is caught in a quick blur and carried upward into the leaves. Then the hunter settles, and the nearby canopy quiets not from terror but from recognition. Afterward, scraps fall. The scraps are received by beetles, ants, frogs, fungi, and the small pale lizards that live in the bark. Nothing reaches the mud unchanged.

The snakes are many, though they are seldom seen whole. Most resemble vines until they move. Some are thin as cords and patterned in leaf-shadows; others have broad, wet-looking heads and bodies furred with tiny backward-pointing scales. They hang beneath branches where bird nests cluster, waiting not for chicks alone but for all the smaller life attracted to a nest: mites, scavenging beetles, egg-thieves, nesting birds returning with food. A snake in the basin can feed from a single branch for days without changing its place.

There are insects so numerous that they have ceased to seem individual.

The black beetles in the mud are the basin’s quiet architects. Their shells reflect the cloud-light in green and violet, and their feet leave delicate stitched patterns wherever they go. They tunnel around roots, carry spores beneath their wing cases, and pile tiny turrets of mineral-rich mud around the bases of plants. Many flowers will not open until beetles have walked over their closed petals. The flowers recognize the weight and chemical shine of those feet; then their waxy cups loosen, exposing nectar thick enough to hold a small fly without drowning it.

The pale jointed things beneath the leaves are not insects at all, or not entirely. They fold themselves into wet pearls when disturbed. In stillness, they open into delicate many-legged fans and graze the invisible films growing on leaf surfaces. Their bodies collect moisture from the air. When they die, their shells soften rather than decay, becoming a clear nutrient jelly that roots drink through their skins.

The red runners in the mud travel in temporary rivers. Each is no longer than a finger joint, with a bright segmented body and a dark wet head. They move where heat rises closest to the surface. Their swarms are not destructive. They clean. Fallen fruit, dead skin, shed feathers, drowned insects, the softened rind of a fungal cap: all disappear beneath their passing. But they also carry seeds, eggs, and spores in the grooves of their bodies. Wherever a red river goes, new things sprout afterward.

The birds are perhaps the loudest citizens, and the least settled in form. Some have feathers, though the feathers are greasy and hairlike, adapted to the wet air. Some have broad, membranous wings stretched between long finger-bones. Some glide only briefly before climbing again through vines and branches with hooked feet. Their colors are dark in the dimness: oil-blue, black-purple, brown-red, green like bruised leaves. Yet when they turn their throats upward, flashes of impossible brightness appear—a sudden yellow pulse, a white crescent, a red mouth held open in song.

Many do not make nests. The basin offers too many sheltered folds, too much rot, too much living architecture. They lay eggs inside curled leaves, in the hollowed crowns of fungi, beneath the soft armor plates discarded by grazers. A bird may return to the same tree for generations, while the tree itself grows around the old nesting chambers until its trunk is full of bones, shells, feathers, and sleeping insects.

There are also things that do not fit easily into animal categories.

The vines are watchful in a diffuse, vegetable way. They respond to weight, warmth, sweat, and breath. Some descend from the canopy and root again in the mud, making loops through which animals pass. Over time, a loop that is frequently used as a path becomes broader, smoother, less sticky; a loop that is avoided tightens and fills with thorns. There are vine species that borrow the movement of animals: they cling lightly to a grazer’s hide, extend leaves from its back, and drop away only when they have crossed into a patch of ground the parent vine has not yet reached.

The white-stemmed plants among the black stones are especially strange. Their leaves close around insects with great care, sealing them in soft pale chambers. Inside, the insect is held in warmth and perfume. Pollen gathers over its legs, its wings, its eyes. After a time the leaf opens, and the insect leaves dazed but unharmed, carrying the plant’s dust to another knob of black stone. Some insects appear to seek this enclosure. They crawl deliberately into the closing leaves, remain inside longer than necessary, and emerge slow-winged and glittering.

No one knows whether the black knobs are stones, eggs, roots, or organs of the ground itself.

Small creatures gather around them in patterns too regular to be accidental. The frogs arrange themselves in rings. The reeds bow outward. Insects settle on the knobs but do not crawl across them. At certain intervals—not marked by day or night, since neither changes—the knobs exhale a faint warmth and a mineral scent, like wet iron struck with lightning. Then the basin’s deepest sounds soften. The pool-dwellers stop calling. The birds lower their wings. Even the perpetual buzz seems to gather itself into a single low note.

In those moments, the whole basin appears to listen.

Perhaps there is something beneath the mud: not a monster buried in sleep, but a slower life whose body is the basin’s unfinished shape. The warm ground may be its metabolism. The persistent cloud vault may be its breath caught overhead. The roots may be its nerves, passing pressure and sweetness and alarm from one wet patch of earth to another. The stone knobs may be the nearest parts of it, rising through the skin of the world because it has not yet learned, or has never needed, to remain fully underground.

If this is so, the creatures above do not worship it. They do not fear it. They inhabit it as fish inhabit water and birds inhabit air: without distinction between their own lives and the condition that makes life possible.

The basin contains hunters, grazers, parasites, scavengers, pollinators, singers, burrowers, sleepers, swimmers, and things that spend their whole existence attached to the underside of a single leaf. But none of them live *in* a wilderness that stands apart from them.

They are the wilderness’s innumerable small expressions.

They are its pulse made visible: feather, shell, throat, root, eye, mud, egg, wing, and warm breathing flesh—each one feeding, sheltering, carrying, or becoming another in a world too full to permit an edge.
