What lives there is not one remnant people, but a treaty of survivors.

The people call themselves, in the closest rendering, the **Near Ones**. Not because they are near the surface—few of them think much about the surface—but because nearness is the measure by which they understand life. Near enough to hear another person over the roof-roar. Near enough to know the route home by the feel of a wall. Near enough to the fern beds, the animal pens, the fungus bands, the warm stone, the breathing galleries. Anything beyond the range of use, memory, or touch is simply *far*, and far is nearly the same thing as dead.

They are human, but the city has made its revisions.

Their eyes are large without being monstrous, their pupils quick to widen in the changing gray. Many are born with irises so pale that, in fungal light, they seem almost blank: pearl, smoke, diluted blue. Their sight is good at the middle distances of the basin, where a person is a moving dark shape against a dim wall, but they do not trust sight alone. Their ears are finely tuned to direction and alteration. A child learns to distinguish the ordinary moan of the Bell Steps from the note it makes when a burrowing cow has blocked a lower vent; the ordinary insect-rattle of the Court from the subtle silence that means a fox pack is moving through.

They speak little across open ground. Their language has become compact, physical, and close-held. Much is spoken with fingertips: two taps at the wrist for family, knuckles brushed together for agreement, a thumb drawn down the palm for a path descending. In crowded places they use cords braided with ridges, beads, knots, and small bone plates. A message can be passed from hand to hand through a market without a word being audible beneath the bats.

Their spoken speech is not silent, only economical. It is built to survive noise. Words are low, clipped, heavily shaped at the lips. Names often contain a hum, a click of the tongue, or a breath against the teeth—sounds that can be felt by someone standing close. Public speeches, when they must happen, are performed from stone platforms where the wind has a known behavior. The speaker does not try to overcome the city. Instead, listeners press palms to the walls, to the steps, to long taut ropes stretched along the terrace. The speech travels partly as vibration. A good speaker is one whose words can be understood through the soles of the feet.

They do not keep fires, except in sealed kiln chambers opened only for rare work: hardening clay, firing a new knife-edge from mineral-rich stone, burning a body when disease makes burial unsafe. Ordinary warmth comes from bodies, fur, layered cloth, fermentation pits, and rooms built against the low roof where the stone holds the dayless heat. Their houses are not homes in the surface sense. A household may occupy three old chambers joined by curtains of woven grass: one for sleeping in close stacks, one for food and tools, one for animals too young or injured to remain in the yards.

They cultivate less than they tend.

The dark ferns are harvested in careful strips, never cleared. Their youngest curls are bitter but nourishing once dried and pounded. The grasses provide seed, bedding, and fiber. The pale shrubs yield waxy leaves used as wrappers, salves, and water-sparing poultices. The low white trees are revered not for fruit—though some produce hard, sour pods—but for their roots, which find the deep clay. Around the oldest trees, people dig narrow shaft-gardens and lower woven baskets into cool soil where blind beetles, root grubs, and pale edible molds can be raised.

Their most precious crop is not plant or animal but a faint crust that grows on certain sheltered stones: a blue-green lichen, almost invisible until scraped. It is rich in salts and takes decades to return after harvest. Every family has a licensed wall, an inherited patch of stone, and children are taught never to take lichen in anger. To strip another household’s wall is worse than stealing meat. It is theft from their grandchildren.

The bats are not domesticated, but they are managed.

Above the warmward districts, the Near Ones have built tall lattices of bone, reed, and old rope beneath the ceiling cracks. These offer roosting places and guide droppings into covered channels. Bat guano is gathered for gardens, fermentation, medicinal salts, and the breeding of the fat white larvae that form a major part of the diet. During certain seasons, when the upper colonies swell and young bats fall from the roosts, people collect them gently in nets. Most are returned. A small number are dried whole, their wings folded around their bodies like black leaves.

The cave birds are more intimate companions. They nest above the terraces and return, in loose generations, to the same ledges. The people take eggs according to an old count, never from the lowest nests and never after the first deep-cold condensation. They build white shelves of bone and plaster near their homes, and some birds have learned to use them. At dusk, the birds descend in swarming, screaming folds; children stand beneath with padded hoods and baskets, laughing soundlessly in the tumult.

The blind deer are not truly tame. They are courted.

A herd may wander freely through three or four districts, sleeping in old bathhouses or under colonnades, returning each year to the same fern gardens. The people leave mineral cakes near the paths and hang soft rattles from doorways to keep the animals from entering dangerous ruins. Deer are slaughtered only after a long ceremony of contact: the animal is held, fed, and sung to through the chest. Every usable part is taken. Antler becomes tool, needle, wedge, comb, flute, and structural peg. Hide becomes mantle, sling, drum-skin, water-tight wrapping. The pale sinew cords that run along household walls are often deer sinew, polished by decades of hands.

The thick-furred cattle are closer to livestock, though they too retain a stubborn independence. They sleep in former storage halls where old stone bins have been converted into bedding pits. Their breath makes enough warmth to keep certain fungi alive through the basin’s coldest cycle. They give little milk, but what they give is dense and blue-white, souring almost immediately. It is made into a sharp cheese that can last for years in sealed pottery. Cattle dung, dried and powdered, feeds insects. Insects feed people, birds, fishless fermentation vats, and the small blind lizards kept in wall-cages to consume pests.

There are creatures the Near Ones do not claim.

The long-bodied foxes are called **the Borrowers**, because they enter any house that has been carelessly opened. They carry off eggs, infants of deer, strings of dried fern, sleeping bats, sometimes a finger from a person who has dozed too close to the ground. Yet killing a fox is avoided. They clear the city of weak animals, uncover buried food caches, and are thought to know routes through the lower galleries that no human has survived. A fox seen walking deliberately along a clear avenue is an omen that someone below has died—or is about to return.

There are also things in the deep galleries.

The people do not deny them, but neither do they describe them in the same way twice.

Some say there are immense blind salamanders in the lower country, white as cut stone and slow as thought, feeding on roots and beetle swarms beneath the dust. Others say the scraping heard far below is only the movement of rock under old pressure. There are stories of herds of something large passing through chambers where no light grows: animals with broad feet, wet mouths, and skins that drink sound. There are stories of people who followed the cold exhalation beyond the Long Throat and returned years later with their hair gone white, carrying unfamiliar seeds in the hems of their clothes.

Most people do not go down.

Those who do are a distinct profession: vent-walkers, corpse-seekers, mineral gatherers, scouts for new fungus blooms. They are lean people with scarred mouths and extraordinary calm. They travel in threes, tied by a single long cord, carrying little bells that cannot be heard in the general roar but can be felt through the rope. Their work is necessary because the lower chambers provide salts, rare clays, certain insects, and sometimes the lost: a child following a deer, a cattle keeper caught in a rooffall, an old person who has gone walking with no intention of returning.

The dead remain within the city.

Burial rooms exist beneath many houses, but burial is not immediate. A body is laid in a dry chamber lined with fern ash and mineral salt until the flesh has given up what it can. Then the bones are cleaned, wrapped, and carried to communal shelves cut high into the limestone. The skull is usually retained by the family for one generation. It may sit in a niche near the sleeping room, rubbed smooth by touch, its eye sockets filled with dried grass, shells, beads, or tiny folded strips of fungus cloth bearing the names of descendants.

After enough years, the skull joins the others in the deep ancestral halls.

These halls are among the oldest spaces still kept clean. Their walls are fitted with thousands of faces, not carved, but natural: skulls placed in stone sockets and covered in a translucent mineral glaze. In the fungal light they appear to emerge and recede as one walks past. Children are taken there only after they have learned the city’s principal sounds. They stand in the dark among the dead while an elder asks them to identify the distant hum of their own district. When they can do so, they are considered old enough to travel alone between the neighborhoods.

The Near Ones believe the city is alive, though not in the simple way of an animal or god.

It has lungs: the galleries.

It has skin: the ceiling fungus.

It has veins: the root systems under paving.

It has memory: dust, which keeps every passing shape for a little while.

It has appetite: the slow settling of walls, the constant taking-in of rooms, courtyards, names, and bones.

They do not think the city protects them. Protection is a surface idea, they say. The city contains them. Sometimes containing is kind. Sometimes it is not.

There are perhaps several thousand people in the basin, scattered among districts that have developed their own habits. The people of the Hissing Market wear soft leather masks over their mouths because their streets are full of bitter dust. The Bell Steps folk are known for their stonework and their elaborate ankle ornaments, which let them communicate by vibration on the broad terraces. In the Court of Insects, houses are built with raised sleeping platforms and every child learns to identify edible larvae before they learn the names of distant relatives. The Long Throat settlements keep their doors low and heavy, weighted against the permanent cold air, and their inhabitants tend to be taller, quieter, and more superstitious than the rest.

They trade constantly, though trade looks like migration to an outsider. A procession of cattle and covered baskets may move down an avenue for days. Fern meal goes toward the terraces; bird eggs and mineral glaze come back. A family may owe another family twenty lengths of rope, a cured hide, three jars of beetle oil, and the labor of two adults for one roof-repair season. Nothing is written in the surviving marks of the old city; those marks belong to people no one remembers. Debts are remembered through song patterns tapped on beams, through knotted cords, through witnesses whose role is to carry agreements in their bodies.

Their history is shallow in one direction and immense in another.

They know there was once a country above. They know roads came down from it. Some old people say their ancestors arrived from that country when the upper world was still green and loud with weather. Others insist the Near Ones were born below and the road-builders were merely visitors who came seeking them. Neither claim can be proven, and most find the question unimportant.

The surface is called **the Unroofed Place**.

It is spoken of as a realm of dangerous abundance: water falling from the air, plants that grow too quickly, fires that spread without permission, distances with no walls around them. Children are frightened with tales of its open sky. There is no ceiling to hold the light, they are told. The brightness can enter your eyes and take your name away. The wind comes in sudden attacks. The ground itself can turn to mud.

A few young people, in every generation, try to find the old roads.

Some return after only a day or two, coughing, blinded by unfamiliar daylight leaking through cracks, terrified by the upward slope. Some do not return. Their names are not removed from family cords; that would be too final. Instead, a short loose end is left unknotted, hanging among the household records. A person gone toward the Unroofed Place is considered neither fully alive nor properly dead. They have become far.

At the center terrace, when the ceiling glow enters its dimmest phase and the birds have quieted for a few hours, the Near Ones gather.

Not everyone. The city is too large and labor is too constant. But enough people come to fill the broad steps with pale mantles and animal-smell and the small movements of hands. The cattle shift in the lower courts. Deer watch from the fern margins. Bats boil and turn far above, invisible except when their wings cross a bright fungal band.

There, without flame or banner or inscription, the inhabitants renew the work that keeps them from becoming ruins themselves.

They exchange children between households. They settle disputes over gardens and roosts. They name new pathways around fallen walls. They decide which districts can spare food for a roof-collapse, which old chamber should be abandoned, which vent-walkers will go down into the cold galleries. They remember the dead. They listen for changes in the city’s voice.

And occasionally, in the great noise, all of them hear the same new sound: a note in a tower that has not sounded before, a hush where insects should be, a deep impact under the streets.

Then the whole gathering becomes still—not silent, because silence does not exist there, but still in the way animals become still.

They place hands on the stone.

They wait to feel what, beneath them, has shifted.
