What lives in the high country is not one people, nor one kind of beast, but an old agreement among things that eat, flee, root, rot, hunt, bloom, and remember.

The great browsers are perhaps its most visible citizens. They are tall at the shoulder and loose-jointed, with necks made for reaching into thorn and leaf-vault, and broad hooves that do not sink deeply into the wet ground. Their coats are usually the colors of the country: russet, peat-black, damp gray, the dull gold of dead grass. In mist they appear first as moving absences, tall backs passing above the fern. Their noses are black and soft, always shining. They travel in family bands rather than great disciplined herds: mothers, young, old females, a few scarred males tolerated at the edges. Their young are born long-legged and trembling, then vanish into grass that is taller than their ears.

Where the browsers feed, smaller lives gather. Certain birds follow them to take insects shaken from the grasses. Burrowing things use their hoofprints as pools after rain. Root-mice and striped peat-rats steal fallen leaves from their mouths. The people watch the shape of a browsing herd from afar. A herd moving calmly means the open slope is open. A herd bunched tight and staring downhill means something has begun to hunt.

The woolly grazers keep to colder ground and windward shelves. They are not sheep exactly, though from a distance they can be mistaken for pale boulders clustered beneath a ridge. Their wool grows in long wet ropes, carrying seeds, burrs, and sometimes whole sprays of tiny flowers. Their horns curl low and outward, useful for digging through snow in the rare hard winters and for fighting with a blunt, terrible momentum. They are stupid only in the way mountains are stupid: steady in purpose, difficult to frighten once they have chosen a direction. In the deepest fog, their bells are not metal but hollow stones tied at the throat. They make a low knocking sound as the animals move, and travelers sometimes follow it toward shelter.

There are smaller grazers too: quick, sharp-faced creatures that live in the bracken and emerge at evening in groups. They have pale inner ears and the habit of freezing with one forefoot raised. Their alarm call is a thin bark that travels a long way over wet grass. Children imitate it when they are lost, and adults do not approve of this, because the call can bring ridge-hounds.

The ridge-hounds are not dogs, though some people keep an uneasy companionship with them. They are lean animals with deep ribs, long muzzles, and coats that shift between gray, brown, and lichen-green according to season. Their eyes catch little light; at dusk they seem to have no eyes until they turn their heads. They hunt in packs, but they are not always hungry. Much of their time is spent listening at burrows, sleeping in the lee of stones, or trailing the old paths of large beasts simply to know who has crossed them.

A lone ridge-hound is usually harmless if given space. A silent pack is not. The people say that if one barks, it wants you to leave; if none bark, they have already decided what you are.

There are larger predators, few enough that most people see them only in signs: a broad depression in wet grass, a tree trunk peeled by claws, the sudden disappearance of bird sound from a hollow. These are the undergrass cats, low-bodied and immensely muscled, patterned in shadowy bars that resemble wet roots beneath shallow water. They do not climb trees often, because the braided thorn trees are too dense even for them. Instead they use the old animal tunnels beneath the grasslands, moving unseen just below the level of the stems. A person may walk for an hour with one keeping pace ten yards away and never know it.

When an undergrass cat kills, it drags its prey into a leaf-vault or beneath an overturned root mass. The scavengers arrive quickly after: black-necked carrion birds with white wing patches; beetles that come up from the soil; little red-faced foxes that wait at the edge of safety. Nothing remains long on the plateau. Bone is cracked, hide is pulled apart for nest material, blood darkens the moss, and within days green things begin to rise through the disturbed earth.

The birds are too numerous for any one name to hold them.

There are the bright ground birds, broad and heavy in the body, with plumes of cobalt, vermilion, yellow, and oily green. They live in fern beds and burst upward with alarming violence, making newcomers stumble or drop what they carry. During the breeding season, the males stand on stones and inflate the skin of their throats until their calls become deep enough to be felt through the soles of the feet.

There are ridge-whistlers, small ash-colored birds whose notes travel farther than their bodies suggest. Families use their calls as signals between slopes. A certain descending sequence means water found; another means fog; another, rarely heard except in old songs, means that someone has died far from camp.

There are nectar-feeders with needle beaks and iridescent throats, almost too bright for the muted country. They hover around the wet red flowers that bloom in sheltered gullies. They are said to carry fire in their chests. Children are taught never to catch one, not because it is forbidden exactly, but because a caught nectar-feeder will beat itself to death against the hand, and its tiny heart is considered an unlucky thing to feel stop.

Above all of these circle the broad-winged eaters of the dead. Their feathers are dark except beneath, where the wings flash pale when they bank. They are patient, ugly birds, bald around the face, with a grave attentiveness that makes people uncomfortable. Yet they are welcome near a settlement. Their arrival means waste will not linger, sickness will not gather, and the dead—animal or human—will not be left entirely alone.

In the wet hollows live the voices of night: frogs swollen with color, moss-skinned salamanders, blind pale eels in the peat pools. Some frogs have calls like struck pottery; some trill in overlapping waves until the air seems threaded with wire. The people believe each spring has its own frog-song and that a person who has traveled too long can recognize home by it before seeing any familiar stone.

Insects are everywhere, carrying the true labor of the land. Metallic beetles tunnel through fallen wood. Soft-winged moths rise in pale clouds from the grass after rain. Midges gather over pools in shifting columns. There are leaf-cutter ants beneath the thorn groves, and the ground above their nests is always warmer than the surrounding soil. There are fat green grubs inside certain root bulbs, prized as food in the lean season. There are stinging flies whose bite causes fever dreams, and small blue flies that settle on wounds but eat only dead flesh, leaving the living tissue clean. Those are carefully welcomed by healers.

The people themselves are a sparse animal in this abundance.

They do not call themselves masters of the plateau. In many of their languages, there is no single word that means wilderness, because the land beyond a shelter is not empty or unmanaged; it is simply occupied by those whose customs are not human. They speak instead of places with particular tempers: the slope where the woolly ones lamb, the black-water hollow, the ridge that keeps thunder, the thorn-vault where nothing should be taken, the grass sea that closes behind a traveler.

They are usually dark-haired and weather-marked, wrapped in layered hide, woven grass cloth, and felted wool taken from the high grazers after shedding. Their clothing is made to resist wet more than cold. A hood, a shoulder cape, a water-shedding skirt of braided reeds, soft boots patched again and again: these matter more than ornament. What ornament they wear is portable—bone beads, polished seed cases, bird-feather knots, copper rings inherited from dead kin. Many carry small bells made of shell, wood, or hollow stone, not to announce themselves but to speak gently to the grass and warn certain animals that a human body is coming.

Their camps are often family camps, though family includes more than blood. An old hunter may belong to the household that fed him after an injury. A child may be raised among the people who happened to find her after a fog took her parents. A woman who knows herbs may be claimed in kinship by half a dozen scattered shelters because everyone has needed her at some point. Descent is remembered, but obligation is remembered more strongly.

They move according to the animal year.

When the browsing herds rise toward the high shelves, people follow at a distance, gathering shed hair, dropped antlers, edible roots exposed by hoofwork, and the mushrooms that appear in churned soil. When the grazers descend, families go to the sheltered valleys to repair roofs, smoke meat, and trade. During the great flowering of the high grass, certain camps remain still for weeks, because the land is busy with nesting birds and new young, and to travel carelessly then is considered a form of vandalism.

They hunt, but hunting is not casual. A kill requires a reason named aloud: hunger, hide, bone, danger, ceremony, a promise made to the sick. A hunter who takes an animal merely because it was easy is spoken of with distrust. The meat may still be eaten—waste would be worse—but that person will not be given first choice of the next weapon or the next sheltered sleeping place.

They know the plateau by pressure, scent, and sound as much as by sight. They can tell which animal used a trail by the bent direction of grass stems. They can find water by noticing a particular kind of fly at dusk. They can hear, under wind and birdsong, whether a distant herd is feeding or fleeing. Children learn to sleep through ordinary calls and wake at the sound of the one thing that does not belong.

Among them are path-keepers, people who remember routes no longer visible. They know where a ridge can be crossed when fog has erased every landmark, where the old ground is firm beneath a flowering bog, where a buried shelter can be opened after two generations. Some say these people do not memorize paths so much as enter into conversation with them. They pause at certain stones, touch certain roots, whistle into certain folds of land. The country replies, in its manner: water moves, birds rise, grass leans, fog thins.

There are also listeners, usually older people, though not always. They tend the customs around the responsive plants. They do not claim the grasses think as humans think. That would be foolish, they say. But they know that root-mats carry news, that leaf-vaults close around repeated disturbance, that a slope trodden in anger feels different beneath the foot from one crossed in need. They advise camps where to walk, where not to cut, which old trail must be allowed to vanish.

A listener may spend a morning sitting beside a patch of flowering grass, saying nothing. This is not prayer in the formal sense. It is closer to waiting for a shy animal to decide whether one is safe.

Their dead are not buried deeply. Deep ground belongs to water, roots, and sleeping things. The dead are placed instead in high stone cradles, wrapped in woven grass and wool, with a little food, a tool, and something that will make a familiar sound: a flute, a seed rattle, a stone bell. Scavenger birds are allowed to come. Bones are gathered later if they can be found, cleaned by weather, and set into the walls of family shelters or hung near doorways. In this way the dead continue to hear the camp and the living continue to touch them when entering from rain.

There are stories of older inhabitants still.

Not gods exactly, and not beasts that can be pointed to in daylight. Some speak of the Deep Grazer, a creature so large that whole hillsides are said to be the folded hide of its back. It moves only once in an age, and when it does, springs change course, stones crack, and grass grows differently for a generation. Others speak of the Rooted Ones: slow presences in the oldest braided groves, neither tree nor animal, which remember every cut ever made in their wood. A hunter may offer them a lock of hair before taking even a dead branch.

Most people do not speak of such things with certainty. Certainty is for weathered stone, for tracks in mud, for the need to keep a fire dry. The old presences belong to another category: not proven, not dismissed, and best treated politely.

So what lives there is a dense republic of hungers and habits.

The hounds own the dusk trails. The frogs own the wet dark. The bright ground birds own the first shock of morning. The huge browsers own the open slopes by weight alone. Roots own everything eventually. Humans possess only their fires, their carried songs, their patched shelters, and the brief permissions granted by season.

Even those permissions are not permanent.

If a family leaves a camp too long, the grass takes the paths. Moss enters the hearth stones. A thorn tree leans over the roof. Beetles move into the drying racks. In a few years, only a smooth stone, a buried bone, or the faint smell of old smoke after rain may show that people ever rested there.

The plateau does not erase them out of cruelty.

It simply has so much life of its own that no life, human or otherwise, can remain separate from it for very long.
