What lives there is not one thing.

The first answer is: a municipal organism, old enough to have habits.

It lives in the root roads, in the swollen walls around the old steel frames, in the pressure changes that make doors open and close. It lives as sap and current at once. A tower is not merely grown, though it grows; it has circulation schedules, dormancies, appetites, repairs. The factories are not merely factories, though they manufacture. Their harvesting arms and furnace controls still follow instructions written by people long dead, but the timber they cut remembers the shape of the halls that raised it. The rails are fed, cleared, and warmed by systems whose original purpose has become difficult to separate from their present need.

The city may not think in language. If it thinks, it may think in gradients: wetness here, strain there, too much rust around a joint, insufficient potassium in a northern wall, a familiar sequence of footfalls crossing a root bridge at dawn.

It knows its inhabitants by their heat.

The people who remain are called, in some districts, the Tenders. Elsewhere they are simply citizens, workers, residents, the shift. They do not agree that the city is alive in any meaningful way. They would say that it is managed. They would say this with the patience of people accustomed to outsiders using the wrong word for everything.

They are mountain people: narrow-shouldered or broad-backed according to family, pale from indoor work or darkened by the reflected green of the plantworks, always dressed for dampness. Their coats are waxed fiber, their gloves resin-treated, their boots fitted with flexible cleats for walking on bark, wet root, and soft paving. Most carry knives, pruning hooks, voltage testers, and little instruments that listen through walls.

Children learn to identify neighborhoods by taste.

The southern freight district tastes faintly of copper when rain comes. The old civic terraces have a dusting of bitter yellow pollen every third spring. In the northern housing towers, the water gathered from the wall taps is sweeter on the upper levels and mineral-heavy below. A child sent for milk or medicine may return by following a warm draft through an unfamiliar stairwell, or by pressing a cheek to a wall and listening for the slow hollow pulse that means the domestic conduits are near.

Some families have lived in the same grown apartment for six or seven generations. They mark height on the interior cambium. They train windows open with careful cuts. They negotiate constantly with the structure: discouraging roots from lifting a kitchen floor, coaxing a ventilation throat away from a bedroom, harvesting edible emergency pith from a wall without compromising its load-bearing layers.

The oldest homes have names inherited from the plants that first made them: Alder House, Red Vein, the Nine-Stem Block, the Quiet Skin. But these names are unreliable. A building may divide. Two adjoining residences may fuse during a wet decade. An address sometimes becomes a description: third warm door after the freight lift, below the blue-spored arch, if the stair is still there.

There are also people who live more deeply within the industrial system than anyone admits.

The graft crews enter the sealed halls where the city produces its structural woods and industrial saps. They wear masks not because the air is poisonous exactly, but because certain pollens make the body dream of roots. Their work is delicate and unpleasant. They cut diseased cambium out of bridge supports. They bind new load-bearing branches into factory roofs. They introduce carefully cultivated fungi to places where rust has begun to outrun the plant tissue. They manage the old chemical gardens, where vats of black water feed stands of translucent reeds that distill solvents, insulation gums, anesthetics, and fuels.

A good grafter can feel, through a knife handle, whether a wall wants to close.

A bad one may leave a corridor permanently blind.

Then there are the rail people: operators, track-clearers, pod sealers, tunnel keepers. Their freight trains move resin blocks, fiber bales, machine parts, food cultures, mineral salts, and sometimes passengers in dim compartments that smell of wet wool and electricity. They know the old route maps but trust them less than the live signal systems embedded in the rails. A route can be technically open while a tunnel has begun to fruit. A platform can be clear while the walls above it are preparing to shed bark in heavy plates. The rail people carry long hooked poles to pull aside vines, and short brass hammers to test whether steel is still steel beneath its green wrapping.

The city’s few public spaces are never as empty as they seem.

In covered courtyards, people cultivate vegetables that have not entirely surrendered their independence: potatoes in insulated troughs, bitter greens under glass, beans trained along copper wire. In old office towers whose walls have become thick and warm, there are schools. The classrooms are lined with cut panels of pale wood, and the children are taught mathematics, machine maintenance, weather, grafting etiquette, and the dangerous older history: how the city was once made of separate objects.

They find this difficult to imagine.

A building that did not heal.  
A street that did not thicken.  
A roof that was dead material from the moment it was installed.

Some children are afraid of old masonry when they encounter it exposed. They dislike its refusal. Stone does not respond to warmth. Brick cannot be persuaded. Concrete does not alter its grain to admit a pipe or make space for a family. To them, the few bare ruins in the upper districts are uncanny things: hard, square, silent, incapable of forgiveness.

There are people who prefer them.

They live in the older high-slope neighborhoods, where the city’s growth is less vigorous and old industrial architecture still breaks through. They maintain dry rooms in abandoned substations and machine shops. They keep paper maps, mechanical clocks, hand tools, stored seed. Some are practical preservationists; others are said to be waiting for a future in which the city grows too far, draws too much from the valley, and finally closes itself around its inhabitants.

They call themselves Keepers, though the Tenders use the name with varying degrees of respect.

The Keepers believe the plant-city is not a single organism but an accumulation: thousands of engineered species, feral tissues, machine routines, fungal webs, human interventions, and accidents layered into one another until the distinction no longer matters. They do not trust its apparent benevolence. A wall that opens for a resident may open because it has learned that resident’s electrical rhythm, not because it recognizes a person. A roof that turns over its leaves before a storm may be protecting itself, not the homes beneath it.

Their argument is ancient and mostly quiet.

Nobody has enough certainty to win it.

The city also contains the altered.

Not many. Perhaps fewer each year. Perhaps more than anyone knows.

There are workers whose skin bears branching green-brown traceries after decades in the sap halls. There are elders with a persistent scent of crushed leaves, whose wounds close with strange clean speed. There are people who claim to hear pressure changes before storms, not as sound but as a tightening in the teeth. A few have taken grafts deliberately: vascular ports grown beneath the collarbone for medical access, heat-exchange filaments in the wrists, small symbiotic tissues that allow a person to sleep safely in cold maintenance chambers.

Most alterations are modest, useful, and denied in public.

A person may have a root-sensitive scar on one palm and still insist they are entirely ordinary.

There are rumors of more complete incorporations.

A maintenance worker lost in a flooding root cellar who later appears at a different station, days older in the face, carrying no lamp, speaking slowly as though translating from a language with no nouns.

A woman in the western works who has not left her grafting loft in thirty years, yet whose instructions arrive wherever the city is pruned, written as pale raised veins on fresh-cut bark.

Figures glimpsed through the translucent walls of closed districts: upright, broad-headed, motionless until a person approaches, after which the wall slowly folds around them and they are gone.

No official registry contains these stories. No one calls them ghosts. Ghosts are a lower-country superstition, involving houses that remain unchanged and dead people who somehow do not become soil.

Here, death is treated differently.

The dead are often given to particular civic uses. A person may choose, in life, to be composted into the retaining gardens above the flood channels, or rendered into nutrient broth for the nursery halls, or buried in one of the slow root crypts beneath their home tower. These are not considered grim fates. The city has always required material. The living require it too.

Yet not all the dead are surrendered.

In some homes, a small section of household wall is kept hollow and dry. Inside are photographs, tools, bones, letters, bits of old wiring, a parent’s work badge, the sealed seed of a plant that no longer exists. The wall grows around these archives but is carefully prevented from consuming them. Every generation opens the chamber, checks for damp, removes roots, repacks the objects, and closes it again.

That may be the most human activity left in the valley: making a place where growth is not allowed.

Other inhabitants are harder to name.

The insects are not ordinary insects, though they may have begun that way. In the warm ducts, glass-winged flies pollinate flowering insulation membranes. Beetles with iron-dark shells clean fungal films from electrical contacts. Tiny moths gather around the streetlight bracts, their wings carrying conductive dust from one lamp cluster to another. The city’s maintenance manuals list some of them as pests, some as components, and some simply as “persistent local phenomena.”

The pale birds in the vents have learned to build nests from stripped copper, hair, root fiber, and machine insulation. Their chicks hatch warm from the stack heat. Their calls imitate warning tones badly enough that crews sometimes stop work to listen. In winter, when the outer slopes are buried in cloud, the birds descend into the city and perch in the old tram wires, looking down through the mist at people walking beneath lamps that have flowered open.

And in the reed fields beyond the built basin, something grazes.

It is large, but no account agrees on its shape. Some say it is a herd animal with a white skull and dark, leaf-strewn flanks. Some say it is a machine left from the construction era, overgrown and still moving on a schedule no one remembers. Some say it is an exposed organ of the greater botanical system, sent outward to test soil, crush unwanted growth, spread seed, or find mineral deposits.

It leaves hoofprints often enough.

The prints are warm for several minutes after they are found.

The people do not hunt it. They do not follow it far. At most, they take measurements from the edge of the reeds and leave small offerings of salt, metal filings, or fruit in shallow bowls. By morning the bowls are usually empty, though no tracks approach them.

There may be others in the closed districts: descendants of workers who adapted to spaces too wet, too dark, too warm for ordinary habitation; feral maintenance colonies living among the upper girders; communities whose doors no longer recognize anyone from outside. During certain rains, lights appear deep in the northern overgrowth, arranged in straight rows where no building is visible. Sometimes distant music travels down the drainage channels: not recordings, not radio, but voices keeping time with tools.

The central city insists these are merely night crews.

The night crews do not insist anything.

Ultimately, what lives in the mountain city is a population bound by maintenance.

People maintain the growth. Growth maintains the buildings. Buildings preserve the machines. Machines regulate the water, heat, light, and mineral supply that allow the growth to continue. Rails feed the factories; factories feed the walls; walls house the people who keep the rails moving. Even the abandoned places are not outside this arrangement. They are fallow organs, forgotten chambers, scar tissue, reserve tissue, spaces waiting for some pressure or need to give them purpose again.

The city is not a forest that has swallowed industry.

It is not an industry that has conquered a forest.

It is a long compromise that learned how to reproduce.

And within it live human beings who have become very skilled at calling that compromise home.
