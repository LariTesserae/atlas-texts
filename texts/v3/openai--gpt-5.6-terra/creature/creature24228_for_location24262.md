The first thing that lives there is the noise.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way people say a house has a voice when its beams creak in wind. The under-river has occupied the hollow longer than anything with a name has occupied the fields above it. It fills the chamber, presses into the wood of the shelter, sets the spider’s web trembling, shakes drops loose from the ceiling. It has moods which are not moods, habits which are not intentions. Those who know it can tell what weather has fallen three valleys away by the pitch of its throat.

It does not permit ordinary conversation. Anyone who stays must learn another way of speaking: a hand on a shoulder, a lantern lifted once or twice, a cup set down with care. People who have spent too long in the chamber come out with the look of those who have been standing beside a great engine. Their ears ring in quiet rooms. They sleep badly where there is no noise.

There is, or has been, a keeper of the place.

No one would call him a hermit, because a hermit is imagined to have chosen solitude for a reason he can explain. This man—if it is still the same man people remember—belongs to the hollow as moss belongs to its wet stone. He comes and goes. Sometimes the shelter stands empty for months, door swollen tight, candles reduced to bent white lumps in their bottles. Then smoke is seen at the entrance, not rising in a plume but crawling out low among the brambles. A few days later there are fresh axe marks on the woodpile and a narrow track through the pasture.

He is not old in a way anyone can agree on. The nearest farmers have described him as an old man for forty years. They remember him with a black beard, with gray hair to his shoulders, with a face seamed like peat, with a young man’s back under a rain cape. They remember a woman once, too: broad-shouldered, carrying a basket of roots, her hair tied in a rag. They may be remembering the same person under different weather.

The keeper wears wool that never dries and boots darkened almost to black by wet limestone. He carries a short-handled axe, though he rarely cuts anything large, and a lantern with a tin hood blackened on one side. In his pockets are lengths of string, nails that have outlasted their usefulness, a whetstone, a little packet of salt, and pieces of chalk. The chalk is used to mark the walls near the inner slope—not for direction, but for the water. A line is made after a flood. Another line beside it, dated only by a notch or a shape. The highest marks are old enough to have been softened by dripping water.

He does not try to make the place comfortable.

That is one of the signs that he understands it.

The brazier is used only near the entrance, and never for long. The firewood is mostly for boiling water in a lidded pot, drying a pair of gloves, or making smoke when the air changes in a particular way. He sleeps, if sleeping is what happens there, on the dry shelf behind the fieldstone wall, wrapped in old blankets that smell of wool, clay, and the faint sourness of long damp. He leaves food in tins hung from wire: oatcakes, onions, hard cheese, sometimes a piece of cured meat. Anything softer becomes food for something else.

He knows the hidden water by touch. Standing barefoot on the floor, he can feel when the underground current has shifted its force from one channel to another. He puts his palm against the wall and waits. He watches the roots. When they quiver in a certain pattern, he closes the shelter door and goes out before the rain arrives.

The locals say he keeps the hollow from taking people.

This is not entirely true. The hollow does not want people. Want is too lively a word for it. But there are places near the roaring floor where a person can lose balance, and places where the ground is solid one year and a thin crust above black water the next. The keeper replaces a loose stone in the low wall. He knots a rope to an iron ring set in the rock. He clears the entrance after storms, not enough to make it obvious from the field, only enough that someone who knows where to kneel can get through.

Children are told that he has no ears.

This is because he seldom answers when called to. It is also because the roar has remade his hearing. He hears things other people do not: the ticking of a beetle in rotten wood, the fret of roots against stone, the click of a bat unfolding itself in the ceiling. But spoken words arrive to him broken apart, stripped of their edges. He watches mouths. He watches hands. Outside the hollow, in the ordinary silence of a kitchen or lane, he speaks too loudly, then too softly, then gives up and smiles as if he has remembered something private.

There are smaller inhabitants.

Bats hold the driest fold of the ceiling, where the rock rises just enough to keep their claws from the seep. They are few, and they do not always remain through winter. Their droppings have darkened a ledge beneath them, and tiny pale creatures live in that darkening: mites, beetles, pale thread-legged things that vanish when a lamp is held too close.

The spider above the shelter is older than seems possible. It may not be a single spider. It may be a succession of nearly identical spiders inheriting the same crack and the same trembling web. The keeper never brushes it down. When moths come in from the entrance, confused by a candle’s small light, the web takes them without drama.

There are blind insects in the deeper seams, colorless as flakes of peeled root. There are snails with shells so thin they seem made of old fingernail. In wet weather, a salamander-colored thing appears in the shallow cups along the wall, though no one has caught it and no one agrees whether it has eyes.

The frog is the strangest familiar resident.

It sits in its hand-sized pool facing the blackness, its throat scarcely moving, while the whole hill roars beneath it. It is sometimes there in late autumn and sometimes absent for years. The keeper leaves it alone. Once, perhaps, he built up the rim of its pool with three tiny stones to keep the drip from washing it out. That is the kind of care he is capable of: quiet, practical, and so small that nobody would think to call it kindness.

And beneath all these lives, perhaps, another thing.

Not a beast in the stream. No pale fish with human hands, no drowned shape rolling in the current. The water is too fast and too buried for such stories. But the chamber has gathered an awareness from repetition. Every footstep has been given back as vibration. Every flood mark, every candle flame, every winter root pressing farther through the ceiling has become part of the place’s slow attention.

The keeper may be listening to that attention.

Or he may simply be waiting for the water to change.

There are nights when the hard knocking begins below the floor: stone against stone, again and again, at a rhythm almost regular enough to be a signal. On those nights the shelter remains shut. No lantern moves in the entrance. The bats are still. Even the frog, if it is there, lowers itself into its dark cup until only its eyes show.

By morning there may be nothing altered but a fresh strip of mud on the wall, a fallen slab from the shelter roof, a root newly exposed in the ceiling.

And the keeper, if he is there, will come out into the pasture carrying his lantern unlit. He will stand for a while among the nettles, looking not at the sky but down at the grass over the buried channel, listening through the soles of his boots.
