What lives in the basin is not one population but an arrangement of tolerances.

The people who remain are called, by outsiders, basinfolk, groundhands, shift-born, rootwardens, worksmen—names that suggest an occupation more than a lineage. They use none of them consistently. Most say where in the city they are from, though districts move, merge, sink, and acquire new names faster than maps can keep up. A person might be from Old East Pump, though the pump was relocated thirty years ago; from Nursery Edge, though their apartment now overlooks a freight marsh; from the Third Rise, which was not there when they were born.

They tend to be compact in their habits, if not in their bodies. They keep possessions light, shelves bolted, dishes shallow, furniture on lockable casters. Their doors have wide hinges and adjustable frames. Their children learn to sleep through the low groan of stressed steel and wake at the sound of a pipe changing pitch. They do not ask whether a building is safe. They ask how it is safe: safe from frost, safe from settlement, safe from root pressure, safe until the next municipal inspection, safe if nobody runs the washing machine during a lateral shift.

A basin household has equipment that elsewhere would be considered industrial: hand winches, line levels, masks, flex-seal tape, emergency wedges, a listening cone for walls, waterproof chalk, folding ladders, a tin of bright markers to indicate cracks and changes. Even the poorest apartments keep a small tool roll near the door. It is not unusual for a grandmother to interrupt dinner, take a spirit level from the wall, and decide that the whole table must be moved two centimeters before the soup can be served.

They have become skilled at provisional domesticity. Kitchens are built in modules; beds are slung from ceiling rails; bookshelves hang rather than stand. A room can be dismantled in an afternoon, carried down a stairwell, and rebuilt three blocks away before dark. There are rituals around this. Neighbors come with straps and flasks of hot root broth. Someone plays music from a battery radio. Children are given the task of carrying houseplants, though the plants are usually more resilient than the people handling them. At the end, the old home is not mourned as lost. It is marked, photographed, and added to the long local vocabulary of places that were useful for a while.

Their clothing reflects the climate and the labor without becoming uniform. Thick coats in dark colors, often patched at elbow and shoulder. Waterproof trousers tucked into yellow or orange boots. Wool caps beneath helmet liners. Gloves with fingertips removed for fine work. Almost everyone carries a scarf, a torch, and some form of respirator, whether they need them that day or not. Children wear bright colors—not for cheer, exactly, but so they can be seen among cranes, reeds, vapor, and construction sheeting.

The oldest residents have a peculiar gait. They walk as though every surface might move under them, not timidly, but with an economical readiness. They test thresholds with their heel. They avoid standing beneath unsupported pipe runs. They look up when a bird flock turns abruptly. They pause in doorways during rain, listening to the runoff travel through the building. Outsiders mistake this for superstition. It is more like literacy.

There are surveyors everywhere: municipal ones in reflective coats; private ones hired by insurers, developers, neighborhood associations, and old families who claim a slope behind their building; unofficial ones who work out of workshops and kitchens. They carry folded measuring staffs, handheld vibration readers, chalk reels, soil samplers, and little paper flags in colors too specific for anyone not trained to understand them. Red is not simply danger. Red may mean heat. Or depth. Or pressure from below. Orange may indicate a known shift, or a suspected void, or an area where the city has accepted responsibility but has not yet sent anyone.

The best surveyors are listened to with the seriousness elsewhere reserved for doctors or judges. They can kneel beside a crack, press two fingers to its edge, smell the wetness rising from it, and tell a family whether they should leave tonight or whether it can wait until morning. Some of them claim they can distinguish the movement of roots from the movement of the burrowers. They never explain how.

There are also rootkeepers, who tend the aggressive vegetation not as gardeners so much as negotiators. They prune the black-stemmed vines from support trusses before the plants can turn a walkway into a trellis. They harvest the fast forests and decide which stumps may regrow. They cultivate lichens in trays, on walls, around exhaust ports. Their work is cold, wet, and perpetually stained green beneath the nails.

Many rootkeepers are descended from families who came to the basin for factory work and discovered that the factory floor was slowly becoming woodland. Their knowledge is passed on in cautions.

Never cut a root that has gone warm.

Never burn the blue-glazed reeds indoors.

If a tree grows through a window, leave the window open until the tree chooses a direction.

If the moss begins flowering in a room where it has never flowered before, call the district office before you clean it.

They are not all believed. They are all repeated.

The workers of the Original Works are a separate kind of citizen in the public imagination. They come and go through elevated doors with their faces covered, their boots caked in mud that is not quite soil. They are paid well enough to be envied and poorly enough to be pitied. Their shifts are irregular, because whatever the Works produces or processes does not always obey clocks. Some go in at dawn and return four days later. Some enter for a routine maintenance assignment and emerge with new scars, new silences, or a little fern growth caught in the seams of their clothing.

No one knows exactly what they do down there, including, apparently, many of them.

They are welders, sediment technicians, heat-exchange operators, structural divers, fungal containment staff, probe mechanics, material reclaimers. They repair systems built before their grandparents were born and encounter spaces that were not on the plans the day before. They speak of “old levels” and “wet levels,” “the underframes,” “the green galleries,” “the rooms that have closed.” They rarely speak of the deepest portions at all.

Their children sometimes inherit small physical habits: an aversion to closed elevators, a fondness for sleeping with a fan running, an unusual ability to tell hot metal from cold metal by smell. Whether this is real or merely the city making stories out of families, nobody can establish.

Aboveground, there are market people, tram people, boiler people, repair people. There are teachers whose lesson plans include evacuation routes and basic load-bearing principles. There are cooks who specialize in vegetables grown in rooftop slag beds: bitter greens, thick pale tubers, black beans, moss-fed mushrooms, grapes from the conveyor vineyards. There are electricians who know which old substations are inhabited by dogs and which by beetles, and who leave food out accordingly. There are scavengers who work the abandoned districts, recovering door fittings, intact wire, glazed tile, copper pipe, usable glass, and occasionally entire rooms preserved beneath sheets of slowly advancing soil.

There are people who make a living moving other people’s buildings.

Their crews arrive with jacks, tracks, cables, sledges, hydraulic pads, and cups of tea in dented flasks. They lift apartments while residents remain inside. They slide shops clear of widening cracks without removing the stock from the shelves. They rotate row houses by fractions of a degree to align them with a road that has shifted in the night. The work requires immense strength, patience, and an almost comic gentleness. A moving crew may relocate a whole clinic across a drainage channel while preserving the patients’ appointments.

These crews are among the most respected inhabitants of the basin. At weddings, people toast them. During hard winters, district councils give them priority heat. If a moving crew says a house cannot be saved, no one argues for long.

The city’s children are perhaps its most native life-form. They play in places adults regard as hazardous but manageable: under pipe bridges, along fenced observation paths, in the dry troughs of drainage channels, beneath the raised feet of storage tanks. Their games involve mapping, bracing, claiming, evacuating. They trade old warning signs, polished slag, bright beetle shells, fragments of ceramic conduit, and the colored caps from municipal survey stakes.

They know which canal birds can be fed and which will take a finger. They know that foxes are never to be followed onto a roof. They know how to hear the difference between a construction blast and a deeper sound that means everyone should leave the street. Their schools issue them survey flags not merely as a precaution but as a point of civic pride. The first flag a child plants beside a new crack is often photographed.

Teenagers gather on crane platforms and unused gantries, where they can see over the packed districts toward the broad unfinished fields. They go there for privacy, for romance, for the sensation of looking at distance. They paint unofficial maps on concrete walls: ridges that appeared and vanished, safe footpaths through the substrate, places where strange warm vapor rises in winter. Some dream of leaving. Many do leave, briefly. A number return with money, injuries, new clothes too clean for the basin, and an inability to sleep in a city where the ground does not answer back.

The animals are not pets, though some become neighbors.

The dogs have their own routes between transformer cabinets, canteens, loading yards, and warm ventilation outlets. District residents recognize packs by the color of their coats and the tags left by animal-health crews. A dog with pale lichen-green fur sleeping outside a building is often taken as a good sign: the foundations there are warm, the electrical supply steady, the pipes not leaking anything immediately lethal.

The white foxes are less trusted. They appear where refrigeration units fail, where insulated freight has been abandoned, where snow accumulates in oddly persistent patches. They move with an irritating certainty, as if following routes no human can see. Some say they are attracted to the burrowers. Others say they are scouts for the vegetation. Children leave offerings for them; engineers curse them for chewing sensor cable.

The canal birds are citizens in the way pigeons are citizens elsewhere, except that these are tall, hard-eyed creatures with orange feet and beaks built to puncture something tougher than fish. They nest in drainage infrastructure, steal from food stalls, and gather in enormous quiet lines before changes in pressure. When every bird on a canal lifts at once and flies inland, tram conductors slow their vehicles without being told.

The rats, for all their size, are seldom seen. Their absence is part of their power. Buildings known to house them have a different character: insulation replaced in strange neat layers, low scratching at three in the morning, occasional warmth in the walls where no pipe runs. Some households claim the rats chew through invasive roots and prevent structural damage. Others claim they carry messages between buried neighborhoods. Nobody tries very hard to prove either theory.

And beneath all this, there are the burrowers.

No one agrees whether they live in the ordinary sense.

They are sometimes described as machines left over from the first excavation: blind boring engines with root systems for cables and mouths that melt stone. They are sometimes described as animals grown from industrial waste and subterranean seed stock: vast pale bodies, jointed or segmented, warm inside, driven by hunger or instinct. There are people who insist the burrowers are not creatures at all, but processes—the land correcting itself through a language of pressure, heat, water, and mineral growth.

Still, people have heard them.

Not often. Not clearly. A low scrape beneath foundations. A rhythm like slow breathing through a blocked pipe. A distant impact that travels up chair legs and into the bones. Sometimes a basement wall sweats mud for an hour. Sometimes all the glasses in a cupboard turn themselves slightly toward the east. Sometimes the new ridges appear with a pattern on their flanks: parallel grooves, shallow enough to be erosion, regular enough to cause silence.

There are unofficial shrines at listening posts. Reflective tape tied to railings. Small dishes of oil. Strings of colored wire. Children leave beetle shells there. Works employees leave nothing, but some touch the nearest metal surface before descending into a lower level.

The city officially discourages these practices while quietly replacing the tape when it fades.

Perhaps the deepest inhabitants are not the burrowers themselves but the things that follow them: heat-loving fungi, soft white roots, beetles with translucent shells, mats of pale fiber that appear in newly opened ground and retract when exposed to too much air. Samples are taken, catalogued, sealed, misplaced, reclassified. Some are used in filtration systems, insulation, medical dressings, agricultural substrate. Some are forbidden to cultivate. Some turn up anyway in private greenhouse beds, where people claim they make tomatoes grow through winter.

Everything in the basin is learning to make use of disturbance.

That is what lives there: people who can build a home on a moving surface without pretending it is fixed; plants that regard steel frames as forests; animals that have adapted to heat vents and rail lines; institutions that have learned to issue permits for hills; old machinery sunk so deep into the young earth that it has become part of the earth’s metabolism.

And under the city, turning slowly in whatever dark medium lies below clay, roots, slag, and buried foundations, something continues to make room for all of them.
