The people of the basin call themselves residents before they call themselves anything else.

This is partly civic habit, partly caution. Family names are used in private, guild names at work, old regional names in songs that no one sings above a murmur. But *resident* is the word stamped on ration seals, school records, veterinary tags, rain permits, and the little ceramic plaques fixed beside every dwelling door. It means someone has been measured by the valley and found able to remain in it.

They are not a uniform people. The lower canal districts are full of narrow-shouldered families whose grandparents arrived by freight convoy, carrying languages adapted to close rooms and machine noise. The western terraces hold descendants of the weather crews: pale-eyed, broad-handed people with a custom of turning their faces upward whenever the pressure changes. High on the walls live the repair lineages, who are said to have blackened fingernails even at birth, though this is not true except metaphorically. Their children learn to identify a broken pump by touching the rail beside it.

There are also those who were born in the valley and have never seen a horizon that was not engineered.

They tend to walk differently from newcomers. They place their feet lightly at expansion joints. They hesitate before crossing a threshold where the floor changes material. They can tell, by the taste of air through their teeth, whether a corridor is connected to a live ventilation shaft. They do not look startled when a building emits a deep internal settling sound. They pause, wait, and go on.

Most residents are slender, though not from hunger exactly. Food is plentiful enough in its regulated forms: protein loaves, vat vegetables, fish meal, goat milk, fungal cheeses, occasional fruit from the suspect trees. But the valley’s altitude, its stairways, and its compressed air make bodies economical. Older people develop the stooped, deliberate posture of those who have spent decades ducking under pipes and reading signs at floor level. Young people have long calves from climbing. Nearly everyone has a small mark somewhere on their face or hands from mineral dust, adhesive burns, or the fine cuts made by maintenance wire.

Their clothing is layered, practical, and quietly coded. A cuff stitched in green thread means someone has completed rain-response training. A patch at the shoulder, removed and resewn several times, means a person has changed districts but kept their residency. Guild workers wear nothing flamboyant, but each trade has a permitted angle of reflective striping: diagonal for structural crews, vertical for water workers, broken horizontal bars for the lift engineers. Children imitate these insignia with chalk and foil until they are old enough to earn something real.

The valley does not produce many loud people.

Those who are naturally loud become performers, warning officers, livestock callers, teachers of the very young, or else they leave for the lower works, where no one knows what the sound level is. Most people learn an expressive quiet. They speak with fingers against sleeves, with head tilts, with glances toward vents and drains. In markets, bargaining occurs through samples placed on a table and then moved an inch at a time. A seller may indicate that the price is final by covering an item with their palm and waiting. Buyers understand.

There are arguments, of course. There are marriages that end over the allocation of a roof garden, feuds about inherited workshop space, accusations that a neighbor has diverted condensate, cruel jokes about those assigned to the odor-control plants. But even anger has acquired a valley form. Doors close softly. Tools are returned with excessive care. Someone’s name is omitted from the evening water list.

Children are less restrained. They race through the tube bridges, slapping their palms against the cloudy plastic to make thunderous little bursts that echo over the terraces. They collect the polymer fruit of the columnar trees, the wing scales shed by chimney swifts, obsolete bolts, bright scraps of warning tape, pigeon quills, fish membrane. They play a game on the upper rooflands in which one child stands still as a survey pole while the others argue whether the landscape has moved around them. The game is forbidden near the new hills, which makes it irresistible.

They grow up knowing the animals not as scenery but as fellow residents of a more conditional kind.

The pigeons are fed at fixed intervals by licensed elders, generally people with failing knees or no remaining family. This is considered appropriate because pigeons recognize routine better than faces. A person who feeds one roof flock for long enough will find birds waiting outside their window even after retirement. When such a feeder dies, the flock often gathers on the roof in unusual silence. No one says the pigeons mourn. They simply leave a dish of grain out for an extra week.

Foxes belong to no one, though several districts insist that certain tagged animals are theirs. The foxes know the cable corridors, the heat leaks, the doors that do not seal. They steal gloves, insulation strips, wrapped food, and occasionally babies’ shoes. A fox entering a home is a nuisance. A fox refusing to leave a home is an omen, but nobody agrees of what. Old women make nests for them in unused lift shafts. Maintenance officers deny this happens.

The goats are employed, more or less. They are issued routes, led by patient children or remote light signals through the higher gardens and along fungus-thick walls. They eat what people cannot safely touch. They are clever enough to recognize which inspectors can be bribed with warmth from a hand or a scrap of mineral biscuit. Their ears carry colored collars showing their grazing rights. The bright collars, usually reserved for official warnings, are allowed because goats cannot read.

The blind canal fish are eaten only in emergencies and festivals. It is said they carry the memory of every membrane they have fed from, and that a person who eats one will dream of pipes for a month. This is probably not true, but people still cook them carefully, with great amounts of sour herbs grown in the filtration troughs.

The chimney swifts are another matter.

There are people who watch them professionally: swift clerks, nest wardens, stack attendants. Their work is almost entirely observation. They record the timing of the creatures’ emergence, the texture of their wing-folds, the condition of their nests, the direction in which they drift when the exhaust is low. The official reports are dry. The unofficial records are not. Whole private libraries exist of swift sketches, shed wing membranes, overheard stories, and lists of improbable coincidences.

Some residents believe the swifts are unfinished people: those whose names were given to structures before the structures had decided what they would become. Others say they are the basin’s first true native species. A few maintenance guilds maintain, without evidence and with great dignity, that the creatures are a kind of exhaust-filtering device whose biological components have escaped documentation.

The swifts themselves offer no clarification. They nest, breed, drift through hot air, and sometimes gather around a particular chimney mouth for three or four days before the ground beneath a district shifts.

There are also those who live below.

Nobody uses that phrase casually. Below means the unmarked levels, the old excavation works, the zones where the loud labor was moved generations ago and never entirely brought back. Officially, below is occupied by rotational crews, machine custodians, waste processors, seismic welders, and a number of automated systems. Officially, all personnel return to their assigned districts between shifts.

But people in the upper terraces speak of relatives who took below-work and came back altered in small ways: unable to sleep except beside a running appliance; unable to tolerate open sky; unable to remember the color red unless shown it. They speak of children born in sublevels whose pupils are adapted to flickering work light, whose voices are trained low enough to pass through ducts. They speak of communities built in cavities behind the maps, where the ceilings tremble constantly and every household keeps an emergency bucket of warm stones.

Sometimes, from a service lift that should be empty, a person emerges wearing an unfamiliar kind of respirator, buys a cup of condensed tea, asks whether the upper rain has begun, and disappears before anyone thinks to question them.

These people are not treated as ghosts. Ghosts are simpler.

Above all, the valley is inhabited by caretakers.

Even people who make no claim to virtue become caretakers eventually. They scrub mineral bloom from handrails. They carry injured pigeons to roof clinics. They check whether an elderly neighbor’s condensation trap has frozen. They know which stair treads have begun to tilt and which market stalls must be moved before the next survey. They repair things that will not last, because not repairing them would be an insult to the brief period in which they can.

There are priests, too, though their temples are mostly disused control rooms and old switching stations. They do not preach that the basin is alive. That would be too easy, and perhaps too frightening. Instead they teach that a place may have preferences without having intentions. A wall may prefer to crack along one seam. Water may prefer a route no engineer has approved. A hill may prefer, over seventeen years, to become a hill.

Their ceremonies involve listening.

At the brief dimming, when the lamps retract their brightness and the structural ticking carries across the manufactured valley, residents open windows if they have them. Those without windows stand in doorways, on balconies, beneath awnings, beside goat pens, in the warm mouths of freight lifts. They listen to the ridges cooling and the pipes equalizing and the millions of small wings settling into seams.

Then, one by one, lights come on again.

The pigeons stir. The first sweeper begins its slow route. Somewhere, a child is told not to go near the unlit corridor. Somewhere else, a person marks a new hairline crease on a wall and dates it in pencil. On the upper plains, among the frost-ringed chimneys, the chimney swifts rise through the pressured brightness, unfolding themselves with great patience into the air.
