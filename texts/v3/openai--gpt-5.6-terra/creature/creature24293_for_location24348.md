The Green Exchange is inhabited by things that have learned not to distinguish between shelter, food, tool, weather, and kin.

Some are plainly animals. Some are plainly plants. Some have been working too long beside the turning machine to remain entirely one or the other.

The most numerous residents are the small recyclers: beetles, crabs, mites, pale worms living in the wet insulation, and the innumerable varieties of fly that breed in condensation trays, flower throats, warm motor housings, and the velvet behind old switch panels. They keep the Exchange from becoming still. Walnut beetles carry flakes of rust in their mandibles and bury them beneath fungus beds. Their larvae grow inside the soft rot of cable jackets and emerge with faint coppery stripes. The drainage crabs are more deliberate. They maintain routes.

A file of them may be seen dismantling a blockage in a channel: one crab loosening leaf matter, another passing it backward, another sorting wire from bone, another carrying a bright screw away like treasure. Their colonies occupy the hollows of collapsed conduits. At low water they come out in armies, each with a leaf scrap held above its shell as though carrying a flag. They do not flee larger animals unless stepped on. They have learned the schedules of hooves, paws, wheels, and falling droplets.

The moths are not one species either, though the people who once descended here called all of them lamp-moths. Some are soft and gray, with wings like old paper. Some are black and blue, their scales flashing only when they cross an amber flower’s light. Some have transparent wings through which their internal greenish fluids can be seen moving. The oldest ones gather near the rotor.

They enter it willingly.

No one has watched closely enough to say what happens inside. A moth disappears through a seam in the warm metal and, days later, a different moth may emerge—larger, pollen-dusted, with a peculiar steadiness in its flight. These returnees do not orbit lamps. They fly the high dark routes beneath the root vault and settle on the largest leaves, where they tremble their wings in slow concert. The vibration travels down the plant walls. In this way, perhaps, the moths help tell the Exchange when its own growth has become too dense.

The pipe-birds are more visible, and more troublesome. They are narrow-bodied things with slate feathers, long hooked toes, and beaks hard enough to dent thin steel. Their nests are built inside vent stacks, hollow girders, cable trays, and the empty throats of pressure pipes. They decorate these nests obsessively. A pipe-bird will carry a bottle cap for half a mile, evade a reed-hound, fight three rivals, and nearly drown in a sump to add one blue circle to its collection.

They speak in stolen noises.

A flock may greet a traveler with the squeal of old brakes, the descending chime of a platform announcement, the cough of a maintenance worker long dead, or the exact three-note alarm of a flooded substation. Their young learn the calls before they learn their own species’ voices. In the deeper galleries, there are pipe-birds whose songs consist entirely of machinery that no longer exists aboveground. They preserve vanished elevators, obsolete ticket gates, ancient factory whistles. Their territories are loud with ghosts, though the birds mean no haunting by it.

The red-faced tick birds that follow the boars are smaller and uglier, with bare scarlet skin around the eyes and feathers the color of wet ash. They are fierce social creatures. They do not merely pick parasites from the boars; they inspect them. A bird will peck mud from a boar’s ear, pull fungus from its shoulder plates, remove a thorn from its snout, then scream a report to the flock. The boars tolerate this with grave patience.

Blind boars are among the Exchange’s oldest large inhabitants. Their ancestors may have entered through storm tunnels, or may have been farm animals lost during some long-ago flood. Now they are low, broad, almost armored creatures, their hides dark and sparsely bristled, their backs overgrown with clay plates and rootlets. Young boars are born almost clean. By their third year, the Exchange begins clothing them: mud hardens over their shoulders; moss takes hold in the grooves; small white fungi emerge after long rests.

They cannot see, but each carries an astonishing map in its snout. A boar can identify a path by its mineral dampness, distinguish warm pipe-water from rainwater, and recognize individual root colonies by smell. They excavate the beds around swollen pipes, opening pockets of soil for seeds and insects. They clear channels with their bodies. When a growth has sealed a route too thoroughly, a sounder of boars may spend weeks rooting a passage back into being.

The Green Exchange makes use of them, and they make use of it.

At the central rotor, boars bring leaf bundles. This is not training in the human sense. No one taught them. The oldest boars simply do it, and the young copy them. A bundle may contain the bitter leaves that make root-columns feverish, the choking white runners that smother lamp fungus, or the spent husks of switchblooms after an electrical surge. The boar pushes the offering into the grate, snorts once, and waits.

Sometimes the machine answers with warmth.

The reed-hounds are less trusted. They move through the aisles in packs of three to seven, tall as ponies at the shoulder but narrower, their bodies hidden beneath long wet fibers resembling river reeds. At a distance they appear almost vegetal: a gust in a stand of grass, a bundle of trailing stems moving against the air. Up close, their heads are pale and smooth, with blunt muzzles, lidless black eyes, and mouths crowded with flat grinding teeth.

They eat roots, mushrooms, insects, carrion, insulation foam, and occasionally one another.

Their great talent is listening. A reed-hound can place its jaw against a pipe and feel the movement of water for several chambers in either direction. Packs use this to hunt, but also to navigate. They know when the steam lines will pulse, when the pumping station will wake, when a section of tunnel is about to flood. They avoid people unless cornered, yet they often follow them from a distance, attracted to the irregularity of human footfalls.

When two packs scent each other across a plaza, they raise their heads and roar. The sound is not a bark. It begins as a breath drawn through many wet reeds, then deepens into a mechanical bellow that seems to come from the walls as much as from the animals. Root systems shudder under it. Moths lift from lamps. The rotor, it is said, turns a little faster.

The antlered grazers are gentler, though no less strange. Their forelimbs are short, their hind limbs powerful, and they rise upright to feed from the vertical gardens. They possess long flexible lips suited to stripping leaves without tearing the stem. Their antlers branch wide and low, and every branch supports a traveling garden: ferns, liverworts, tiny orchids, mosses, and the occasional small crab clinging in a wet crevice.

Each grazer carries a distinct ecology on its head.

The younger ones have sparse antlers, mostly bare and polished by rubbing against root walls. Mature females wear dense green crowns. The oldest males can scarcely pass through narrow corridors. Their antlers bear hanging moss curtains and pools where insects breed. Pipe-birds nest between the tines. A gray frog may ride there for months. When an old grazer dies, its antlers are not discarded by the Exchange. They become planted structures, quickly seized by roots, and eventually vanish into the walls as ornamental arches.

There are people below as well.

They call themselves, when speaking to outsiders, the Tenders. Among themselves they use names that change with occupation: Lamp-Keeper, Sump-Reader, Boar-Sister, Wire-Father, Moth-Born, Root-Cutter, Listener. There may be thirty living in the Exchange at once, or twice that number, depending on whether one counts the children who sleep in the upper maintenance rooms and the old people who rarely leave the warm chambers near the rotor.

They are not hermits, exactly. They trade with the city above through certain laundries, basement workshops, station kiosks, and locked utility doors. They exchange mushrooms, medicinal molds, repaired copper, recovered objects, and occasionally electricity for salt, cloth, clean tools, lamp oil, antibiotics, paper, and news. Yet they are not fully of the upper city anymore. Their calendars follow pressure changes. Their sense of distance is measured in valves and root corridors. Their children learn to recognize danger by the behavior of switchblooms before they learn street names.

The Tenders do not control the Exchange. They would consider the idea absurd. They negotiate with it.

They prune only where a path must remain open. They divert water rather than attempting to stop it. They lay warm stones near vulnerable nests during outages. They mark dangerous root movements with colored wire. They leave food—not for the animals in general, but for particular relationships: mineral blocks for grazers, scrap bundles for crabs, old cloth soaked in broth for reed-hounds during lean periods. They know which boar matriarch will permit a person to cross her herd, which pipe-bird flocks will steal a lens from one’s glasses, which moth clouds indicate a hidden electrical leak.

They also tend the machine.

No Tender claims to understand it. Their oldest manuals, written in a mixture of municipal notation and family corrections, disagree on its original function. Some say it aerated sewage. Some say it regulated flood pressure. Some say it was installed as part of an emergency system and never used correctly. Its housing bears no readable manufacturer’s plate. Beneath the roots there may be cables leading to the entire city, or only to a dead pumping station. Nobody knows.

But the Tenders know its needs.

It must not go dry.

It must not be choked with certain roots.

It must be fed green material after a long grid failure.

It must be given sound when the chamber has fallen too quiet.

This last task is performed by the Listeners. During dangerous silences—when the lamps fail, the frogs cease calling, and even the reed-hounds retreat into the walls—the Listeners descend to the rotor chamber carrying drums made from oil barrels, pipe organs built from salvaged conduit, bells cast from melted wire, and the cracked brass horns of obsolete ventilation systems. They play until the machine answers.

No two accounts agree on what that answer is. Some say the rotor begins to turn visibly. Some say warm rain begins falling. Some say a low note rises through the floor and enters the bones. Some say all the animals of the Exchange gather at once, not frightened, but attentive.

There are other inhabitants that the Tenders do not name confidently.

Long white shapes drift in the black pools beneath floating mats, too slow to be fish and too large to be larvae. They surface only when the city above has endured several days of heavy rain. Their backs resemble drowned pipes until they breathe.

In the upper root vault, things with many jointed wings move through the slow-falling rain. They may be insects. They may be leaves caught in updrafts. They may be the shed skins of something that lives above the visible ceiling.

Within some of the oldest root-columns are faces: not human faces exactly, but arrangements of knots, pale fungal shelves, and dark cavities that become expressive when watched too long. Tenders avoid cutting these columns. Pipe-birds leave offerings at their bases. The antlered grazers lick mineral seepage from them with unusual care.

And there is the Exchange itself, which is perhaps the largest resident of all.

Not the plants alone, nor the animals, nor the machine. The arrangement of them. The way a cable fault causes flowers to close, which changes insect routes, which draws birds, which startles boars into opening a blocked channel, which alters the water around the rotor. The way the central turning answers to leaves, sound, heat, and living weight. The way routes remain open until they are no longer needed, then vanish beneath a night’s worth of growth.

The Tenders speak to it sometimes, though they do not expect speech in return.

The animals speak to it continually.

A boar pressing its leaf bundle into the grate is speaking. A pipe-bird hammering a pipe is speaking. A moth entering the warm seam of the rotor is speaking. A reed-hound roaring across a plaza is speaking. Roots tightening around a failing wall are speaking.

Below the oldest district, the Green Exchange is inhabited by a city that has forgotten it is made of separate lives.
