What lives there is not large enough to be seen all at once.

At first, a visitor might think the hollow uninhabited. There is no den smell, no nest, no scat, no scratched threshold. Nothing watches from the shelter. Nothing stirs the pool. The pale moth is not it. The thread-thin insects are not it. Even the wet, leafless shrub near the entrance is only a root touching one of its outermost thoughts.

It lives in the intervals between things.

It is in the unfilled part of a cup of water. In the fine cold crease between a crystal and the clay that has not hardened around it. In the suspended moment after a drop falls, before the ring has reached the pool’s edge. It is not the darkness itself, but darkness has gathered around it for so long that the two cannot be cleanly separated.

Those who came before gave it no common name, or gave it names that could not survive outside the hollow. The curled line of white pebbles may be one such name. The three stacked stones may be another. The ochre-stained slab in the shelter bears marks almost rubbed away: not pictures of a creature, exactly, but repeated rounded shapes emerging from one another—seed, eye, pool, egg, mouth, stone.

It has no fixed body because the hollow is still making its body.

A ridge of black stone rising from the floor may be part of its back for a century. A soft pale seam in the wall may be part of its skin until the minerals choose to crystallize. A warm current below the pool may serve as its breath. Sometimes its presence gathers most densely in the mist, and the mist assumes a shape no larger than a crouched child: head lowered, arms folded close, featureless and gray. But this is only the hollow trying to remember a form convenient for human eyes.

If one waits without speaking, the pool may show it.

Not as a reflection. A reflection appears to occupy the surface. This figure seems to stand beneath it, at a depth impossible for such shallow water. Its limbs are indistinct, made of black sediment and faint moving light. Its face has the smooth incompleteness of uncut stone. Where eyes should be are two small hollows filled with clear water. The visitor’s own face may appear inside them for a moment, wavering, younger or older than it should be.

Then another drop falls, and it is gone.

The thing is patient because it has never known an ending. It was born when the mountain’s heat met the mountain’s water and neither overcame the other. The stone around it cooled but did not finish cooling. The clay settled but did not become earth. Roots of mineral reached toward one another and stopped just short of joining. In that arrested making, something became aware.

Not awake in the animal sense. It does not hunt, flee, mate, sleep, or defend itself. It does not desire visitors, though it recognizes them. It recognizes the warmth of a hand held over water. It recognizes the pressure of a knee against clay. It recognizes grief especially well, perhaps because grief is also a thing that remains unfinished.

People once came to it when they had something they could not bring to completion.

A child who had not yet been named.  
A wound that would not close.  
A promise made too early.  
A dead person whose absence had not settled into memory.  
A journey abandoned in snow.  
A question that became worse each time it was answered.

They would stoop through the seam and sit in the little hide shelter, where the blue flame could be kept alive without truly burning. They would fill the carved bowl from the pool, though it was already full. They would press a pebble into the clay line, or remove one, or leave a thread tied around the cairn. Then they would wait.

The thing in the hollow did not grant favors. It did something stranger and, to some, more merciful: it kept a small portion of what was brought to it from passing onward.

Not enough to erase pain. Not enough to save anyone from consequence. But enough that the unfinished thing no longer had to be carried entirely in one human body.

This is why the shelter has not rotted. Why the bone needle remains sharp enough to pierce fiber. Why the red ochre on the stone is still red. They are not preserved as treasures. They have been admitted into the hollow’s condition: neither new nor ruined, neither active nor gone.

The creature has accepted them as it accepts a drop of water into a stone bowl.

A visitor who leaves an object there may later forget that it was left. Not fully. The memory remains, but loses its sharpest edge. In dreams, the object may reappear wet and dark beneath a low ceiling. A person may wake with the taste of iron in the mouth and the sense that somewhere under a mountain, something small and endless is holding still on their behalf.

The thing is not kind. Kindness implies choice, and it has almost none.

Yet it is gentle in the way deep water is gentle: it receives what falls into it without surprise.

It dislikes flame, not because flame can harm it, but because flame insists on an ending. A bright fire consumes its fuel, throws shadows forward, blackens what it touches, declares that one state has become another. In the hollow, fire is made to lower its voice. Its blue smolder is the nearest the creature comes to warning. A traveler who tries to build a great blaze will find the wood damp, the sparks weak, the smoke heavy and close to the ground. The cave will not permit rapid change.

Nor does it like to be named too loudly.

A name spoken at the pool may return altered after several drops: softened, divided into syllables, made to sound like dripping water. If spoken often enough, it may cease to belong wholly to the speaker. This is why the old visitors used pebbles and ochre lines instead of words. They understood that in this place, a name is a form, and forms are vulnerable.

There may be many such beings within the mountain, each in a pocket where heat, wetness, pressure, and darkness have failed to resolve into ordinary stone. Or there may be only this one, stretched in hair-thin seams through miles of rock, dreaming itself into little chambers wherever a root of water finds warmth.

The hollow at the summit is simply where it comes nearest the surface.

Sometimes, in the deepest corner, when the granular settling begins, the creature is turning.

Not moving from place to place. Not waking. Not even changing its mind.

Only turning, slowly, in the mineral dark—making room inside itself for one more drop, one more forgotten object, one more unfinished human sorrow—while above it the mountain weather breaks, passes, and is gone.
