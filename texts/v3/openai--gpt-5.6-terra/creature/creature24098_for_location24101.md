The first inhabitants are not the things that were put there.

They are the things that stayed.

At some point—perhaps after the first crew stopped returning, perhaps after the upper works ceased to have an overseer, perhaps before anyone now living could remember—the small animals learned that the chamber had moods. They learned that its moods were not random. Warmth gathered ahead of pressure changes. The pipes made a certain dry ticking before a vent opened. A blue gleam passed beneath the floor tiles before the water began to run uphill. The creatures that recognized these signs lived. Their offspring recognized more.

Now the district is full of readers.

The tunnel rats are among the best of them. They are no larger than a hand, narrow through the shoulders, with translucent ears veined like wet leaves. In the brown-gold wall glow their ears shine faint rose. Their eyes are poor, but their whiskers are elaborate, forked at the tips and sensitive to the slightest current of heated air. A rat can stand with its forepaws against a pipe and tell, by the tremor in its claws, whether a purge is three breaths away or three hundred.

They do not build burrows in the old manner. There is nowhere to dig. Instead they make folded settlements behind removable panels, under the lips of drainage channels, inside disused junction boxes. Their nests are dense knots of moss, stripped cable insulation, shed moth-wing dust, bird down, and long shining fibers stolen from filters. In the deeper nests, the materials are sorted: warm things near the center, dry things toward the outer ring, sharp things set deliberately around entrances. A rat nest is never merely a nest. It is a pressure map, a pantry, a nursery, a weather station, and often a small theft from the machine.

They are bold around sleeping creatures and very cautious around waking ones. They have a particular fondness for the pale moss, which they harvest with their front teeth and carry away in green-grey scrolls. They also groom the coppery mineral crust from the lower pipes. Whether this harms the pipes or preserves them is unclear. The chamber has not stopped them.

The blue-black frogs are older in manner, if not in years.

They sit in the shallow inspection wells like fragments of night given muscles. Their skin is dark enough to swallow the chamber’s light, but along their backs run dim cobalt lines that brighten before water reversals. The frogs do not call often. When they do, it is less a croak than a hard, resonant knock, as though someone has struck a ceramic vessel beneath the water.

They are small enough to fit in a cupped hand and heavy enough to surprise anyone who lifts one. Their bodies are dense with minerals. Their eggs, laid in strings along the warm rims of sumps, are threaded through the chamber’s cleaning cycle: algae grow upon them, tiny filter-worms feed on the algae, insects feed on the worms, and the frogs feed on the insects. The wall machinery seems to recognize this arrangement. It directs faint nutrient seepage toward occupied wells and closes the wrong drains before a clutch can be pulled away.

There are stories among the people that the frogs were engineered for this role. There are other stories that they arrived as stowaways in a water tank and taught the machine to keep them. The frogs offer no preference between these explanations. They continue sitting in their wells, grave and shining, watching everything with copper-colored eyes.

Above them, the long-legged birds conduct the chamber.

They are thin, awkward creatures, taller than a person’s knee, with feathers the color of damp paper and pipe ash. Their legs are jointed for ladders and narrow rails rather than branches. They can stand on a cable no thicker than a finger and sleep there while the cable sways in the breath of the vents. Their beaks are long, hinged, and plated at the tips. With them they pick midges from steam, pry beetles from wall seams, and strike particular pipes in complicated patterns.

The striking is not play.

Each flock possesses a set of calls distributed through the chamber’s metal. A single bird may tap a low pipe beside the basin. The sound travels outward, altered by bends, pressure chambers, old support ribs, and water-filled conduits. Somewhere two levels above, another bird hears the reply in the rail beneath its feet. It taps back. In this way, the birds announce food, warn of climbing scavengers, gather before a sudden flood, and perhaps exchange matters too elaborate for any visitor to understand.

When an aperture opens and daylight spears down into the steam, the birds become briefly strange. They turn their heads upward and hold still. Their pupils tighten almost closed. Some spread their wings—not to fly, since there is scarcely room—but to expose pale underfeathers that have never been warmed by a real sun. Then the aperture shuts, the cold ray withdraws, and the birds go back to their pipes.

The moths are less wise but more prophetic.

They live in the upper warm dark, where fans turn beyond the grilles and the air tastes faintly of oil. Most of the time they cling in pale drifts to overhead mesh, their wings closed into narrow glowing slivers. At rest they resemble lichen, frost, or a residue left by electrical discharge. Disturbed, they lift together and make the ceiling appear to be shedding light.

Their glow comes not from flame but from the soft work of their bodies. Their wing scales hold compounds gathered from mineral seepage and processed heat. In darkness, the moths shine strongest near a failing conduit, a cracked insulation sheath, or a place where the air has become too dry. The local inhabitants watch them carefully. A single moth wandering low may mean little. A thousand moths taking flight in one direction means everyone should find a ledge, seal a nest, and wait for the chamber to scour itself with heat.

Some moths are born without wings. They spend their short lives crawling through the felted moss of warm seams, eating fungal threads and leaving behind a dust that attracts the tiny metallic beetles. This, too, is useful. Nothing here has only one occupation.

The beetles are perhaps the most visibly altered by the place.

They are coin-sized, oval, and hard-backed, but their shells are not all shell. They collect thin flakes of foil, flakes of old paint, insulation glitter, and the mica-like residue of worn components, fastening these pieces to themselves with secretions that harden into a dark lacquer. A mature beetle may wear a bright scrap of alloy across its back like a shield, or a little torn crescent of warning label, or a tiny stamped fragment of machine script. They become walking archives, though no one can read them while they move.

The six-limbed scavengers follow the beetles’ work.

These are narrow-bodied, quick, and communal, with long forelimbs suited for pulling material from cracks. Their middle limbs are used for walking; their rear limbs brace against the wet tiles or curl around rails. They travel in lines, each individual touching the one ahead with its antennae. From a distance they look like a spill of black cord. Up close, they have mild, intelligent faces and glittering jaws made for sorting rather than killing.

They dismantle everything.

A lost button becomes bedding fiber. A dead moth becomes food for larvae. A broken clip becomes a brace for a nest. A strip of softened sealant becomes a dam around a frog’s egg well. They are tolerated everywhere because they are quicker than corrosion. If one of the chamber’s small repairs fails, scavenger packs arrive before any visible mechanical attendant does. They clear the fragments, strip away damp organic matter, and often leave the site clean enough for the wall’s hidden workers to seal.

The eel-things belong to the drains and do not entirely belong to the chamber.

No one sees them in full unless the pressure is very wrong.

They are whiskered, blunt-headed animals, pale below and dark above, with bodies that narrow into ribboning tails. Their skin has the wet satin look of river fish, but they breathe air as readily as water and can climb a vertical channel by flattening themselves against the ribbing. During ordinary hours they remain in the dark plumbing, feeding on mineral film, drowned insects, and whatever the water brings.

During pressure changes, they rise.

First come the whiskers through the circular mouths. Then the small blind faces, tasting the steam. Then several bodies at once, folding over one another until a drain seems to be flowering with slick grey roots. They do not attack unless cornered. They are too intent on the weather. They climb to the basin’s highest edges and press their bellies to the tiles, sensing the change through their skin.

When the water reverses, they are ready. They ride it upward.

The things in the ceiling are called softbats by those who need a name for them.

They are not bats, though they fly with the same hurried, folded motion. Their bodies are round and warm, covered in close brown fur. Their faces are blunt, almost childlike in their softness, with broad nostrils and large ears curled inward around delicate membranes. They navigate by sound, but not in the simple click-and-return fashion of ordinary bats. They listen to the entire chamber. The fan rhythm. The valve shriek. The distant striking of bird beaks. The tactile voices of people speaking into wall panels. The deep, nearly inaudible groan of the outer shell cooling after nightfall.

To a softbat, the district may be visible as a vast continual chord.

They nest inside ceiling baffles in colonies so crowded that a maintenance grille sometimes appears to breathe. Their young cling to pipes and to one another. When a fan changes speed, the colony shifts as a whole, flowing through cracks and ducts into a safer cavity. They consume immense numbers of midges. In exchange, their droppings feed the mosses, the mosses feed the insects, and the insects return to the softbats.

There are also the people.

There are never many.

They are not the owners of the district, nor its masters, even when they speak of maintenance and jurisdiction and inherited responsibility. The place has outlasted too many such claims. At any given time there may be three people living there, or seven, or only one old keeper moving from platform to platform with a basket of mineral flakes. Sometimes a family stays for several pressure seasons. Sometimes a crew comes through from a higher corridor and leaves as soon as they can bear the sound.

Those who remain develop the same habits as the animals.

They sleep in hammocks strung near reliable warmth, never directly over a drain. Their clothes are layered in waxed fabric and scavenged membrane, with soft pads sewn into the palms so they can speak to the walls without bruising themselves. They wear hair cropped close or gathered high, because loose strands collect steam and grit. Their skin often has the faint dry sheen of mineral salt around the eyelids and knuckles. Their voices, when heard aloud, are gentle from disuse.

Most communication is done by touch.

A resident may enter the chamber, place one hand against a wall panel, and receive a message through the heel of the palm: a rapid tremble for *water rising,* three broad pulses for *do not use the eastern ladder,* a long low shiver for *someone is waiting above.* Children learn the patterns before they can speak clearly. They learn the animal signs alongside them: moth flight, frog brightness, rat drumming, the uneasy stillness of birds.

The people tend the chamber without imagining they control it. They clean the inspection wells. They redistribute moss when a patch grows thick enough to choke a vent. They keep the blue-black frogs supplied with the right mineral flakes. They retrieve birds caught in cable loops. They leave small trays of condensed water in high ledges for the softbats during dry intervals. They mark unsafe pipes with braided wire and bits of reflective foil, though the beetles frequently steal the markers and wear them away.

Their deepest work is listening.

There are vibrations that belong to ordinary operation: the big fans, the alternating pumps, the slow relief of pressure. And there are vibrations that do not. A missing thud. A valve closing too cleanly. A shift in the low concussion that makes the teeth ache. The oldest residents can tell when the chamber is repairing itself, when it is searching for a blockage, when an outer weather front has reached the mountain’s shell, and when something distant in the upper works has opened a route that should have remained closed.

They do not call themselves citizens. The word is too dry, too flat, too much like a line on a map.

Some call themselves wetkeepers. Some call themselves the echoed. One old term, rarely used except in tactile messages, translates roughly as *those held in the breath.*

That may be the closest thing to the truth.

Because all the inhabitants are held there: rats in their warm cable nests, frogs in mineral wells, birds on resonant pipes, moths under the grilles, eels in the climbing water, people in their suspended hammocks. They live inside the vast unfinished action of the machine. It breathes, and they bend with it. It rains, and they make room. It changes its mind about the direction of water, and they climb.

The district’s most ancient resident may be the chamber itself—not as a mind in the human sense, but as a body that has learned the weight and warmth of its creatures.

It does not love them.

It does not need to.

But its drains avoid the frog clutches more often than chance should allow. Its warmth lingers near occupied nests. Its repairs grow around scavenger paths instead of through them. And when the high outer rain begins to strike the shell, making every plate tremble with the distant weather of a world no one in the basin can see, the chamber answers with a fuller, warmer rain of its own.

Then every living thing pauses.

For a moment, beneath the roar, the whole small district seems to listen to itself living.
