What lives on the plain is not, at first, easy to distinguish from what is being made.

There are the **mortar-bodies**.

They emerge in places where walls have failed to decide whether they are walls. A corridor may stand chest-high for a few hours, wet brick curving through the vapor, and then one section of it will detach from the line. Bricks draw inward. Joints unseal and reseal. A cavity appears where no cavity was planned. By the time the glow has passed beneath the paving, there is something standing there: narrow, hooded in dangling fiber, its limbs made from bundled rods and broken tile.

They are roughly human only from a distance.

Up close, a mortar-body has too many articulations. Its arms may divide at the elbow into three small working limbs, each fitted with some naturalized tool: a trowel edge, a hooked awl, a smooth tamping stone. What appears to be a head is often a kiln-brick with a dark opening through it. Warm vapor leaves this opening in intermittent breaths. Some have no heads at all, only a high collar of stacked ceramic segments rotating slowly around an empty center.

They do not walk in the usual sense. The paving changes beneath them, rising in low steps where they need to go and flattening behind them. When they cross water, small blocks push up through the channel in a line before them. A mortar-body may travel for miles without visibly lifting a foot.

They are almost always working.

Not building, exactly. The distinction matters. They make corrections.

A loose copper plate will be lifted and carried until it finds a seam that wants it. A wall that has curved too early will be braced, unbraced, and pressed downward. A foundation that has begun to form a room will be given a door, though the door may lead into the soil. The bodies show no distress at such mistakes. They seem to understand that a door opening into solid earth is still, in some deep accounting, a completed door.

When one encounters an object that cannot be reconciled—a bucket with no place in the current arrangement, a sledge split beyond use, a line of bricks from a vanished structure—it may stand beside it for a very long time. Its upper portions angle toward the object. Steam passes through the holes in its body. Eventually the thing begins to move, often by itself, toward some purpose neither observer nor object could have anticipated.

The mortar-bodies do not appear to notice one another. They may work within arm’s reach, their tools touching, their limbs passing through the same drifting vapor, without acknowledgment. Yet their actions are coordinated in ways too exact for accident. A hundred miles apart, a hundred of them may turn at once toward the same subterranean knock. Their brick faces darken. Their empty collars cease rotating. Then they resume their labor in a new direction.

There are larger ones that never fully rise.

These are called, by no one who lives long enough to establish a name, the **buried masons**. One recognizes them only by consequences. A district begins to settle in a pattern resembling ribs. A row of kiln chimneys bends inward as if around a shoulder. Hot water vanishes from a canal and returns through a crack several miles away, carrying red clay and fragments of white ceramic.

Then, perhaps, a shape becomes visible under the translucent paving.

It is enormous: a hand with fingers made from retaining walls; a face implied by drainage basins; the arc of a spine under miles of terraced ground. It moves with unbearable slowness. A buried mason may take a season to close one hand. When it does, whole neighborhoods of unfinished architecture are gathered, crushed, softened by heat, and incorporated into new strata.

The buried masons are not asleep. Sleep implies an interval between acts. These creatures are simply too large for their acts to be recognized as acts.

The cranes at the edge of sight may be attached to them.

Or they may be their tools.

There are also the **joint-things**, which are smaller and more common, though rarely seen clearly. They inhabit the hairline spaces between plates. In dry worlds they might be called insects, but they have neither the quickness nor the appetite associated with insects. A joint-thing is a pale segmented wedge no longer than a thumb. Its body is made of damp chalk, copper wire, translucent mineral film, and something that looks disturbingly like old fingernail.

They move through seams with soft clicking sounds.

Their work is minute. They test edges. They carry grains of mortar in their mouthparts. They lay down tiny loops of fiber that tighten when heated. Where they gather in sufficient numbers, a crack closes. Where they flee, a crack opens. The plain’s continual rearrangement may be their collective labor on a scale too vast to comprehend.

Sometimes a person-sized mortar-body kneels and opens a cavity in its chest. Joint-things pour out in streams, pale against the wet bricks, and vanish into the nearest divisions in the ground. At other times they climb into the body through its feet or joints and remain there. Whether they are offspring, organs, tools, or residents is impossible to say.

The joint-things react to speech.

Not language, necessarily. Breath and vibration are enough. A word spoken over an open seam may bring them boiling upward from below. They gather around the speaker’s boots, arrange themselves in rings, and listen with the blind attentiveness of mechanisms awaiting instruction. If the voice continues, they may begin to construct an answer: a low ridge of brick dust, a pattern of pebbles, a shallow trench filled with hot water. The answer is seldom recognizable as speech. It may be a diagram of a room that has not yet been built, or a route leading toward a place that no longer exists.

The most unsettling inhabitants are the **unplaced**.

They look like travelers.

One sees them through the mist at the ends of corridors, standing ankle-deep in hot water, carrying bundles or tools. They wear garments of dark woven material that never seem soaked despite the constant damp. Their outlines are human enough to provoke immediate hope. Some raise an arm in greeting. Some sit beside kiln walls. Some appear to shelter beneath the tripod towers, heads bowed as though exhausted.

Approaching them is difficult. The ground changes.

A channel appears where there was none. A wall rises across the route. The distance lengthens, though the figure remains precisely the same size. If one does reach it, the unplaced has often become something else: a cluster of upright bricks wrapped in wet rope; a wooden sled half submerged in clay; a stack of empty baskets; a section of wall whose gaps resemble eyes only because the observer needed them to.

But not always.

On rare occasions, an unplaced will remain human at close range.

Its skin is usually the color of fired clay. Its hair, if it has hair, is a mat of black mineral thread. Water beads on it but does not run off. Its eyes are covered by thin plates of mica, beneath which a faint orange light moves in horizontal lines. It speaks quietly, with a sound like wet bricks being separated.

The unplaced do not know where they came from.

They know routes, however. Not directions—routes. They can tell which channel will become a wall by evening, which wall will become paving by morning, where a person must stand during an upward rain, where the ground will briefly remember the shape of a stairway. They can lead a traveler across the plain, but never to a destination named beforehand. If asked where they are going, they often answer with the name of a material.

“Toward copper.”

“Down into mortar.”

“Where the red bricks are still soft.”

Some carry old tools with great care. These tools are not used. A bone-handled awl may be wrapped in cloth and held like an infant. A cracked mallet may be carried for years through the vapor. When an unplaced finally sets such an item down, the landscape notices. Nearby seams brighten. Joint-things emerge. A wall may begin to grow around the tool, incorporating it with reverence into its structure.

It is possible that the unplaced were once makers.

It is equally possible that the plain made them in the image of makers because hands, shoulders, faces, and burdens are useful shapes for unfinished work.

They seem afraid of becoming architecture.

When they rest too long, little changes begin around them. Their footprints harden into tiles. Damp mortar gathers at the hems of their clothes. Their fingers acquire seams. One may wake after a short sleep to find a thin row of bricks growing from the side of the hand. They cut these away before the joints set. Some bind themselves in copper wire to preserve their outlines. Some keep moving continuously, following the routes of collapse and reassembly, because motion delays incorporation.

None escapes indefinitely.

The plain has another kind of life, less visible than any body: the **intentions**.

They inhabit objects that have been left mid-use. A mallet lying beside a foundation may retain the urge to strike. A wedge retains the urge to divide. A bucket retains the urge to carry. In ordinary places these would be qualities, memories of labor. Here they acquire force.

A mallet may knock once in the night, though no hand touches it. A rope may pull itself taut toward an empty pulley. A row of bricks may shift because somewhere beneath them a trowel remembers being drawn across mortar. The intentions are weak individually. Together, they create the feeling that the entire plain is occupied even when no figure can be seen.

They are perhaps the most numerous inhabitants.

Every constructed object contains one. Every abandoned tool is full of them. Every wall that folds down carries its own confused desire to stand again. The plain’s ceaseless construction may not be the work of a master intelligence, nor of the mortar-bodies, nor of buried masons. It may be the accumulated insistence of countless things refusing to accept that their purpose has ended.

And beneath all of these lives something that has no shape at all.

It lives in the heat rising through the joints.

It lives in the sound of slabs finding one another under impossible pressure.

It lives in the moment when two broken objects, separated by miles and years of remaking, turn toward each other and begin to cross the wet ground.

The mortar-bodies serve it, perhaps. The buried masons dream within it. The joint-things maintain its skin. The unplaced flee from its patience. The tools repeat its smallest thoughts.

It is not a god, unless a god can be unfinished.

It is not a machine, unless a machine can hesitate.

It is the maker that has forgotten what it meant to make.

It continues anyway.
