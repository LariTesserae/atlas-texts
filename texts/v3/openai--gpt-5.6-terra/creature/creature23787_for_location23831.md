What lives there is not one kingdom but a treaty between wetness and exposure.

The first inhabitants are the things that do not move fast enough to be noticed: the dark skins on stone, the fine green films in cracks, the pale crusts that can dry hard as chalk and wake within minutes of rain. Liverworts swell along seep-lines. Black algae velvet the undersides of shelves. In the salt-pale clay, low bladder plants lie almost flat, their leaves so small they resemble spilled seeds, each holding a reserve of water in its flesh. The rock seems bare only from a distance. Up close it is inhabited grain by grain.

There are snails with tall, narrow shells banded like old ivory, climbing wet limestone at night and sealing themselves into shallow cavities through the hotter days. There are broad, flattened crawlers that travel beneath the mud’s skin, leaving only a raised wandering seam behind them. Little crabs, or animals much like crabs, inhabit the places where fresh seep-water meets mineral pools; they have pale jointed legs, dark carapaces glazed with mud, and eyes held high on stalks to watch for shadows. When startled, they vanish not into holes but straight down into the soft earth, folding their legs beneath them and becoming stones.

The pools contain shoals of silver-sided fish whose bodies flash so brightly that birds sometimes strike at reflections rather than prey. They breed whenever a new basin fills, laying adhesive eggs among sedge roots and flooded grass. Some survive the drying months by retreating into the deeper channels. Others are creatures of temporary water: small, quick-growing fish that hatch, feed, spawn, and die before their birthplace shrinks to a black puddle full of cracked shells.

Eel-bodied animals occupy the margins between water and mud. Their heads are blunt, their skin is mottled in wet browns and greens, and they can cross a surprising distance over saturated ground after rain. In the evening they leave the pools in numbers, threading through grass and under stone plates, hunting worms, larvae, frogspawn, and whatever has been stranded by receding water. Their trails braid the clay with shining lines.

The frogs are among the plain’s most various citizens. Some are thumb-sized and live in the dripping rock curtains, where their eggs cling to wet stone in bead-like clusters. Some are heavy, mud-colored animals that bury themselves during the drier intervals and emerge at the first sustained rain, their calls deep enough to be felt in the chest. Others are reed frogs, green-brown and long-limbed, that sit along vertical stems with their throats inflated into translucent globes. Their overlapping voices give each basin its own identity. A traveler who knew the place well could navigate in cloud or darkness by the frogs alone.

The insects are more populous still. Needle-winged midges dance over hidden channels. Broad-bodied dragonflies patrol the glare, their wings catching blue, copper, and white. Water beetles tumble beneath the surface in quick metallic loops. On the clay flats, small black beetles travel in straight purposeful lines between shade and dampness, vanishing into cracks before the rain arrives. There are insects whose young live in water and whose adults emerge all at once after a storm, climbing reeds by the thousand and splitting their old skins open beneath the sun.

The wading birds belong to the open distances. Some are narrow and gray, with knees that bend backward in appearance and bills long enough to probe beneath a stone lip. Some are white except for black masks and dark wing tips, so that from far away they look like scraps of cloud moving over the mud. There are little shore-runners that never seem to stop, their feet making rapid stitched patterns along the pool edges. And there are patient birds with earth-colored plumage that stand perfectly still among sparse reeds, becoming visible only when their necks strike forward.

Above them hunt the glare-divers: long-winged fish-eaters that circle without flapping until a silver turn beneath the water gives them a target. Their dives are sudden and nearly vertical. In late afternoon, when pools have become coppery mirrors, their shadows pass over the land more clearly than their bodies.

The few trees along the deepest courses house a separate, compressed world. Their roots hold pockets of cooler mud where amphibians shelter and small fish remain after floods recede. Their branches host nest colonies of loud, untidy birds. Their bark is worked by ants that build mud galleries up the trunk, and by small lizards with flat bodies the color of dried lichen. At dusk, pale-faced fruit bats—or something close to bats—come to feed on the trees’ sparse seasonal offerings, then skim low over the pools for insects.

The herd animals are not built for forests. They are broad-eyed, light-footed browsers with split hooves that spread in mud and narrow on stone. Their coats are dun, gray, rust, or the pale color of drying clay. Some have short swept-back horns; others carry no obvious weapons except their vigilance. They feed on thorn leaves, sedge tips, seed heads, and the tender growth that follows rain. They move in loose companies, each animal keeping enough distance to see danger coming from every direction.

Their young are born on the higher ribs of ground, where the soil is coarse and the floodwater arrives late. For their first days they lie still among grass and stone, marked in stripes or pale broken patches that imitate sunlit rock. The adults do not defend them with dramatic violence. They watch. They stamp. They call. And if something approaches, the whole scattered herd turns at once toward it, making the flat land feel suddenly full of eyes.

Predators live by remaining absent.

There are long-bodied hunters in the watercourses, low enough that only their nostrils, brow ridges, and eyes show above the brown surface. Their hides take the colors of algae, clay, and wet limestone. They wait where animals cross between basins, especially at the points where firm ground narrows and hoofprints overlap for generations.

On the drier stone live lean catlike hunters with spotted coats broken into pale and dark islands. They are not truly hidden in the sparse country; they survive because they understand when not to move. A standing animal becomes a shrub. A crouched one becomes a shadow under a shelf. A tail laid along a crack becomes a root. They hunt at the hour when the heat has softened but the light remains broad enough to betray every mistake.

There are also smaller hunters: fox-faced scavengers with oversized ears; long-legged birds that eat frogs, eggs, and hatchlings; snakes patterned like the drying mud; and little bright-eyed mammals that raid nests, burrow in old bank collapse, and eat almost anything that can be carried away.

But perhaps the most characteristic creatures are the weather-readers.

They are small, dark, long-toed animals that live among the reed margins and rock seepages. Before rain, they leave cover and gather on exposed stone, lifting their faces into the pressure-heavy air. No one watching them could easily say whether they are lizards, frogs, or some older form between the two. Their skin shifts from dusty gray to nearly black when wet. Their eggs are laid above the usual waterline in narrow cracks, and their young fall or crawl down to the pools only when the rains have made enough water for them.

The people of the plain, if people came to call it home, would learn from these creatures rather than try to command the land. They would build on the ribs of higher ground, with raised floors and broad roofs to catch rain. They would know each pool by its taste, its depth, its fish, its flood history. They would travel by old crossings marked with stone cairns, bleached bone, or rows of thorn stakes. They would avoid walking near brown water at dusk. They would keep their children close when the frogs fall silent.

Nothing here is entirely terrestrial, and nothing is entirely aquatic. Every animal carries some compromise in its body: spreading feet, sealed skin, high-set eyes, eggs timed to rain, lungs tolerant of dampness, colors made for mud and glare. Even the birds belong partly to the ground, because their reflections travel beneath them; even the fish belong partly to the sky, because the pools are open enough to hold whole clouds.

The plain is full of lives that have learned patience. They wait beneath stone. They wait in eggs, in mud, in roots, in deep water. Then rain comes, or water withdraws, or a cloud crosses the sun, and the apparently empty country answers all at once.
