What lives on the plateau is not one people, nor one kind of animal, but a dense fellowship of things adapted to wetness, cold, and patient pressure.

The largest creatures are the pale grazers. They are not quite cattle, not quite elk: broad through the chest and shoulders, with heavy heads, damp woolly coats, and hooves spread wide enough to cross soft ground without wholly sinking. Their color changes with the weather. In rain they are the gray of old bone; in sun, cream and pale gold; in fog, little more than moving concentrations of whiteness among the sedges. They know the firm routes through the bog by inheritance rather than sight. Calves follow their mothers through water that reaches their bellies, learning where the turf bears weight and where it opens without warning.

Their paths are among the plateau’s principal works. A herd returning year after year presses channels into the peat. Water enters the channels. Rushes grow along their sides. Frogs and beetles breed there. Eventually a trail becomes a stream, and the herd moves aside, making another. The animals do not alter the plateau against its nature. They help it revise itself.

In the alder margins live smaller browsers, slight creatures with narrow faces and enormous soft ears. Their coats are dark in summer and nearly black in winter, so that only the pale inner sides of their ears betray them in the thickets. They feed on willow tips, water plants, tender bark, and the new shoots that rise wherever a tree has fallen. They are alert without being nervous. On this plateau, sudden movement is dangerous: it may be a cat, a collapsing bank, a hidden channel, or merely a gust of cloud arriving low through the trees.

The cats are few and difficult to prove. A traveler may see a shape on wet stone: low, long-bodied, patterned in gray, lichen green, and the brown-black of rain-dark granite. It may have a tail, or it may be a branch shifting. Their paws leave no clear print in moss, only a compression where droplets collect differently. They hunt birds in the reeds, young grazers at the edge of the herd, browsing animals caught in alder tangles, and fish where streams narrow between stones. They are said to sleep in the warm air around the winter vents, their spotted bodies half obscured by vapor.

There are also the long-legged scavengers of the upper slopes: narrow-chested, rough-coated animals with muzzles built for working meat from bone and noses sensitive enough to find a dead thing beneath snow, water, or a week of rain. They travel in small family bands, not packs in the loud lowland sense. They do not pursue much. The plateau provides what weather, mud, distance, and stone have already taken. When they call, it is a low wavering sound that may be mistaken for wind passing through a cleft in the rock.

The water has its own inhabitants, layered by depth.

At the surfaces of pools live skating insects with dark, needle-thin legs. They make tiny dents in the reflected sky. Beneath them drift rafts of larvae, transparent and busy, feeding on silt, root-fuzz, and the bodies of smaller things. In the clear upper rills there are fish like strips of beaten silver, holding themselves with impossible steadiness in current. In the peat-dark channels live heavier fish, broad-headed and slow, colored the black-green of drowned wood. They emerge after dark to take insects or smaller fish from beneath mats of floating vegetation.

In the cold lakes, where old water lies below new water, there are pale blind things. Some are fish; some are perhaps only fish-shaped, their bodies thin and colorless from generations spent beneath weed beds and dark shelves of stone. They feed by touch. Their movements are so slight that they are known less by seeing than by the faint disturbances they make in the silt.

Otter-like creatures travel between these waters. They have dense dark fur and flexible bodies, but their faces are flatter and more solemn than an otter’s. Their whiskers are long, their paws almost handlike. They build no visible dens. They disappear into banks beneath root systems, into gaps under stone, into drowned spaces where the water enters the mountain. At dusk they move along the channels with a purposefulness that gives them the appearance of workers going home.

The marshes are alive with small voices, though few are loud enough to be heard above rain. Frogs, dark-backed and copper-bellied, cling to grass stems or lie half buried in moss. Their spawn gathers in warm, shallow water around mineral vents, where winter keeps a little green open amid the frost. Newts with bright undersides crawl beneath rotting logs. Snails live on reed blades. Beetles occupy every possible task: some clean carrion, some eat fungus, some bore into wet wood, some swim under the water’s skin like black dropped seeds.

The insects are never merely insects here. Clouds of midges feed fish and birds. Stoneflies cling to clean streams and announce, by their presence, that the water has remained cold and oxygen-rich. Iron-colored flies gather around the red mineral seeps, their larvae living in rust-stained slime where almost nothing else can thrive. In late summer, winged forms rise from the bogs in such numbers that ravens alter their flight paths to pass through them.

Birds inhabit every height.

The reed beds belong to secretive waterfowl, ground-nesting birds, and narrow-winged marsh hunters. The cliffs belong to silent black gliders whose long wings catch the rising air without visible effort. They nest on ledges hidden behind falling water, their chicks raised in places no land creature can reach. Small brown birds work through the sedges, taking insects from stems. Blue-throated birds flash briefly in alder shade. In the pine folds live heavier birds with feathered feet, adapted to snow and rock; in winter they burrow beneath drifts and emerge after storms to feed on needles and buds.

There are ravens, too, though they do not dominate the sky. They are wet-looking birds, larger than lowland ravens, with plumage that catches green and violet only when the cloud breaks. They follow the grazers, the scavengers, the rivers, and sometimes the vapor roads that form over the vents. They have learned that warm air brings insects, and insects bring smaller birds. Their calls are rare but varied: clicks, stone-on-stone knocks, and deep notes that seem to come from the ravines rather than from throats.

Among roots and beneath fallen timber live the hidden majority: mice with water-shedding fur; shrews that hunt through moss tunnels; pale grub-like burrowers that process the peat; blind worms that feed in the saturated soil; small pale spiders that string their webs between reeds whenever the rain pauses. Fungi occupy the dead and the nearly dead, but also the living. White fans work through the roots of alder. Orange shelves appear on wet birch. Black crusts spread over drowned branches. Some fungi glow faintly in deep hollows, not enough to light a way, only enough to make the dark seem inhabited.

And the stone is inhabited as fully as any tree.

Lichens make maps across the black slabs: gray coins, yellow powder, branching black marks, pale green crusts that thicken over centuries. Moss fills cracks and holds water long after rain has passed. Algae fur the edges of every stream. Iron bacteria gather at mineral seepages and build the red-brown curtains on the cliffs, feeding on the mountain’s dissolved metals. In certain springs, white microbial films tremble in the current like old silk. They are not beautiful in the ordinary sense, but they are alive, and they turn the hidden chemistry of the plateau into matter other creatures can use.

Even the vapor vents have their residents. Around them grow rings of green moss, liverwort, and delicate ferns that remain unfrozen when the rest of the marsh hardens. Tiny flies breed in the warmth. Spiders hunt the flies. Birds come for the spiders. Small mammals shelter in the thawed ground. In the deepest cold, these vent-rings become islands of activity, each with its own tracks leading in and out across the snow.

Nothing there lives independently for long. The grazer’s trail becomes a channel. The channel feeds sedge. The sedge holds peat. The peat darkens water. The water feeds fish and larvae. The larvae feed birds. The birds feed cats, ravens, and the soil after death. Trees rise where silt settles. Trees fall into pools. Their roots make shelter; their wood becomes fungus; their fungus becomes beetle; their beetles become everything else.

What lives there, finally, is an immense wet continuity: horn, feather, root, fin, fur, spore, algae, stone-film, mist, and mineral. No inhabitant is entirely separate from the plateau. Each is a moving part of its long operation, carried by water, held by peat, fed by decay, and slowly returned to the green, crowded machinery of the high country.
