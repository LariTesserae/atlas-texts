They are not born in the basin so much as precipitated by it.

A new crack opens, a wet line appears in the stone, and after some span of heat—hours, years, generations; the distinction is difficult here—a pale knot gathers around it. At first it is only a film, a little mineral lace drinking the seepage. Then the film folds. Segments stiffen. A dark shell glazes over one side. Legs, if they are legs, emerge in pairs from beneath it and begin to test the rock.

The basin makes them in several languages.

The most numerous are the glassbacks: low, many-jointed creatures no longer than a human thumb, though the oldest may grow to the breadth of a hand. Their upper shells are black, brown, or smoky green, formed in translucent layers like cooled obsidian. In certain light one can see inclusions suspended inside them: grains of iron, bubbles of vapor, tiny trapped flecks of sulfur that gleam like captive sparks. Their undersides are almost colorless. There, protected from the heat and the hard glare, their bodies are soft and pale, divided into wet folds. A glassback overturned by accident does not thrash. It slowly unfolds its limbs in a calm radial pattern, finds purchase with hooked feet, and rights itself with the patience of a stone settling.

They eat the mountain's residues. Salt blossoms. Metallic stains. The faint translucent skins that grow in the wet margins of hot cracks. Their mouths are not obvious until they feed: a small circular rasp opens beneath the front rim of the shell and turns against the rock. They leave clean, curved grazings behind them, crescents etched into the colored crust.

Their shells are never entirely their own.

A glassback may be born with a clear, fragile carapace and spend its whole life armoring it. It carries flakes of black stone against its back in the way another animal might carry leaves. It drags thin plates of mica from one fissure to another. It seeks the hottest seams, where the edges of volcanic glass soften almost imperceptibly, and presses old fractures together. More than one may work on the same shell. There is no visible debt afterward. No guarding of the repair. The repaired creature simply goes on scraping at the wall while another takes its place near the heat.

The larger ones are called, if any naming is possible, the kiln shells.

They are rounded, heavy-bodied animals the size of bowls, their backs ridged with old repairs. Their mineral armor is whitened by salts and scarred with dark fused seams. Their limbs tuck so completely beneath them that, in stillness, they are indistinguishable from the stones around the central seep. Only their warmth gives them away. In the coldest hour before dawn, when the basin's steam thins, the kiln shells retain a dim internal heat; droplets striking them turn immediately to vapor.

No one sees them feed often. They seem to live on a slower chemistry than the smaller animals. They wait beside vents and open mineral blisters with carefully selected obsidian picks. From inside they draw threads of soft, glittering matter and fold these into the seams of their bodies. Sometimes a kiln shell will remain over a crack for days, becoming crusted in fresh deposits until it appears to have rooted there. Then, at some unnoticed moment, it moves away, leaving behind a hollow shaped like itself and a small clutch of pale scale-bodied young clustered in the warmth.

Those young are the basin's restless scavengers. They have flattened white plates overlapping from head to tail, each plate formed from salts drawn out of the water. They move in quick shivering increments, gathering around fresh mineral falls and around the discarded fragments of older shells. Their limbs are fine as hairs but strong enough to lift grains much larger than their bodies. They build little arrangements in the crevices: arches of pebble, ramps of glass, shallow basins lined with reflective flakes. Whether these are nests, traps, shrines, or merely the result of their need to put certain stones beside other certain stones cannot be known.

The ribbon animals are less easy to call animals at all.

They emerge from warm water as if the water has remembered a shape. Long, dark, and nearly featureless, they wind through the crevices in loops that tighten and loosen with the pulse of the central stone. Their skin is thin enough to show internal currents moving beneath it: red-brown fluids, bright mineral grains, occasional flashes of blue-white conductivity. They have no visible eyes. Along their flanks are rows of minute pits that open and close, tasting heat, acidity, vibration, the metal content of a droplet.

A ribbon will knot itself around a rivulet and remain there while the water changes. Over time, the knot gathers a coating of crystal. Other creatures use these coated bodies as bridges, handrails, warm resting places. The ribbon does not resist. When the flow dries, it unthreads itself with immense slowness and seeks another line of water.

Some of them descend into cracks so narrow that they must become nearly liquid. They vanish for long periods into the mountain. When they return, their skins are altered: striped with new ores, studded with tiny fused stones, or trailing pale threads that cling to the walls and eventually harden into mineral tubes. It may be that the basin's creatures do not merely inhabit the rock. They carry messages through it. They may be its mobile nerves, its exploratory roots, its way of learning where the heat has gone.

There are other lives at scales too small for easy notice.

In the wet films live colonies that creep in branching stains: orange, violet, black-green. They bloom where the chemistry changes and fade where a new mineral overwhelms them. Tiny transparent shells drift through the surface water, opening and closing like eyelids. Hair-thin worms thread between crystals. Certain patches of red crust, when touched by a drop from the central stone, contract almost visibly, folding their surface inward as though protecting a hidden softness.

The larger creatures graze among these lives but do not destroy them entirely. A glassback scrapes one wall bare and leaves another untouched. The white-plated young gather a particular mineral skin and carry it to a different fissure. A kiln shell breaks open a blister whose interior has never met the air, and within days a new color begins to spread around the wound.

Their society, if it is one, has no center except the wet stone.

They do not gather around it in ranks. They do not bow, defend it, court it, or fight for access to its water. Yet every movement in the basin takes account of its pulse. When it swells, the nearest bodies become still. When it opens, creatures along the new runnel turn toward it one by one. The smallest arrive first, clustering at the margins where the water cools. The ribbons coil farther down the flow. The kiln shells shift only after the path has established itself, placing their heavy bodies in ways that redirect the water toward old cracks and thirsty shelves.

They appear indifferent to the rising droplets because their lives have been shaped by stranger obediences.

A bead climbing the wall is not a violation to them. It is a migration. The inverted lens under the overhang is not a marvel. It is a temporary surface, another kind of weather. Some of the smaller glassbacks climb toward it whenever it forms, their hooked feet finding traction on stone made slick by ascending rain. They stop beneath the lens and lift the fronts of their bodies. Droplets detach from their shells and rise away.

Perhaps they are shedding something into the upward water: spores, salts, eggs, memory.

Perhaps the mountain receives them.

In the hottest seams, one sometimes finds creatures that have ceased moving but are not dead. Their shells have fused into the wall. Their limbs have dissolved beneath mineral overflow. A ribbon may enter one and emerge bearing a new coating; white-plated young may scrape around its edges; glassbacks may incorporate pieces of its shell into their own repairs. No corpse remains separate for long in the basin. Everything is returned to heat, water, stone, and use.

And still new lives appear.

At the edge of a fresh crack, where steam sighs from blackness and a line of water begins to shine, a translucent skin gathers. A grain of salt catches in it. Then another. The skin thickens, folds, and makes the first almost invisible decision: to hold its shape against the flowing world.

That is what lives in the hidden basin: not a kingdom of beasts, not quite a colony, not merely an accident of chemistry, but a dense, patient population of small mineral bodies learning the rules of a mountain that has not yet decided what kind of world it will become.
