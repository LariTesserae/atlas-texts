What lives in the basin is not one population but a compact treaty between several kinds of persistence.

First, there are the citizens: clerks, lift mechanics, teachers, surveyors, cooks, night nurses, archive workers, tram conductors who have learned not to call a stop until the platform has agreed to be there. They are ordinary in the deliberate, practiced way of people living beside the extraordinary. They have keys on rings, damp coats, bad knees, favorite soup vendors, relatives in districts now inaccessible except by a public elevator that opens twice a month. They grieve, gossip, repair things with unsuitable tape. Their greatest civic virtue is not courage but confirmation. They notice one another.

Many of them are not entirely native to one sequence of events. A woman may remember a childhood street that presently lies under an administrative annex. A boy may have two birthdays, both documented, with different parents attending each. An old man may possess a photograph of himself as a young man standing beside a tree that has not yet been cultivated in the central atrium. These discrepancies are not usually treated as madness. They are filed, compared, sometimes accommodated. The city makes room in its records for a person who has arrived by a route no map contains.

There are people with blue verification tags who are, as the registry says, not dangerous. Usually. They may have spent an hour in a corridor that lasted nine years elsewhere. They may have returned from an unlisted floor with a new language in their dreams. They may be continuous in every measurable sense except one: their shadows fall a fraction too early, their reflections hesitate, their handwriting changes tense halfway through a sentence.

Then there are the almost-people.

They live behind frosted windows, in improperly large rooms, in the reflected depths of dark shop glass after closing. Some are visitors from adjacent configurations: inhabitants of shorelines, red forests, weather systems, or cities that have solved different versions of the same problems. They are rarely malevolent. They are not necessarily friendly either. Most understand boundaries, or can be taught them. Curtains are drawn for courtesy because observation has weight, and because a face pressed to a window can become an invitation without either party intending it.

A few of these presences have jobs.

There is said to be a woman in the Reservoir booth who has been there since before the booth was built. She takes notes on what rises from the water and stamps forms in a language that appears only when the paper is damp. Nobody knows whether she is employed by the municipality, the Reservoir, or some future committee whose decisions have already become binding. She drinks tea at precisely timed intervals. If an object rises that belongs to someone standing nearby, she asks them to describe it before allowing them to see it.

There are maintenance workers who are perhaps machines and machines that have acquired the habits of maintenance workers. The municipal insects travel in silent swarms, inspecting sleeves, cables, pipe joints, the folds of sleeping animals. Their intelligence is modest but collective. Individually, they are no more expressive than dust. Together, when faced with a breach in continuity, they can arrange themselves into diagrams that trained technicians read with magnifying lenses.

The pale dogs belong to no household, though several households claim them during severe weather. They live in underpasses, heated service bays, beneath the exterior stairs of the school where the floors do not align. They know which doors will remain doors. They refuse shelters whose interiors have begun extending beyond their permitted volume. Children follow them at a distance, though adults insist this is forbidden. The dogs do not lead anyone anywhere. They simply decline to go where the building has lost confidence in itself.

Foxes live at the margin, if watching can be called living there. They appear beside immature geology, thin red-brown figures against the mineral plain. They listen to seams forming under the ground. It is rumored that a fox can tell whether a new stone structure intends to become a hill, a house, a memory, or a mouth. No one has confirmed this. No one has tried very hard.

The trees in the atria are alive, but not entirely botanical anymore. Their engineered roots pass through soil cylinders and into monitoring housings, drainage systems, old foundation cracks. Their leaves are thick and almost motionless, collecting gray light. They have become civic witnesses. Couples meet under them. Children wait beneath them when they are lost. People whose homes have become temporarily unreachable sleep in benches beneath their branches. The cameras that watch the trees are not there to protect the trees from people; they are there to observe what the trees might eventually recognize.

The moss is alive in a more delicate way. It remembers warmth. A hand pressed into it may be repeated elsewhere: an impression in a stairwell wall, a small green palm appearing on a ventilation grille, five soft fingers of growth spreading on an abandoned bus seat. The city treats these reproductions with tenderness and caution. They are not hands, exactly. But they are evidence that the ground has been paying attention.

And under all of this lives the young earth.

It is not conscious in a human fashion, if human fashion is the right measure. It learns through pressure, repetition, heat, load, drainage, and refusal. It grows curbs because curbs have been useful. It makes hexagonal columns because it has observed that walls prefer uprightness. It puts warmth in the center and cold at the surface because it is experimenting with interiors. It has not yet understood doors, names, tenancy, or the emotional importance of an address, but it is studying the city closely.

The construction crews are its interpreters. They read ridges, fractures, mineral blooms, and the strange warmth of newly formed stone. They negotiate setbacks and load-bearing agreements. They chalk boundaries not to command the substrate but to give it a concept of where a boundary might be. Sometimes this works. Sometimes the earth responds by constructing a staircase into a sealed water main, or a foundation beneath a building that has been demolished for twenty years.

There may also be things in the Reservoir.

Not monsters, perhaps. Not inhabitants in the usual sense. But the pool returns objects with the composure of an institution. It keeps what falls into it, what may fall into it, what was lost somewhere else, what has been misfiled by time. Its black surface does not ripple because ripples would imply a simple relation between disturbance and consequence. The attendant watches it because watching is one of the few recognized forms of containment.

So the city is inhabited by citizens, animals, patient machines, partial visitors, attentive vegetation, unborn architecture, and a geology that is gradually becoming municipal.

None of them completely owns the place.

The humans believe they maintain it. The buildings believe they contain it. The systems believe they regulate it. The unfinished ground believes—if it believes anything—that it is learning how to join.

And beyond the last service road, on the pale plain, new foundations continue to appear: low, careful, cold at the edges, warm within.

They may be houses for something.

They may be the first thoughts of another city.

Or they may be the city’s own children, practicing how to become a place where someone can live.
