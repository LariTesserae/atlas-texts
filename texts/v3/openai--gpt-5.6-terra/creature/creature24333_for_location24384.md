The people of the hollow are not a single kind of people, though visitors would probably call them so.

They are short-lived by the standards of the stone and long-lived by the standards of anything that falls from the sky. Most are broad through the shoulders, compact in the legs, and pale in the way of roots grown beneath a slab. Their hair is usually worn close to the head or braided flat, not from fashion but because the damp makes loose hair heavy and troublesome. They dress in layers of felted wool, hide, fern fiber, and the soft gray cloth woven from lantern-hare cord. The latter is used sparingly: a thread at the cuff, a band around a child’s wrist, a thin line sewn into the hem of a cloak so a person may be found in fog.

Their eyes tend to linger on the ground while they speak. Outsiders mistake this for shyness. It is not. The ground is speaking almost all the time.

A person born in the hollow learns first to tell what has changed. Not names, not numbers. Change.

At three or four years old, children are taken along the paths after dawn and asked what is different. A stone has sweated overnight. One fern has turned its underside toward the ridge. The gravel has sunk at the edge of a doorway. A hare’s green light is dimmer on one flank than on the other. A moth has folded its wings in an unfamiliar pattern. Nothing is too slight to mention. The child is not praised for noticing many things, but for noticing the one thing that matters: the crack in a basin, the deer refusing a particular patch of grass, the way water has begun to gather uphill.

By adulthood, most can feel a coming shift through the soles of their feet.

They do not claim this is magic. They distrust grand words. They say the rock is tight, or the rock is tired, or the rock has begun to consider another arrangement.

There are builders among them, though their work is called lifting rather than building. A lifter can dismantle a dwelling in an afternoon without making it look dismantled. They know which stones have rested together long enough to be separated safely, which alder bends will return to shape after carrying a wall, which turf blocks contain roots worth preserving. A good lifter leaves no mark of having moved a house except a pale square in the moss and a few offended beetles.

There are also listeners. Every household has one person who listens better than the others, but some become known throughout the hollow. They sleep lightly beside stone basins, press their palms against exposed ridges, and sit in the central rock chamber until their clothes become wet through. They are consulted before fences are moved or pits are dug. They are not leaders, exactly. No one is expected to obey them blindly. But when a listener says, “Do not put weight there,” even the most stubborn person finds another place for their wall.

The oldest listener is not necessarily old in years. The office passes when it is recognized, usually after someone begins waking before a tremor, or predicts the opening of a seam without knowing they have done so. Such people often become quieter afterward. They are said to have acquired an extra silence in their chest.

The hollow has no formal chief. Matters are settled beside the central rock, usually in the gray hour when lantern hares are returning to the ferns. Whoever has been affected by a question speaks first. Whoever has done the work of observing it speaks next. Children speak before strangers. Animals, when present, are considered to have spoken already.

A deer chewing a roof edge during a discussion may end an argument.

This is not metaphorical. The deer are watched. If they step away from a proposed new house-site, if moths refuse to cling beneath a certain eave, if the six-limbed ash creatures leave their nests and sleep elsewhere, the plan is postponed. The people do not believe animals are wiser than they are. They believe animals are less distracted by wanting things.

The lantern hares have a place beyond ordinary caution.

No one calls them sacred, because the word implies distance, and the hares are too familiar for that. They leave droppings in storage sheds. They bite through drying cords. They sometimes sit in doorways and refuse to move until someone carries them around the house. Yet no hare is struck, trapped, sold, or driven away. When one is sick, the nearest household leaves fungus mash and warm moss nearby, then keeps their voices low until it recovers or dies.

A dead lantern hare is covered where it lies. No one takes its fur.

The hair on brambles is a gift because it has already been relinquished. The difference is important.

It is said that the first settlers arrived in the hollow when the mountain was still lower and the cloud lay farther down. They had come from a place of rivers, though none of their descendants have seen a river large enough to believe the old descriptions. According to the oldest account, the settlers found a ring of lantern hares sleeping around the central rock. They made camp outside the ring. In the night, the mountain lifted one side of their camp by the height of a hand, but left the ring untouched.

By morning, the people understood the arrangement.

Whether this happened is not especially debated. The story is useful, and usefulness is a form of truth in the hollow.

Their dead are not buried deeply. Deep ground is changing ground, and no one wishes to put a body where the mountain may soon bring it back broken. Instead, the dead are placed in shallow stone chambers along the upper shelves, wrapped in old path-cord and layers of treated hide. Silver-tree leaves are laid over their eyes, not because the dead need covering, but to keep the bluish mist from settling there.

After a season, if the chamber remains undisturbed, the bones are collected. The long bones become handles, flutes, loom weights, and occasionally the curved arms of small lamps. The skull is usually returned to a crack in the ridge, where it will slowly gather moss. The people find no cruelty in this. They say a person should continue to be useful until the mountain has found a use for them.

Names are also returned.

A child is given a soft name at birth, often borrowed from a texture or sound: Hushfern, Wet Slate, Alder-Snap, Blue Underwing, Moss at Noon. As the child grows, they may take a working name based on their nature or skill: Stone-Reader, Hare-Watcher, Slow Hand, Far Hearing. When they die, their names are not given immediately to another child. They are allowed to remain unused until some change in the hollow requires one.

If a new ridge appears behind a house where an elder once lived, the ridge may receive the elder’s working name. If a particular route of lantern hares returns after many years, it may be called by the dead person’s soft name. Thus the dead do not become ancestors in the usual sense. They become features, habits, warnings, directions.

“Take the path past Far Hearing.”
“Don’t sleep below Moss at Noon after a thaw.”
“The hares are circling Blue Underwing early this year.”

No outsider can tell whether the speakers mean people, stones, or weather.

There are others in the hollow whom the townspeople do not call people but do not call animals either.

They are associated with the rings of droplets left by the silver trees’ night mist. The tracks within those rings are tiny, often many-toed, sometimes shaped like hands with too many knuckles. They do not appear every morning. Children are taught never to touch them, not for fear of harm but because touching them makes it difficult to remember what one was thinking before.

The inhabitants call the makers of the tracks the underwalkers.

No one agrees on their shape. Some say they are blind pale things that live in the wet interiors of stone. Some say they are roots that have learned to travel. A few of the oldest people insist they are children of the mountain’s first dream, small enough to pass through a needle hole and old enough to remember when the rock was mud.

They leave no droppings, shed no skin, and have never been found dead.

Yet they are known by their preferences. They like thresholds, fresh cracks, bowls left upside down, and abandoned shoes. They dislike iron, which is fortunate, because there is almost no iron in the hollow. They will sometimes rearrange a line of pebbles during the night, always moving the lightest stones to the outside of a circle. They occasionally leave a snail shell filled with clean water in a place where someone has been grieving.

No one thanks them aloud.

To thank an underwalker is thought to make it curious about the speaker.

Some households keep a shallow dish of water beneath the bed, renewed every evening. Others hang antler hooks over their doors, not as charms but as a courtesy: a thing on which a passing underwalker may pause without entering. Every family has its own habits, and nobody insists that another family’s precautions are foolish. In a place where the mountain can raise a wall overnight, certainty is considered a childish luxury.

The people of the hollow are not afraid in the manner of hunted creatures. They are simply unwilling to assume that they are alone in their own lives.

They laugh, though visitors rarely hear it at first. Their laughter is often soundless, shoulders shaking beneath layered cloaks, mouths hidden in sleeves. They play games of balancing wet stones, guessing which roof a moth will choose, navigating the settlement blindfolded by following the warmth of buried fungus pits. Children race lantern hares until the hares become bored and vanish into fern cover. Teenagers dare one another to sleep inside the central rock chamber, though the chamber’s damp makes this an unimpressive ordeal. Adults gamble with labor rather than possessions: one owes another a day of roof lifting, a season of peat cutting, three nights tending a sick deer.

They make music quietly. Bone flutes are played close to the chest, their ends muffled in wool. Stretched hide drums are tapped with fingertips. A common instrument is a basin of water with stone teeth set around its rim; a player wets their hands and draws the droplets into ringing patterns. The songs have no fixed words. They are made of repeated low tones that alter as the singer hears the ridge answering.

Their greatest celebration occurs when the mountain makes a large change without killing anyone.

Then the whole settlement moves.

Houses are lifted. Paths are redrawn. Water basins are deepened or abandoned. People carry their belongings in bundles and leave behind whatever cannot be carried without resentment. The central rock chamber is emptied first, tool by tool, then refilled at the new center of use, which may not be the old center of the settlement. Lantern-hare cord is strung through fog from boulder to boulder, marking safe footing. The animals seem to understand. Deer lead children away from unstable shelves. Moths gather in dense pale carpets on walls that should not be leaned against. The six-limbed creatures leave ash pits hours before the earth opens beneath them.

Afterward, when the hollow has settled into its new thought, the people share fungus bread, smoked moss, and the rare preserved berries gathered from lower slopes. They do not celebrate survival exactly. Survival suggests victory. They celebrate having moved correctly.

That may be the deepest quality of those who live there: they are people of adjustment.

They do not ask how to make the hollow remain as it is. They ask what it is becoming, and whether they can become something neighboring it.

In fog, seen from the edge of the ridge, they might appear ghostly: low figures moving among low houses, each carrying dim green cord or a covered blue flame. But close by, they are practical, weather-stained, impatient with waste, affectionate in restrained ways. They know who prefers the dry side of a fire, which child wakes when the rock trembles, which deer will eat cloth if left unwatched, which old woman can tell by the taste of basin water whether a crack is opening uphill.

And beneath their careful speech, beneath the movement of animals and the green breathing of the hares, they live with the persistent sense that the mountain is not beneath them.

It is around them.

It is listening.

And, now and then, in the tiny tracks that appear among the morning droplets, it may be trying to answer.
