What lives on that wet shoulder of mountain is not one people so much as an agreement among many lives.

The human inhabitants call themselves, in their own language, something that means roughly *those who keep the water moving*. Outsiders give them other names: moss-people, slope-folk, root-builders, duck-herders, cloud-dwellers. They tolerate most of these with the patient amusement of people who know that travelers from dry places will mistake dampness for hardship. To them, a dry wall is an unfinished wall, a silent gutter is a warning, and a house without something growing through it feels temporary.

They are compact, sure-footed people, not necessarily small but shaped by generations of climbing, carrying, crouching under eaves, and balancing loads on wet stone. Their calves are hard, their toes spread broad in soft leather sandals or bare on familiar paths, and even the elders move with an economy that looks like caution until one sees how quickly they can cross a rain-slick roof. Children learn the settlement first from below: crawling through root tunnels, following chickens beneath platforms, wading in ankle-deep channels, being shouted at for damming a drinking run with stones. By the time they are old enough to carry a basket, they know which bridge rails are ornamental and which must take a person’s full weight.

Their clothing is built for dampness rather than for elegance, though it becomes elegant in use. Wool is heavily oiled. Cloaks are woven from coarse dark fiber and layered with waxed bark cloth. Hooded rain capes hang from every doorway, patched so often that a good old cape may carry bits of three generations’ clothing. Most people wear short tunics or wrapped skirts that leave the knees free, with leggings tied close against leeches, mud, and thorny brush. In wet weather, the whole village seems to wear the colors of its surroundings: cedar-brown, lichen-gray, pond-green, duck-egg blue, the rust-red of old iron-rich mud. Bright dyes are prized because the mountain swallows color so quickly. A child in yellow wool can be found in fog. A trader wearing red can be recognized from three terraces away.

Their hair is usually kept bound, braided, knotted, wrapped in cloth, or pinned with polished bone, copper, and carved wood. Loose hair catches rain, smoke, burrs, and the fingers of goats. Many adults wear small practical ornaments: fish-bone needles tucked into collars, awls on cords, keys to store sheds, herb pouches, little whistles used to call dogs or children through fog. The wealthier households may have bronze clasps, beads from the valleys, or strings of bright foreign shells. But the most respected things are often not portable: a healthy living wall, a well-kept pond, a roof tree that has survived three storms, a stair that does not shift beneath the feet.

They are gardeners, herders, carpenters, ditch-cutters, animal healers, mushroom-tenders, basket-makers, roof-climbers, and negotiators with roots. Few occupations are cleanly separate. The woman who raises ducks may also oversee a pond gate. The person known for carving spoons may be the one everyone summons when a retaining wall begins to bulge. A child sent to gather herbs learns which leaves stop a cough, which make soap, which soothe a goat’s swollen udder, and which must never be handled before rain because their sap raises blisters.

Some families are known by the water they tend rather than by a house name. There are the people of the Upper Spillway, the Reed-Pond household, those beneath the Alder Steps, those who hold the Blackstone Wall. A marriage may join two hearths, but it also joins work obligations: whose channels are cleared, whose ducks may graze where, who is responsible if a garden terrace fails. Arguments are rarely about abstractions. They are about a gate left open, a pig in cabbage beds, a gutter diverted too sharply, a goat tethered where it chewed through the new growth of a shared wall.

Their oldest experts are not chiefs in the manner valley people imagine. They are remembered for what they can read.

One old woman can smell a coming slope-fall in the soil before anyone sees it. Another can put her palm to a living wall and tell where rot has entered its roots. There are pond-keepers who know the difference between ordinary frog silence and the silence that means bad water. There are roof-growers who can bend a young trunk over a doorway without killing it, then return for years to guide its branches until it bears the weight of thatch and winter snow. There are weather-watchers who read cloud not as omen but as direction, weight, temperature, and timing: rain in an hour, wind by nightfall, hail only on the upper terraces, fog low enough to lose a child in.

Their reverence, where it exists, is practical and intimate. They do not pray for the mountain to spare them, because mountains do not bargain. They honor the dead by returning useful things to circulation: tools passed on, wool unpicked and rewoven, house beams incorporated into newer walls, ashes added to gardens. Some dead are buried in the upper groves where the ground is stable; others, especially those whose families keep living-root houses, are placed beneath chosen trees. A sapling may be bent over the grave and trained into an arch. Years later, children run beneath it without knowing whose memory holds up the green doorway.

They have songs for labor because labor must keep time. Pond-clearing songs have short repeated phrases, easy to shout across water. Wall-building songs are slower, paced to lifting stone. There are mock songs for goats, increasingly elaborate insults for pigs, lullabies that list the names of safe stepping stones in order. Their stories tend to be crowded with animals that are clever but not always admirable: the duck who outargues a widow, the raven who steals an iron nail and thereby saves a roof, the goat who eats the village treaty and is appointed witness to the next one.

The animals live among them not as a background but as neighbors of unequal standing.

The goats are thieves, climbers, escape artists, and reluctant providers. Every household claims its own are the cleverest, usually after those goats have done something infuriating. Goatherds carry long hooked staffs and pockets full of salt, and have the permanently raised voices of people calling animals downhill. Children learn goat names before they learn the names of distant kin.

The pigs are treated with a mixture of affection and fatalism. They know the paths to kitchens, the places where warm ash is thrown, the weak points in every pen. A sow with piglets is granted more practical authority than many adults. If she chooses to give birth beneath a stair, the stair becomes hers until the piglets can walk. People detour.

The ducks belong partly to households and partly to the ponds themselves. They are counted, traded, argued over, and blamed for fouling water they are not permitted to enter. Their eggs are valuable, their feathers are used for bedding and fletching, and their ceaseless noise is so common that visitors sometimes wake in the night when it stops.

The shaggy cattle are respected more quietly. They are warmth, milk, hide, manure, patience, and danger in a narrow lane. Their sheds are often built into the lower parts of houses, so that animal heat rises through floorboards. On cold fog mornings, families may wake to the sound of breathing below them and the heavy sweet smell of cattle steam.

Dogs are essential. They are bred for wet feet, sharp ears, and good sense around cliffs. No one expects them to be obedient in the abstract; a good dog knows when a command is foolish. They guard children at the outer platforms, drive goats from gardens, alert sleepers when boar come near the food stores, and bark themselves hoarse at thunder. The oldest dogs, deaf and gray around the muzzle, are allowed to sleep beneath the communal hall.

Cats are less loved publicly and more loved privately. They keep grain sheds free of mice, sleep in woven baskets, disappear for days during mating season, and return with the conviction that they have never been absent. A household that loses its cat may borrow another’s, though everyone pretends this is a matter of pest control rather than attachment.

And there are lives the people do not own but make room for: swallows nesting beneath bridges, frogs in the pond stones, bees in hollow trunks, eels in hidden pools, ravens on the poles. A raven that learns a person’s voice may be fed scraps for years. A swarm of bees in a wall is not destroyed unless it threatens children; it is smoked gently out and given a better hollow farther from the paths. Spiders are tolerated in roof corners because they eat flies. Snakes are watched carefully, left alone if harmless, killed if venomous, and afterward discussed in detail by everyone who saw them.

The settlement also holds a population of temporary lives: valley traders with salt and metalwork; wandering healers seeking mountain herbs; marriage kin from lower farms; hunters waiting out rain; children sent uphill to be fostered with relatives; widows who have no terrace elsewhere; injured travelers who can work a loom even if they cannot walk far. The place is too small to hide anyone for long. Newcomers are known first by how they step: whether they hurry over moss, whether they look up before crossing beneath a loaded roof, whether they understand that a bowl left outside will fill with rain, leaf mold, bird droppings, and perhaps a frog.

The people who thrive there are not those who conquer the mountain. Such people leave, or die tired.

The people who thrive are those who can accept that a wall needs tending like a goat, that a path may move, that a house is partly a plant, that a pig may alter the day’s plans, that the water must always be given somewhere to go. They are quick to laugh when dryland visitors complain that nothing stays clean. They would say nothing is meant to stay clean. Things are meant to be used, washed, mended, fed back into the slope, and grown again.

At evening, when fog rises from below and the outer houses become dim green shapes, the settlement’s inhabitants gather in layers. Ducks complain in the ponds. Goats knock their horns against stall rails. Children are called indoors by name and by lineage. Smoke rises through leaf roofs. Someone carries a bucket uphill because a channel has clogged. Someone else walks downhill with a lantern to check the bridge. Dogs make their rounds. Ravens settle black against the pale cloud.

And all through it, beneath people and animal feet, beneath houses and roots and stone stairs, the water continues moving: held, guided, borrowed, argued over, thanked without ceremony, and never entirely tamed.
