What lives there is not one population but a treaty of populations, none of them fully in charge.

The people call themselves residents, workers, tenants, shift families, lift-side, west-wall, depot-born. Very few call themselves miners anymore, though the town was made for mining and still sends ore upward in sealed bins. The word has become too narrow. A miner is supposed to go underground and come back. These people have arranged schools, marriages, debts, kitchens, arguments, elections, and funerals beneath the ground. They are not visitors to the chamber. They have become one of its recurring conditions, like seepage or heat.

Most have the look of people who live near steam: damp hair, softened clothes, a permanent shine at the temples. Their hands are marked less by hard labor than by maintenance—small burns, rust cuts, ingrained grease in the creases of the knuckles, white mineral scars where the water has dried on skin. They carry keys in quantities that would seem absurd aboveground. Every resident knows a few valves, a few breaker panels, a few doors that must be kicked before they open. Children learn the pipe colors before they learn all the letters. Old people can identify a failing pump by the way cups tremble on a table.

They do not move through the town quickly unless something has gone wrong. The wet concrete punishes running, the shadows hide hoses and dropped tools, and every shortcut has a history. People walk in the marked lanes with lunch tins, coil lamps, masks hooked under their chins, babies wrapped against the damp. They pause under particular sections of ceiling and listen. They greet animals as colleagues.

The dogs belong to nobody, which is how they belong to the town.

They are descended from security dogs, abandoned pets, and whatever came down in freight cages during the years when the lift still brought whole families to work the expansion. Their coats are all the colors of corrosion: copper, red mud, old gold, charcoal dampened black. They sleep in rotating groups under stairs, beneath warm generator housings, beside the bakery vent when the bakery is operating. They know shift changes better than clocks. At first lift they appear near the depot; at meal hours they occupy the lane outside the kitchens; during a ventilation failure they vanish into cracks and culverts before anyone notices the air turning bad.

Each dog has several names. One name belongs to the person who feeds it. One belongs to the children. One belongs to the maintenance crews, usually based on a fault it once discovered or caused: Tripwire, Sump, Relay, Bite-Through, Little Alarm. There is always one older dog with a white muzzle who is said to remember the town before some disaster no one agrees on. It is said that when this dog refuses to cross the square, people take the long way around.

The goats are more openly political.

They came down as livestock, then became scavengers, then became citizens by attrition. They live on the high dry ledges near the ventilation works, where their hooves find purchase on mineral shelves and old cable trays. They have eaten insulation, laundry, notices, survey flags, wiring sleeves, fungus, cardboard, the soft edges of work boots, and once the payroll records for an entire quarter. Yet the residents defend them fiercely. A goat’s judgment of a surface is trusted. If the herd abandons a ledge, someone inspects it. If they crowd together in a place that should be empty, it usually means warm air is leaking there, and warm air means a pipe, a shaft, or a future repair.

There are goats old enough to have acquired reputations. One black doe is known to enter the control room whenever the door is left open, stand by the monitors, and stare at the shifting colored lines. A broad-horned wether sleeps above the east pump house and must be negotiated with whenever a technician needs access to the fan controls. The town has tried fencing them out of things. The goats have taught it the limits of fencing.

The pigeons are the only inhabitants who remember the shape of the upper world.

Not clearly, perhaps. Not as a place of sky or trees or distance. But they retain an upwardness in themselves. Their nests are packed into beam joints, duct collars, crane housings, the undersides of platforms where the heat rises. Their feathers are gray beneath the gray, dusted with limestone and soot until they seem made of the cavern’s own powder. When disturbed, they lift in a mass that makes the chamber briefly look vast. Their wings strike the darkness above the lamps, and for a moment there is the sound of rain where there is no rain.

People watch them more closely than they admit. Pigeons are unreliable in every ordinary way—foul, noisy, stupid around machinery—but their fear is precise. When the air in a shaft turns sour, they leave. When a vibration begins too low for a human body to register, they shift from one beam to another and settle facing the same direction. When they disappear from a section of upper ironwork, someone eventually finds a crack, a gas pocket, a loose panel, a hanging slab.

The cats are not loved in the same way as dogs. They are employed.

Every kitchen, bunkhouse, store room, and workshop has at least one. They come in from the lift in carriers or hidden in supply crates; they disappear for days; they return heavier, narrower-eyed, and unwilling to be handled. Their territories are marked by warmth. A boiler cat will not become a pantry cat. A pantry cat will not cross into the washhouse district. There are cats that live almost entirely in the crawlspaces behind walls, seen only as a pair of eyes at floor level or a tail withdrawing beneath a pipe. Their litters appear under lockers and among rolled insulation. Their deaths are noted quietly, because a missing cat means the rats will begin behaving differently.

The rats are not merely numerous. They are organized by routes.

They know which pipes heat first after a power interruption. They know when grain will be unloaded. They know which doors fail to latch in damp weather, which drains overflow after pump service, which apartments leave food cooling by the windows. Their runs have altered the town. Pallets are raised because of them. Wiring is sleeved because of them. Flour is stored in metal bins because of them. A new wall is built with rat gaps in mind before anyone thinks about its paint.

There are children who keep rat maps, drawn in pencil on the backs of old forms. They trade sightings as others might trade cards: a white one near the settling tanks, a one-eyed giant in South Cut No. 4, a nest discovered inside the hollow base of the crane. Adults tell them not to encourage the animals. Adults also leave scraps in certain corners, especially in the winter months they do not have belowground but recognize by changes in supply schedules and the cold smell that comes down the lift shaft.

Then there are the insects, which may be the true majority.

Moths inhabit the lamps so completely that every bulb has its own weather. The ones around the lift are broad and pale, occasionally arriving with cargo from above; the ones farther inside are smaller, brown-gray, warmed into activity by machine heat. They die against the lamp cages, accumulate in the grooves of metal housings, and are swept away in little drifts that look like ash. Yet each evening more arrive. Nobody knows where they breed in such numbers. Somewhere beyond the ducts and unfinished cuts, there must be chambers no person has entered for years, perhaps ever, where the dark beats softly with wings.

The roaches prefer the parts of town people pretend are sealed. They live under the boiler skins, behind the lunch-hall walls, inside old electrical boxes whose power has been disconnected but whose warmth remains. They are quick enough to seem imaginary. A worker sees one, curses, turns away, and then wonders whether it was a loose screw, a drip, a tremor in the eye.

The crickets have a more ceremonial role. No one sees them, but everyone knows when they have stopped. Their sound is threaded through the pumps and fans, almost beneath hearing. When it vanishes from a particular wall, people become uneasy. It may mean flooding. It may mean a chemical leak. It may mean nothing at all. But the silence feels intentional.

The pale gnats live over water that is not supposed to be there. They rise from drainage channels and foundation pits in soft veils, gathering where lamps reflect on black surfaces. They are harmless individually. In a cloud, they make a person feel briefly submerged. They enter ears, settle on eyelashes, coat wet paint. They are especially thick near the old pit at the square’s edge, where the town’s reflected lights shake among mineral colors. Rats drink through them. Dogs snap at them in their sleep. Goats stare through them at their own warped silhouettes.

There are also things that live there without being counted as alive.

The moss lives, certainly, in the strict sense: green-black beards along wet seams, soft patches behind pipes, small velvet territories in places no broom reaches. It survives on almost no light, taking what it can from bulbs and welders and the pale leak of daylight around the lift shaft. The algae are bolder. They turn concrete slick, fur the rims of tanks, make their own low country in the channels. Residents hate them because they cause falls, but they recognize them as a kind of proof: where algae grow, water has chosen a route.

Fungus lives in the walls, in the bedding, beneath old paint, inside the insulation wrapping the fig tree’s heat pipes. Some kinds are scraped away in masks. Some are harvested by a few older residents who know which pale shelves are edible after boiling and which will make a person ill for days. There is a fungus that grows near the warmest exhaust duct and glows faintly when fresh. Children are forbidden to touch it. Children touch it whenever they can.

And the town itself lives there, in the way a body lives around a foreign object.

It sweats, clots, scars, makes new channels. It sends its people to repair its edges. It grows hard around leaks and soft around heat. It has memories embedded in mismatched bolts, in painted arrows leading to doors that no longer exist, in a depot sign naming a cut that was never made or was made and later swallowed. It is inhabited by the workers, certainly, but it also inhabits them. They sleep with fan noise in their bones. They judge time by lift bells and pump cycles. They dream of ceilings.

The newest arrivals are easiest to recognize. They look upward too often. They flinch at every drop. Their voices carry too far because they have not learned to speak into the cavern without waking it. After a few months, they begin naming water by location and temperament. After a year, they know which darkness contains dogs, which contains rats, which contains only unused equipment. After longer, they develop the town’s patient suspicion of dry things.

Eventually, if they stay, they stop saying they are underground.

They say they are home.
