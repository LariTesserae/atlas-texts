What lives there has learned not to announce itself.

The first inhabitants are the mats.

They are not single plants, though from a distance each basin seems covered by one continuous dark organism. Up close, the green surface divides into innumerable folded bodies: tough ribbons, frilled fans, spiraled filaments, and close-packed cups of waxy tissue. Each is rooted only lightly in the dust. Beneath them is a felt of finer growth, pale as bone and dense with mineral grains, extending through cracks and pores in the stone.

They live as much below the surface as above it. During the day their upper leaves darken, close, and turn their narrow edges toward the sun. At night they loosen. Frost settles into their folds. A mat in the dawn is a field of minute glasswork, every groove bright with ice. By morning the frost has vanished from sight, but it has passed inward, carried along threads too small to see.

No one crossing the country quickly would understand the mats. They appear passive. They do not bend much in the wind. They do not flower in the ordinary sense. Yet entire basins change color over the course of a season as different layers rise and withdraw. In colder months they become almost black, absorbing what little warmth the sun gives. During the mineral snow, they fade to iron-green and seal themselves beneath a crust of their own secretions. When the air clears, they open again in patterns that follow invisible warmth beneath the ground.

Their oldest portions may be thousands of years old. A mat does not die all at once. Its outer edges dry, bleach, and become dust; its center keeps dividing. Sometimes a whole patch lifts in a storm and rolls across a flat in a slow green coil, shedding fragments as it goes. Those fragments lodge in cracks, take hold, and begin new provinces.

Among the mats live the small clear-bodied insects.

They are called insects only because they have six limbs and jointed shells. In many ways they are closer to wind-borne crystals that have learned to move. Their outer skins are nearly transparent, except for dark bands along the legs and a little colored mass in the abdomen: amber, blue-green, deep red, or the cloudy violet of bruised mineral. In sunlight they disappear and reappear as they crawl, visible only when their bodies catch a particular angle of light.

They spend most of their lives hidden in the warm interior layers of the vegetation. There they feed on sweet mineral films produced by the mats, on fungal threads, on the microscopic crusts that form wherever frost repeatedly gathers and melts. When the surface becomes warm enough, they emerge in great numbers. They do not fly far. Their wings, if wings they are, are mostly thin static membranes used to leap from one fold of vegetation to another. Dust clings to them. Pollen clings to the dust. They carry both through the low forest of curled leaves.

At sunset, their color seems to drain away. One by one they slip down between the folds, leaving the mats apparently empty.

There are also the burrowing lights.

These are seldom seen directly. In the sheltered portions of the basins, where geothermal breath rises slowly through porous stone, small clustered organisms grow beneath the crust. They feed on heat gradients, on sulfur compounds, on metals dissolved in the faintest films of subsurface moisture. Their bodies are soft and branching, with translucent mineral skins. By day they are invisible under the ground. At night, especially after a sharp freeze, they give off a dim blue-green glow.

The light does not shine steadily. It pulses through the crust in long, patient intervals, passing from one cluster to another beneath the stone. A traveler might mistake it for moonlight caught in cracks, until it moves.

In some dry amphitheaters, the dark circles at the centers are not only warmed ground. They are colonies of these buried lives. The red-leaved shrubs that grow around them depend upon their chemical waste. The colonies, in turn, are fed by the fallen leaves and roots of the shrubs. Neither can travel. Both spread slowly outward, generation by generation, making rings so regular that they seem arranged by an intelligence with a taste for geometry.

The tall columnar groves have their own inhabitants.

Inside the mineral bark of each trunk are hollow chambers, dry and warm by comparison with the open air. Tiny pale crawlers live there in communal swarms. They have broad, flattened bodies and many fine legs, which they use to climb the ribbed inner walls. They harvest flakes of bark, fungal growth, and the salts that gather around old wounds in the trunk.

Their most important work is hidden. They carry spores from one columnar plant to another, not across the open air but along roots and underground fractures. When a grove is healthy, its members are linked beneath the plateau by a web of pale tissues and crawling things. A tree that has lost too much light or suffered too much cold may be sustained for years by its neighbors. Its leaves will thin, its crown will become sparse, but it will not fall. There is little in that country to make a fallen trunk useful.

Sometimes, in the deepest cold of night, the groves make sounds.

Not a rustling. Not branches knocking together. The sound comes from the contraction of their ribbed tissues as trapped vapor moves through internal channels. It is a sequence of soft clicks, low creaks, and distant hollow notes. A whole grove can seem to be speaking quietly to itself. The sound travels farther than it should in the thin air.

The pale grazers are among the few things that truly cross the land.

They are not hurried creatures. Their bodies are long and low, their necks carried close to the horizontal, their heads narrow enough to move between the folded vegetation without tearing it. The broad feet that support them are lined with flexible pads, each pad divided by mineral-hard ridges. They leave shallow tracks that weather away quickly, but their passage is known to the plants. Wherever they graze, the mats grow back denser at the edges. Their waste is dry, dark, and rich in salts, often becoming the beginning of a new green patch.

A grazer may spend half a year walking from one viable basin to another. It eats little at any one place and sleeps in the lee of stone shelves, folding its limbs beneath itself until it resembles a pale outcrop. During mineral snowstorms they gather, not in herds exactly, but in loose silent assemblages around warm ground. They stand with their backs toward the wind, their heads lowered, receiving the gray accumulation along the ridges of their hides.

They recognize one another at great distances. Not by sound, for they have almost no call, but by scent carried low along the ground. When two meet on an open plain, they may stand together for a day or more before separating again. Their young travel beside their mothers for several cold seasons, learning which apparently empty slopes contain hidden mats, which ravines trap killing frost, which pale crusts will bear their weight.

The gliders belong to the cliffs.

Their wings are dark because they are made to gather heat. Spread in sunlight, they drink warmth from the rock below and from the hard brilliance above. The rigid crests over their faces protect their eyes from dust and glare, but they also serve as resonant chambers. Their rare calls are not simple cries. Each dry note contains a pattern of clicks and overtones that carries through ravines and along stone walls.

The gliders use these calls to map the country.

They do not see the land only as shape and color. They know it as a field of returning sound: a cliff that gives back a sharp answer, an arch that produces a hollow delayed tone, a newly fallen slope that swallows the call entirely. In this way they notice changes no ground-dweller could detect. A collapse far beyond the horizon may alter the voice of a valley. A new fissure opening in a wall may become known to them long before it is visible.

Their nests are shallow affairs, lined with mineral flakes, shed bark, and the translucent shells of the small mat-dwellers. They lay very few eggs. A pair may return to the same shelf for decades, though the shelf itself is slowly changing beneath them. When a nesting wall finally crumbles, the gliders do not mourn it in any visible way. They circle once or twice through the falling dust, then depart to find another ledge.

There are things in the ravines that do not come out.

In narrow cracks where the sun reaches for only a few minutes each day, pale jointed scavengers wait among the fallen stone. They are segmented, almost flat, and so close in color to the rock that they become visible only when they move. They feed on anything that has died: insects caught in frost, grazing creatures weakened by age, roots exposed by erosion, spores gathered in sheltered seams. They can survive for years on almost nothing.

Their young are carried by the wind.

When the season is right, the adults climb to the upper lips of the ravines and release dry, fragile capsules. The capsules split in the moving air. Inside are tiny forms with filamented limbs, light enough to be drawn into the wind’s long courses. Most are lost. A few settle in a suitable crack a hundred miles away and become still until food arrives.

The land has slower animals too, if animal is the right word.

On some gypsum flats, especially where dawn frost is dependable, broad disks lie half-buried in the powder. They look like mineral plates: dull white, faintly ridged, sometimes several yards across. For years they may not visibly change position. At night, however, they extend a fringe of transparent feeding threads into the frost-darkened air. The threads gather crystals, dust, spores, and microscopic life. By morning they draw back beneath the disk.

Over decades, these disk-creatures migrate across the flats at the pace of a few handspans per year. Behind them they leave a subtly altered surface, enriched with darker minerals and scattered with dormant seeds. The green mats often appear in their wake. No one knows whether the disks cultivate the mats deliberately, or whether the mats merely follow the conditions they leave behind.

There may be no distinction in that country between a creature’s intention and its endurance.

The oldest living things are probably inside the stone.

Deep in the cliff bands, where copper-green layers meet black volcanic glass, there are mineral-fed colonies whose bodies extend through veins of rock. They grow by dissolving one substance and precipitating another, slowly widening some cracks while sealing others. They live in darkness, though they respond to the daily heating and cooling of the surface. Their movements are measured in centuries. A wall that seems solid can contain a living network older than the ravine around it.

When such a colony reaches an exposed face, it sometimes forms thin fans or branching crusts of color: blue-black, rust-red, pearl gray. These are not lichens, though they resemble them. They drink almost nothing. They consume the difference between night and day, between sunlit stone and shadowed stone, between cold air and the slow warmth below.

They are responsible for some of the land’s quietest changes.

A crack opens a fraction wider. A surface softens. A mineral vein brightens. A flake detaches in a season of wind. Eventually a cliff wall falls—not suddenly in the life of the wall, but suddenly to everything that watches it. The fallen stone becomes shelter, then dust, then soil enough for a mat. A mat brings insects. Insects bring pollen. Grazers come to feed. The gliders begin using the new ledges.

Nothing arrives alone.

Even the empty places have inhabitants. Fine crusts of salt-tolerant life lie over the pale floors of the amphitheaters, waking briefly under frost and sleeping under sun. Spores collect in the shadow behind a single stone and wait years for conditions that may never return. Minute shelled organisms drift in dust, sealing themselves against cold and opening only when a film of moisture touches them. The air carries them from basin to basin, though it carries them so slowly and so selectively that they may spend generations in transit.

What lives in the high country is sparse because it must be.

Each creature requires distance: distance from the next feeding patch, from the next warm fracture, from the next reliable frostfall. Each plant requires a particular fold of shade, a mineral seam, a basin where wind deposits rather than removes. The country cannot support crowds. It supports continuities.

A pale grazer crossing a flat may be the only large body visible for a hundred miles. A single glider may ride above an escarpment all afternoon without seeing another of its kind. A grove may stand alone on a plateau, each trunk rooted into the same deep chemical source, waiting through seasons of clear sky.

Yet none of them are solitary in the full sense.

The frost belongs to the mats. The mats belong to the grazers. The grazers leave their salts for the soil. The soil feeds the shrubs. The shrubs shelter the insects and feed the buried lights. The buried lights warm the ground where creatures survive the mineral snow. The wind carries spores, shells, dust, and the smallest beginnings onward.

The continent is full of lives that touch one another rarely, gently, and across immense spans of time.

That is how they live there: not by conquering the stone, but by entering its long patience.
