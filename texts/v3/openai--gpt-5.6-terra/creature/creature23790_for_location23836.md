What lives there is not one species, though from a distance it can seem to be.

The first inhabitants are the mats themselves: the old green country spread over stone. They are not passive vegetation. Beneath each visible shelf of moss, each shrub-rampart, each trembling plain of sedge and water, there are layers of decision—or something near enough to decision that the difference matters only to outsiders. Roots sample pressure. Hyphae carry warmth, salts, alarms, remembered seasons. Peat holds impressions with more fidelity than wood holds rings. A hoofprint made by an animal five hundred years dead may remain active below the surface, a compressed route through which water, gas, larvae, and signals still prefer to travel.

The land has no central mind. It has innumerable partial minds, slow enough that weather is part of their thought. A lichen shelf may spend a century detecting the angle of a prevailing wind and another century responding by changing its mineral skin. A peat basin may recognize the chemistry of a particular herd and open wetter ground before the herd arrives. The enormous vegetative structures on the ridges—those bent, trunkless walls—are colonies of many kinds bound together by habit: woody stems, mosses, buried fungal webs, seed plants, bacteria that make heat, tiny pale animals that excavate and fertilize them from within. They have lived so closely for so long that none can be usefully called separate.

They make shelter, but not in the way a house makes shelter.

A depression in a moss wall may close around a storm-beaten grazer and keep it warm until morning. A root lattice may raise itself beneath a nesting bird’s eggs, lifting them above floodwater. A whole reed bed may lean over a carcass, not to conceal it, but to give its dying heat somewhere to go. The country is full of such accommodations. They are not mercy. Nor are they hunger. They are simply the old expertise of a living system that has learned that what remains close becomes useful.

The great pale grazers belong to this expertise.

They are called, in the few places where sound-names are possible, **mirehorns**, though they have no horns in the ordinary sense. From their shoulders rise paired shelves of hardened fiber, broad and ridged, in which plants root during the wet season. Seen through rain, a herd can be mistaken for a migrating strip of upland: pale hummocks advancing through darker moss. Their many joints allow them to distribute their immense weight across the unstable ground. They step delicately, almost tenderly, and the bog accepts them.

Their mouths are elaborate combs. They graze the surface without stripping it, drawing out old moss, seed husks, fungal cords, and the bloated soft bodies of things too slow to move away. What passes through them returns to the ground changed: compacted, warmed, seeded, full of mineral grit. A mirehorn does not merely feed in the country. It edits it. The troughs it leaves become channels. Its dung is the beginning of floating islands. Its shed coat becomes bird nesting, insect refuge, root scaffolding, and eventually peat.

Young mirehorns are born almost black, slick with iron-rich fluid and covered in folded skin. For their first years they live in the dense fur of older animals, climbing among the fibers and feeding on the small creatures that live there. Only after they have acquired a covering of moss and pale weatherproof strands do they walk on the open ground. The oldest carry entire communities: flowering patches along the spine, pools in the shoulder hollows, colonies of insects that glow beneath their belly fibers at dusk. When one dies, it falls slowly. The country does not let a body strike hard.

There are other herds, more difficult to classify. There are the **reed-backed walkers**, narrow as boats and so low to the ground that wind can pass over them. They migrate through flooded valleys in long files, using the submerged paths made by mirehorns. Their transparent eyelids are not for seeing underwater but for protecting their eyes from the living dust of the fog. In spring—or the nearest equivalent, during the brief weeks when the rain carries less cold—they gather in black pools and sing through inflated throat membranes. The sound is low enough to be felt in the soles of the feet. It stirs mud from the lake bottoms. It wakes things buried in the peat.

There are small, quick inhabitants too: six-legged carrion eaters whose backs bloom with gray-green lichen. They do not disguise themselves as stones or moss; they cultivate the resemblance, tending their own camouflage with hooked feet. They gather around wounds, fallen branches, mineral leaks, and vents. Where they feed, the lichen on their bodies changes color. A traveler might see a whole pack moving across a slope as a patch of landscape that has decided, briefly, to hurry.

The birds are perhaps the least attached to the ground and therefore the least trusted by it. Many never land on bare stone. Their feet are adapted for moss, hide, wet reed, and the fibrous backs of the grazers. Their wings are broad, blunt, and weather-stained, made for lifting through fog rather than crossing great distances. Some nest in the shoulder gardens of mirehorns. Some lay their eggs in warm peat vents. Some enter the open mouths of the largest animals to feed on the mineral crust there, and are carried for days before leaving again.

At night the birds turn in rings around the underground lights.

No one knows whether they are feeding, guarding, worshiping, or listening. The distinction may not exist for them. They descend in widening spirals and settle at the edge of glowing seepage, their heads tilted inward. Their calls become synchronized. From far away, the rings look like dark punctuation marks written around the green branches beneath the ground.

The predators are real, but the word predator is only partly adequate.

They are called **the weather-eaters** by anything that has survived an encounter with them, which is not a name so much as a warning. They do not have one stable shape. A weather-eater may appear as a gray movement climbing where no movement should climb, or as a gathering blackness low in the reeds, or as sheets of leaflike skin skating across a pond against the wind. It has a body, certainly: muscles, joints, digestive sacs, organs that must rest and reproduce. But those are hidden in the way a river’s source is hidden.

Most of the time a weather-eater travels dispersed. Portions of it pass through grass, fog, water, and the skin of the ground. It comes together only when it feeds. Then the landscape behaves oddly around it. Water draws back from shallow margins. Moss pales. Birds become loud. Grazers form circles, their backs outward, their mouths still working as if grazing might be a kind of defense. The predator gathers density, becomes briefly visible, and takes not only flesh but heat, scent, stored fat, breath, and fear. After it has passed, the place is often greener than before.

The country recovers what is spent. This is why the predators are tolerated.

And there are people.

They are uncommon enough that a person may live several lifetimes without seeing another, yet numerous enough that the peat remembers all of them. They do not build houses because the country does not permit fixed edges for long. A wall becomes a root lattice. A roof accumulates spores. A doorway fills with a membrane that opens and closes according to humidity. Instead, the people make temporary agreements with places.

They sleep inside the hollowed flanks of the old vegetative ramparts, in chambers formed when stems grow around heat and breath. They travel on broad living skiffs cut from the buoyant mats of lake growth, then return the skiffs to the water before they become too accustomed to being vessels. They wear woven reed fiber, cured hide, and layered films made from the translucent skins shed by amphibious beasts. Their clothing is deliberately hospitable: moss at the cuffs, small insect nests under the collar, spores sewn into the hems. To be entirely clean is to be ill, exiled from the exchange of the land.

Their bodies are marked by the place. The older among them are never bare-skinned. Fine green nap gathers in the hairline and behind the ears. Lichen freckles form at the knuckles. Their boots are not removed but gradually replaced, sole by sole, by tough root-growth shaped around the foot. When someone stops moving for too long, companions brush them, warm them, speak close to them, and sometimes cut away the first soft threads of incorporation.

This is not because incorporation is feared.

It is because it must be entered willingly.

The people have no word for death that means only ending. They distinguish between being taken by a weather-eater, being given to the pools, sinking into the working peat, and **becoming available**. The last is a chosen condition. Those who become available walk alone into low country during the luminous hours. They stand where the ground is most wet and the light moves uphill. By morning they may be gone, or they may still be standing with roots around their ankles and flowers opening in their sleeves. In later years, their voices are sometimes heard in the vent rhythms beneath the basin—not as speech, exactly, but as a recognizable irregularity in the pulse.

The people do not govern the country. They interpret its shifts.

They are watchers of leanings, listeners for the long groans beneath the hills, readers of the green and amber lights. They know that when all the plants bend toward one horizon, something is approaching—not always a storm. Sometimes it is the movement of a deep herd beneath the peat. Sometimes a distant basin has ruptured and released a new weather-eater. Sometimes an old pattern is changing: a lake becoming land, a ridge becoming a marsh, a buried body beginning to remember its own shape.

The oldest people say the country listens because it is lonely.

The youngest say this is sentimental nonsense. The country listens because listening is what keeps it alive.

But when the wind changes and every lichen shelf, reed bed, moss wall, and flowering skull turns toward the same unseen distance, the people fall quiet with everything else. They place their palms on the wet ground. They wait.

Then, if the land begins to answer, they move with it.
