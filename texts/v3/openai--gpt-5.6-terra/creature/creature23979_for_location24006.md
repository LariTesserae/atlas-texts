What lives in the low country is not one population but a succession of dependences.

At the surface, the waders are the most visible claimants. They belong to the distances. Their white legs make pale moving punctuation across every brown basin, every flooded apron, every shallowed canal where warm water slows before entering a grate. They are not graceful, exactly. Their joints bend with a mechanical economy, and their dark heads are too blunt, too heavy-looking, as if shaped to endure falling debris. But they are patient. A rank of them can stand in chemical rain for hours, necks lowered, feet feeling through sediment for the soft filter colonies that bloom around discharge mouths.

They do not build nests in the usual sense. They gather bolts, broken gauge caps, the ceramic collars from obsolete pipes, and arrange these on dry ledges above the waterline. Their young hatch already able to balance on broad, yielding feet. For several days they remain under the parents’ bodies, pale and nearly invisible against the surrounding equipment. Then they join the marching ranks.

Waders remember the floor better than anyone.

They know which old plates flex over submerged rooms. They know the harmless heat of condensate trenches and the lethal heat of return lines. When a basin is about to be drained, they leave before the water level changes. When an old roof is going to give way beneath a service deck, they collect along the nearest railings and stare down into it. No signal tells them. The country tells them through vibration, altered taste, a pressure in the soles of their feet.

Other animals follow them because of this.

The small armored grazers gather in the stirred water behind their legs, feeding on what the waders uncover. Canal beasts drift beneath them, waiting for dropped matter to pass through the grates. Threadworms gather around the waders’ nests, living in the wet mineral paste that accumulates between bolts. The tall creatures are not rulers of the marsh, but they are its walking weather vanes. A traveler who sees every wader facing the same direction should turn around.

The canal beasts are older-looking, though perhaps nothing here is truly old in the biological sense. They are thick-backed and low, with too many limbs folded invisibly along their sides. In open water, their bodies move with a slow peristaltic confidence. In narrow channels, they use the limbs and climb the walls. Their skin has a dark, dull shine, not unlike industrial rubber, but it is warm to the touch. Fine pale whiskers grow around their mouths and fan outward in the current.

They eat blockage.

A canal beast can live for years on mineral scale, bacterial mats, shed shell, grease, drowned insects, soft insulation, and the fatty residue washed from larger animals. They gather in pump wells when the machinery changes rhythm. There they press their bodies against intake cages and feed until the grates are clean enough for water to pass. Whether this is instinct or design is difficult to say. Their mouths are full of dense black plates, and when they bite a clogged pipe seam the sound carries far through the deck: a deliberate, wet cracking.

The largest ones are never seen in full.

They live below the visible basin, in the drowned transit levels and heat galleries where water has remained trapped for generations. Their backs rise occasionally beneath inspection platforms, moving a whole sheet of floating debris upward before sinking again. In some places, the maintenance hatches are bent outward from below. In others, the old tunnel walls bear long polished marks where something enormous has passed repeatedly through spaces too narrow for it.

People call them fold-beasts because of the sound they make in the dark.

There are smaller lives that have become almost inseparable from the works themselves. Pipe mites gather around hot joints in soft gray congregations, lifting and closing their bodies as pressure changes. They consume the mineral crust that would otherwise seal valves shut. Their young are carried in droplets of condensation, clinging to the undersides of rails until the drops fall. Bead grazers move in black streams across concrete, entering cracks narrower than a fingernail. They eat rust, or something close enough to rust that no one has found a useful distinction.

The winged membrane animals are neither birds nor insects, despite the ways they are spoken of. They cling in layers beneath heat exchangers and on the shaded sides of towers. At dawn, when sunlight reaches the wet walls, they unfold. Their wings are broad, veined, and almost translucent at the edges. Heat passes through them and is stored in their dense central bodies. By midday they have become limp with warmth and hang again in folds.

When they migrate, the sky changes.

Not dramatically. Nothing in the low country does anything dramatically. But over several days, the upper air acquires a slow drift of pale shapes moving west above the clouds. They do not flap. They release from one thermal column, rise until the air cools, then settle into another. Many never arrive. Their bodies collect on distant spillways, where they are consumed by smaller scavengers and by colonies of engineered bacteria. The survivors gather in the cooling fields and breed in the warm condensate trays.

Their offspring emerge not as flying things but as little crawling pads, drawn to vibration. They travel along pipes, enter ducts, and climb until they find enough heat to open their first wings.

There are also the people.

They are few enough that the animals do not always acknowledge them, but numerous enough that no part of the low country is entirely without their traces. They live in service shelters, old control rooms, sealed freight cars, the hollow bases of pylons, and in apartments that were once intended for maintenance crews and were later built over, flooded, excavated, and built over again. Some communities occupy long elevated galleries above the breeding channels. Others live below the decks in dry chambers where the air is hot and metallic and every wall sweats.

They are called lowfolk by outsiders, though they rarely call themselves anything so broad.

A person here belongs first to a water district, a pump lineage, a known tower shadow, a particular stairwell that still reaches the upper light. Names are often practical and inherited from equipment: Third-Sluice, West Ladder, Bent Rail, Marrow Valve, Sixteen South. Children learn the sound of their home pumps before they learn directions. They are taught that the distant throb beneath their beds is not merely machinery; it is the proof that the water still has somewhere to go.

The lowfolk do not control the country. They maintain the small margins in which they can live.

They clear nests from emergency ladders without destroying the nests. They patch walking decks where the plates have loosened. They cultivate bacterial films in shallow ceramic trays, harvesting them for food, sealant, medicine, and dye. They trap canal beasts only when a channel has become too crowded, and then use every part: the dark hide for flexible covers, the plates of the mouth for cutting tools, the rendered fat for waterproofing. Wader eggs are collected only from particular districts, at particular times, and never from nests built on red-marked surfaces.

Their law is mostly a map of things not to disturb.

Do not dam a warm channel.  
Do not sleep beside a pressure pipe.  
Do not cross a wader rank during a drainage shift.  
Do not enter a dry tunnel that smells of flowers.  
Do not follow a fold-beast into a lower level.  
Do not open a sealed door merely because it has become visible.

Some of these rules preserve life. Others preserve the larger system, which may be the same thing here.

The people are damp nearly all the time. Their clothes are layered, waxed, patched with membrane wing and treated hide. Many wear smooth white plates strapped beneath the feet to cross soft flats without sinking. Their homes contain lines of hooks, constantly occupied by drying garments, tools, food strips, and small glowing cultures in glass tubes. They sleep lightly during rain, because rain changes the levels in hidden places. They sleep deeply only during the scheduled shadows of the central cloudbanks, when the temperature falls by a degree or two and the familiar pumps have not yet changed pitch.

They do not think of the country as artificial, though they know every part of it was made.

A wall is a cliff if it keeps water from taking your house. A channel is a river if it has drowned people. A tower is a mountain if weather forms around it. A beast is wild if it can kill you, even if it was once printed from a sequence stored in a forgotten archive. The lowfolk have inherited no clean boundary between machine, animal, building, and landscape. Their prayers, where they have them, are addressed to flow, pressure, return, and remainder.

Some of the oldest people tell stories about workers.

In these stories, workers came from the escarpments above with bright helmets and clean hands. They spoke into radios. They made decisions. They argued about efficiency. They built the first terraces, set the first depth gauges, released the first maintenance animals into the warm water. Then their children stayed. Then the work became too large to understand. Then the systems began repairing systems that repaired systems, and no worker could say which task had been the original one.

In some versions, the workers left.

In others, they are still here, but distributed so completely through the country that they can no longer be recognized as people: a hand in the timing of rain, a preference built into the waders’ migration, a hesitation in a pump before it floods a nesting ground. The lowfolk do not dismiss these stories. They have seen too many things that behave like customs without anyone remembering who taught them.

And deeper still, below the human rooms and animal routes, there are lives that may not know the surface exists.

Blind things pulse in drowned concourses. Long colonies grow around warm cable bundles, feeding on the slow leakage of current. In abandoned treatment chambers, pale fans open and close in water that has not moved in decades. Some lower creatures are said to have bodies made mostly of symbiotic machinery: flesh threaded with conductive fiber, bone replaced by mineral lattice, organs that make use of heat gradients as other animals use breath.

Occasionally one comes upward.

It is usually found after a structural shift: something pale wedged in a spillway gate, or a many-jointed shape lying exhausted on an access ramp, its skin shining like wet porcelain. The lowfolk do not touch these visitors. The waders avoid them. Even the small scavengers circle at a distance. By morning, if it has not returned to the water, it has often been covered in a fine white bloom of mites and mineral dust, as though the country were already incorporating it into a new surface.

Nothing here lives alone for long.

A dead membrane feeds a drain colony. A clogged channel feeds a canal beast. A canal beast feeds a family. A family’s waste enters a settling basin, where waders stir it into the water and filter colonies take it up. The filtered water rises as vapor, becomes cloud, becomes warm rain, becomes runoff, becomes the same water moving again beneath the same decks.

The inhabitants of the low country are therefore not merely animals, or people, or machines left running after their makers vanished.

They are the country’s way of keeping itself inhabited.

They move through its layers like thoughts through a mind too vast to wake.
