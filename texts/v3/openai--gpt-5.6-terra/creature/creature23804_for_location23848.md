What lives there is not an animal, though it has habits.

It begins at the intake, where the high water enters with enough force to whiten itself. In the first days after the reservoir was filled, there was only water, only machinery, only the bright strict architecture of use. Then the currents learned the room. They divided themselves along the tile seams. They found the submerged ledges and the blind corners behind the wheel. They worried at loose fibers of rope, carried lime dust into slow spirals, pressed their weight against the bronze disk at the drain.

Something gathered in those repetitions.

The workers do not name it, because the reservoir is new and men are reluctant to say a new thing has already acquired an old inhabitant. They say the wheel has settled. They say the air pocket is making noise. They say the sluice has a bad temper. But when one of them descends alone, with a lamp hooded against the wet glare, he knows there is another rhythm beneath the machinery’s rhythm.

The wheel turns.

The cups rise and strike their rail.

Water falls into the trough.

And, after a little while, the chamber seems to draw breath.

It is most present in the narrow space between water and vault. There, where the heat is trapped, the air does not lie still. It gathers itself in a long wavering pressure from one end of the chamber to the other. Condensation forms along the brick in beads, but not evenly. A run of droplets will appear on the east wall, slide downward together, then stop. On the west wall, dry bricks darken one after another, as if a wet hand has passed over them beneath the surface.

There is no hand.

There is only the reservoir’s attention.

It attends to weak things. The swollen seam in a paddle. The fraying of a rope knot. A chipped lip on one of the earthenware cups. The softening mortar around an iron ring. It does not repair them; it has no tenderness and no wish to preserve. But water begins to speak more loudly around a weakness. A leak becomes a cold thread. A loose brace begins to knock at a particular hour. Sediment gathers near a crack in a way that makes the crack visible. The reservoir notices through pressure, through resistance, through the minute changes in the work it was made to do.

This is how it knows the men.

When a worker descends, the thing in the chamber cannot see his face. It knows him by interruption. A body enters the water and the bright lines from the shafts break around it. Warm water is displaced. The wheel’s returning wash strikes a new shape. Breath comes down through the rope ladder in little bursts and mingles with the sour air beneath the vault.

The reservoir receives these disturbances.

A careful worker is known as a weight that pauses before touching. A fearful worker is a quick, irregular trembling in the ladder rungs. A tired worker leans too heavily on the iron rings and leaves a heat behind in the brick. The chamber keeps these impressions for a while. Its water closes over them, but not immediately. For several turns of the wheel, a man’s crossing can remain in the currents as a faint confusion, a remnant movement circling the drain.

The oldest of the workers—though none of them are old in this place—have begun to avoid speaking loudly beneath the vault. Not from superstition exactly. Speech comes back wrong. A word sent toward the far wall returns broadened by water and brick, its edges washed away. Commands become murmurs. Names become something rounded and impersonal.

Once, a worker shouted for the gate to be raised.

The echo answered after the gate had already moved.

It sounded less like an answer than an imitation made by something that had heard a human voice often enough to understand that voices were part of the mechanism.

When the reservoir is drained, its inhabitant changes shape.

The water falls away through the bronze disk with terrible speed. The bright floor appears. The black seams between tiles emerge one by one. Reed roots hang exposed in their baskets, thin and pale, dripping brown threads. The wheel slows, then hangs with its paddles pointing down like the limbs of some drowned wooden creature raised briefly into air.

For a little while, the chamber loses its body.

But not entirely.

In the wet tile, in the rivulets running toward the drain, in the dark gleam under the filter troughs, something remains awake. The walls sweat more heavily then. The air is hotter. Sound becomes sharp without its underwater softness: boot soles strike the floor, tools clink against bronze, workers curse at grit lodged in the drain holes. Every noise rises to the vault and comes down altered.

This is the only time the reservoir seems hungry.

It has been made for fullness. Its rules are laws of water: enter high, leave low; turn, lift, cleanse; hold, release. Emptiness is a temporary violation. In the drained chamber, the thing that lives there gathers in the dampness and waits with a pressure that can be felt in the teeth. Workers hurry. They tell themselves they hurry because the basin must be cleaned before the next release from the upper cistern.

But they also hurry because the room is unfinished when dry.

Then the gate closes. There is a pause, almost silent except for the ticking of cooling wood and the long drip from the vault.

The first returning water slips over the tiles in a thin sheet.

It moves around the workers’ footprints without erasing them at once. It fills the black-pitched joins. It takes the dust, the scraps of reed, the pale mineral residue, and carries them downhill. It reaches the drain, circles, rises. The wheel takes its first reluctant shove. One paddle moves. Then another.

The chamber becomes loud again.

At that moment, whatever lives there returns not from a hiding place but from everywhere at once: from the force in the intake, from the spin of the wheel, from the hot air forced into the vault, from the hundred small currents joining and parting over the floor. It has no single body to glimpse in the white shafts of noon. It has no face below the rippling light. It is the made room discovering that it is being used.

It will grow as the reservoir grows older, though not in the manner of moss or rats or roots.

The pitch will loosen in places. The copper bindings will green. The tiles will acquire hairline cracks invisible until drained. The reeds will thicken, then be cut back. Workers will learn which noises can be ignored and which cannot. The wheel’s groan will change pitch in winter. The air pocket will hold different heat in different seasons. Sediment will make its rust-colored halo wider around the drain.

And through all of it, the inhabitant will become more exact.

Not a god. Not a ghost. Not even a spirit, unless a spirit can be made from weight, repetition, enclosure, and the patient insistence of water upon every boundary given to it.

It lives in the reservoir as a current lives in a riverbed: because the shape has taught movement how to remember itself.
