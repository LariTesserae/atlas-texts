What lives there is not a single kind of person, though the people of the pocket would probably insist that it is. They call themselves, depending on who is speaking and how formal the occasion is, the Below, the Basin, the Warmward, or simply the town. To the surface settlements they are often called furnace folk, though most of them dislike the name. A furnace consumes. Their city survives by not consuming anything too quickly.

They are descended from workers who came down to open the geothermal converters, from families who followed the workers with food and tools, from drought refugees who arrived carrying seed jars and paper documents wrapped in oilcloth, from mechanics who had nowhere useful to be aboveground after the old pumping systems failed. No one family founded the place cleanly enough to claim it. The original builders are remembered by initials stamped into obsolete pipe fittings, by surnames on patched electrical panels, by the names of passages that no longer lead where they once did.

There are people who have never stood under an open sky.

They know the surface exists, certainly. They have seen it in old recordings, in the glare that spills down access shafts, in the dust-coated boots of caravan traders. They know that there are horizons, storms visible from far away, stars which do not belong to any ceiling. But for some of the youngest children, the sky remains an uncomfortable abstraction: a ceiling that cannot be repaired, a lamp too bright to look at, a source of heat without any pipe connected to it.

Those who go above wear wide cloth hoods, tinted lenses, layered wraps, and boots fitted with removable grit plates. They return sun-cracked and thirsty, carrying salt in the seams of their clothes. For a day or two afterward they speak too loudly without meaning to. Surface people say the underground citizens move strangely: slowly at first, then suddenly and precisely, as if always aware of walls just beyond sight. Underground people say surface dwellers waste motion. They turn their whole bodies to look at things. They leave containers open. They walk away from shade.

The inhabitants of the pocket tend to be compactly dressed and elaborately patched. Their clothing has to tolerate wet heat, mineral dust, grease, fungus spores, and the occasional brush with hot pipework. Nobody owns a garment that has not belonged to someone else first. Sleeves are detachable. Knees are reinforced with conveyor-belt rubber. Children wear bright bands of woven plastic so they can be spotted among hanging laundry, grow racks, and machinery. Older people often have pockets sewn everywhere: inside collars, along calves, beneath waist wraps. A person may carry a pruning hook, a packet of salt-balanced tea, three screws of uncertain origin, a roll of bandage cloth, a tiny inspection mirror, and a seed from a tree that no longer grows in the city.

Their hands show their trades before they say them.

The condenser keepers have white mineral lines in their knuckles and under their fingernails. Garden workers smell of crushed leaves and compost heat. Machine tenders have tiny burns on the forearms, always in pairs or rows where they have reached past protective grilles. The cable winders develop a particular bend in the first finger. The people who clean and maintain the old air shafts are recognizable by the habit of pausing in doorways, listening to the change in airflow.

Children grow up with this listening.

They are taught not merely what a sound means, but what it is supposed to mean. The normal drip of the west-side collection pipe. The healthy, low vibration of the turbine chamber. The stuttering fan belt in the clinic vent. The troubling silence after a pump should have engaged. Before they can read well, they learn to lay a palm against a wall and feel whether the warmth beneath it has changed.

Their games reflect this education without becoming solemn. They play route games, crawling through approved service passages with a loop of string and a lamp. They make miniature cities out of discarded fittings and damp clay, then argue over water rights. They trade polished bits of slag as though they were rare jewels. They dare one another to stand for three breaths outside the marked boundary of the west gallery, though the older children never allow the younger ones to do it alone.

There are always old people in the city, though few become old in the way surface people imagine. The heat softens some joints and worsens others. The damp finds the lungs. Mineral exposure leaves its slow signatures. But the town is careful with its elders because memory is infrastructure here.

An old woman may remember where a sealed pipe actually leads. An old man may know which apparent dead end opens when the market canopy is removed. Someone who can no longer climb ladders may still be consulted before any wall is cut. The most respected people are not necessarily the elected coordinators or the skilled machinists. They are the ones who can say, with enough confidence to stop work, “There used to be a void behind that.”

Some inhabitants carry maps in their heads that do not exist anywhere else.

The city does have records: bundles of waterproofed diagrams, maintenance books, school notebooks, circuit charts, family registries, fungal cultures labeled in several hands. But paper swells, inks fade, formats become unreadable, and a map is often out of date before it has dried. So routes are preserved orally. Directions are given not in distances but in sequences of conditions.

Go past the warm wall with the three green valves.  
Turn where the ceiling lowers and the air tastes metallic.  
Take the stairs that sound hollow, not the stairs with the red tile.  
If you reach the room with the painted fish, you have gone too far.

The people speak softly, but they are not timid. Their quietness is a form of citizenship. A raised voice travels into other households. A slammed door can startle someone sleeping after a night shift. Unnecessary noise is a kind of taking. Arguments happen, of course; in such density they must. But many disputes are conducted through elaborate restraint: a pot placed pointedly in the wrong communal hook, a name omitted from a work roster, a too-careful greeting in the market.

When anger does become loud, it is usually because of water.

Water disputes are never about a cup alone. They are about old favors, failing filters, a sick child, a household that irrigated too much, a repair delayed because someone saved a gasket for a private project. The city has mediators for such matters, though they are rarely called judges. They are vessel keepers, account mothers, channel listeners. Their work is not to decide who deserves water. Everyone deserves it. Their work is to find where it went.

There are also people whose jobs would be hard to name aboveground.

Root-guides inspect the living walls and decide which roots may remain, which must be redirected, and which must be cut ceremonially because they have entered dangerous places. Mold readers work in the fungus vaults, studying color, smell, softness, and the speed with which a bloom crosses its trays. Bell keepers maintain the pressure alarm system and are trusted enough that a single pull on a cord can empty entire levels without anyone asking why.

There are heat listeners, who sit in silence near grilles and fractures with ceramic cups of water, watching the surface tremble.

There are stitchers who make clothing, but also stitch insulation, pressure curtains, water bladders, and the skins around vulnerable cable joints. There are scavengers who travel upward with caravans and return with treasures that would look like trash to anyone else: intact bearings, heatproof glass, a spool of copper, a box of unsplit screws, an old school microscope, a truck mirror, a rain-damaged book about orchards.

And there are people who make beauty without apology.

A room may have no more floor space than a bedroll and still contain a painted wall. Broken ceramic is arranged into small mosaics: moons, hands, birds that none of the children have seen, the wide branching roots of imagined trees. The brass casters etch tiny patterns into fittings that will vanish inside machines. The glass workers sometimes make beads from imperfect ampoules. These are worn in the hair, tied to tool handles, or hung near beds to catch the colored spill from grow lamps.

Music is quiet, but it exists. Thumb instruments, breath flutes with cloth-wrapped holes, stringed boxes made from old tins and wire, drumheads muted under layers of felt. The city favors rhythms that can be heard close by rather than melodies meant to cross a field. A song often begins in one kitchen and is picked up elsewhere, altered by distance and walls until it returns to its starting point as something almost new.

The dead live there too, in the cool niches.

They are not sent upward unless they asked to be. Surface burial is expensive, dangerous, and considered by many beneath the city’s dignity. The dead are washed with the careful economy applied to everything else. Their names are entered into the water ledger for the last time. They are laid in the cool levels behind insulated doors, among seed vaults and medicine stores—not because the living confuse them with supplies, but because those are the places best protected from heat.

A person’s tools may be distributed after death, but never immediately. For a while, the tools remain where their owner left them. A pair of pruning shears may hang untouched beside a garden channel. A soldering iron may stay on its hook. This gives the household time to understand the shape of the absence.

The city’s nonhuman inhabitants are no less woven into its life.

The moss is not decoration, though it is sometimes spoken to like a pet. It cools surfaces, catches dust, softens echoes, and gives children a living measure of humidity. The fungus beds are fed, monitored, feared, and loved. Different strains have different temperaments in the imagination of the residents: one is greedy, one is patient, one sulks when the air is too dry, one “likes” old paper, though everyone knows this is just another way of saying it has to be kept away from the archives.

The dwarf trees in the hot courtyards are treated almost as citizens. Their fruit is often too small to be efficient, and their soil takes labor that could be spent on more productive crops. But they are old enough to have become part of the settlement’s moral landscape. Children are measured against their trunks. Couples make promises beneath their tied-back branches. When one loses a limb to rot or heat damage, people bring home the wood splinters.

Even the machines have acquired social positions.

The oldest condenser tower is touched because it is old, useful, and still working. The eastern turbine is referred to with affectionate irritation because it complains before dawn. The ventilation fan above the school is said to be a light sleeper. A particular pump has been repaired so many times by so many hands that no one claims to understand it completely; its housing is covered in dates, initials, warnings, jokes, and a painted pair of eyes.

The citizens do not mistake machines for people. Not exactly. But they know that anything which keeps breathing beside you for generations deserves manners.

And in the west gallery, something lives that is not acknowledged in the same way.

Not a creature, most would say. There is no animal large enough to hide in its wet black passages, no ghost anyone can agree on, no secret population waiting behind the sealed doors. What lives there is the unfinished behavior of the earth: pressure, heat, water, mineral growth, the slow unreadable adjustment of rock to rock.

Still, children are told that the gallery listens when they are careless.

Adults do not say this. Adults say that the fracture is active. That the thermal gradient is unstable. That accumulated gas may shift without warning. They use good technical words. Then they lower their voices as they pass its barred entrance.

Sometimes, at the deepest hour of the common rest cycle, the town feels the west gallery move.

It is a nearly private event. A cup turns in place. A sleeping person wakes with the certainty that someone has entered the room. Dust loosens from a beam. Somewhere, a nail works itself a little farther out of old wood. The condenser tower gives a long, damp shudder through its ribs.

No alarm rings unless the movement becomes dangerous. Most people remain in bed. They hold still. They listen for pipes, for water, for the familiar breathing of fans. In the morning, they check their shelves, tighten loose fittings, sweep up grit, and continue.

That may be the deepest thing living in the city: not merely the humans packed into rooms, or the gardens crowded around the pipes, or the patient machines working in the dark. It is the shared discipline of continuation.

The people below have made a life out of attention.

They notice a new crack in plaster. They notice when a neighbor’s usual kettle does not steam. They notice when the crickets leave the boiler drains, when the moss pales, when a child has stopped playing route games and begun sitting too long beside the clinic door. They know that survival in a small hot pocket of stone is not heroism most days. It is maintenance. It is bringing someone a clean jar. It is replacing a seal before it fails. It is leaving a little room around a root. It is listening to the walls and believing them when they say, in their slow mineral language, that something has changed.
