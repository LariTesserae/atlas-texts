What lives in the Kiln is not, at first, a collection of species.

It is a pressure.

It has the shape of an ecology, certainly: feeders and grazers, scavengers and hunters, bodies that molt and sleep and mate in the hot dust. But every life there has been pressed so close to every other life, so long, that the boundaries have begun to lose their importance. The Kiln has made a single crowded animal of them all. Its blood is heat. Its nerves are vibrations in stone. Its skin is the shifting black surface of the basin.

The smallest parts of it are the soot fans.

They are not truly still. Each black velvety patch around a vent is made of minute, hard-bodied things, smaller than grit, each with a ring of glassy hooks around its underside. They cling to hot stone and drink the exhalations that would poison anything with wet lungs. In a strong pulse they unfurl into hair-fine fronds, multiplying their exposed surface. When the air cools they fold down, layer over layer, until a whole colony looks like nothing more than a scorch mark.

Ash runners eat them.

The runners are born pale, almost clear. Their shells darken only after they have survived their first several heat pulses, when mineral soot begins to build inside their translucent bellies. By adulthood they carry a dim orange or red light in their abdomens, not enough to illuminate anything, but enough that in a deep crevice one can see them traveling as slow embers.

There are always runners traveling.

Their six legs move in patterns that appear frantic to an outsider, but no runner wastes a movement. They know the basin through inherited pressure: the slight tremor of a grazing herd above, the soft clicking of a predator settling its weight, the changing hum of a vent. A stream of runners will divide around a fallen plate or a sleeping gull without breaking pace. They pass food along their lines mouth to mouth. They pass warmth as well. A runner that has fed at a rich vent may be surrounded by others, pressed belly-to-belly until its stored heat has been shared thinly among dozens.

The older runners do not become larger.

They become darker, slower, and increasingly mineralized. Eventually their joints seize. They choose cracks in the walls or gaps beneath the arches and arrange themselves among the stone. In time they cannot be distinguished from the pale veins running through the basalt. Their descendants walk over them without knowing.

The plated grazers know, in some manner, where these old runner-bones are. Their herds avoid crushing the densest deposits.

They are called grazers because there is no better word, but they do not eat like animals of green places. Their mouths are broad grinding plates lined with silicate teeth. They kneel at the lips of vents and rasp the living black films away from the rock, then chew the hot mineral paste slowly. Their faces are almost featureless except for their heat pits and the narrow folds of their mouths. Their eyes, if they once had any, are buried under armored ridges before they leave infancy.

Their horn-crowns are more alive than the rest of them.

Each crown begins as a cluster of soft black tendrils on the skull of a newborn. As the animal ages, the tendrils harden into branching rods with hollow interiors. They open and close with tiny movements. They swivel toward warmth. When a herd rests, dozens of crowns rise and turn together, making the group resemble a forest of burnt antlers listening beneath the ground.

The grazers have no leader.

A herd moves when enough bodies agree to move, and agreement passes through flank contact. One animal leans. The next feels it. A slow force travels across the packed bodies. A herd can turn around a dangerous vent with no cry, no visible command, simply by becoming briefly more solid on one side than the other.

Their young are kept in the centers of these masses.

A calf is not small for long. It begins as a soft, pale lump with flexible plates and a horn-crown no higher than a hand. For its first cycles it is almost entirely hidden beneath adults. It feeds from heat condensed in the folds of their bellies and learns the pulse of the mountain through the pressure of their bodies. When it first steps into open ash, the herd does not celebrate. It closes around it and resumes grazing.

Some grazers carry things on their backs.

Not parasites exactly. Not companions either. There are pale, many-limbed shell creatures that fasten themselves between the plates and feed on the dust gathered there. In return, perhaps, they clean the seams where heat can build too sharply. A grazer may carry hundreds of them. When it shifts in the fissure-light, they withdraw all at once, and its back seems to become a field of closed white flowers.

The kiln gulls follow the herds, but they are not fond of them.

Their long necks bend with an unpleasant precision, each vertebra visible under dry, gray skin. Their forelimbs carry wide membranes strengthened by finger-bones like black wires. They can glide, in brief uncertain intervals, on the upward surge of hot air; more often they launch themselves from high ledges and let the basin decide where they will land.

They eat what the rest of the Kiln overlooks.

The dead, of course. Shed horn-sheaths. Molted runner shells. The soft interiors of a grazer’s cracked plate. They also feed on breath. A gull will stand before a vent with its mouth open, throat pulsing, and draw in the mineral heat until the skin under its jaw glows dully. Then it will climb to a colder ledge and exhale the stored vapor in a thin cloud, where smaller scavengers gather beneath it.

No one has seen a kiln gull hatch.

Their nests, if they have nests, are above the ash lid or inside the mountain’s throat. Sometimes a young gull appears in the basin: too large to be carried, too awkward to climb, its membrane wings still wrinkled and wet-looking despite the dry air. The adults ignore it except to drive it away from food. It survives by following runners until it understands hunger. By its first great molt it has learned to perch upside down beneath a ledge, wrapped in its own wings like a burned banner.

The hunters are the Kiln’s quietest inhabitants.

They do not have a single shape. The basin makes several kinds of blind predator, and they seem to recognize one another only as rival silences.

There are the long ones, jointed too many times, whose bodies bend around rocks without seeming to turn. Their legs are thin and hard, ending in pads that can feel the tremble of a runner at distances no eye could measure. There are squat burrowers with hinged skulls that open along the sides, swallowing prey in a sudden widening of stone-colored flesh. There are wall hunters, nearly flat, which cling to the basalt for days at a time and drop only when something passes beneath them.

But the oldest hunters are called the Listener Forms.

They are broad, low creatures, often mistaken for fallen slabs. Their backs are ridged with concentric mineral plates. Their faces have no mouths visible from the front. Beneath the head is a narrow seam that opens only when feeding. They do not chase. They settle into a place where the ground is thin above a warm passage and remain there for weeks, reading the tremors of the entire basin.

A Listener Form knows when a herd is uneasy before the herd does.

It knows which runner stream has been diverted by a crack in the floor. It knows the weight of each kiln gull landing on a particular ledge. It knows when the deep breath is gathering far below, because the vibration changes from a trembling to a held tension.

When it hunts, it does not rush.

It simply appears where the prey is about to be.

For this reason the creatures of the Kiln fear not movement, but interruption. A sudden stillness in the runner streams. A patch of ash that does not stir when heat passes over it. The absence of the small scraping sounds that should be there. These are signs of a Listener Form.

Yet even the Listener Forms enter the arches.

They enter carefully.

The three arches are older than any living memory, and their rule is older than hunger. A predator may wait outside them. It may follow prey to their threshold. It may place one forelimb within the smooth black shade. But it will not cross while intending harm.

No creature does.

Inside the passage, the smallest runner may pass beneath the belly of a starving hunter. A wounded grazer may lean its weight against a wall while gulls perch above it. Shell-cleaners may crawl freely over the claws of things that would crush them elsewhere. There is no peace in the human sense. There is no comfort. There is only a pause in the usual arrangement of need.

The arches have altered the animals that use them.

Their surfaces are polished not merely by contact but by generations of warmth. The stone within is always slightly hotter than the stone without. Runners linger there to molt. Grazers rub their plated flanks against the pillars until flakes of mineral hide fall away. Gulls press their throats to the inner walls. Listener Forms lie across the entrance, their ridged backs gathering ash, and allow the traffic to pass over them.

Sometimes, after a pulse, an animal that has been taken by the breath is found partly beneath an arch.

No one feeds on it until the next pulse has passed.

This is not learned behavior in any simple way. Hatchlings and newborns know it. A runner will veer around the body. A gull will lower its head and wait. Even the small shell-cleaners withdraw into their hosts’ plates.

Perhaps the dead beneath the arches are not dead in the same way.

There are stories, if the Kiln has stories, of creatures entering the arches during a great deep exhalation and emerging into the solid western wall. Not crushed. Not buried. Gone through. The runner streams sometimes disappear into the passage in numbers too great to account for. A grazer may press its head against the dead-end stone and remain there until its horn-crown retracts entirely. A gull may call into the wall—a low, dry click that does not echo—and then fly upward into the ash.

Nothing comes back bearing proof.

Still, the animals keep using the arches. That is proof enough for them.

And beneath all of these lives, in cracks too narrow for claws, there are the heat-worms.

They are scarcely animals at all: braided threads of mineral tissue, red at their cores, moving through hot stone as if stone were soft mud. They feed on the chemical richness left by the mountain’s breath. Sometimes their passage opens a new seam in the basin floor. Sometimes a vent collapses because they have hollowed the rock beneath it. Sometimes, in the darkest places, they rise close enough to the surface that the ash glows in thin wandering lines.

The grazers follow these lines. The runners follow the grazers. The gulls follow everything. The hunters follow the changes.

The heat-worms do not know they are feeding the Kiln.

Perhaps the mountain does not know either.

But when it exhales, every body in the basin answers: the black films flatten, the runners vanish, the horns turn, the wings spread, the hunters become stone, and the old arches fill with passing life. For a brief interval, all the separate creatures align themselves around the same enormous breath.

Then the pulse is gone, and the Kiln resumes its dense, dark labor.

It eats heat.

It eats ash.

It eats its dead.

And in the narrow spaces between black stones, it goes on living with more patience than anything beneath the sky.
