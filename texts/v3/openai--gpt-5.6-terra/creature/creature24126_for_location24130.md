What lives in the pocket is not one thing, though the town sometimes speaks of it as if it were.

They say the ground is soft there. They say there are frogs. They say a snake has a way through, and that children should not cut across after rain. But these are only the names people give to the separate visible pieces of a larger inhabitant.

The larger thing lives low.

It lives in the black water under the cracked skin of the rise, in the white roots that keep finding mortar, in the fine movements of soil grains making room for one another. It is not awake in the manner of an animal. It does not look outward. It has no face among the leaves, no voice that could be heard over a cooking fire. Yet it has preferences.

It prefers seepage to flood. It prefers a wall already weakened by a fig root. It prefers the places where plaster has fallen away and the old clay brick can breathe. It prefers the cool behind jars, the packed dark beneath leaf litter, the narrow shadows cast by the wash trough in the hour before noon. When people place stones across a wet hollow, it accepts them, lets mud gather at their edges, lets moss and minute grasses make them part of its body. When people try to harden it too quickly—with ash, rubble, crushed brick—it receives those things for a while and then quietly rearranges the ground beneath them until they tilt.

The frogs belong to it most visibly.

They are small, mud-colored things, hardly distinct from a thumbprint in wet earth. During daylight they withdraw into the invisible country below: cavities around roots, old beetle channels, the humid space beneath loosened stones. At dusk they emerge and take up stations around the hollows. They do not sing loudly because the pocket has no need to announce itself. Their calls are a form of measuring. A throat pulses; another answers from beside the citrus; one moves closer to a puddle that has thinned overnight. By morning, the frogs know where the water has gone.

The tortoise is older than the pocket, or seems to be. It moves through the basin with the grave certainty of something that has outlived several arrangements of walls. Its shell is scratched by brick corners and low branches. Mud dries in maps along its feet. It knows which banana stem will fall first, which damp places conceal tender shoots, which household leaves vegetable scraps near the back step. When the raw rise has shifted after a season of rain, the tortoise is often the first creature to cross it. It tests the earth without understanding that it is testing anything. Its weight is small, but the ground appears to take note.

There are also the root-keepers: beetles with polished backs, white grubs curled in the dark, slow red worms that surface after heavy rain and vanish again before the sun is high. They digest the pocket. They take the dropped fig, the banana fiber, the dead moth, the soft edge of an old leaf, and make each of them available to another mouth, another root, another season. Under the visible soil, they have made a soft architecture of tunnels and chambers. Water follows their work. Seeds settle in it. The young earth holds together because of their patient disturbance.

The geckos live on the walls but belong to the basin’s weather. In the evenings they descend toward the low places, pale bellies close to the plaster, hunting the moths that gather under eaves. They are attentive to falling drops. A gecko can remain utterly still while rain thickens in the air, then dart aside an instant before a bead of water lands where its head had been. Their feet leave no mark, but they know every wall face that has become cooler since the rise began pressing dampness against the foundations.

The heron does not live there all the time. It comes as a visitor comes to a house where it has once been fed. At dawn it stands in the deepest puddle, narrow and gray against the green, waiting for movement beneath the surface. It has learned that water in the pocket gathers small lives quickly: larvae, water beetles, the fragile first stages of insects no one notices until they become wings. The heron’s expectation gives the puddle a temporary importance. For an hour it is not an accident of rain; it is a pond, an appointment, a place within the bird’s remembered world.

And sometimes, perhaps, there is something beneath even these.

The people who wake before sunrise have seen the raw rise breathing—not rising visibly, not like the chest of an animal, but darkening and easing as water moves below its surface. In certain mornings after warm rain, a seam opens in the soil and releases a smell like iron, roots, and old clay jars brought up from a cellar. By afternoon the seam may be gone. A bright green shoot may stand where it was.

No one agrees whether the hillock is growing.

The oldest woman in the nearest house says the earth is only settling after a long time underground, finding the shape it was denied when the town was laid out. A mason says there is buried rubble beneath it, perhaps the fallen corner of some earlier building. A child once claimed a large animal slept there, curled so deeply that its back had become earth. The child was laughed at, though not harshly. That night, no one walked across the rise.

Whatever lives under it does not threaten the town. It has had every opportunity. It could have taken the walls more quickly, opened the basin into a sinkhole, drawn water from the wells, split the street. Instead it makes small requests.

A door no longer shuts.

A paving stone sinks.

A drain clogs with roots.

A pale brick appears where there had been mud.

The town answers by adjusting. A latch is lifted. A stone is reset. Water is sent another way. A wall is repaired, though not too rigidly. The houses have learned the pocket’s language without admitting it. Their crookedness is a kind of conversation.

At night, when the doors are shut and the ember-light has gone out, the pocket becomes crowded with its true inhabitants. The frogs take the hollows. The beetles open their concealed roads. The cat watches from the border, its tail tucked around its paws, unwilling to trust the center. Vines release the day’s held moisture. The fig’s roots make minute pressures in the wall. Somewhere under the loose dark slope, grains of soil slide into a new arrangement.

Then the whole place seems to listen to itself.

Not for danger. Not for a command.

For the next small change.
