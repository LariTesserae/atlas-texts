What lives on the plateau is not animal life, and not quite plant life, though from a distance it has the organization of a flora.

The first inhabitants are the things that make the vents habitable: thin mineral skins within the cracks, invisible except where they thicken into iridescent films. They feed on what the land exhales. Ammonia, sulfur compounds, iron vapor, carbon-rich gases—substances that would be poison or waste elsewhere—pass over membranes only a fraction of a grain thick. There, in darkness or under full sun, they are taken apart, rearranged, made into tissue. The films spread through stone pores and along the undersides of salt plates. They stain fractures green-black, bronze, violet, and occasionally a dense blue so dark it seems to absorb the daylight.

They are the oldest living things here, though “old” is difficult to assign in a country with so little decay. A film may remain in one seam for centuries, growing outward by barely a finger-width, becoming layered beneath itself as newer life forms over older life. If broken, it does not bleed or wither. It simply exposes another active layer below.

From these skins rise the larger gas-feeders: the ribbed columns, the enamel fans, the blade-groves. They are colonies as much as individuals. A single pale column may appear separate aboveground, but beneath the dust it joins a broad, flattened body threaded through with mineral fibers. Hundreds of columns can therefore belong to one buried organism, arranged around a vent in a deliberate-looking circumference. Their hollow interiors draw in warm vapor at their bases. Within, folded tissues strain particular gases from the flow.

In the morning, they are still. By noon, when the vent gases have warmed, a close observer might notice their ribs tightening almost imperceptibly. They do not sway. They pulse.

The dark-green fans are slower and more armored. Their glossy surfaces are not leaves but protective lids over moist chemistry kept deep inside. The copper and violet margins are active growth zones, where the organism deposits compounds scavenged from the air and ground. When a fan reaches the edge of its viable mineral band, it stops. It does not search beyond its chemistry. It cannot afford curiosity. Its life is an exact agreement with the stone beneath it.

The low meadows are perhaps the most numerous inhabitants. Each scale-like plate is a sealed little furnace of metabolism, opening its central pore when the air is cool enough to receive gas and closing it against the white heat of midday. A basin covered in them is not one meadow but millions of near-identical living units, all descended from a dust that settled there generations ago. They do not compete in the ordinary sense. They touch edge to edge, recognizing related chemistry through minute exchanges in the dust, and form continuous mats wherever the ground offers the right salts.

At certain hours, especially near dawn, the pores across an entire basin open together. Nothing visible occurs at first. Then a faint darkening passes over the meadow, like breath drawn through cloth. The sound is too low to hear unless one presses an ear close to the surface: a dry, collective intake.

The white groves are the tallest residents and the nearest thing the plateau has to forests. Their trunks precipitate themselves as much as they grow. Each ridge of their crystalline bark is a record of a period when the gases were slightly richer in one metal or another. The green blades above are exposed organs, broad enough to gather light but thick enough to endure the mineral air. They do not shed in a season. When a blade becomes too heavily crusted with salts, the trunk seals it off at the base. It remains attached for years: gray, rigid, and dead among the living green fins, until a tremor or thermal crack brings it down.

When such a blade falls, it does not feed animals or rot into soil. The mats around its base gradually dissolve its softer tissues with secreted acids. Its mineral framework remains. Over decades it becomes one more pale shard in the dust.

There are reproductive forms, though none are showy in the way flowers are showy. The red-black cups that open toward vents are spore chambers. Their sparkling crystals are not nectar but carefully built packets: salts, carbon structures, dormant membranes, perhaps enough stored chemistry to establish life on a newly opened fracture. When a cup splits, the dust it releases travels only a little way. There is no wind to carry it across the world, and no animal to bear it on fur or feather. The dust rolls, settles, waits. Most of it remains inert forever. A few grains find a warm crack whose vapor matches the chemistry of their parent body closely enough. There they wake.

That is how the plateau fills: not by conquest, but by exact recurrence.

There may also be life beneath the mineral ribs—organisms so deeply joined to the black carbon stone that they cannot be separated from it without killing both. The ribs are not alive in the way a grove is alive, but they may be inhabited through their entire depth by slow conductive tissues. These tissues take energy from heat differences, chemical gradients, perhaps even from the faint electrical tensions generated as minerals expand and contract. Their growth is measured in laminations. A dark rib lengthens not visibly from year to year, but its internal layers may be continually remade, old carbon converted into new structures, new structures pressed into stone.

Near sunset, when the ribs begin their muted ticking, some of that sound may be life responding—not speech, not alarm, but the adjustment of living mineral membranes to cooling rock.

There are no hunters because there is nothing that needs to run. No grazers because the green things are defended by acid, metal, and unpalatable crystal. No pollinators because reproduction is handled by heat, fracture, and dust. No scavengers because death is rarely sudden and almost never abundant. Each organism, when its vent weakens or its salts are exhausted, closes itself gradually. Its surfaces harden. Its inner tissues become dry and glassy. In time it is indistinguishable from a mineral formation except for the faint chemical activity persisting in its deepest seams.

If there are creatures at all in the animal sense, they are microscopic and vent-bound: tiny armored cells moving through warm films, drifting from pore to pore in condensate too slight to call water. They may feed on the living skins, or serve them, or be merely tolerated in their interiors. They would never cross the open dust. Between one green island and the next lies not merely distance but an uninhabitable emptiness.

So the plateau is inhabited thoroughly, though sparsely in the human meaning of the word. Its residents do not look outward. They do not watch the horizon, make paths, gather, flee, sing, or mark territory. They are fixed to particular vapors, temperatures, salts, and fractures. Each one is less a body occupying the land than a local expression of the land’s chemistry.

The groves are the stone breathing through green blades.  
The mats are the dust learning to seal itself.  
The columns are vents given structure.  
The black ribs are perhaps the earth’s slow metabolism exposed to daylight.

Nothing crosses the plateau.

But everywhere the ground is occupied.
