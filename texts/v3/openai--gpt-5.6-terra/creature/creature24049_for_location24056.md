There are people on the plateau, though a visitor could remain there for weeks and decide there are none.

They do not live in the towers. The towers are older than their memory and belong, depending on whom one asks, to the east wind, the gliders, the dead, or the stone itself. The people use them as one uses a good tree: for shade, for bearings, for leaving offerings in, for knowing where one is when the weather has come up from below and erased the far ground.

Their homes are harder to see.

Most are set into the shallow folds of the plateau, where a shelf of rock leans over another shelf and makes a pocket of shade. From above, a dwelling resembles a natural outcrop patched with lichen. Its walls are low, made of fitted slabs with dust packed in the seams. Its roof is usually the land itself: a broad, flat stone, supported from beneath by ribs of old wood brought up from the ravines or by the curved plates shed from the great walkers.

They build downward whenever they can. Beneath each household is a cool chamber, then another, smaller one, and sometimes a narrow stone throat leading to a seep or a cistern. The deepest rooms smell of wet clay and roots. Children sleep there in the hottest months. Seeds are kept there, wrapped in waxy leaves. So are the bones of kin, not buried exactly, but laid in wall niches where the slow breath of the earth can reach them.

The plateau people are slight, but not frail. Their bodies are made for heat and distance. Their skin has the muted colors of dry stone: umber, red-brown, gray, ocher, sometimes nearly black where families have long lived near basalt seams. Their hair is kept close to the scalp or braided in thin cords and wound around the head, not for fashion alone but to keep dust from settling in the ears and eyes during the high winds.

Their eyes are often pale around the iris, ringed with gold or silver-gray, an adaptation elders say came from generations spent looking across bright ground. They narrow easily in sunlight. At dusk, when the landscape loses its colors, they see better than strangers expect.

They are not a numerous people. No one on the plateau can say how many there are without pausing for a long time.

They count households, not persons. They count water mouths, not households. They count the basins that have held rain this season, the great walkers that have crossed the southern road, the nests that have successfully fledged on the ravine walls. A person may belong to several places over the course of a year: a ridge settlement in the dry months, a ravine camp when the fruit trees flower below, a basin enclosure during rain season. To ask where someone is from is considered incomplete. One must ask which water remembers them.

Their language carries poorly by design.

At close range it is low and precise, full of breaths taken through the teeth, clicks of the tongue, and notes held in the chest rather than sent outward. Across distance they use stone. A slab struck with one kind of bone announces a safe arrival. Another rhythm says that a basin has filled. A long pause between two knocks warns of a predator near a trail. These signals do not travel far in the ordinary sense; they pass through the ground. Someone sitting with bare feet on a shaded shelf may feel news from a neighboring ravine before hearing any sound at all.

Visitors tend to mistake the people’s silence for solemnity. It is not solemnity. It is economy.

The plateau has taught them that every motion declares something and every noise invites an answer. A dropped tool may startle a glider from its nest, which may rouse a herd, which may bring scavengers, which may attract the attention of something patient in a ravine. So they move carefully, but they are not joyless. Their laughter is mostly silent: shoulders shaking, mouths open, hands pressed against one another’s wrists. When they sing, they sing underground or during rain, when the entire plateau is already occupied with listening to water.

They keep no large livestock. The long-legged grazers are not owned, though certain herds know certain settlements and will approach at dawn for salt scraped from specific rocks. The people follow them at a distance, reading their choices. Where the grazers nibble, tender growth will be found. Where they stop and lift their ears, water may be near, or danger, or simply a change in the wind.

The great plated walkers are treated with a respect that is not worship but comes close to kinship. Each has a name that is not a name: a sequence of pauses, hand signs, and a description of the trail it favors. Children are taught to recognize them by the fractures in their plates, the moss that grows on their upper ridges, the particular moths nesting along their shells.

When a walker dies, the event alters a region for years.

The people do not hasten its death and do not strip it immediately. First they wait for the smaller lives to arrive: mites, lizards, pale moths, beetles that have no visible wings. Then the scavengers. Then the roots of the finger-shrubs begin to explore the softened ground beneath its body. Only after one full wet season do the people take fallen plates from the edges, never from the center. Those plates become roof ribs, cistern lids, wind shields, and occasionally the curved doors of burial niches.

A house roofed in walker-plate is considered fortunate, but it must be maintained with humility. If it creaks at night, the family opens the door and lets the cool air pass through. To seal such a house completely would be rude.

Their principal labor is water.

Every child learns the basins before learning the names of distant places. They know which bowls drink quickly into fractures, which remain muddy for three days, which fill only from rain falling directly overhead, which collect runoff from gullies several ridges away. They keep some basins open by brushing dust and dead lichen from their channels. Others they deliberately leave untouched because the frogs—small, pale, nearly transparent creatures—breed only in water clouded by old leaf matter.

They have ways of catching what the plateau gives reluctantly. Dew cloths are stretched between stones before dawn: woven meshes of grass fiber, moth silk gathered from abandoned cocoons, and the fine translucent skins shed by the finger-shrubs. At sunrise, these cloths are heavy enough to wring into cups. In deeper ravines they hang polished stone plates beneath seep lines, so that a day’s slow dripping gathers in shaded jars.

Their vessels are mostly stone, shell, and fired clay from ravine bottoms. They do not make much pottery, because firewood is scarce and fire on the open plateau is considered a dangerous sort of speech. But they make excellent small things: needles from glider bones, scrapers from basalt flakes, flexible cord from grass roots, water stoppers carved from the dense wood of the ancient pool trees.

They wear layered cloth in the day despite the heat: loose pale wraps that keep the sun from touching skin directly. At night, when the warmth leaves the rock, they add mantles lined with the soft underfur gathered from the resting places of the foxlike ravine animals. Nothing is taken during breeding season. The foxes are said to have whiskers through which they can taste dreams, and whether this is true or not, no plateau child will sleep near a fox den without covering their face.

The people do hunt, though infrequently. They prefer traps that require waiting over weapons that require pursuit. A hunter may spend an entire afternoon arranging a sequence of stones near a burrow, placing scentless loops where an animal will not notice them until it has already chosen its path. Even then, an animal caught too young, too lean, or carrying young is released. The plateau’s people are practical, but their practicality includes the next dry season.

Their dead are not all treated alike.

Those who die in the wet months are often placed in the high towers, wrapped in grass cloth, with their feet facing east. The gliders may nest there afterward. This is considered good. Those who die in the dry months may be laid in deep stone chambers, near water that cannot be seen. Children and those who have drowned—rare, but possible in sudden rain floods—are given to the ravines, where the walls are full of roots and quiet life.

But some elders choose another ending.

When they feel their bodies becoming too slow for the heat, they leave at dusk with a water cup, a strip of dried fruit, and a small chime made from two stones. They go to sit near the routes of the great walkers. No one follows. In the morning there may be footprints, a cup, a chime, or nothing at all. The family then watches that route for a year. If a walker pauses there, the elder is spoken of as having found a patient body. If rain gathers there unexpectedly, they are said to have opened a door.

There are children everywhere, though they too are difficult to spot. They play in the old enclosures, building tiny towers that lean impossible distances before falling. They float seed husks in temporary pools. They collect the copper-bright mites from walker plates and persuade them to crawl over their hands. They know the calls of every common glider but imitate only the harmless ones.

Their games are exercises in attention.

One game consists of standing still beside a basin and naming every living thing that arrives before the shadow of a chosen rock reaches another chosen rock. Another is played at dusk: children scatter across a flat and must find one another by feeling faint clicks through the ground. The winner is not the first to arrive, but the one who leaves the fewest tracks.

In adolescence, each person spends several days alone in a ravine, with no tool except a water cup and a wrapped meal. They are not expected to see a vision. The plateau people distrust visions that announce themselves too clearly. They are expected to return with an answer to a smaller question: where did water last move? Which wall is warmer after sunset? What creature did you hear but fail to see? What did you mistake for stone?

The last question matters most.

Because the plateau is full of things that resemble what they are not. A folded plate may be a sleeping walker. A dark seam may be a burrow mouth. A patch of lichen may conceal a colony of soft-bodied wall creatures. A low mound may be an old house abandoned so long ago that its descendants have built another house beside it without knowing.

And perhaps the people themselves resemble stone more than they realize.

They age slowly in the eyes of outsiders. Their faces become lined, but their movements remain spare and balanced. Old people can walk the ridges long after their knees would have failed elsewhere, because they have never spent themselves on unnecessary speed. They sit in shade with their palms on the ground, listening to the insect-hum and the minute shifts beneath the surface. Some can identify a familiar walker by the vibration of its step from far beyond sight.

At sunset, the people come out.

Not all at once. A figure appears beside a water mouth. Another rises from behind a lichen-dark wall. Two children run, briefly and extravagantly, across a red flat before an adult calls them back with a hand lifted low. Smoke may emerge from a sheltered cooking hollow, thin and quickly dispersed. In the distance, someone strikes a tower stone once, then once again.

The grazing herds drift toward the basins. The scavengers arrange themselves at the edge of sight. The walkers settle against the cliffs. Above, the gliders turn their patterned wings toward the fading light.

Then the people sit among the old structures and the living stone, eating little, speaking softly, waiting for dew.

From far away, they vanish into the plateau.

From close by, one sees that the plateau has not merely endured them.

It has taught them how to belong without becoming separate from anything else.
