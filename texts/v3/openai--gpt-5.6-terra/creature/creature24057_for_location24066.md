What lives there is not easily separated into animal, plant, weather, or ruin.

At first, a visitor would notice only the small lives.

There are the glass-backed snails that travel the undersides of stones, their bodies clear enough to show the green threads of what they have eaten. They leave no visible trail, but where several have passed, a skin of pale mineral sheen remains. In rain-country, there are reed moths: soft-bodied things with wings too sodden to fly in the ordinary way. They open their wings in the falling water and let themselves be lifted or lowered by the pressure of it. Their wing scales collect on moss in faint blue drifts.

Inside the purple bladders of the reeds live colonies of minute lamps. They may be insects, or larvae, or a form of flowering that learned to move. Most of the time they remain still, shining through the bladder wall like buried stars. When a bladder bursts, the lights emerge in a slow cloud. They do not scatter. They settle on nearby wet surfaces and begin, very gradually, to grow new bladders around themselves.

There are root-eaters in the drowned forests: narrow animals with jointless white bodies, too large to be worms and too deliberate to be eels. They enter a trunk through a wound or an old knot and spend years inside it. A healthy tree does not seem to suffer from them. In fact, forests containing many root-eaters are brighter and more responsive to the warm currents. When one of these animals finally leaves a tree, it carries a small parcel of dark wood in its mouth. It sets this parcel into a crack in stone, where a new trunk may later begin.

The transparent snails avoid them.

The small silver things in the crevices avoid almost everything. They are flat, quick, and reflective only when observed from directly above. From any other angle they vanish into the mineral patterns of the rock. They gather around the mouths of the breathing cliffs, where warm water emerges, and hold themselves in place with tiny hooked fins. During a surge they disappear for months. No one knows where they go. Sometimes, after a great rearrangement of the landscape, they are found in a newly exposed basin before any current could reasonably have carried them there.

The long white creatures of the deep are called, by the few things capable of naming, the Veins.

This is not because they resemble veins. They do not. They are immense and pale, with bodies composed of many soft segments that pass through one another’s shadows. From a distance, one may look like a white river moving uphill through black water. Up close—if there is such a thing as up close with a Vein—it has no obvious eyes, no mouth, no fins, no clear beginning or end. Its skin is covered in faded folds, like old cloth dissolved almost to transparency.

A Vein does not seem to feed. It moves through regions where the water is stagnant or overburdened with rot, silt, broken spores, and the residue of collapsed things. After it has passed, the water is clearer. Not clean, exactly. More coherent. Suspended matter gathers into slow ribbons, roots straighten, small creatures return to the stone.

The drowned forests recognize them. Every frond turns toward their wakes. The membrane-leaves open wide, as though drinking a signal rather than water.

Sometimes a Vein brushes an ancient pillar or a buried stair. Then the carvings on the stone brighten briefly beneath the algae. Not with light, but with a darkening around their lines, as if the stone has remembered where its surfaces were meant to be. This may be coincidence. In this place, coincidence often has the patience of an organism.

There are also the black-winged gliders above the rainline.

They are not birds, though they have the shape of birds from below: a narrow body, immense wings, a pointed head always angled slightly toward the water. Their wings are not feathered. They are made of black layered membranes, with pale channels visible inside them when the filtered light catches at the right angle. They fly through rain as fish move through kelp. They can fold their wings completely around themselves and fall for miles, becoming a black seed in the water.

They nest nowhere visible.

Their cries have never been heard. Yet when one circles a plateau for several days, the moss beneath its path changes. New rivulets form in the moss. Ferns uncurl. The purple reed bladders swell. After the glider leaves, the plateau may shed an entire section of itself into the cloud below, as if its passing has completed a long-delayed instruction.

The creatures most likely to be mistaken for people are the Gatherers.

They inhabit the borderlands between water and fog, especially around the old walls, the pillar circles, and the high stone bowls where rain collects. A Gatherer has a torso of woven reed, root fiber, shed skin, and dark peat. Its limbs are made from whatever the current has brought: branches, pale bone-like mineral growths, braided vines, lengths of algae hardened with silt. It is never built the same way twice. It may stand upright for a while. It may crawl. It may hang beneath an overhang with its limbs spread like roots.

From a distance, a Gatherer can resemble a person in soaked clothing.

It does not possess a face. Where a face might be, there is usually a hollow full of water, and in that water drift seeds, pollen, insect lights, fragments of leaf, and sometimes a smooth stone. When it is active, these things circle slowly in the hollow. When it is resting, they settle.

Gatherers do not speak. They make small arrangements.

They clear silt from stairways, though they do not restore the stairs. They place fallen stones upright in the old circles. They carry loosened pieces of wall from one ridge to another and set them down where no wall could ever be completed. They collect the white sheddings of the deep creatures and wind them around the roots of drowned trees. They sometimes lift a drowned animal or a dead plant out of one current and place it in another.

It is tempting to call this burial, repair, or ritual.

The Gatherers make no distinction.

At intervals, several of them will come together around a bowl of rainwater on a plateau. They stand or kneel at its edge. The water in the bowl begins to rise, though no rain falls into it. The gathered materials in their bodies loosen. Seeds release. Threads unbraid. Their limbs slump into the moss. By the end, nothing remains but an ordinary-looking patch of wet ground thick with roots.

Months or centuries later, depending on the local direction of time, a new Gatherer may pull itself from that patch carrying a different assortment of debris.

There are older inhabitants than these.

They live in the mountain interiors, where water enters stone and stone enters water. They are not seen as bodies. One becomes aware of them through response.

A cliff exhales warm mineral water. Nearby vines tighten.

A basin fills from below.

A suspended pool trembles but does not fall.

Roots descend from a floating ceiling of leaf-soil and turn, all at once, toward a crack no wider than a finger.

These are signs of the Interior Ones, if they are ones at all. They may be a distributed kind of consciousness spread among faults, roots, buried chambers, and the porous bones of the mountains. They do not move across the landscape. The landscape moves through them. A collapse is one of their gestures. A new channel is another. The slow migration of a peat grove may be an accident of buoyancy, or it may be the deliberate repositioning of an organ.

The old fitted walls seem particularly close to them.

During the dim internal illumination, the narrow openings in the wall below the rainline sometimes fill with a deeper darkness than the water around them. The darkness does not merely occupy the openings. It appears to look outward. Nearby moss flattens. The rain changes direction for several breaths. Then the wall is only a wall again.

Whether the Interior Ones made the ruins cannot be known.

It may be more accurate to say that the ruins are where they have learned to hold still.

And beneath even the warm black depths, below the channels where the Veins pass, something else may live: not a creature, but a condition that has become watchful.

It is what the small fauna avoids when they freeze in place. It is what makes noisy life rare. It does not pursue. It does not lunge from the dark. There are no teeth glimpsed, no pressure wave, no sudden absence.

Instead, disturbances simply fail to continue.

A frantic animal enters a deep current and becomes calm. A storm of silt settles in a single downward curl. A broken branch spinning violently through the water comes to rest upright, root-end down, as if planted. Sound traveling toward the deepest basins diminishes before distance can explain it.

The Veins do not avoid this presence. The Gatherers do not approach it. The black-winged gliders never cross directly above it.

Perhaps it is the old body’s sleeping mind. Perhaps it is only the accumulated stillness of immeasurable water. Perhaps the mountains, softened beyond resistance, have learned the final defense of all ancient things: not to fight what enters them, but to absorb it so completely that it can no longer remember having been separate.

So the place is inhabited.

By snails and lamps, by root-eaters and silver crevice-fish. By silent gliders and pale deep-passers. By temporary figures assembled from the remains of every season. By forests that turn together toward warmth. By stone that breathes. By water that carries memory upward from a ground no shore has touched in ages.

And perhaps by the whole of it at once: the vast submerged body, wearing its creatures as nerves, spores, thoughts, and dreams.
