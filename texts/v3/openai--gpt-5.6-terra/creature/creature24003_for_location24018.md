Nothing on the shelf lives alone.

Even the things that appear solitary—a reed tower, a cliff bird circling in mist, a person standing beside a warm spring—are part of arrangements too large and slow to be easily seen. The plateau has no true wilderness in the sense of untouched land, and no true settlement in the sense of land subdued. Its inhabitants are participants in a country that has been inhabited for so long that dwelling, cultivation, migration, and maintenance have become difficult to separate.

The first inhabitants are the root-webs.

They lie beneath almost every soft place on the plateau: pale, heat-loving filaments threaded through porous stone, wrapped around mineral chimneys, spreading beneath marshes and forests in dense felted sheets. They are not one organism, though people often speak of them as if they were. They are a federation of roots, fungal veils, burrowing larvae, bacterial colonies, and the fine white tendrils of plants that have forgotten how to grow downward into ordinary soil. They drink heat as much as water. In cold weather—which arrives only in rare, wind-scoured seasons—they draw warmth upward from deep stone and keep whole gullies green.

Where the root-web is thick, the ground yields like a mattress. Hooved animals know how to cross it. So do the people. Outsiders do not, at first. They step too heavily, break the crust, and find their boots filling with water that is hot enough to sting. The root-web responds slowly to damage. It closes pores. It redirects water. It raises mats of new growth over scars. In a century, a collapsed slope may become a meadow again; in two centuries, no one can find the break unless they know how the grasses lean in rain.

The plateau people call these living layers the Underhold.

They do not regard it as sacred in the way a temple is sacred. It is closer than that. It is household, foundation, ancestor, drainage system, garden, archive. Children are taught to listen with their palms pressed flat to the warm ground. There are places where one can feel groundwater moving beneath the roots in separate currents: one fast and bright, one heavy with iron, one so hot it makes the skin ache. Experienced listeners can tell when a spring is preparing to shift its source, when a marsh will rise, when a vent is becoming dangerous, when the deep mountain has begun one of its long internal adjustments.

The people themselves are few by lowland standards, but no one on the shelf thinks of them as scarce. They are distributed. One household may occupy an entire ravine, another a series of rooms beneath a reed marsh, another a cluster of cliff hollows linked by pressure bridges. Their settlements have names that are often also descriptions of water: Warm-Under-the-East-Roots, Three Falling Channels, Lake-that-Holds-Still, the Moss Stair, the Long Exhale.

They are not a single people in appearance, custom, or ancestry. The plateau has received travelers for centuries: survivors of airship failures, pilgrims, researchers, refugees, merchants who came for medicinal minerals, people who climbed too high and discovered they could not bear to leave. The mountain changes those who remain, though not quickly enough for anyone to call it transformation.

Most plateau-born people are broad-chested and careful-footed. Their lungs develop large and efficient in the thin air, and their skin often carries a faint sheen from the oils they use against constant damp. Their hair is worn close against the head during the wet season or braided with water-shedding threads. Many have the pale irregular markings common among the grazing animals—not the same markings, but analogous ones: patches that darken or brighten with temperature, fatigue, excitement. On a misty path, an entire family may seem to emerge from the landscape in fragments: one bright cheek, a wet shoulder, a hand raised briefly through fern fronds.

They dress for water, heat, and abrasion rather than for cold. Their outer garments are made from reed fiber, softened bark, treated vine-skin, and thin membranes grown in household vats. The best of these fabrics shed rain without becoming rigid and can be opened at the shoulders or ribs when steam rises from the ground. Their boots are usually split-soled, flexible enough to feel unstable surfaces. Many go barefoot in familiar places, especially in the warm hours before evening cloud.

They are not silent people, but they have learned not to compete with the plateau’s sound.

Conversation is often carried by touch, glances, short whistles, and tones pitched to pass through running water. Across ravines, messages may be played on reed resonators: sequences of knocks and breathy notes that travel farther than a shout and do not disturb nesting birds. In dense fog, people sometimes wear small throat devices that answer one another with low pulses. A household moving through cloud can locate itself by the pattern of vibrations felt in the sternum.

Their language, where it has not been influenced by lowland trade speech, contains many kinds of duration. There are separate words for a rain that lasts until sunset, a rain that lasts until the roots begin drinking differently, and a rain that returns often enough to change the color of stone. There are verbs for healing that mean closing cleanly, healing that means growing around a wound, healing that means remaining damaged but useful. There is no simple word for “empty.” A place without people is still full of water routes, bird claims, root memory, dormant equipment, and future weather.

Their houses are grown, guided, or persuaded more often than built.

A young dwelling begins as a prepared hollow: a fold in rock, a cavity beneath tree roots, a dry pocket in the living embankment of a marsh. Its walls are seeded with mineral-eating lichen and slow-growing structural tissue. Over years, the rooms thicken and seal. The house learns the local moisture cycle. It opens pores when the air is stale, closes them when sulphur mist rolls through, shifts color and roughness according to season. A house on a sunny ridge may become pale and granular in dry months, then dark and slick-looking under the wet cloud season. A marsh house may lift a few inches over decades as peat accumulates beneath it.

No household owns a dwelling absolutely. One is said to keep it, or listen for it, or be responsible for its temper. If a house begins to fail—if it sweats too much mineral water, develops warm spots in the sleeping walls, stops releasing collected rain cleanly—the family consults a keeper of structures, but they also consult the marsh, the nearest root-web, the local bird colony, and sometimes the spire.

The spires have keepers, though the keepers do not claim to understand them.

These are usually patient people, often older, though not always. Their work consists largely of attention. They record the paths of fog around the spire, the behavior of insects in its field, the texture of its reflected light, the dreams reported by anyone sleeping nearby. They inspect the ground for changes in mineral deposition. They bring injured animals to the threshold of the effect when appropriate, and carry them away if the animal shows distress. They remove vines only when the vines threaten to close a surface that must remain exposed; otherwise they let green growth cover the old machines as it will.

A spire keeper may spend three days doing nothing visible but sitting under a rain cape beside a thin silver mast, eating steamed roots and making marks on a water-resistant slate.

This is regarded as skilled labor.

The plateau’s technology is not operated so much as tended through relationship. It responds badly to extraction, haste, or command. A pressure bridge will not stabilize merely because someone asks it to. It must be fed the right local conditions: the airflow understood, the anchor stones cleaned, the root-web beneath checked for distress, the crossing load kept within a pattern it recognizes. People say the bridges dislike certainty. A person who marches onto one impatiently is more likely to find it wavering. A person who pauses, waits for the shimmer to strengthen, and crosses at the pace of the breeze will feel it harden beneath the feet.

The old lake rings are tended by fishers who do not fish inside them.

They live at the margins of the warm lakes in low houses roofed with floating gardens. Their work is to watch the silver congregations below the pillars, to count spawning movements, to measure the water’s strange stillness, and to harvest only what the lake releases beyond the ring. They use broad shallow nets that permit young fish, bright-scaled scavengers, and certain revered eel-like species to pass through untouched. The fishers cultivate edible water plants in woven frames and raise small marsh birds for eggs, though the birds are allowed to nest where they please and are considered partners rather than stock.

Some of the lake people bear tiny scars along their forearms from handling mineral-rich reeds. Their hands are almost always wet. They can tell the health of a lake by taste, which alarms visitors: a fingertip dipped in water, touched to the tongue, followed by a long thoughtful silence.

There are herders, too, but their animals are never fully domesticated.

The long-legged grazers move in bands across the grassy shelves, following warmth, fresh mineral growth, and the seasonal movements of ground water. Each band knows certain human families, and certain families know its routes. In the morning, herders walk ahead, testing the turf with poles and singing low, repetitive phrases that the animals recognize. They do not drive the herds so much as negotiate with them. At dangerous vents, they lay down strips of fragrant moss. At river crossings, they wait. If a band refuses a route, it is assumed that the animals have sensed something the humans have not.

Their milk is thin, hot, faintly salty, and prized in the wet season. Their shed skin, gathered from rubbing stones, is worked into waterproof membranes. The animals are never slaughtered casually. When one dies, the entire band may remain near the body for a day, breathing steam into its hide. The people wait as well. They take what can be used only after the birds have begun their circling and the root-web has received the blood.

Children grow up among all of this with unusual freedom and unusually strict instruction.

They are allowed to climb, wade, follow insects, sleep in reed nests during warm nights, and ride in broad baskets across pressure bridges. But they are taught the map of hazards before they are taught formal history: white steam kills, yellow steam blinds, clear water can burn, still water can pull, soft ground can be safe if it sings beneath the foot and deadly if it does not. They learn to recognize the click-pattern of reed towers before a heavy cloud descent. They learn which blue forest lamps belong to harmless leaves and which indicate plants that have opened to feed on insects.

Their games resemble maintenance. They make miniature channels in rain-softened stone and compete to direct water without eroding the moss. They build tiny bridges from tensioned grass and test them with beetles. They imitate the calls of cliff birds, then listen for real answers. They hide offerings of polished mineral in old machinery and return years later to see whether the objects have been absorbed, displaced, or left untouched.

The oldest inhabitants, according to some, are neither the root-webs nor the devices nor the mountain itself.

They are the cloud animals.

These are rarely seen clearly. They live along the shelf edges and beneath the higher overhangs, moving in and out of the white country below. At a distance they resemble vast manta-like shapes, or pale kites, or folds of weather with darker bodies moving inside them. They ride the thermal updrafts for days without landing. When they do come to the plateau, it is at night, usually in the months when warm water changes course under the western terraces.

They feed, perhaps, on airborne insects, mineral vapor, electrical discharge, or something less comprehensible. Their undersides shine with rows of dim organs that pulse blue-green through cloud. Their calls are so low that they are felt in water vessels and in the bones of sleeping people. Old stories say they are not animals at all but the mountain’s discarded dreams. Practical people dismiss this, but practical people still cover their roof vents when the cloud animals pass, and no one walks alone near the western falls on those nights.

The cliff birds are more familiar and no less important.

They nest beneath the shelf where the world drops away. Their nests are built from reed stalks, shed herd-skin, bright scraps of old structural membrane, and branches carried from forests far below. They are enormous birds, black-backed and pale-bellied, with hooked beaks adapted for fish, carrion, and the soft mineral nodules exposed after landslides. Their wings catch the rising heat from the mountain and hold them aloft with almost no movement.

The people read their behavior as one reads instruments. A sudden inland flight can mean electrical currents rising from a fissure. Low nesting means the coming wet season will be heavy. If the birds circle silently over a lake ring, the fishers suspend all work and wait for the water to explain itself.

There are smaller residents, too numerous to name individually: glass-backed beetles that warm themselves on vent stone; blind white crabs that emerge from hot channels after dark; narrow-faced marsh mammals that weave nests from flower stems; bright amphibians whose deep calls synchronize across entire valleys; lizards with leaflike frills that live among the blue lamps of the forest canopy. Many are harmless. Some are not. The plateau’s dangers are not dramatic predators so much as abundance with teeth, heat, poison, or patience.

A person may die there from beauty as readily as from violence: by following glowing flowers into a gas hollow, by sleeping beneath a dripping ledge whose water carries too much metal, by trusting a path that has shifted overnight because the mountain decided to breathe elsewhere.

This is why the people are attentive.

Attention is their principal art. It shapes their cooking, travel, architecture, medicine, courtship, mourning, and governance. A council meeting may begin with weather reports, animal reports, water reports, root reports, and only afterward turn to disputes between people. To make a decision that harms a ravine, a migration route, or a lake is not merely foolish; it is nearly impossible to carry out, because the harmed thing will respond through a thousand inconvenient changes.

Their dead are not buried deeply. The ground below is too warm and too alive.

Instead, bodies are placed in open stone chambers near chosen springs, wrapped in reed cloth and lined with mineral salts. Over time the chambers become gardens. Roots enter through carefully made cracks. Moss covers the walls. The water that emerges below is not used for drinking for several years, but it is used to nourish particular trees, especially the pale-barked ones whose paper curls are collected for records and ritual messages. In this way, a person’s final contribution is said to be not return, but continuation.

There are visitors who come to the plateau seeking its healing devices, its warm high air, its impossible agriculture, its old intelligence. Most are disappointed at first.

They expect a hidden city, a command center, a revelation in metal and light. Instead they find wet paths, quiet homes, a great deal of waiting, and people who ask whether they have learned to distinguish safe steam from dangerous steam. They are given work before answers: clearing a channel, carrying reed bundles, sitting with an injured grazer, monitoring a lake for three nights, learning how to cross a bridge that cannot be seen.

Some leave frustrated.

Some remain.

Those who remain eventually understand that the plateau is not guarded by secrecy. It is guarded by scale. To live there, one must accept that the mountain’s decisions take longer than a human life, that a piece of machinery may have obligations older than any village, and that convenience is a poor reason to interrupt a root, a bird route, a cloud current, or a marsh.

What lives there, finally, is a civilization of custodianship: people, animals, plants, waters, and patient machines bound into habits of mutual allowance.

The people do not believe the mountain belongs to them.

Nor do they think they belong to the mountain in any simple way.

They say instead that the mountain has made room.
