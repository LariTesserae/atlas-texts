What lives in the basin is not one species, though at first a visitor might mistake it for one.

The first impression is of dust in motion.

Under the lamps, the floor seems to breathe in patches. Gray powder gathers itself into slow-edged stains, thins, flows around bolts and cracks, then settles again. Look longer and the dust resolves into bodies: the mineral grazers, countless hard grains on six legs, each carrying a dull sheen of whatever it last consumed. There are colonies colored by place. The ones that live on iron scale are red-brown and black. Those on concrete salts wear a chalky pallor. Those that feed around the leaking wires have a faint cobalt glimmer in their seams.

They are not blind exactly. Their heads have no eyes, but each has a fan of sensitive pits around its mouthparts. They read heat, electrical potential, pressure, and the minute taste of airborne metal. A human footfall reaches them through the floor long before it becomes audible. At that signal they tighten into pellets by the thousands, and the basin changes texture: what appeared to be dust becomes a scatter of tiny, rolling stones.

Then, when the danger does not crush or burn them, they open again.

Their opened shells make much of the basin’s sound. It is a delicate sound, nearly below hearing: millions of dry little clicks, like a rainstorm occurring inside a box of broken watches.

The grazers are the basis of almost everything. They feed on the slow ruin of the place and turn it into their own shells, their own warmth, their own brief soft interiors. The runners hunt them in bursts, flicking their jointed tails through the dust. A runner catches one by rolling it between its front limbs until the shell splits. It eats quickly, leaving half-polished husks behind. Those husks are eaten in turn by smaller things—transparent-bodied mites that live in wire insulation, bristled worms that emerge from the packed floor, pale scavengers which cannot feed until something else has already opened the meal.

Nothing is wasted, but nothing is clean.

The crablike wall-clingers are among the most patient animals. They live beside junction boxes and broken conduits, where the stone is slightly warm. Their bodies are as flat as flakes of old paint. Most have no color except the pale yellow in their joint sockets, visible only when they move. They wait with their magnetic pads spread against the wall, feeling the minute tremor of current in buried cables. When the current shifts, the grazers gather. When the grazers gather, the clingers fold themselves down from above.

They do not pounce. They let go.

A clinger drops without warning, lands on a swarm, and closes all six limbs around a handful of bodies. The swarm breaks outward in a wave. The clinger stays where it fell, feeding until the warmth leaves its prey, then climbs back to its waiting place.

The burrowers are the animals a person would notice last and remember longest.

Their backs rarely appear above the dust. Usually there is only the line of disturbance: a narrow ridge traveling toward a cluster of grazers, bending around the legs of machinery, pausing at the edge of the copper bus bar. The ridge has a purposefulness that feels uncomfortable to watch. When it reaches prey, the floor bursts upward. A ribbed black body comes through, all folded sections and pale sensing filaments. It has no obvious face until it opens. Its mouth is a round, muscular disk surrounded by gripping plates.

The burrowers do not eat every day. They live on long accumulations of vibration and heat, storing what they can in thick tissues beneath their hides. A passing train feeds them as surely as it feeds the grazers. The freight yard above shakes; the burrowers wake, rise, and hunt. In the minutes after a train has passed, the basin’s floor is alive with them, sensing limbs lifted like grass in a wind that no human can feel.

The largest ones may be older than the abandoned yard.

They are the white-backed scavengers under the overturned locker: broad, blunt-headed things with too many legs and armored plates that overlap like chipped ceramic. Their backs are not truly white. They are coated in calcium dust, powdered concrete, shell fragments, and the mineral bloom that grows from their own skin. When one moves, it leaves a clean dark trail behind it.

They emerge at the coldest part of the night, when the shaft above admits air that has touched the tracks and the sleeping city. They collect whatever has failed: runners dried into curled wires, grazers after a heat-frenzy, burrowers wounded in the crush around a live coil. They carry the remains beneath the locker. There, in chambers packed with old insulation and powdered rust, their young feed.

The young are soft for several weeks.

During that time the adults become dangerous. Not to a human, perhaps—not unless the human reaches into the locker—but to everything else. The whole local food web shifts away from that overturned metal box. Even the wall-clingers abandon warm junctions nearby. The mineral grazers leave a clean crescent around it, as if the floor itself has been swept.

Yet the basin has another resident, less easily seen than any animal.

It lives in the behavior of the animals together.

There are moments, especially before the relay closes, when every species seems to receive the same instruction. The grazers stop rasping. Runners lift their heads. Wall-clingers flatten tighter to stone. Burrowers halt beneath the floor. For a few seconds the chamber becomes quiet enough that the hum of the lamps can be heard.

Then the relay clacks.

Light sharpens. Somewhere inside the compressor, a coil begins to glow. The old machine does not start, not really. Its flywheel stays fixed. Its belts remain cracked and slack. But a portion of it becomes warm, and all life turns toward it.

The grazers arrive first, pouring over the floor in converging streams. They form ridges against the machine’s base, bodies climbing over bodies, their shells flashing gray and blue-black. Runners dart along the edges of the streams. The clingers rain from walls and ducts. The burrowers strike up through the dust wherever the swarms become densest.

For several minutes there is no separate creature in the basin. There is only a single event: metal warming, bodies responding, heat being divided into pursuit, feeding, mating, panic, and death.

The white-backed scavengers wait outside it all.

They know the frenzy will end.

After the current fades, the survivors retreat. The grazers return to rust seams and salt blooms. The hunters limp or molt or disappear into cracks with their food. The floor closes over the tunnels. The scavengers emerge and begin the long work of removing what remains. By morning—if morning can be said to reach that far down—there is little evidence except disturbed dust, cleaned patches of stone, and new pale trails around the compressor.

Beyond the low opening, in the older room, the rules are different.

The animals do enter during vibration storms, when trains pass overhead in dense succession and the whole basin becomes a shaking instrument. They flee through the crawlspace in numbers, carrying their young, their prey, their eggs tucked beneath limbs. But once the vibrations stop, they leave.

They leave even if the outer chamber is full of predators. They leave even if the lamps have gone out. They leave even when the old room is warmer than the basin.

The crawlers cross its threshold reluctantly, legs tapping the worn stone. The grazers never form their feeding sheets there. The burrowers surface at its edge and turn aside. White-backed scavengers will enter only to retrieve a dead adult, and even then they move quickly, plates pulled close, as if ashamed to be seen.

Something lives in that room too, perhaps.

Not a large beast hidden among the jars. Not a thing that waits in the dark with teeth. If there is a resident there, it is older and quieter than appetite. It may be in the smoothness of the floor, which has been polished not by water but by generations of tiny mouths that no longer feed there. It may be in the wrench without rust, the sealed ceramic jars, the warning sign whose painted letters have been erased by dust but whose shape still causes the smallest animals to turn away.

Or it may be the memory of a chemistry the basin has outgrown.

The creatures do not know fear as people know it. They know gradients: warmth and cold, current and stillness, edible mineral and dead stone, pressure that promises a train and pressure that promises a predator. Yet at the lip of that old chamber they hesitate in a way that resembles fear closely enough to matter.

So the basin is inhabited by shelled grains, by hunters, by vibrating dark bodies under the crust, by pale scavengers in a rusted locker.

And it is inhabited by their ceaseless agreement.

They have made a nation out of leakage and corrosion. Their roads are warm cables. Their borders are copper. Their seasons are passing trains. Their sun is a failing coil inside a dead compressor.

Above them, the freight yard has been abandoned.

Below it, nothing has been abandoned at all.
