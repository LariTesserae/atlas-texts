The people of the Cistern Quarter are not, as surface people sometimes imagine, pale remnants or hidden poor waiting to be brought into daylight. They are a settled people. They have occupations, grudges, inherited jokes, rivalries over water pressure, preferences in lamp fat, and opinions about the proper sound of a well-fitted valve. They are as particular to their chamber as the channels beneath their floors.

Most are born there, though no one speaks of being *from below* in the way outsiders expect. Their word for the place is simply *here*. The upper city is *overhead*, or, when speaking of it with a little irony, *the weather*. A person may say, “My sister went into the weather,” meaning she married a baker on the surface; or “There is weather on your boots,” meaning dust, leaves, horse filth, or sunlight-baked mud has been tracked into the quarter.

They tend to be compact, though this is not universally true. A tall person learns early to bend at the right beams and turns their head sideways when entering certain rooms. Children grow up knowing the chamber by bruises: this lintel catches the forehead, that stair bites the shin, this pipe swings low when the wheel is turning. Their gait is peculiar to outsiders—careful but never tentative. They walk as though following music only they can hear, stepping over channels without looking, ducking beneath braces in mid-conversation, catching a swaying handline with one hand while carrying a basin in the other.

Their skin is not colorless. Lamp light makes everyone seem brown, gold, or smoke-dark, and the mineral damp leaves a faint dry sheen on hands and cheeks. Most have cracked knuckles. Many have pale scars along the forearms from slate edges, hot pots, slipped knives, and the teeth of old gears. Their clothes are mended beyond any original pattern. A child may wear a sleeve cut from a sailcloth sack, a bodice patched with old account leather, trousers that have passed through three cousins and been turned inside out twice. They favor close sleeves, tied cuffs, and hems short enough to avoid the gutter water. Loose fabric is distrusted; it catches, drags, dips, snags, knocks over lamps.

Hair is often cropped, braided tight, or wrapped. Not for fashion alone, though fashions exist and are intensely debated, but because hair near a wheel or a wick becomes everyone’s problem. The elderly, who have earned the right to be impractical, sometimes wear it long. An old woman may sit on a threshold with white hair falling nearly to her waist, combing it slowly while two grandchildren wind thread around a broken bucket handle beside her.

Their eyes are accustomed to distances of only a few paces. They notice movement in dimness, the tremor of a flame, a fresh bead of water on a wall. Many dislike open plazas when they go above. The sky is not frightening exactly, but exhausting. It provides nowhere for the eye to settle. Surface visitors often mistake the quarter-dwellers’ upward glances for superstition. More often they are simply checking the ceiling, as everyone does: listening with the body for a change in weight, a new grit falling, a vibration that does not belong.

They are rarely alone, even when they wish to be.

Privacy in the Cistern Quarter is made rather than found. It consists of a curtain doubled over its rod; a kettle placed outside a doorway, signaling that the room beyond is occupied; a certain way of coughing before speaking; a request to send children on an errand. Lovers learn one another through partial concealment: hands meeting beneath a table, murmured conversations in the throat of a disused conduit, a shared lamp shielded by a coat. Arguments are seldom private either. A quarrel can begin between two people at one door and arrive, transformed, at the far end of the chamber before either has finished it.

For this reason, the inhabitants have developed a great precision about what is said plainly and what is said by means of objects. A cup placed upside down outside a room means grief. A strip of red thread tied around a gutter hook means someone inside has a fever. Three chalk dots beside a valve mean it has been adjusted but not trusted. A bowl of dry salt left on a stair is an apology awaiting acceptance. Children learn these signs before they can read surface letters.

There are families, but their boundaries are porous. A household is less a set of parents and children than a cluster of people responsible for the same stretch of wall, roof, or water. One dwelling may contain an old valve-keeper, her sister’s widower, two boarders from a collapsed niche, a baby passed between three women, and a boy who has no relation to any of them but sleeps on their shelf because his own home is too close to the spring shaft. Everyone knows who belongs to whom in the complicated way of kinship, but practical belonging is measured differently: who brings your lamp oil when you are ill, who knows where your winter blanket is stored, who will hear if your pipe begins to knock at night.

The dead remain part of these arrangements.

There is no room for burial in the ordinary manner. Bodies are taken upward before dawn by a small number of licensed carriers, wrapped in old linen and carried through routes few others use. But the dead are not said to have gone “above.” They have gone *out of the stone*. Their tools, however, stay. A deceased person’s spoon may hang for years beside a shared basin. Their name may remain attached to a patch of ceiling, a particular wheel, a repair technique. “That is Sel’s drip,” someone might say, though Sel has been out of the stone for eighty years. “Don’t brace it that way. Old Perren tried that and heard the wall complain.”

There are specialists whose work makes the quarter possible.

The gutter families keep the paths from flooding. They know every fall and obstruction, every place where sediment naturally gathers. Their children are taught to distinguish a blocked channel by sound alone: the quick hard chatter of free water, the swollen throat-noise of a slow one, the dangerous silence of a channel that ought to be moving.

The wheelwrights make small machines from wood so old and reworked that some pieces have no known original purpose. They polish axles with animal fat and ash. They speak of wheels almost affectionately, not because they romanticize them but because a wheel must be listened to. A good wheel has moods. It complains before it breaks. A foolish person says wood is dead; a wheelwright says wood has memory, especially when it has been wet for a hundred years.

There are lamp tenders, though almost every household tends its own flame. The professional tenders render fat, skim oils, mend glass chimneys, recover wick fibers, and oversee the few lamps kept burning at all hours: those near the spring, the narrowest crossings, the infirmary shelf, and the central passage where notices are read. They know who has begun using too much oil, who is stretching poor fuel with water, who has been sitting awake at night.

There are listeners. Not an official guild, perhaps, but everyone acknowledges them. They are usually older people with damaged knees or fading sight, stationed naturally near walls, pipes, and common thresholds. They can identify a neighbor by footfall and tell a loose beam from a settling stone. One may put an ear to a plaster wall and say, “The upper cistern is drinking badly,” or “The north household has a new baby and does not yet know it cries differently when cold.” They are consulted before repairs, marriages, departures, and sometimes before accusations. To have heard something through the stone is not proof, but it is rarely ignored.

Children belong to the soundscape before they belong to any trade. They play games that make little sense on the surface: guessing games based on drips, races conducted by touch with lamps extinguished, hide-and-seek in spaces too narrow for adults, contests to name the source of a knock. They build tiny dams in side gutters and are punished for it with sternness softened by reluctant admiration. They collect chalk-white pebbles from places no one recalls depositing them and stack these in careful towers on ledges. When asked why, they often say they are making homes for the moths.

They fear the surface rats more than they fear the spring.

The spring is not thought of as evil. It is necessary, ancient, and too close to be made simple. People speak to it only when working near it, and then in practical terms: “Hold steady,” “Give clean,” “Not today.” A child caught leaning between the bars will be pulled back sharply, but not beaten. Adults are more alarmed than angry. The rule about reflections is taught as a rule about respect: do not offer the water an image it has not asked to keep.

There are a few who have looked into it too long. They are not treated as cursed. They are watched. Sometimes they become quiet. Sometimes they begin rearranging objects, trying to make rooms conform to a pattern they will not explain. Sometimes they leave for the surface within a year. More often nothing happens at all, which is perhaps worse for the story and better for the person.

The white moth is treated with a gentleness not extended to many human visitors. When one appears, conversation alters but does not stop. Lamps are shielded, not extinguished. Children are lifted away from reaching hands. Someone will fetch a clean cup, and the moth may be coaxed onto its rim and carried to a quiet ledge near the central shaft. No one claims it brings luck. No one claims it carries souls. Such explanations are considered surface habits, a desire to turn rarity into a message. The moth is simply a thing that has found its way into the chamber and must not be harmed.

There are surface-born people in the quarter, though they remain visible for years by their habits. They look upward too often. They speak at a volume that makes everyone wince, then lower their voices so far nobody can hear them. They leave objects where they are convenient rather than where they belong. They are surprised when a stranger knows they have been crying. Some adapt. They learn to sleep through the wheels and wake at the wrong drip. They begin to recognize people by silhouette, cough, and the sound of their work. Eventually, on returning to the streets above, they may find themselves unable to rest in a room where nothing touches the next room.

The Cistern Quarter’s people are conservative not because they lack imagination, but because imagination has consequences when there is no empty space in which to make a mistake. They are inventive in miniature. They can make a broken comb into a latch spring, a cracked bowl into a valve seat, a child’s worn shoe into a float for measuring water rise. They compose songs with verses short enough to survive the echo. They tell stories in installments because no one can ever gather far enough from everyone else to tell a long tale without interruption.

They laugh often, though their laughter is practical: quick bursts, shoulders shaking, hands covering mouths so as not to carry too far. Their jokes are usually about inheritance, moisture, and the absurd specificity of their world.

A person who has lived there all their life may know the exact warm patch of stone where bread dough rises best, though no plant will sprout. They may know that the east rail is smooth because six generations have held it while carrying water. They may recognize a neighbor’s sorrow from the speed at which she turns a reel. They may consider a beam that has supported three homes, two shops, and a marriage bed to be more reliable than any promise made in open air.

What lives in the Cistern Quarter, more than anything, is attention.

The people have made a discipline of noticing. They notice the first clouding of lamp glass, the faint mineral taste that precedes a change in the spring, the silence behind a familiar curtain, the small new space left by an absent body. They live crowded against one another and against the unchanging stone, held between the pressure of the city above and the dark, remembering water below. They have learned not to mistake endurance for stillness.

Nothing there grows.

But everything is tended.
