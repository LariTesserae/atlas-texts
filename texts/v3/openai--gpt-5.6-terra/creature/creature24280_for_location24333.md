What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense. Nothing could build a home and expect it to remain where it was built. Nothing could mark a boundary, plant a garden, keep a fire, raise a wall against weather. The weather enters every room.

The nearest thing to citizens are the pale keepers of the joints.

They are narrow, jointed animals, each perhaps the length of an arm, with six hooked limbs and a body made of overlapping translucent plates. In the upper terraces they can be mistaken for strips of peeled mortar. Their plates are clouded white, and beneath them a slow blue fluid moves in pulses. They live in the seams between the fitted paving stones, where the old builders left deliberate gaps for expansion and drainage. The keepers were not made by those builders, but they fit the old work too perfectly to seem accidental.

They come out after a major current.

For hours, sometimes days, the streets are full of them. They travel in lines along the channels cut by water, stopping wherever silt has packed into a joint or a dead fish has lodged beneath a threshold. Their front limbs open like calipers. They scrape, nibble, pry, and carry away. They do not clear the city in any useful or complete way. They are too small, and the city is too vast. But wherever they have passed, the paving seams are dark and open again, ready to accept the next pulse of water.

In the lowest districts there are older keepers, broad as doors, whose shells have become encrusted with tiles, wirelike corals of mineral, and scraps of the drowned city itself. One may have a bronze plate fused along its flank. Another may drag a length of carved railing that has grown into the ridge of its back. They move rarely. When they do, the smaller creatures fall away from them, not in terror but in recognition of a moving piece of terrain.

There are also the lantern-throats.

They live in the vertical towers with their centers hollowed dark. A lantern-throat is mostly mouth: a tall, soft cylinder fixed within a shaft, ringed inside with pale cilia that catch whatever the currents bring. In still water they shrink nearly out of sight, folded into the tower’s darkness like wet cloth. When the water begins to turn, they rise.

Their bodies inflate until they fill the stone rings. Their inner membranes shine with a diluted blue light, not bright enough to illuminate the water, but enough to show as a sequence of dim wells receding down a corridor. They filter silt, spores, shed skins, bone dust, the soft bodies of lesser animals. They have no faces. Their only visible expression is the widening and narrowing of their mouths.

In some towers, several lantern-throats have joined together over centuries. Their flesh bridges from ring to ring; their internal glow travels slowly from one chamber to another. Such a tower appears inhabited by a single immense organism breathing inside the architecture. When a pressure wave passes, all the lights go dark at once.

The fish are not merely fish.

The thin transparent ones that hold themselves in torrents have learned the shape of each street current more accurately than any map could record it. Their bodies are nearly invisible except for a black thread of spine and a pair of bright sensory organs under the skull. They live facing into force. If placed in still water, they drift helplessly, turn pale, and die. In the avenues where cold water accelerates between retaining walls, they form loose constellations behind every obstacle: a parapet, a broken statue, the heel of a fallen tower.

They feed on the minute life carried in from above. They breed only during the great reversals, when an entire basin changes direction. Then they leave the sheltering lee of walls and rise into the water-column in vast silent spirals. Their eggs are not laid on stone. They are released into the moving water and must remain moving until they hatch. The young are born already knowing, somehow, to seek a current.

The broad winged drifters are more secretive. Their bodies are flat and diamond-shaped, with no obvious eyes, and their pewter skins carry patterns like rain on lead. They do not hunt in the usual fashion. They listen through their undersides to the minute vibrations of stone.

A broken slab settling in a buried chamber may call them from miles away.

They arrive one by one, settle on the walls near the sound, and wait. Often nothing follows. Sometimes an old structure gives way: a lintel collapses, an aqueduct throat opens, a buried stairwell exhales a century of silt. Then the drifters enter the disturbance, folding and unfolding their bodies against the new current. They feed on whatever has been dislodged, but this seems incidental. Their true attachment is to change itself. A district that has shifted may be surrounded by them for months, their dull bodies drifting through its new passages as though taking its measure.

There are things that live inside the pressure.

They cannot be seen directly, because they have no settled outline. In the deepest descending avenues, where the pale glow no longer reaches, water moves in slow dense layers that rarely mingle. Within those layers are colonies of minute armored creatures, each smaller than a grain of sand. Alone, they are nothing. Together they make sheets and curtains that alter the water’s texture. They gather in the wake of the large segmented animals; they fill the scars on old stone; they pour from narrow cracks when a wall is displaced.

Sometimes a whole cloud of them organizes around an eddy. It becomes a shape: a low arch, a coiling pillar, the suggestion of a procession moving through a black court. If light reaches it, the particles shine briefly like iron filings. Then the current changes and the shape ceases to exist.

The many-jointed silhouettes belong to the oldest animals known here, though “animal” may be too small a word for them.

They are called, if they are called anything, the traversers.

A traverser has too many legs to count in the murk. Its body is long enough to bridge a plaza, segmented in blunt chalk-colored plates, each plate scarred by impacts and fringed with mineral growth. It does not swim because it has no need to. Its limbs find purchase on walls, ceilings, paving, the undersides of collapsed structures. When the water surges, it braces. When the water slackens, it walks.

They feed from the city itself.

Not the stone exactly, though their mouthparts can crush softened mortar and scrape mineral films from bronze. They eat the living coatings that form on submerged surfaces, the blind things that graze the carvings, the glove-colonies beneath bridges, the white crabs that gather in drainage mouths. Yet no one has seen a traverser actively hunting. It proceeds through the drowned country with the patience of erosion. Where it has passed, an avenue may be cleaner, emptier, more open. A doorway may have become wider. A wall may be missing a corner.

Small creatures follow in its wake. The keeper-things clean the fragments. Eels enter the newly opened joints. Transparent fish occupy the altered current. The city changes around a traverser, but not quickly enough for the change to feel like an event.

In chambers protected from the strongest flows live the memory animals.

They are soft, round-bodied, and grey, no larger than a human head. Their skins are covered in fine dark lines that rearrange themselves slowly. They cluster in hollows under staircases, within the mouths of dry-looking aqueducts, behind fallen bronze gates. They do not move much. When disturbed, they do not flee. Instead their markings begin to ripple.

The lines are maps.

Not maps of the city as it stands, but of currents that once passed through it. A memory animal carries the trace of a flood long gone: the branching route of a cold surge, the location of a vanished wall, the pressure signature of a tower before it fell. When two meet, they press their bodies together and exchange some portion of these patterns. Over time the older maps fade, overwritten by new ones. Thus the still places remember movement, but only imperfectly.

There may be a larger intelligence among them. Or perhaps the city itself supplies the intelligence, and these quiet animals are only its temporary organs.

At intervals, far below the last visible stair, something knocks from inside the stone.

The sound is not the boom of a shifting roof slab. It has spacing. Three impacts, a pause, one long vibration. The eels retreat from the seams. The lantern-throats close. Even the traversers become still, fixing themselves against walls with every limb.

Then, after some span of time too long to count in the dark, the stone answers from elsewhere.

No creature has been seen making these sounds. It may be an animal enclosed in the deepest foundations, vast and slow, existing in chambers that the drowning never reached. It may be the remaining machinery of the ancient country, still flexing under pressure with no maker left to hear it. It may be neither.

Whatever it is, the living things recognize it as part of the climate.

They do not worship it. They do not fear it in the way surface creatures fear a predator. They accommodate it. They close, wedge, turn, descend, and wait.

That is how life persists in the drowned country: not by claiming a place, but by learning the instant at which a place is about to become water again.
