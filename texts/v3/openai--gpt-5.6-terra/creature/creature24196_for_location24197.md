What lives there is not a people in the usual sense. There are no villages because nothing in the high dark needs to gather for protection from weather; the weather is the oldest resident, and everything else has learned its habits.

The first inhabitants are the slow ones.

They live in the ice.

Not merely beneath it, but within its layered body: pale threadlike worms in airless blue seams, bacterial mats that stain old snow red and green, minute dark mites that travel in windblown dust and wake when the surface softens. In summerless years they may not move at all, only persist in a kind of frozen waiting. Then a pressure crack opens, water enters, and whole invisible populations begin working at once. They eat mineral dust, trapped pollen, the bodies of things carried down from the upper slopes. Their work gives the glacier its faint colors: rusty bands, green-black films, grey fur along the melt channels.

There are larger creatures under the ice as well, though they are rarely seen whole.

Some are blind, white, and jointed, living in the caves where meltwater hollows the glacier from below. They pick across submerged stone with soft many-fingered mouths, grazing on the pale growth that gathers wherever air touches cold water. Others are long-bodied and nearly transparent, their blood dark enough to show through their skins. They move in the pressure-fed streams beneath the ice, pressing themselves through cracks too narrow for a hand. When a glacier calves into a tarn, or when a crevasse gives its muffled iron report, these things sometimes appear briefly in the disturbed water before slipping back into blackness.

The pool-creature beneath the cliff is one of their old cousins, but it has become almost entirely still. It is not blind because darkness has taken its eyes; it is blind because its ancestors ceased needing them long ago. Its external gills open and close like wet leaves in a current no one else can feel. In the cold months it slows until it seems part of the pool’s floor. In the wet season, when droplet-water carries insects and spores down through the moss, it rises through the dark to feed. It may live for centuries. Its kind measure time not in days but in changes of mineral taste.

On the slopes live the hoofed herds, but even they are not simple grazers.

The pale long-limbed animals are called, if they are called anything, the star-deer by the few nights they are visible in profile. Their fur is thick and hollow, holding warmth close to the body. Their legs are too long for beauty and too fine for the weight of the mountains, yet they cross shale with a careful certainty that makes them seem almost weightless. They eat lichen, seedheads, frost-burned flowers, and the skins of mossy stones. In their coats they carry whole meadows from valley to valley: spores, seeds, tiny dormant insects, grains of fungus, even fragments of the blue luminous molds that grow near warm fissures.

They do not call to one another often. Sound travels strangely in that country. A noise may reach a neighboring slope with such clarity that it feels near, while a companion standing behind a boulder vanishes completely. So the star-deer use other signals: the pale side of an ear turned toward moonlight, the scent left on a stone, a hoofprint placed in a particular runnel of mud. At certain times of year, herds pass through the same valleys in the same order, not because any leader remembers the path, but because the path exists in the accumulated decisions of generations.

The heavy horned things are older-looking and less easily startled. Their bodies are dark, their shoulders steep, their horns swept back and grooved with years of rubbing against stone. They sleep in groups beneath overhangs where the mountain exhales a little warmth. Snow gathers on them and melts slowly into their coats. In a blizzard they may stand unmoving for days, their broad backs becoming part of the slope.

Their calves are born in the brief dimming of spring, when the snow begins to loosen around buried moss. The mothers hide them in hollows dense with lichen and old grass. Few survive their first year. Those that do become patient, formidable animals, carrying scars from icefall, from rutting contests, from the narrow teeth of predators. When they dig for moss, they turn over stones and expose wet soil. That disturbance feeds roots, beetles, fungi, and the small black flowers that grow only in hoof-churned ground. A meadow may owe its entire pattern to where the horned herds stood a hundred years before.

The great cats follow both kinds of grazer, though they are not always hunting them.

They are slate-grey, long-bodied, and broad-pawed, with fur hanging around their feet in mats that erase the sharpness of tracks. Their faces are blunt, almost solemn. In old animals the whiskers turn white and the fur around the muzzle gathers mineral stains from drinking at seep holes. They move along the glacier margins because that is where the mountain brings life together: birds come to drink, small mammals shelter in the warmer stone, wounded herd animals seek the hard clean ground where predators are easier to see.

A cat may wait beside a melt hole for an entire night without lowering its head. Frost forms on its back. Its eyes close, then open. A white-winged bird lands at the water. The cat does not strike. Another bird lands. Still it waits. When it finally moves, it is less like an attack than a piece of darkness becoming suddenly heavier.

But the cats are also scavengers of the mountain’s casualties. They find animals buried in shallow snow, birds struck down by wind, creatures swept into ravines by meltwater. They eat what the glaciers release. They carry bones into caves. In the caves, their old kills nourish a second kingdom: white beetles, cave moths, molds like velvet, and small foxlike animals with enormous ears that listen for dripping water through stone.

Above all these things travel the owls.

They are so large that in flight they seem briefly mistaken for weather. Their wings are narrow and soft-edged, adapted not for power but for silence. Their bodies are lean beneath their feathers, built for the thin air of the upper shelves. They nest in clefts where no animal can approach from below, lining the rock with moss, hare fur, shed horn-casings, and the dry feathers of their prey.

The owls do not hunt only mice and birds. They listen to the hidden world. They hear a vole moving under snow, of course, but they also hear the tremor of insects clustered above a warm fissure, the scratching of burrowing creatures beneath moss, the faint clicks of cave animals in the walls of an ice tunnel. Their hearing is so fine that they sometimes turn their heads toward sounds no surface creature would recognize: a stone settling deep underground, a stream shifting its course under the glacier, the slow breakage of an old root.

Their calls are rare because their territories are immense. One owl’s wooden creak may be answered hours later from a ridge no winged thing could reach before dawn. Between those calls lies an enormous silence, but it is not emptiness. It is a map.

There are ravens too: thick-necked, glossy, clever enough to treat the whole mountain as a puzzle. They follow the cats, the herds, the owls, and the weather. They know which cliffs shed carrion after a storm, which fissures warm enough to keep insects alive, which snowfields conceal the bodies of animals that fell in autumn. They carry bright useless things into their nests: mica flakes, snail shells, polished vertebrae, blue fungal caps, scraps of shed horn. In one valley, a raven lineage may know the location of every dependable pool for miles around.

The smallest lives are perhaps the most numerous, and the least comprehensible.

In the moss are beetles with copper-green shells, wingless flies that creep instead of flying, soft-bodied mites bright as grains of paprika. Tiny frogs no larger than thumbnails inhabit the wet roots under boulders, their skins nearly black to gather what little heat exists. There are moths whose caterpillars eat lichen all winter beneath snow, and whose adults emerge only when fog fills the valleys. Their wings are pale and powdery, almost indistinguishable from drifting ash.

Around the warm fractures gather stranger things. Fungi with glassy caps push up through mineral crusts. Their blue-green glow is not bright enough to light a path, but bright enough to show the passage of a snail, the wet edge of a leaf, the underside of a grazing muzzle. Some insects spend their whole lives within a few feet of these vents. Their bodies have lost color, or gained metallic sheens. They feed on microbial films that smell faintly of iron and wet stone. They are the mountain’s furnace-keepers, though there is no furnace, only buried heat passing upward through centuries of rock.

And in the deepest cracks, perhaps, live things that do not come out at all.

They eat dissolved metals. They feed on sulfur, iron, methane, whatever the mountain gives them. They make their homes in water too dark and pressurized for fur, feather, or root. Their colonies coat submerged rock in pale films and red-brown velvet. They breathe chemicals that would poison surface creatures. In their own small way, they are as ancient as the glacier valleys: not survivors of the cold so much as creatures for whom cold, darkness, pressure, and mineral water are the ordinary conditions of life.

If there is an intelligence in the place beyond animal cleverness, it belongs partly to these distributed lives.

The moss remembers water.  
The lichen remembers wind.  
The herd remembers passages through its feet.  
The raven remembers opportunity.  
The cat remembers stillness.  
The owl remembers sound.  
The glacier remembers weight.

Nothing rules the mountains. Nothing has named them. But every creature inhabits an interval in the mountain’s labor: a pause beneath an overhang, a seam of warmth in frozen ground, the brief opening of a flower, the collapse of a snow bridge, the black water exposed at the edge of ice.

The place is alive not because it has conquered its harshness, but because its inhabitants have become precise enough to live inside it.
