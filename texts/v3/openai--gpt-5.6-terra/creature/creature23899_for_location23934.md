What lives in the Greenworks is not one thing.

At first, a visitor might say it is inhabited by workers: pump-minders, growers, mechanics, roof-gardeners, dye-boilers, dredgers, tram loaders, reed-cutters, and the small army of people who know how to keep a wet building from becoming a dead one. This is true, but insufficient. The people of the Greenworks do not live *among* its plants and machines in the ordinary way. They live in a three-way arrangement with them, each correcting and sustaining the other.

The oldest inhabitants are the roots.

Some may be older than the buildings they hold apart. Beneath the streets, roots from vanished yards and former canal banks have persisted in black water, feeding on rot, pipe leakage, and whatever light finds a crack. They have become pale, rope-thick things in the foundations; networks of fine brown hairs along culvert walls; glossy knots swelling under thresholds. They enter brickwork without haste. They lift tiles. They drink from broken mains and leave old pipes dry as bone. A root will spend twenty years crossing a cellar and then emerge, silently, through the mortar behind a stove.

People know particular root systems as they know neighbors.

The willow under the Number Nine workshop is blamed for cracked paving, but its roots stabilize the east wall during floods. The old fig in the soap factory has a trunk no thicker than a person’s leg, though its underground body has spread beneath three buildings and a service lane. It fruits rarely, producing hard green figs that no one eats, but its roots have found an abandoned warm-water line and keep half the block from freezing in winter.

There are roof plants that have never touched ground. Their ancestors came as seed caught in chimney grit, in birdless wind, in sacks of fiber, in the treads of carts. They have adapted to tar, brick dust, hot metal, and the constant mild vapor rising from ducts. Their leaves are waxy or hairy or dark enough to drink weak light. They grow across skylights and over machine housings. Their roots occupy the shallow soil of roofs with extraordinary discipline, knitting it together rather than breaking it apart—until they do not.

There are vines that seem to understand infrastructure. One common creeper favors cable trays and insulated pipes, wrapping them in spirals of green. It does not kill the cables; no one knows whether this is luck, heat preference, or a tendency to avoid whatever burns its roots. Another plant grows only where old brick sweats. Its leaves are narrow and almost transparent, and in dry weather they curl into black threads. When rain comes, whole walls bloom green within a day.

The Greenworks people cultivate some of these things and tolerate others, but few believe they fully control them.

The district has its gardeners, though many would object to the title. They call themselves roof hands, line tenders, drain crew, vat growers, or simply maintenance. They prune with hooked knives and insulated shears. They carry sacks of compost up iron stairs. They inspect roof beds for weight, rot, and root depth. They know how much reed a culvert can hold before it becomes a blockage, how much duckweed a pond can bear before it starves, how close a fig may grow to a boiler wall before it begins taking heat from a pipe meant for people.

They work in rubber boots, patched trousers, old rain capes, or sleeveless work shirts in the summer humidity. Most have a permanent green-brown stain around the cuffs and hems. Their hands are marked by cuts, oil, leaf tannin, and the pale softening caused by too much wet work. They carry tools that would look familiar anywhere—spanners, hammers, rakes, screwdrivers—but altered for the place: handles wrapped against damp, blades shaped for cutting roots from drains, meters protected in waxed cases, ladders fitted with broad feet so they do not sink into roof soil.

Children live there too, though the Greenworks is not particularly made for children. They learn early which boards float, which water is merely rain, which is runoff, which has a chemical sheen, and which channels will pull a shoe off a foot. They know to listen for the deep change in a pump’s rhythm that means a lane may flood. Their games make use of the district’s vertical layers: racing along raised walkways, trading cuttings, building little bridges across gutters, trying to identify buildings from the smell of their steam.

Some keep gardens in improbable containers: cracked buckets, dead pressure tanks, sections of gutter, old hard hats, the cavities in a broken brick wall. They grow peppers, bitter greens, small flowers bred for no purpose except color. There are children who can repair a cistern valve before they can properly write, and children who have never seen a street that did not shine with standing water after rain.

The elderly tend to be the district’s true archive. They remember where the canal ran before the western sheds were built over it. They remember the flood that lifted floorboards in three streets at once, the year the sump pumps failed, the year the blue cistern vine appeared and spread across seven walls. They can identify old construction styles by the sound made when rain hits the roof: slate, sheet iron, tar board, planted soil, salvaged plastic, or the dense soft patter of moss.

Many refuse offers to move elsewhere. Other districts are too dry, too exposed, too quiet. There, they say, walls have no give. Water behaves badly because it is forced to disappear. Plants are ornamental and therefore lonely.

The Greenworks also contains things that are not exactly residents but have settled into residence.

There are machines that no manufacturer would recognize. Pumps with new motors bolted onto bodies cast a century earlier. Conveyor belts reinforced with woven plant fiber. Drying frames fitted with heater coils, damp sensors, and vines trained deliberately across their outer sides for shade. A press in an old paper works that has been running since before anyone currently alive was born. Its casing is painted green in layers so thick that the bolts look grown rather than installed.

Some machines have names. Not official names, but working names: Old Mouth, the North Thumper, Sister Valve, the Red Worm, the Sleeping Engine. The names are used with affection and accusation. When one stops, someone says it is sulking. When a pump begins knocking, they say it has found its voice again. This is not superstition exactly. It is the practical language of people who have listened to the same equipment for decades and know that a machine’s character is made from its small failures.

And perhaps the machines do live there, in their way. They breathe through vents, sweat condensation, grow moss in their seams, develop habits, require attention, and become impossible to remove because everything around them has been built in response to their presence.

There are also the unseen small lives.

The frogs in the drains are rarely encountered directly. Their calls come up through grates on certain hot nights, brief and hollow, as if the buried canal itself were making sounds in its sleep. There are silver fish beneath some of the covered reaches, visible only when maintenance crews open a grate and shine a light into the moving water. Moths live in boiler rooms and gather around lamps with wings powdered the color of old paper. Small beetles inhabit compost heaps, root mats, and the dark spaces behind warm control panels.

Nobody calls these creatures pests unless they interfere with a crop, a wire, or a meal. They are considered part of the district’s accounting: something consumes what falls, something moves through the water, something lives where human hands cannot reach.

But more than roots, workers, machines, frogs, or moths, the Greenworks is inhabited by a shared habit of repair.

This may be its most numerous resident.

It lives in the way a tenant puts a brick beneath a door before a storm. In the way a shop owner leads a vine away from a vent rather than cutting it. In the way a crew reinforces a roof from below because the garden above has become too established to destroy. In the way people leave marked planks over soft ground for strangers, or hang spare buckets under a new leak before deciding what the leak means.

No one in the Greenworks expects permanence from a dry wall, a clear drain, a bare roof, or a quiet machine. They expect all things to alter. A building is not finished when it is built; it is merely introduced to water. A pipe is not repaired once but kept in conversation with pressure, roots, and rust. A plant is not wild simply because it was not planted. A machine is not broken simply because it has changed shape around a vine.

So the district is inhabited by people who have learned to live without victory.

They do not conquer the wet ground. They do not tame the growth. They do not restore the old canal or abandon the old factories. They make room. They redirect. They brace. They harvest. They listen.

And in the warm, damp spaces between their efforts, the Greenworks continues to make more of itself: leaf by leaf, drip by drip, repair by repair.
