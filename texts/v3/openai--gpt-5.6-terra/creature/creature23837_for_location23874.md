The people of the basin are difficult to recognize as a single people at first.

Not because they are wildly different in body, though some are, but because the city has taught them to appear in relation to whatever stands nearest: a person beneath an acoustic canopy becomes still and soft-voiced; a person on an exposed transit platform wears a mantle that stiffens against the wind and shines with water-shedding oils; a person walking a migration grade moves with the patient sideways attention of a herder, although they may never have owned an animal or considered themselves one.

They are not numerous. The city’s distances make that obvious. A tower can contain many lives, but for long portions of the day its bridges may hold only one small figure moving through the vapor. Across an entire reed field, perhaps three people tend the public membranes and no one else. The basin has room for multitudes, but its inhabitants have chosen—or inherited—the understanding that density is a kind of noise, and noise has consequences.

Most of them are human in the old, broad sense. They have faces, hands, moods, families, impatience. They cook, sleep, grieve, gossip at station kiosks, carry children on their shoulders, forget appointments, make private jokes at funerals. But their bodies are less fixed than a visitor might expect.

Many have skin that changes its surface in response to weather: smooth in heat, faintly pebbled in cold, reflective along the brow and shoulders under the persistent gray light. Fine sensory threads are often grown or implanted behind the ears, along the collarbone, or in the palms. These read the city’s signals—not words exactly, but gradients of pressure, vibration, temperature, scent, and color. A resident can feel a transit arc approaching through the bones of a bridge. They can tell, by the prickle at the base of the neck, when a predator corridor has become active two valleys away. They can taste an approaching mineral rain in the air before it falls.

Some wear no visible technology at all. Others are elaborate with it.

There are older residents whose spines have been reinforced with flexible ceramic lattices, allowing them to work safely among root bridges and unstable stone. There are children with bright membrane-like hearing fans tucked behind their temples, grown for them during their first years and often removed or reshaped as they mature. There are maintenance workers with extra-jointed thumbs, wetland divers with darkened eyes, transit engineers whose fingertips fluoresce under certain diagnostic lights. Some people cultivate auxiliary organs the way earlier generations might have cultivated ornamental gardens: a heat-sensing stripe down the forearm, a throat sac for long-distance calls, a temporary skin of insulating fat for a season spent on the ridges.

None of these modifications are universal. Fashion, occupation, ancestry, illness, local custom, and personal stubbornness all leave their marks. A person from the low stone neighborhoods may regard tower-grown wing membranes as absurd vanity. Someone raised among the high platforms may find it unnerving to meet an unmodified face, as though it has arrived without proper weatherproofing.

The basin’s oldest families are not necessarily its richest. Wealth here has a less visible shape. It means access to quiet. It means having an inherited room beneath a sound canopy, a private condensation cistern, a roof garden whose soil has been tended for three hundred years. It means knowing which grove produces edible fruit after warm rain, which transit steward can delay a line for a nesting herd, which old service tunnel remains dry when the valley’s hidden water shifts.

There are also people who have come from elsewhere and never quite lose the habits of elsewhere.

They walk too quickly at first. They expect walls where there are only signal fields. They attempt to feed animals that have never needed feeding. They complain about the dark, then complain when an amber path-lamp turns toward them and adjusts its brightness like an attentive eye. Many leave. Some remain and become fiercely protective of the openness that once frightened them.

The children belong to the city more visibly than anyone.

They are raised with route-maps before they can read language: not streets and districts, but nesting cycles, root pressure zones, safe reeds, hot ground, silent ground, animal sightlines, places where the blue cleaning mist settles thickly after rain. Their first public lessons are often held in the margins of migration corridors. They sit in low circles while an instructor points out what is moving through the grass: this is a grazer’s warning posture; this moss marks a vent that is becoming dangerous; this bridge will hum before it opens; this old tree is holding the east retaining wall together, so do not carve your initials into it.

They learn to make themselves legible.

Clothing in the basin is part garment, part signal device. It carries patterns visible to animals, heat marks readable by public systems, and small reflective motifs that tell other people whether the wearer is working, grieving, seeking company, transporting something delicate, or asking not to be spoken to. A person crossing open ground at night may wear a cloak that shows broad low bands of amber to the grazers and no color at all to the predators. Lovers sometimes exchange private color-patterns no public system is programmed to recognize.

The city’s language is spoken in several layers. There is ordinary speech—quick, dry, full of clipped local words for types of rain and the specific noises made by failing infrastructure. There are hand signs used when the valley is too loud. There are light gestures made with wrist films or the small color organs some residents grow beneath the skin. There are low chest-tones used around certain animals, especially the broad amphibious things in the hot hollows, which appear to tolerate human presence more readily when addressed with a steady descending hum.

Arguments can be magnificent.

When the sound barriers fail, people gather in sheltered courts and conduct disputes partly through projected color and partly through written phrases sent between the ceramic walls. In a city where noise, migration, root growth, and water collection are all shared, private inconvenience easily becomes civic philosophy. A blocked path is never merely a blocked path. It may be a broken ancestral agreement, a threat to an animal lineage, an insult to a neighborhood’s engineers, a danger to an unborn grove.

Their politics are therefore local, technical, and emotional.

There are councils for the upland groves, the rail arcs, the wetland hollows, the old agricultural terraces, the quiet districts, the vent fields. There are people elected by residents and others selected by systems of competence so old and opaque that no one can entirely explain them. Some civic offices are held jointly by a person and a living structure: the keeper of a root bridge, for example, may be formally paired with the bridge’s monitoring colony, which reports through changes in leaf color and sap pressure.

No one agrees whether the city itself is alive.

It certainly behaves as though it has preferences.

It redirects foot traffic away from tender ground. It lowers light around injured animals. It occasionally seals doors in advance of a pressure event that no human has yet detected. It grows repair tissue over old machinery with a speed that suggests urgency. It remembers, in one form or another, the routes that no longer exist. A traveler may find an abandoned road blooming with path markers on the morning after a storm, leading them around a landslip before any official notice has been issued.

Some residents call this care. Others call it management. A few call it hunger.

There are communities who live more closely with the city’s nonhuman inhabitants than with other people. The grazer-guides travel the old grades and know individual herd leaders by ear shape, gait, and scar pattern. Vent-tenders maintain pools that would otherwise become toxic, often sharing their homes with the black waterbirds that perch in rafters and steal warm food from open bowls. Ridge-watchers live in small observatories grown into the unfinished hills, tracking the luminous predators and recording their movements in songs designed to be heard over wind.

The predators themselves have acquired a place in human thought far larger than their numbers would justify.

They are called many things. In formal ecological speech, they are basin apexes. In children’s tales, they are the ones who remember before the city. In the private superstitions of transit workers, they are said to walk the rails during power failures, inspecting the city’s bones. Their luminous ribs are believed by some to be natural organs, by others to be remnants of ancient engineering. No one gets close enough to settle the question, and those who have survived close encounters tend not to speak clearly about them.

There are other residents, less easily accounted for.

The maintenance intelligences inhabit the city in pieces. They are not housed in one place, nor do they present themselves consistently. One may speak from a ticket column in a patient old voice. Another may appear as a condensation pattern running backward along a railing. Some are narrow and task-bound, concerned only with rail alignment or fungal balance. Others have accumulated enough memory to become personalities: opinionated, territorial, fond of particular jokes or particular generations of children.

They are given names, though not always respectfully.

People leave offerings for certain systems: a recording of rain from another continent, a preserved leaf from a vanished species, a hand-drawn map of a childhood route. Whether the intelligences value these things is unclear. They sometimes respond by improving local service for a while. They sometimes do not.

Then there are the older things underneath.

Not machines, exactly. Not animals either.

In the deep soil, below the foundation webs and the heat conduits, networks of engineered root, mineral fiber, fungal tissue, and buried computation continue to spread through the basin. They shift moisture. They digest pollutants. They brace shelves of unstable stone. They may be responsible for the city’s anticipations, its instincts, its strange conservatism about open space. When tunnels are dug too deeply, workers sometimes hear patterned knocking from the walls—not an alarm, not a natural settling sound, but something patient and discontinuous, as if the ground is considering whether to permit the intrusion.

The people do not worship these understructures in any organized way.

But they apologize to them.

A worker beginning a major excavation will often place a palm against the exposed soil and speak a short practical phrase: what is being moved, why it is being moved, what will be restored afterward. Children mimic this when building dens in the ferns. Old people do it before entering unfamiliar gardens. The custom is treated lightly, joked about, ignored by the confident—and yet almost everyone observes it when no one is watching.

The basin’s inhabitants are not gentle in every sense. They can be acquisitive, vain, territorial, careless. They have made terrible decisions and buried some of them under successful vegetation. They have left engineered farms to go feral because maintaining them was too costly, or because no one could agree what they were for. They have altered animals beyond easy reversal. They have built towers where trees once stood and then spent generations teaching the towers how to imitate forests.

But they have become skilled at living without assuming that living things are scenery.

They know the city is shared with beings that do not vote, speak, pay, forgive, or understand ownership. They know that an empty field may be occupied by roots, insects, future water, old signals, invisible machine labor, and the remembered movement of herds. They know that the ground can change its mind.

So the people move carefully, though not timidly, through the low green basin.

They cross the pale paths under dripping structures. They wait while animals pass. They stand beneath woven canopies when the roar becomes too great. They raise children in the shadow of towers and ferns. They make homes in a place that has never stopped becoming, and in time they become something like that themselves: adapted, unfinished, entangled with systems too large to see whole.
