They are called, by the few outsiders who know of them, the Basin People. Among themselves they use no single name that means *the people*. There is no need for one. A person says, “from under the north roots,” or “of Larch-Step,” or “born in the house with the blue drainstone.” Identity is positional. It belongs to a wall, a water channel, a sequence of kin, a task learned beside another task.

They are human, though the basin has taught them a manner of being human that would look strange elsewhere.

They tend to be small, not frail but compact: broad through the ribs, steady in the legs, with hands thickened by cold water, rope, soil, and repair. Their skin is often pale or weather-darkened in irregular places—the nose, cheekbones, wrists, the small exposed triangle at the throat. Many have gray or light-sensitive eyes and habitually narrow them against reflected frost. Their hair is kept long enough to bind against the ears and neck, but seldom worn loose. It is braided, wrapped, tucked under wool, threaded through with dried grass, gut cord, willow fiber, or the occasional strip of blue-dyed hide.

Their clothes are not uniform, but they are all made for brushing past walls without catching, kneeling on wet stone, passing through low doorways, and working with both hands free. Tunics are close-fitted at the wrist. Trousers are patched at the knee and seat until patches become patterns. The outer garments—vests, short capes, sleeved wraps—are pieced from goat hide, felted hair, bark cloth, and tightly woven reed fabric treated with fat. Children wear garments with soft cords linking cuffs to belts, so nothing drags through channels or catches in roots.

Color is used sparingly but intensely. Most of the town is brown, gray, peat-black, cream, moss-green. Against that, one may see a collar sewn with rust-red thread, a yellow lichen dye along a hem, a little bead of white bone at the end of a braid. Brightness is reserved for things that must be found quickly: the handle of a shared tool, the edge of a medicine basket, the cords around a child’s winter hood, the marker stones beside a dangerous drop.

They have quiet voices, but not weak ones. The old people can speak in a low tone that carries clearly through an entire crowded room. The children are taught early how to make meaning without filling space: a fingertip against a wrist means *wait*; two taps against a doorframe means *I have come but will not enter*; a palm held low and flat means *the goats are sleeping here*. There are dozens of such gestures, some shared by everyone and some belonging to particular households.

They laugh, though outsiders might not recognize it at first. It is usually breathy, shoulders bent forward, hands brought quickly to the mouth. A sudden loud laugh is not forbidden, exactly, but it startles the dogs and sends birds shifting under the eaves. When it happens, people look up—not reproachfully, more as if a bowl has been dropped and everyone is checking whether anything has broken.

Their children are not raised apart from labor. From the time they can walk a lane alone, they are given small guardianships: a basket of drying herbs, a warm stone in a seed bed, a certain corner of roof moss, three hens that must be counted at dusk. They learn the town by responsibility. A child may not know how far the surrounding country extends, but they know which root passage floods first in thaw, which old woman keeps the sharpest bone needle, which stair is safe in darkness, and where salamanders shelter when the channels run full.

They play with grave concentration. They build tiny towns from pebbles, then argue silently about drainage. They arrange goat droppings and feathers in patterns that function as messages. They lie beneath platforms and send one another stories by tapping through the floorboards: one tap for a person, two for an animal, a slow scrape for danger, a drumming pattern for someone foolish enough to step in the peat mud.

Their elders are not secluded. Age gives a person more places to sit. An old person may be found under a west eave sorting seed, at the pool untangling fishing line, in a workshop holding wood steady while younger hands cut it, or simply at a lane corner where they can observe who has forgotten a cloak, who is favoring one foot, who is carrying too much alone.

Memory is regarded as a material resource, like peat or timber. The eldest inhabitants are valued not only for stories but for practical recollection. They remember which wall contains a hollow behind its stones, which families once used a particular dye recipe, where a spring appeared during the long thaw seventy years ago, why a passage bends around an apparently useless patch of moss. To alter an old arrangement without consulting memory is considered a kind of carelessness.

They do not have chiefs in the usual sense. There are keepers: of the pool, of roots, of stores, of births, of the outer willow. These roles are held for a span of years and can pass between households. The keeper of the pool measures its level, watches the fish, decides when water may be drawn for certain uses, and sits beside the central stone when disputes concern boundaries or excess. The keeper of roots knows which trees can be cut, bent, grafted, or left entirely untouched. The keeper of births maintains the small book of arrivals and deaths, though much of the record is kept orally, in a long recitation of names linked to places.

No one owns a house absolutely. A household has use of rooms, shelves, roof plots, hearth niches, and sleeping ledges, but every dwelling is understood to be part of a larger knot. If a wall must be opened to repair a channel, it is opened. If an elderly person can no longer climb to their loft, another family yields a ground-level room. If a new child is born where there is no sleeping space, a storage alcove becomes a bed and the stores move elsewhere. The town’s rule against enlargement has made flexibility into a moral virtue.

Their dead are not buried beyond the willow boundary. The ground there is too exposed, and the people do not like to send their dead into a place the living do not enter often. Instead, bodies are washed with warmed pool water and wrapped in layered bark cloth. They are placed in dry chambers within the bank, where old stone foundations meet the peat. Nothing marks these chambers openly. The dead remain in the town’s structure: below gardens, beneath workshops, beside the passages they once walked. In time, a person’s name becomes attached to a place: “Take the left at Mera’s wall,” even if Mera has been gone for two hundred years.

They do not fear death as departure so much as they fear waste. To waste food, water, warmth, wood, attention, or a person’s knowledge is a serious failing. Their mourning is therefore active. After a death, relatives and neighbors spend several days repairing something that belonged to the dead person’s care: a net, a roof edge, a goat pen, a bed of herbs, a cracked stair. The work is done quietly. When it is finished, they share a meal in the repaired place.

There are craft families, though the trades are never wholly closed. One lineage may be known for lightwork: polishing shell, bone, mica, and pale stone into disks that direct sunlight into the deepest lanes. Another is known for waterwork, able to hear by the sound of a drip whether a roof channel is clogged above or below. Some are goat people, moving among the animals with a patience that makes the goats seem to understand language. Some keep the old blades, each knife passed through so many hands that its iron is almost more patch than original metal.

The most respected craft may be joining.

A joiner is not merely a builder. They understand how two things may meet without either being destroyed: new wood against old stone; a family newly arrived by marriage beside an established household; a drainage channel passing beneath a sacred threshold; a child’s need for privacy inside a town where every sound travels. The best joiners are asked to help settle arguments. They are known for saying little, touching a wall, studying the angle of a roof, and then proposing some arrangement that lets everyone keep enough.

There are people who leave. This is spoken of without drama, though everyone notices. A young person may go beyond the willow boundary to marry into the larger country, trade for salt or iron, serve as a guide, or simply find out whether there are places where one can walk all day without reaching a familiar turn. Those who leave take little: a good knife if there is one to spare, a sewn pouch of dried food, a strip of home-dyed cloth, perhaps a charm made from the pale basin fish’s shed scale.

Some return changed by wide skies and loud weather. They return with a different pace in their walk, with stories that seem too large for a room. For a season, they may speak more loudly than the others. Then the basin works on them again. Their voice lowers. Their steps begin to anticipate narrow corners. Their hands find old tasks. A person who has lived elsewhere is not treated as foreign, but they are expected to learn again how to fit.

The town does not encourage many strangers to enter. It is not hostile. A lost traveler will be fed, warmed, and guided out when weather permits. But visitors are never shown all passages, all cisterns, or the dry doors beneath which the dead lie. They are asked to tie strips of cloth around their boots, to keep their tools sheathed, to speak close to the person they address. Most outsiders find the silence oppressive after a day or two. They begin to feel watched by windows, dogs, roots, and birds.

In truth, they are watched—but not with suspicion alone.

The Basin People pay attention because attention is how they have survived. They notice when a stranger has a cough, when their fingers shake from hunger, when their boots are made for ground too hard or too wet for this place. They notice whether someone steps around moss without being told, whether they pause before crossing a shared threshold, whether they look into the black pool with greed, fear, reverence, or simple curiosity.

Their faith is not elaborate. They do not imagine the pool as a god in the shape of a person, nor the enclosing trees as guardians who listen to prayers. But they believe that things endure only because other things make room for them. A roof lasts because a wall bears it. A wall lasts because roots hold the bank. Roots live because water remains. Water remains because it is not taken thoughtlessly.

This is the closest they come to a sacred law: nothing lives alone, and nothing may become so large that it makes the rest smaller.

At the central stone, when a child is named, an elder moistens two fingers in condensation and touches them to the child’s brow. No blessing is spoken aloud. The gathered people simply stand close enough that their warmth changes the air beneath the bark roof.

Then someone carries the child home through the narrow lanes, past goats and sleeping dogs and dripping eaves, into the crowded old town that has made a place for one more life without becoming any larger at all.
