Nothing lives on the plain in the way an animal lives in a forest.

There are inhabitants, certainly. There are presences that move, consume, make use of one another, respond to changes, preserve patterns. But it is difficult to find the boundary at which one inhabitant ends and the land begins.

The six-legged seed-husk things are the nearest approximation to animals. Up close, they are not glossy in the manner of shell or skin. Their surfaces are folded mineral films, green-black and bronze, layered like the husks of seeds that have learned to close around light. Each has six narrow limbs ending in soft divided pads. They do not step so much as negotiate with the surface beneath them. Dust does not shift because the creature seems, briefly, to make an agreement with every grain it crosses.

Their heads have no visible eyes. Instead, the front of the body divides into a cluster of thin petals, each one dark and translucent. When one presses itself into a root mat, the petals open. Fine white threads pass from its face into the ground. For a while the creature becomes still enough to be mistaken for a fallen cluster of leaves.

It is feeding, perhaps.

Or reporting.

Or being repaired.

Afterward it may depart without crossing the intervening space. A patch of vegetation will fold around it, not visibly, not abruptly, and when the fronds relax the creature is gone. Sometimes, much farther away, another seed-husk creature raises itself from a root cluster with a faint clicking sound, carrying a dusting of copper grains on its back from ground it has never walked across.

There are larger versions in the orchid forests at the basin’s edge. Their bodies are low and many-jointed, their folded surfaces pale violet and white. They travel beneath the vegetation by lifting whole drifts of leaves over themselves. A witness may only notice a procession of fronds rising, inclining, and settling in sequence. They are slow enough to seem geological. Where they pass, damaged stems stand upright again, mineral edges lose fractures, and the roots arrange themselves into cleaner geometries.

These are sometimes called gardeners, though they do not cultivate anything. The plants require no cultivation. The gardeners are more like moving corrections.

At night, when the leaves brighten and the ridges show their hidden seams, another population becomes apparent.

Fine luminous shapes gather in the air around the columns of distortion: transparent knots, rings, lattices, and branching forms that resemble neither insects nor birds. They are visible only indirectly. One sees a star of pale light reflected in a glossy leaf, then looks up and finds nothing; one sees a shadow move across the inside of a translucent trunk, though nothing has passed between it and the light.

Their apparent forms may be consequences rather than bodies. A floating lattice may be the visible trace of some adjustment occurring in the field above it. They collect around newly emerged ridges. They enter the straight ground-seams in descending spirals. They sometimes settle on the broad black leaves, and the leaf beneath them becomes briefly transparent enough to show its inner architecture: channels of colored light, shifting grids, minute geometric cells dividing without producing more material.

The luminous things are not gentle exactly. They are indifferent in the way a mechanism is indifferent, but their indifference contains care. If a leaf is torn, one may see them gather above the wound. By morning the tear will not have healed. It will have been replaced with an exact, unbroken leaf whose veins follow a slightly improved pattern.

Some of the landscape’s inhabitants are so slow they are mistaken for plants.

The padded vines that cross between mineral ridges have pulses. Not the obvious swelling and contracting of sap, but changes in their internal light: sequences traveling through them over hours, or days, or years. Certain vines remember contact. If a hand rests on one, its opaline interior may briefly organize into a pale imprint with the proportions of the hand—not a picture, but a structural echo. Later, other vines elsewhere in the basin may show the same configuration moving through them, carried in silence from stem to stem.

The broad fronds possess a kind of attention. They do not turn toward bodies, nor do they react to touch in any animal fashion. But an observer who remains beside a thicket long enough will begin to notice that the leaves have made room for the observer’s shape. Not physically, at first. Their overlaps become less accidental. Lines of sight open through the growth. The plants seem to determine the most economical way to include an unfamiliar object in their arrangement.

If the visitor leaves, the space remains.

Eventually it becomes a small corridor in the thicket, bordered by leaves that mimic the spacing of shoulders and arms. It may persist for decades after the body that caused it is gone. The plain does not distinguish between a person, a fallen branch, a new ridge, and a recurring atmospheric distortion. It records all of them as conditions requiring a stable response.

There are also things within the mineral ridges.

They do not emerge often. Most evidence for them comes from the faint activity beneath the black-green facets: slow regions of darkness crossing through the stone, or pale seams changing direction where no seam had been before. During certain nights, a ridge may produce a deep, almost inaudible resonance. Nearby roots lift from the ground and hold themselves rigid, as if listening.

Then a section of the ridge opens.

No fracture occurs. The stone separates along lines that were not visible a moment before, exposing an interior crowded with white filaments and suspended crystalline organs. The opening may remain for a few minutes or for an entire day. Inside, shapes unfold: jointed, many-limbed things composed of the same dark mineral as the ridge, though softer at their edges. They move with immense precision, placing each limb into a crack, around a root, against a vein of radiance. Their work is never dramatic. They inspect a root network. They remove a grain of foreign dust. They alter the angle of a newly formed facet by less than a degree.

Then they fold themselves back into the stone.

The ridge closes around them. No door remains.

Perhaps these are individuals. Perhaps each ridge is a single long-lived organism that occasionally extends tools into its surroundings. Perhaps the things seen inside are only temporary articulations of the ridge’s thought.

In the seams beneath the ground, there may be still stranger inhabitants: beings made of route rather than body.

The moving diagrams are not merely energy. At times the colored lines pause around one another, form dense knots, and proceed in altered configurations. Roots entering these patterns can emerge far away with new leaves already grown, or emerge with their colors changed, or emerge as something that cannot be called a root at all: a thin arch of green light standing unsupported over the dust, a field of tiny glassy flowers, a cluster of seed-husk animals waiting motionlessly with their faces pressed into the ground.

Such entities do not occupy a place. They occupy a relation between places. The basin may be full of them. A vine that continues overhead from no support may be their visible limb. A distant band of orchid color may be their body at rest. The fixed clouds may be marks left by their passage through the sky’s underlying arrangements.

The plain’s oldest inhabitants are perhaps the youngest things in it: the newly raised ridges, the fresh root mats, the leafless transparent trunks just beginning to fill with slow opaline flow. Nothing here seems born from a parent in a simple way. A shape is noticed by the system, found useful, and gradually given persistence. A footprint becomes a depression, then a ring of leaves, then perhaps a root node. A root node becomes a seed-husk creature. A creature’s repeated resting place becomes a mineral spur. The categories circulate.

That may be why there is no fear among the inhabitants. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing competes in the familiar sense. Things are absorbed into patterns, revised by them, and reappear in forms more suited to the current arrangement of the land.

A visitor is not prey, not guest, not intruder.

At first, the plain treats them as a temporary irregularity: warmth, weight, motion, breath, a source of unfamiliar chemistry. The leaves make room. The dust records the pressure of their feet. The silent columns bend, perhaps too subtly to measure. At night, pale lattice-things may collect at the edge of their camp without approaching.

If the visitor remains long enough, the plain begins to learn their outline.

Not their name. Not their memories. Not the inwardness that makes them a person.

Only their pattern.

It will preserve the shape of their paths in low green monuments. It may reproduce the cadence of their footsteps in the arrangement of small mineral ridges. It may grow translucent leaves whose inner veins briefly organize into the geometry of a hand, a face, a body standing beneath the pale gold sky.

And long after the visitor is gone, something low and folded may cross the copper dust without leaving a trace, pause beside that preserved impression, and press its petaled head into the earth—as though receiving news from the land about someone who was never fully separate from it.
