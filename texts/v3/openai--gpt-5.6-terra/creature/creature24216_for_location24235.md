What lives there has learned not to expect permanence.

The crawlers are the oldest certainty of the flats. They hatch in the warm black margins of receding pools, each no larger than a thumb joint, pale and soft until their first plates harden. For a while they ride on the backs of larger adults, packed into grooves between the armor, feeding on the mineral slime that gathers there. Then they drop away and join the moving bands.

A band is not a herd in the usual sense. It has no visible leader and no fixed direction. It pours around obstacles, divides at channels, joins again on the far side. Thousands of plated backs travel under the rain in a slow gray-green tide of their own. They know safe ground through the pressure of one another’s feet. A crawler at the front tests the mud; if it sinks too deeply, the news travels backward through the band as a rapid clicking under the plates, and the whole mass turns. People sometimes follow crawler migrations at a distance, trusting them more than maps.

When the water rises, the crawlers heap themselves together on stone and shell bars. The outer animals lock their hooked limbs beneath the bodies of those above them. The pile shifts and rattles through the storm, a living mound resisting the cold. Small things shelter inside it: pale mud-mites, needle-legged scavengers, the blind slick larvae of the channel fish. Sometimes even human children are tucked into the lee of a crawler heap while adults struggle to secure a hut or recover a stranded animal.

The tall filter-beasts are less numerous but more important. The people call them, in different settlements, the still-legged, the rain-drinkers, the hollow ones, the white gates. They are born dark and nearly opaque, their young bodies swollen with rich internal fluids. As they age, their flesh thins. In a full-grown animal, the blood-dark branching filters show through the translucent skin along the throat, belly, and flanks. In cold weather these organs pulse visibly, opening and closing like shadows of leaves from a world with trees.

They are gentle except when their young are threatened. A parent can stand almost motionless for hours, head lowered into a shallow sheet of water, drawing mud and rain through the filtering organs in its mouth. The sound it makes is a low, steady suction, easy to miss beneath the wind. But when a flood separates a young one from the herd, the adults lift their narrow heads and call.

The call is not loud at first. It is a thin, flute-like note, too pure for the place, rising through the roar of weather. Other adults answer. Their calls overlap and bend in the wind until the entire flooded plain seems to be whistling through hidden cracks. The young answer from wherever they have been carried, and people say that if one listens carefully, the sound tells not only where the animal is, but how fast the water is rising around it.

The white-gates provide nearly everything. Their shed skin becomes waterproof wrapping and window membrane. Their tendon, braided and greased, holds huts together. Their bones are dense but light enough to carve into awls, scoops, needles, fish-spears, and the thin resonant strips used to test hollow ground. Their fat burns cleanest of any substance known on the flats. A household that has secured a bowl of rendered white-gate fat may have light for several nights; it may also be noticed by everyone for miles.

So the animals are not killed lightly. A dead one is an event. The people do not say they have hunted it. They say the water has given up its body.

Sometimes this is true. A beast caught in a sudden sink, or torn open by predators, will be found lodged against a ridge after the tide falls. Then families come from nearby huts, carrying shell knives and scraping tools. They work quickly, shoulders bent against rain, taking what they can before the flyers arrive. No part is left cleanly behind. The carcass is reduced to a dark skeleton, then to scattered joints, then to nothing but stains in the mud and the memory of where such a thing happened.

The predators are called listeners.

They do not see poorly, though their eyes are small and often filmed over against rain. But sight is secondary to them. Their skulls are built with broad flat chambers against the ground. When they lie down, their jaws spread and their body becomes still enough to feel the movements beneath water and mud: the tremor of crawler legs, the soft pumping of a filter-beast’s throat, the uneven stance of a human carrying weight.

A listener can be close for a long time without being recognized. It may look like a strip of wet rock beside a channel, or a collapsed ridge of black mud. Its skin darkens and pales with the ground. Its many-toed feet lie folded beneath it. Then the earth changes shape.

They attack in silence. The noise comes afterward: the splash, the bellows of herd animals, the high clatter of crawlers scattering into cracks, the people striking stones in alarm. Listeners are feared, but their hides are prized. The pads of their feet make the best mud-shoes, and the narrow vibration bones from their skulls are sometimes hung in doorways. A good vibration bone, held between thumb and forefinger, will quiver before a distant heavy step can be heard.

The flyers belong to the wind more than to the land. Their young are ugly, almost naked things, gray as wet ash, clinging to vertical stone with hooked feet. Adults spend much of their lives aloft. They feed on exposed flesh, small crawling animals, and the swollen bladders of channel creatures brought up by the tide. They are clever enough to watch people work.

A flyer will wait above a carcass site, circling through sheets of rain, until someone turns away. It will drop without warning, wings snapping open at the last instant, and take a strip of meat from a drying rack or a child’s handful of shellfish. Some settlements hang polished metal plates around their roofs. The sudden flashing confuses the flyers briefly. Other settlements stretch lines of tendon across the openings between huts, threaded with small bones that clatter in the wind. This does not stop the flyers either, but it warns those inside that something large has landed nearby.

There are smaller lives too, though people rarely name them unless they matter.

Clear-bodied skimmers drift in rain pools, feeding on whatever invisible bloom has colored the water. In the first hour after a storm, their numbers can turn a shallow basin silver with movement. Black worms emerge from under mineral plates and knot themselves into spinning ropes. Shell-borers live inside the old compacted banks, making a dry ticking sound when the water is low. There are pale fish in the deeper channels, blind in their adulthood, with broad mouths lined in felt-like teeth. They are caught at night by lowering baited hooks into holes that breathe cold air.

The people themselves are low-country people in more than where they live.

They build low. They sit low. They sleep close to the ground, often in shallow pits lined with plates and hide, because anything raised too high is taken by wind. Their clothing is layered membrane, softened crawler horn, woven tendon, and the occasional scrap of traded fabric preserved like a family relic. Their faces are weather-cut, their hair usually cropped or bound down tight. Children wear hoods with weighted flaps so the wind cannot fill them like sails.

Most adults have damaged hearing. Some are nearly deaf by middle age, and many communicate as readily by touch as by voice: a hand on the wrist, a pressure between shoulder blades, fingers drummed in a pattern against a knee. They learn the stone-knock codes early.

Each settlement knows certain speaking rocks. One will carry a warning of tide. Another is for calling boats, if boats can be called. Another belongs to a particular family and is struck only for death. These stones are never used carelessly. A traveler coming over the flats may see no people, no smoke, no hut taller than the mud around it. Then a knock travels through a black ridge beneath their hand: three slow blows, a pause, two more. Somewhere beyond sight, someone has heard them.

The people have little use for ownership of ground. Ground moves. A shell bar that holds three huts this season may be split by water next season; a safe causeway may become a sucking channel; a black shelf may rise newly exposed after years underwater. What is owned, if anything can be owned, is knowledge.

A family owns the memory of a hidden spring that remains drinkable after floodwater turns bitter. Someone owns the method of finding dry pockets in crawler plate. Someone owns the old path beneath the flooded white basin. Someone owns a song whose rhythm remembers the sequence of hard stones across a place where no path can be seen. Knowledge is traded, inherited, married into, stolen, and mourned.

They do not keep calendars in days. Days are too alike and too easily erased. They count by great returns: the tide that drowned the eastern huts, the season of violet-winged flyers, the year the mineral crust held long enough to cross with loaded sleds, the winter when a blue flame burned for eleven nights in one bowl.

Their dead are not buried. The land will not reliably keep a grave, and digging is dangerous. Bodies are wrapped in membrane and stone-weighted, then given to deep channel mouths when the tide is turning outward. This is considered a practical mercy, but also a necessary courtesy. The channels take so much without asking that they must occasionally be offered something willingly.

There are tales of people who live farther out, where the water is deeper and the stone rises in isolated black stacks. These people are said to ride broad-shelled animals, to fish in submerged ruins, to speak mostly through hammered metal plates. There are also tales of people inland, beyond the last mineral flats, who have seen grass. The word for it is retained, though many children grow up thinking it is a kind of green fur that covers the earth.

No one can quite imagine it.

At night, inside a hut, a family may sit around the blue bowl of fat-fire. The roof shudders. Rain ticks and hammers overhead. The walls breathe inward with each hard gust. Somewhere outside, a listener presses its skull to the mud. Crawlers pass in a mass against the hut’s outer plates, making the whole shelter tremble with their clicking. Above, flyers scrape their hooked feet along the roof seam.

Inside, someone repairs a blade. Someone sorts shell fragments by hardness. Someone holds a child close while teaching the pattern of knocks for rising water.

Outside, the country continues its immense work of becoming less solid.

Inside, for one more night, something lives against it.
