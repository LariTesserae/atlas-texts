What lives on the upland is not one people, nor one wilderness, but a federation of hungers.

The great plants are the eldest inhabitants. They do not merely grow there; they make the conditions under which anything else can exist. A tower reed is a reservoir, a landmark, a nesting cliff, a source of fiber and sugar and hollow shelter. A crown-fern is a vertical country with dawn at its upper shelves and a permanent green dusk under its roots. The immense pitchers are ponds that stand upright, each with an interior climate: midges above the waterline, eel-thin swimmers in the digestive pools, pale amphibians clinging to the waxy throats, and scavenging birds that have learned to steal prey without becoming prey themselves.

They communicate, though not in messages a person could translate sentence by sentence. Water pressure passes along roots. Chemical bitterness blooms in the air. Leaves change their angle. Whole groves may shift their behavior after a fire, a migration, or the repeated tread of strangers. Their intelligence—if it is intelligence—is distributed, slow in its memory and startlingly quick in its reflexes. A reed tower can spend a century thickening its walls against a storm that comes only once in three generations, then close a wound before sunrise.

In and upon these plants live countless smaller kingdoms.

There are mosses that flower only on the backs of the pale browsing herds, coloring the animals in seasonal maps of pink, white, and deep blue. The herds carry whole ecologies across the dry distances: mites in their fur, beetles beneath their armored footpads, tiny seed-eating birds nesting in the warm moss along their shoulders. When an old browser dies, it is not left bare. Rootlets seek it within hours. Its flowering coat goes on blooming for several mornings after its breath has stopped.

The leaf-cutters are not simply eaters of fern. Their heavy jaws prune the upper growth, preventing the oldest shelves from collapsing beneath their own burden. In the cavities they expose, rain pools. In those pools breed transparent frogs whose skins flash with moving veins of green. The frogs are eaten by ribbon-wing birds; the birds leave droppings full of seeds; the seeds become hanging gardens; the gardens shelter the leaf-cutters’ young. Nothing in the upland has a single role.

There are root-whales beneath the mat: immense, blind burrowing animals that move through the deep woven layers by swallowing pulp and excreting loosened, fertile ridges. A person standing quietly may feel one pass far below, not as an earthquake but as a long adjustment in the ground, a rising of warmth through the soles of the feet. Their backs sometimes break the surface in dry country, covered in old roots and sprouting weeds so that they resemble moving hills. People avoid killing them. A dead root-whale poisons a wide region with its digestive fluids, but a living one is a maker of wells.

There are creatures that live only in the brief dawn wetness: dew-crabs with flat mirrored shells, tongue-footed lizards that drink from root hairs, moths that open their wings to collect condensation and fold them into drinking cups. By noon many have buried themselves, sealed shut inside hollow stems, or climbed into shaded layers of foliage. The sun is an animal too, in practical terms. It hunts the unprepared.

At night the plateau changes its population. The open intervals between groves, so bright and exposed by day, fill with things that trust darkness more than cover. Long-legged grazers come out to crop the tender white root tips that rise after heat. Gliding mammals travel from fern shelf to fern shelf on skins stretched between their limbs, carrying pollen in the fur of their throats. Black, jointed scavengers emerge from cracks in the root mat and clean the shells, husks, bones, and fallen fruit of the day. Their clicking is so synchronized that distant travelers mistake it for rain.

The climbing cats are feared because they seem to belong to neither ground nor canopy. They have the patient muscularity of hunters, but also the careful manners of parasites. They do not mark territory with scent alone. They learn groves. A cat may spend months in the outer shelves of a single crown-fern, eating what the tree’s own defenses reject: sick nestlings, old fruit bats, larvae that bore too deeply into the bark. Some groves tolerate them until they do not. Then the leaves contract, the aerial roots rise, and the cat finds its paths closing around it. The oldest cats bear scars from trees as much as from prey: pale bands where a root cinched their ribs, glassy punctures from defensive thorns, patches of fur permanently stained by sap.

People live in the intervals, but they are not outside this order. They are route-keepers, listeners, graft-makers, and cautious borrowers. Their greatest practical skill is knowing what the land is about to do. A child learns the noises of nearby towers before learning distant place-names. They can tell by a change in a trunk’s hum that water has risen in the roots; by the smell beneath a fern shelf that a brood of burrowing animals is about to surface; by the behavior of insects that a storm has chosen a particular ridge.

Their villages hold families, certainly, but also adopted plants and resident animals. A house-tree may have a name older than the clan sleeping within it. Its inner hollows support cooking racks and cradles; its outer bark holds lichen gardens; its roots shelter stores of dried food. Around its base live domesticized thorn-hens, broad-bodied birds that peck parasites from the root bark and lay hard blue eggs. Small sap-drinkers are encouraged in roof branches because their alarm calls reveal approaching predators. Children are taught which creatures are kin by agreement, which are merely tolerated, and which must never be fed.

There are people who travel almost constantly: rain-readers who follow the changing song of cisterns, seed-brokers carrying pollen bundles between isolated groves, hunters who track old browsers without killing breeding animals, and path-singers who maintain ancestral trails. The latter are perhaps the most necessary. They walk with brushes, water gourds, pruning hooks, and voices trained to particular tones. Their songs are not prayers alone. The repeated vibration discourages certain root fibers from lifting, lures beneficial insects into the path margins, and lets neighboring settlements know who has passed. A silent traveler on a maintained road is regarded with unease.

There are also those who live farther from people than is considered wise: gatherers in the pitcher fields, hermits in the upper crown-ferns, families who ride the seasonal migrations and build no permanent shelter. Some become experts in a single plant’s moods and are consulted like physicians. Others are believed to have become partly claimed by the upland. They return after years away with sap-stained hands, unusual patience, and a reluctance to sleep indoors. Whether this is a real transformation or only the effect of solitude is not much discussed.

Even the dead remain inhabitants. Bodies are not buried deeply, because there is no true below. They are placed in chosen root hollows or wrapped into the living mat, where heat, fungus, insects, and thirsty fibers receive them. A person may become part of the path they maintained, the shelter they grew, or the cistern whose water their grandchildren drink. This is not imagined as metaphor. On the upland, it is ordinary material fact.

So the place is inhabited by enormous plants, mobile gardens, burrowing makers of ground, birds that ride heat, predators negotiated with by leaves, and people who have learned not to call themselves masters of any of it.

And beneath all of them is the oldest inhabitant of all: the root mat itself—warm, layered, full of spores and sleeping seeds, remembering every fallen leaf and every footstep long after the walker has passed.
