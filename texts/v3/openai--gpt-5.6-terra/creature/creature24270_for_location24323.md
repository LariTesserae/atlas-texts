What lives in the throat of the unfinished mountain is not one people, but an arrangement of lives that have learned to occupy pressure, heat, hunger, and interruption.

The human inhabitants call themselves, collectively, **the Shaft**, though no one is entirely sure whether this means the settlement, its workforce, or the condition of being unable to leave. A person might say, “The Shaft needs another lift mechanic,” or “The Shaft took my brother on lower duty,” or “Don’t speak like that—the Shaft hears.” Outsiders call them miners, smelters, salvage workers, or high-altitude industrials. These names are technically true and socially useless.

Most Shaft-born people are slight in the chest but broad through the shoulders and hands. They grow up climbing before they can walk far. Their knees show the permanent dark bruising of ladder work; their palms are thickly lined even in childhood. They tend to keep their hair cropped, braided close, or tucked under lint caps, because loose hair attracts static dust and can become an ignition hazard around spinning equipment. Older residents often have pale crescents around their eyes from years of mask seals. Their teeth are treated as a public utility: grit ruins them early, and a person who cannot chew the dense ration cakes becomes dependent quickly.

They are not, in general, grim. They are economical.

They do not waste breath on shouting, do not waste light on decoration that serves no second purpose, do not waste affection by making it abstract. To love someone is to patch their mask before one’s own, to remember which rail cage makes them sick, to bring them a warm bolt wrapped in cloth for their glove lining. A proposal may consist of adding one’s name to another household’s air ledger. A divorce is often settled by assigning the disputed filter cartridges to one party and the shared goat to the other.

Children belong to galleries more than to individual homes. They are raised among the particular noises of their level: the cough of the Number Six compressor, the twice-hourly shunt of the ore rail, the shriek of the east ventilation shutters in a crossdraft. A child who gets lost may not know the city’s official names for places, but can identify home by listening through a wall. They play games of stillness in the tunnels, games of balancing hardware on inclined pipes, games in which one child calls out the changing temperatures while the others navigate blindfolded. Their first serious lesson is not reading, though most learn to read schedules and hazard symbols early. It is breath: how to recognize bad air before the lungs tell them, how to move slowly when frightened, how to share a mask without panicking.

The old are not sent upward, because upward is not safer. They become keepers of narrow expertise. One old woman may be responsible for knowing which abandoned conduit still gives warm air on the eighth gallery. An old man may remember the exact sound a bearing made before the 31st hoist cable failed. Some become **draft listeners**, sitting in quiet service bays with strips of foil, feathers, or threads of mineral film hung from their fingers, reading the movements of the mountain’s internal gases. The signal authority employs several people who cannot read instruments but can tell, from the taste of a vent’s breath, whether the lower furnaces are drawing wrong.

There are families who have never seen a river and families who remember them as inherited obscenities: too much water, moving everywhere, touching everything, making the ground soft. The old word is used for industrial brine, condensation accidents, and tears, though tears are uncommon enough to attract notice. Children are told surface stories in which rain falls from above. Many believe these stories only halfway.

The settlement’s humans divide themselves less by ancestry than by height.

The upper galleries have daylight, cold, courier work, signal towers, and people who speak quickly because their shifts are timed to the narrow hours of sky. Their clothing is pale with frost-salt, and they sometimes develop a superstitious contempt for those below, whom they call Deepheads.

The middle galleries contain the actual city: repair yards, food rooms, schools, clinics, animal pens, laundries that clean cloth with vibrating drums and solvent vapor, quiet rooms where people sleep under the constant body-noise of machinery. Middle people are traders, clerks, repairers, cooks, lamp workers, ledger keepers, and scavengers of usable parts. They are considered unreliable by the upper folk and soft by the lower ones, which is useful: it lets them survive both.

The lower galleries belong to the heat crews, ore handlers, bore teams, furnace tenders, capstan workers, and those who inspect the places where the built city gives way to the unbuilt mountain. Deep workers often wear their work lives visibly: copper staining in old burns, silver corrosion at the seams of their tools, lashes whitened by mineral dust. They are paid in hazard increments and have the shortest official workdays, though everyone knows their unofficial days are longer. Many do not bring children down. Many do not marry outside their shifts. Their songs are nearly soundless, made of tapped rhythms carried through railings and pipework.

Among the people live the licensed animals, each of which has become a kind of minor citizen.

The goats are the most openly intelligent. They know the vertical routes better than any map does, and they have preferences in human company. A goat may refuse a new handler for weeks, then choose a child and follow them everywhere. They eat things they should not be able to digest—treated cloth, insulation fibers, old labels, mineral paste—and their keepers say this is because they are descended from mountain creatures, not farm stock. This is not literally believed, but it is not dismissed, either. When a goat stands facing a sealed lower passage and will not move, crews take notice.

The cliff hens are communal property in theory and inveterate thieves in practice. They lay warm, thick-shelled eggs in electrical cavities and under junction housings. Their feathers are gray-white, dust-colored, with bare skin around the eyes and beaks suited to pecking mineral crust from metal. They have no fear of machinery, but are terrified of silence. When a power failure spreads through a gallery, the hens begin screaming before the lamps go out.

The dust ferrets are never really owned. They are tolerated in cableways because they eat lamp moths, and cursed when they chew insulation or steal grease plugs. Their long bodies can compress through gaps no wider than a thumb. Their fur gathers static and sparkles faintly after a crawl through charged dust. Children try to tame them; adults insist they cannot be tamed; every household with a ferret living behind its wall eventually gives it a name.

The oxen are slow, warm, patient creatures whose value cannot be measured entirely by labor. They occupy the lowest stable decks, where their body heat is harvested through heat exchangers built into the floors. They are brushed with oil to protect their hides from the dry grit. Their horns were bred away generations ago, not because they were dangerous but because animals with horns kept catching them in the capstan rigging and dying. An ox’s death is a neighborhood event. Its leather, bones, fat, harness fittings, and accumulated body heat are all accounted for; then, after the accounting, someone usually tells a story about it as though it had been a coworker.

There are unofficial animals too.

Some people keep lamp moths in jars because the larvae eat a particular black residue that otherwise clogs vent grilles. Some maintain colonies of grease beetles in sealed tubs, harvesting their shells for polish and their larvae for feed. In the violet lamp quarter there are tiny, nearly transparent spiders that live behind old bulbs and weave webs strong enough to catch mica dust. Repairers leave them alone. A clean bulb is admired; a bulb with the correct spider is trusted.

And beneath all of these small arrangements, perhaps, something else lives.

No one gives it a stable name. The deep crews call it the **Underpressure**. Children call it the **Maker**. Surveyors, when forced to speak formally, refer to “unmapped geothermal and tectonic activity.” The signal authority has a prohibited term for it—“responsive substrate”—because there were years when too many workers claimed the shaft had answered knocking with knocking.

It may simply be the mountain continuing to form: rock tightening under its own weight, gas finding new channels, hot material cooling into harder shapes, faults adjusting a fraction at a time. Yet the settlement has existed long enough for people to notice patterns that no official chart includes.

A gallery wall sweats silver dust for three nights before a collapse elsewhere.

The lower winds rise warm on the birthdays of certain bore crews.

Tools left near deep vents occasionally acquire a faint magnetic bias, always pointing not north but downward.

Animals react first, but not consistently. The hens fall quiet. The goats press their noses to the floor. The ferrets leave the cableways and gather in the warmest rooms. The oxen stop chewing.

Then the people do what they always do when the mountain remembers them.

They wait.

They bank the furnaces. They close the chain screens. They hold children against their chests, not tenderly, exactly, but securely. They put hands on railings and pipes and walls, listening through metal to the slow vast rearrangement below.

The mountain has not finished becoming a mountain.

And the inhabitants have not finished becoming the things that can live inside one.
