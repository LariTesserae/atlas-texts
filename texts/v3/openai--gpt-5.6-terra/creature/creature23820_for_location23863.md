What lives in the high country is not divided cleanly into animal, person, plant, or remnant.

There are people, though few enough that a traveler might mistake them for cairns until one raises a hand.

They live in the slate huts and the shallow caves where the stone has been hollowed by no visible tool. They wear layered garments cut from the silver coats of the long-backed animals, from glider membrane, from some dark felted fiber that gathers in the joints of ironwood. Their faces are dry, brown, and strangely unlined. Their hair is usually shorn close, not for warmth but because loose hair is said to remember the wind, and the wind has no proper business here.

They do not call themselves highlanders, or mountain people, or anything that suggests possession. Their nearest word for themselves means something like *those still becoming present.*

A child in one of their shelters may be older than the shelter’s doorframe. An old woman may have been born yesterday, depending on which sequence of footprints is believed. They do not mark birthdays. They mark arrivals: the first time a person sees their own shadow waiting somewhere it should not be; the first time an animal permits them to touch its brow; the first time they wake with dust under their nails from a road they have not taken.

Their speech is quiet and economical. Spoken words fail them easily in the open, so much of their conversation is conducted by arranging objects: three black-glass blades beside a lamp means *wait until morning*; a line of quartz beads across a threshold means *someone returned incorrectly*; an overturned bowl means *do not ask where they have been.* Children learn these arrangements before they learn the spoken names of stones.

They herd nothing. The animals gather or depart according to patterns no person claims to understand. Yet each household has creatures that remain near it across years, perhaps generations.

A narrow-horned grazer may stand outside a hut for fifty winters, its coat never growing thicker or thinner. A black-jointed cat may sleep in the same patch of shade, though the boulder casting that shade has shifted through several remembered positions. People give these animals no personal names. To name an animal too closely is to imply that it is only one thing.

Sometimes a person will find a small blind burrower in their sleeve, warm as a coal despite the cold. They will place it beneath a flat stone and leave it there. The next day the stone may have become a cairn, and beside it may be an unfamiliar member of the household, silently grinding grain that no one planted.

There are also the ones that look like animals until they move wrongly.

They may be found among a herd: a silver-haired lizard whose feet touch the ground a heartbeat after its body passes over it; a plate-winged beetle carrying, within its transparent shell, the reflected image of a dead person walking; a glider folded into a cliff face whose veins slowly change position over the course of an afternoon. These are not feared exactly. Fear requires a clean distinction between danger and safety, and the high country has worn that distinction smooth.

The people leave them offerings of mineral salt, shed teeth, black glass, and sometimes written questions. The questions are scratched on strips of mica and tucked into crevices. Most remain unanswered.

A few return years later with an answer already carved into them.

The oldest inhabitants are not the people.

They are the animals that have ceased to be individual animals: immense still gatherings on distant slopes, visible from many miles away. A traveler sees thousands of antlers and assumes a herd. But from close up, the bodies overlap without crowding. Legs emerge from the wrong flanks. A dozen heads share the same slow breath. Their eyes are clouded quartz or deep mineral green. They stand around a particular stone, or a particular old carcass, as if attending an event too gradual for a human life to witness.

No one approaches these assemblies if they can help it.

The country calls them, in the few surviving marks that can be translated, the Held.

It is believed that the Held were once moving creatures. They traveled, fed, bore young, sickened, fought, and died. Then they remained in one place for too long. The land learned their shapes. Their bodies became instructions written into the basin, and the basin began repeating them.

This may also be what happens to people.

At certain passes there are figures seated against the stone, knees drawn up beneath animal-hide cloaks. They appear to be resting travelers. Their skin has the gray grain of weathered slate. Their eyes are closed. A bowl, a walking stick, or a row of black-glass tools lies beside each one. The local people greet them when passing, though they do not wait for replies.

Sometimes the figures are gone on the return journey.

Sometimes there are two.

And beneath all of this—beneath hoof, root, bone bridge, hut floor, and splintered shale—something lives that has no surface shape.

It is not a beast beneath the ground. It has no hunger anyone has observed. It does not tunnel, pulse, speak, or sleep. It may be the cold itself, or the patient pressure that makes stones choose forms and then refuse to relinquish them. The blind bodies in the gravel are perhaps its fingertips. The mineral plants may be its slow thoughts. The lingering shadows may be impressions left where it has briefly noticed something.

The people do not worship it. They do not even agree that it exists.

But they obey its manners.

They do not move stones that have chosen a shape, because a chosen stone is no longer merely stone. It is a sentence in a language too old to be read forward. To alter it is to alter whatever comes next—or whatever has already happened.

So the inhabitants of the high country endure among animals that do not hunt, trees that do not seed, bridges no one built recently enough to remember, and shadows that remain faithful to places rather than bodies.

They keep their lamps unlit.

They sleep lightly.

When a stranger arrives, they offer no welcome in words. They point to the nearest still animal, then to the stranger’s feet, then to the sky.

The meaning is not entirely clear.

Perhaps it means: *Stand where you are.*

Perhaps: *You have been seen.*

Or perhaps it is only the oldest courtesy in a land where nearly everything, eventually, stays.
