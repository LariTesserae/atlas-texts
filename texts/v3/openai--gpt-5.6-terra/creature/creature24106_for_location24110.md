What lives there is not a single fauna but a pressure-system of bodies: a society without leaders, built from hunger, heat, dissolved stone, and the slow turning of the water.

At the rim live the exiles.

They are the least comfortable creatures in the basin, and perhaps for that reason the most visibly inventive. The cold near the overflow is almost fresh compared with the mineral-heavy depths, and the light is too strong. Nothing large stays there for long. But innumerable small lives make their homes in cracks just beneath the lip: hard translucent shells shaped like broken fingernails; bladder-things that wedge themselves into seams and inflate only when spray reaches them; clusters of pale feeding crowns that open for a few minutes each day when the current brings warm water outward.

Some resemble barnacles, though their plates are black and iridescent, growing in spirals that direct water into their mouths. Some are no more than wet membranes spread over stone. They drink thin films of overflow as it passes and withdraw entirely into mineral slits when the sun appears. Their young are not released into the water in the ordinary way. They are loosened as dust—dark motes with hooked tails—and are carried over the rim. Most vanish into the white falls. A few are caught by updrafts, rain, or returning spray and are brought back to the sea from which they came.

The rim creatures are small because large bodies are taken.

Above them circle things that are not birds, though at first glance they can be mistaken for them. They emerge from the basin at dusk, when the mountain’s wet air rises in slow sheets. Their bodies are flat, black, and glistening, with broad fins that stiffen in air. They do not fly by beating; they ride the cold drafts that spill over the crown. For an hour or two they skim over the water, dipping their undersides into the surface to drink, mate, or seize anything that has come too close to the light.

They have no eyes visible from above. Their faces are smooth wedges except for a crescent of sensory pits that taste changes in temperature. When they return to the water, they fold their fins around themselves and plunge like dropped knives. Their young begin as buoyant disks, drifting at the surface among foam and mineral pollen. They are prey to almost everything.

Below the rim, where daylight still arrives as separate beams rather than a general green glow, live the glass herds.

These are the transparent bell-creatures, but they are not simple drifting animals. Each is a body made around a deliberate emptiness. Its bell is filled with a carefully balanced mixture of gases and warm fluids; its trailing threads are dense with grains of magnetized mineral. By changing the tension of its bell and the arrangement of those grains, it can rise, sink, turn, or hold itself motionless in a current that would carry away a fish.

They gather in groups of hundreds or thousands, arranged vertically through the water. From a distance they look like rain falling upward.

The older bells are amber or smoke-colored, scarred by contact with rock and other bodies. The youngest are almost invisible except when they pulse. Their pulses travel through a herd in sequence: one contraction, then another, then another, a message moving from the cold outer water toward the warmer interior. No one knows whether the signal is warning, courtship, feeding instruction, or merely the animal equivalent of a muscle remembering its rhythm. But the basin responds to it. Small swimmers scatter. Shoals change direction. Even the great filter-beasts sometimes close their grilles for several beats afterward.

The glass herds feed by combing the water with threads so fine that they catch particles of skin, microbial light, mineral dust, eggs, and the tiny larval forms of larger animals. Their threads sting, but only weakly. A healthy adult fish can pass through a veil of them with no more than a rash of pale welts. Sick animals avoid them. Dead animals become tangled in them and slowly disassembled, their bodies lifted upward in fragments.

There are creatures that live inside the bells.

Not parasites exactly. Some are thieves, some caretakers, some possibly part of the bell-creature’s own life cycle. Tiny red swimmers shelter in the bell cavity and clean its internal membranes. Needle-bodied worms ride inside the trailing channels and steal captured food. Flat silver mites lay their eggs in the warmest folds of the bell, where the developing young are protected from the cold. When a bell begins to fail, the things inside it leave in a sudden glittering spill. The empty body collapses, sinks, and is eaten before it reaches the darker levels.

The pale blunt-faced fish live everywhere the light can still be measured.

They are the basin’s collective animal. Not because they are identical, but because no individual among them appears to have a complete life. A single fish is slow, incurious, and vulnerable. It can feed, flee, breed, and follow warmth, but it cannot decide much. A shoal can. When enough of them gather, their pale heads angle together and their dark lateral markings align. Their movement becomes exact. They divide around obstacles without breaking formation. They can surround a predator and drive it away merely by crowding its sensory field with thousands of synchronized electrical flickers.

Their faces are blunt because they feed by impact. They strike mineral skins, dead shells, vent deposits, and the armored bodies of slower animals, knocking loose particles which they then swallow. Their teeth are hidden deep inside their throats, not in their mouths. Each has several grinding plates, and their feeding produces a soft communal clicking. In a large shoal this clicking becomes a sound like rainfall on a roof.

They have a custom, if animals can be said to have customs.

At certain stages of the basin’s rotation, when the outward current strengthens and colder water begins cutting inward along the walls, the shoals gather beneath the overflow sheets. They rise until their backs almost touch the surface. Then they hold position for hours, allowing the falling water to strike them. Some are killed by the force. Some are swept over the rim. The survivors return carrying unfamiliar salts in their gills and scales. For weeks afterward, the entire shoal seems altered: it turns more sharply, descends more deeply, and produces low clicks in patterns not heard at other times of year.

No creature in the basin appears to teach them this.

Along the rock live the scrapers and miners.

The shovel-faced crabs are only the most obvious of them. They move sideways in dense ranks over the black stone, their front limbs broad and mineral-hardened. Their faces are flat, almost architectural, with layered plates that can be closed against the vent water. They scrape the metallic living films into paste and pass the paste backward through rows of comb-like mouthparts.

Their shells are never entirely their own. The dark skins growing on the walls also grow on the crabs. A healthy crab carries patches of metallic bloom along its joints and carapace. The films feed on minerals released by the crab’s waste; the crab feeds on the film; both are eaten by larger things. When the crab molts, its old shell is briefly alive with residual glow. Smaller animals swarm it before it cools.

There are miners more secretive than the crabs: long animals with drills of fused teeth, which bore shallow tunnels into the softer seams of the basin wall. They do not eat stone, but cultivate warmth. Their tunnels are lined with an orange microbial fur that thrives on gases leaking from the rock. The miners return again and again to crop this fur with rasping mouths. In some places entire sections of cliff are riddled behind their smooth black surfaces, occupied by hundreds of unseen bodies.

At night—though night changes little below the first few fathoms—these miners extend pale feelers from their burrows. The feelers wave in the current, exchanging chemical traces with neighboring tunnels. A wall covered in them looks furred. If something touches too many at once, every feeler retracts and the cliff becomes bare in an instant.

In the warm sediment around the vents live the fields.

From above, they resemble pale grass moving in a wind. They are colonies of blind white worms, each no thicker than a finger but joined underground by masses of shared tissue. Their individual bodies rise from the sediment to feed, bend in the currents, and sink again. Beneath the sand, however, they are connected in thick, branching mats. A disturbance felt by one body travels through the colony. One worm withdraws; then a hundred; then a whole field vanishes into the ground with a sound like wet cloth being pulled through a narrow opening.

The worms digest heat as much as food. Their inner tissues are crowded with organisms that use the vent chemicals directly, turning poison into flesh. The worms need sediment not for concealment but for stability. In open water they become frantic, knotting themselves into pale ropes. On the ground they are serene.

Their predators know this.

Small armored swimmers hover above the worm fields and wait for the few moments when a body rises too far. The swimmers have many legs but use them mostly as sensory fans. Their shells are hinged and can snap shut around a worm with startling speed. Once captured, the worm is not bitten. It is held alive while the predator’s shell fills with digestive fluid. The process is slow. The worm continues to move inside the closed armor for hours.

The armored swimmers themselves are prey to the black flat animals from the deep.

These are among the basin’s oldest-looking inhabitants, though age means little in a place where the rock itself is young. They have no obvious front or back until they move. Their fins run nearly the length of their bodies, rippling with almost no effort. Their upper surfaces are so black that they remove detail from the water around them. Light touches them and seems to stop.

Their mouths unfold beneath them.

A feeding mouth begins as a narrow seam. When opened, it becomes a series of circular membranes, each ring lined with teeth, hooks, sensory hairs, or soft grasping fronds. The animal settles over prey rather than striking it. It lowers itself until the water beneath its body is trapped. Then the mouth opens in stages. Small animals are drawn inward by the pressure difference. Larger animals are pinned, dissolved, and eaten while the predator remains nearly motionless.

They are patient because they can be.

A black flat animal may wait in the shadow of a glass cone for half a day. It may follow a grazing herd for miles of circular travel. It may ignore easy food if it senses a larger disturbance deeper in the current. When two meet, they do not fight in any recognizable way. They overlap their fins, touch undersides, and exchange slow electrical pulses. Sometimes they separate. Sometimes one gradually folds around the other and, over many hours, becomes the only one that leaves.

In the central dark live the chimney-backed animals.

The ones that rise are only a fraction of what they are.

Their bodies are built for a depth the upper basin cannot properly reveal. Broad as rooms, dense as stone, plated in overlapping shell and soft mineralized flesh, they travel the lower turning current in long, slow circuits. Their trailing limbs are not all limbs. Some are roots, some are mouths, some are sensory organs, and some are old growths that have become inhabited by smaller creatures. A mature chimney-back carries an ecosystem upon itself: scraper crabs in the joints of its plates; luminous grazers in the warm folds around its towers; small fish feeding on its shedding skin; threadlike scavengers nesting among the mineral chimneys.

The towers along its back are alive and hot. Water enters them cold and mineral-poor, passes through chambers filled with blood-dark fluid, and leaves warm, rusty, and changed. The animal may be feeding itself, cultivating the organisms that live upon it, regulating its internal temperature, or speaking to others through heat. Probably all of these are true.

When one rises, the basin knows.

The great filter-beasts halt their pumping or change their rhythm. Shoals compress into bright, anxious clouds. The glass herds lift out of its path. Even the predators retreat, not in terror but in recognition of a larger current entering the water.

A rising chimney-back does not hunt in the ordinary sense. Its ascent makes food. Iron-rich water spills from its towers. Microbial blooms form within it. Tiny animals swarm the bloom. Larger animals arrive to eat them. The chimney-back travels through this gathering like a mountain moving beneath weather.

It feeds on the aftermath: the fragments, wastes, molts, dead bodies, and mineral clouds generated by the life that follows it.

There may be only a few of these animals in the whole basin. Or there may be many more below the lightless floor, moving through passages opened by heat and eruption beneath the visible sea. Sometimes, when the water is especially clear and the central dark seems to have depth beyond depth, shapes can be seen turning far below: towers, shells, slow limbs, or perhaps only the reflections of those above.

The great filter-beasts around the vents are their cousins, their young, their discarded stages, or something entirely separate.

No one could decide by looking.

The filter-beasts are anchored, but they are not fixed. Their jointed shell cylinders clasp the rock with hooked lower limbs. Their bodies form rings around warm cracks, each positioned so that the vent water passes through its grilles before reaching open water. They pump in a staggered sequence. One draws, another rests, another expels. The rhythms overlap until the ground vibrates.

Within their shells, soft red organs fold and pulse behind pale bars of bone. Their porous mouths collect mineral particles, microbes, larvae, dissolved compounds, and whatever else the water offers. Their waste is expelled in dense dark strings that smaller creatures immediately claim. Crabs scrape it. worms rise for it. fish strike at it. luminous grazers roll through it, flashing blue as their microbial folds feed.

They have no faces, but they recognize one another.

If a filter-beast is damaged, the ring changes. Neighboring bodies rotate their open grilles toward the wound. Their pumping synchronizes. Soft tissues extend from seams in their armor and join across the gap. For days, perhaps weeks, the ring behaves as a single organism supporting a failing part. If the wounded body recovers, the connections withdraw. If it dies, the others break it down and absorb its shell into their own plates.

The oldest rings are almost indistinguishable from reef formations. Their discarded shells become foundations. Their vent towers grow through them. New bodies anchor upon old ones. A ring that has survived many eruptions becomes a black, breathing architecture half buried in mineral sand.

And in the spaces between all these larger lives are innumerable small ones.

Dust-sized swimmers that shine only when crushed.

Blind larvae that feed on the shadows cast by other animals.

Flecks of metallic skin that detach from the walls and become briefly mobile, carrying spores or eggs or dormant organs inside them.

Tiny cleaners that enter mouths, grilles, wounds, gills, and chimney towers.

Soft-bodied mimics that resemble strips of shed shell until touched, then wrap around the touching limb and drink from it.

Minute shellmakers that collect grains of glass and arrange them into spirals around their bodies, each shell unique because each current brings different material.

There are scavengers specialized for particular deaths: one for bells, one for crabs, one for fish, one for the old plates shed by chimney-backs. Nothing is generic here. The basin is too full for generalists to remain general for long.

Perhaps there are no true solitary creatures.

Even the black predators carry parasites. Even the mineral skins host invisible grazers. Even the falling overflow bears eggs and spores away from the mountain, carrying pieces of this enclosed sea into the valleys below. In wet caves downstream, small descendants may already be living in darkness: pale crusts on rock, transparent larvae in pools, little armored things feeding on iron-stained water. They do not know the high basin, but their bodies remember it.

The mountain’s sea is a crowded beginning.

It is full of animals that have not settled into elegance yet. Their designs are excessive. They grow shells where a membrane might suffice, towers where a gill might do, rings of teeth where a simple mouth would work. They glow, grind, pump, drum, cultivate, molt, leak heat, carry colonies, and make use of one another until no body can be understood as only itself.

And at the center, where the current turns downward and the light becomes a green-black weight, something larger than the visible ecosystem keeps moving.

Not necessarily a single creature.

Possibly the basin itself, alive in the only way such a place can be alive: as a circulation of stone, warmth, water, and appetite that has learned to hold its own shape beneath the sky.
