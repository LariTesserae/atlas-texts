What lives there is not one people, and perhaps never was.

At first there are only the obvious inhabitants: the grazing fish with blunt mouths, the pale shelled processions, the sleeping salamanders in their niches. They belong to the city as moss belongs to a wall. They eat it, polish it, light it accidentally. Their small routines furnish the drowned country with its tireless noise.

But deeper in the old construction live the gardeners.

They are difficult to see because they are arranged like architecture.

One may mistake an arm for a root, a head for a knot of pale bark, a cluster of folded bodies for the buttressed base of a drowned tree. They settle in the hollows beneath stairs, along the inner curves of archways, among the ribbed vegetable walls of the sealed halls. Their skins are not quite skin: gray-green, fibrous, covered in minute fronds that open and close in the current. On their backs grow branching structures that may be gills, leaves, sensory organs, or inherited remnants of another shape of life. In dim light, these branches carry slow lines of green illumination, like the veins of the forest around them.

They do not swim as fish swim. They release themselves from one surface and are taken, gently and deliberately, by the known current. Their limbs trail behind them. Their long fingers open and close upon passing roots. They cross a plaza in an hour, or a day, and arrive exactly where the water intended.

When they need to move against the current, they climb.

Their hands are made for the old masonry. The softened stones, the seams of clay, the living railings of bridges: all have holds for them. They climb staircases that have no end within sight, pausing at intervals to press their faces against the walls. There, perhaps, they listen. Or feed. Or remember.

Their faces have little expression that an air-breathing person would recognize. Their eyes are large but opaque, each filmed with a pearly layer that reflects blue and green light. Below the eyes are several narrow slits that open rhythmically along the throat. Their mouths are small, hidden in a fold of flesh, and seem poorly suited to speech.

Yet they speak constantly.

Their language travels through bone and water. It is made of low pulses from chambers in their chests, clicks produced far back in the skull, and a trembling pressure that can be felt in the teeth from many rooms away. A single gardener making a call is easy to mistake for the city settling. Several of them speaking across a district sound like the distant work of heavy tools.

Their words are not thrown outward into the dark. They are placed against things.

A gardener lays both hands on a column and sends a sequence of knocks through it. Another answers from the far side of a collapsed hall. The message passes through roots, stone, water, and the hollow trunks of the planted forest. They speak to stairways through their steps. They speak to bridges by pulling certain branches. They speak to the flower basins by letting their chest-pulses tremble through the paving.

The city answers, though perhaps only because they have learned how to hear its ordinary sounds.

The gardeners tend nothing in the hurried sense. They do not weed. They do not build walls anew. They do not prune the immense fronds or drive predators from the spawning pools. Their labor is slower and stranger.

They unbraid roots that have begun to close a passage.

They press their palms to a leaning tower for long periods, and afterward the tower remains leaning but no more so.

They collect the shed luminous plates of crustaceans and lodge them in cracks where the dark has become too complete.

They gather dead animals, not to eat them, but to carry them into deep garden chambers where pale flowers wait in stone basins.

They escort certain schools of fish through the old boulevards at intervals that seem ceremonial until one notices that the schools always emerge from the eastern vaults in their original number and shape.

Sometimes they stand in circles around one of the great filter-creatures as it passes. Their green veins brighten. The creature’s amber organs brighten in answer. Then it continues through the avenue, opening and closing its vast mouth, and the city resumes its breathing.

No young gardener is ever seen.

There are small ones, certainly: thumb-sized pale things concealed in root cavities; hand-length swimmers with translucent fins along their ribs; childlike forms sleeping inside the hollow bulbs of the black-leaved trees. But no one sees them become larger. The small ones remain small. The large ones remain large. A gardener with a scar across its shoulder may be seen at the same arch for centuries, the scar unchanged, its branches trailing in the same green configuration.

They may not grow.

They may simply be revealed, one at a time, when some part of the drowned country requires them.

In the highest vaults live another kind.

These are the blind swimmers mistaken at first for birds. They have the fragile outline of birds only because their forelimbs spread into many-jointed fans, and because they travel in flocks through the open water above the terraces. Their bodies are white, nearly transparent. Within them can be seen delicate organs glowing faintly pink or blue. Their heads are smooth and beakless. Their mouths open along the underside of the throat.

They navigate by sounding the stone.

Each one gives a minute click, almost too high to hear. Thousands clicking together make a thin silver haze in the water. From the return of those sounds they know every broken balustrade, every hanging root, every branch of every submerged tree. They know the city not as darkness or light but as an endless changing shape of echoes.

The gardeners listen to them.

When the blind flocks alter their route, gardeners begin appearing in places they have not visited for a very long time. They gather at sealed doors. They line the edges of terraces. They enter tunnels that even the large salamander things avoid. In this way the city may be warned of something, though nothing new is ever discovered. The warning may concern an old danger arriving again exactly as it has always arrived.

At the lowest level, below the visible paving and the gardens of white flowers, something else lives in the foundation water.

It is too large to be understood as an animal from any one glimpse. One might see a soft white ridge rise between blocks; a translucent membrane drawn tight across a drain; a row of dim gold eyes, far apart as windows, closing beneath the silt. The gardeners do not approach these signs. Fish do not cross them. Even the current changes character near them, becoming slower, warmer, and full of a deep mineral taste.

The things below may be the roots of the city.

They may be the city’s first inhabitants, buried while the terraces were made above them. They may be the builders after all: vast, blind, patient bodies that grew stone chambers around themselves and planted forests into the masonry, then filled their world with cold water because water was the one condition in which their thoughts could continue.

Once in a great while, one of them turns.

The movement is not violent. A distant district simply becomes unsettled. Silt rises in a slow wall. The luminous threads in the roots dim, then brighten in patterns not seen elsewhere. Every gardener in the surrounding terraces stops what it is doing and folds itself against stone. The fish go still. The pale birds gather high in the vaults.

Then comes a sound.

It is lower than a call and longer than a bell. It passes through the drowned country from foundation to ceiling. Arches hum. Stairways tremble. The white flowers close their petals for the only time.

When the sound has gone, the water is exactly as cold as before. The current resumes its spiral. The plants continue their unchanging green fire. The gardeners unfold from the walls and return to their quiet work.

And somewhere, in a chamber hidden by roots, a small pale body may open its opaque eyes for the first time—or for the thousandth time, in a world where those are the same event.
