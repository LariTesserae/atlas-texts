The people of the high country do not think of themselves as living *on* the land.

They live in its pauses.

They make use of the brief agreements the wet world offers: a stand of root-bound ground that will bear a platform for twenty years; a basalt shelf where a cooking fire can survive a season; the hollow beneath the joined buttresses of three old trees; a raft of peat that has stopped drifting for the moment. Their villages are not permanent in the way lowland people mean permanence. A settlement may have a name for three generations, then sink by degrees into a reed bed. Its descendants will still speak of it, but not as a lost place. More as an ancestor with a location.

They build lightly because they have learned that heaviness is a kind of argument the country always wins.

Their houses stand on lattices of hardwood, lashed rather than nailed, with roofs steep enough to shed leaffall and rain. Walls are woven from water reed, split bark, and the long flexible roots of canopy vines. The best houses are not sealed. Sealing is for jars, wounds, and graves. A house is meant to breathe, drip, flex, admit the calls of frogs and the slow green smell of the forest. At night, families sleep in suspended nets above the floor, while below them water may move quietly through the stilts.

No one expects a dwelling to remain unchanged. Children are taught how to guide a root away from a sleeping wall, how to cut a drainage channel without angering the peat, how to recognize when a support tree has begun to rot from within. An elder may point to a broad mossy rise and say that her mother was born there, though the rise is now the roofline of a house long absorbed by soil.

Their paths are not roads. They are habits.

Some are sequences of tied branches, painted stones, or seed pods hung in particular trees. Others are remembered through the body: twelve paces along the firmer rootfelt, step across the shallow run, turn where the white fungus grows in a crescent, listen for the pool with the bottle-voiced frogs. A person can lose a path in one night of rain. They can find it again only by knowing what the water will have done.

Travelers carry poles tipped with bone or polished blackwood, not for balance alone but to test the ground before accepting it. They go barefoot or in woven footwraps that can be discarded when sodden. Their clothing is layered loosely: barkcloth, treated leaf fiber, skins from the long quiet grazers, and mantles sewn with oil-rich feathers that shed rain for a while. Nothing stays dry. Their skill lies in not requiring dryness.

They keep fire, but it is never taken for granted.

Cooking fires burn in clay-lined bowls raised on stone or packed root mats. Coals are treasured, carried in double-walled gourds with dry fungus packed around them. In some settlements there are firekeepers, often older people whose hands have become permanently smoke-stained. They tend ember beds through days of rain and teach children the forms of flame: cooking flame, signal flame, funeral flame, biting flame that dries medicinal leaves, and the pale smoky flame used to drive insects from a birthing room.

Fire is treated with affection, but not reverence. It is too temporary for reverence. Water will have it eventually.

The people’s true craft is fermentation.

The country provides abundance, but much of it is bitter, fibrous, poisonous, or prone to rot in the wrong way. They know how to turn hanging fruits into sour drink, broad starchy roots into soft breads, insect larvae into paste, fish into pungent stores wrapped in leaves and buried beneath cool mud. They grow gardens where they can, though a garden is rarely a cleared patch. It is a managed tangle: edible vines encouraged through certain trees, medicinal mosses spread over chosen logs, mushrooms cultivated in shaded baskets, carnivorous cups kept near sleeping places to reduce biting flies.

They cultivate plants that outsiders would overlook. A small blue-veined fern whose curled shoots ease fever. A bitter bulb that keeps leeches from wounds. A floating reed with seeds that swell into a warm porridge. A luminous flower whose nectar, mixed with ash and frog oil, makes a paint visible in the night forest.

That paint is used for many things: marking a safe route through fog, identifying kin at a distance, tracing symbols on the bellies of pregnant people, or drawing temporary maps on wet bark. In the dark months, children decorate one another until they move through the houses like small constellations.

They do not raise many large animals. The six-limbed grazers are too old in their habits and too quiet in their minds to be domesticated in the ordinary way. But some family lines have agreements with particular herds. They leave salt-rich clay at certain crossings. They protect calves from river predators. In return, the grazers tolerate their presence and sometimes lead them through difficult ground. A herd moving through mist can reveal a ridge that people would otherwise miss, or warn of a sleeping giant farther ahead.

The people have many names for the great animals, but few stories in which anyone kills one.

To kill a creature large enough to reshape the forest is dangerous beyond the practical difficulty. Its body would become a place. Water would collect in its tracks. Predators and scavengers would arrive. Trees would root in its ribs. A dead giant alters the paths of generations. If one dies near a settlement, the people move away for a time, leaving offerings of salt, bright shells, and the first fruits of the season at the edges of its growing mound.

They say the greatest ones are not animals in the same sense as deer, fish, or frogs. They are weather that has chosen a body.

Children are told not to call loudly when fog has pooled beneath the canopy. Not because the giants are cruel, but because a loud voice might become part of a dream, and dreams are difficult to remove from something that large.

The high-country people speak softly even when alone. Their language has many words made with breath rather than voice: clicks for nearby water, low hums for direction, a puffed consonant that means both *careful* and *I am here*. Across a forest basin, one person may communicate with another by striking hollow roots, tapping certain standing trunks, or setting frogs calling with an imitation of their voices. The land is full of sound, but most of it is close sound. They have shaped their lives to travel no farther than a leaf’s underside.

Their dead are given to the country slowly.

In the driest places—usually basalt overhangs far above the wettest valleys—the bodies are wrapped in layered bark and placed on open wooden frames. Rain, beetles, birds, and time take them apart. Bones are later gathered, polished in river sand, and made into needles, flutes, fishhooks, handles, and small pale markers for family pools. No one regards this as disrespect. To leave a body sealed away would be to deny it work.

A person is not believed to vanish after death. They become distributed.

Some part may join the water under a house. Some may rise in the fruit of a tree planted from their funeral basket. Some may become the white fungus that appears after a child has been ill. The dead are addressed in practical situations: when repairing an old walkway, when moving through a grandparent’s former garden, when a roof begins to green over. “Make room,” a person might say to the moss. Or, “You have had enough of this wall.”

There are solitary people, too: moss-herders who follow the movements of peat islands; cliff-dwellers who harvest mineral salts from weeping basalt; listeners who spend seasons on the circular timber platforms in the marshes. The listeners are respected and faintly feared. They claim the water beneath the peat has currents no one sees, and that these currents carry memories from one valley to another.

Sometimes they return with warnings: a basin is filling; a herd has changed its route; a new kind of pale flower is spreading upstream; the clouds have begun to linger too low.

No one rules the high country as a whole. The country would make such rule ridiculous. Communities are small, linked by marriage, trade, shared watersheds, and the complicated obligations of old rescue. A village may owe another village three years of smoked fish because their grandparents were pulled from a flood channel. A family may be forbidden from harvesting a particular orchid grove because an ancestor once sheltered there during a fever.

Their wealth is measured less in objects than in continuities: knowledge of a route, the location of a rare dry shelf, a relationship with a herd, the ability to recognize a safe mushroom in rain-darkness, the memory of how a drowned village once stood.

They are difficult to surprise in the forest, but easily surprised by open ground.

Visitors from dry countries often find them patient, though this is not quite true. They are not patient with one another. They can be quick to laugh, quick to argue, quick to take offense. What they are patient with is change. A bridge disappears. A child’s favorite tree falls. A flood brings fish into a former garden and turns a familiar route into a pond. These are not catastrophes unless someone insists they should not have happened.

The high country has taught its inhabitants the difference between loss and transformation.

Above them, in the bromeliad basins and high cloud crowns, other lives may be as settled and strange as their own. Tiny frogs carry their young through private lakes held in leaves. Snails graze on vapor-fed algae without ever encountering soil. In the drowned forests, blind fish move beneath roots older than local histories. Beneath the deepest peat, there may be creatures that have not seen light for generations, feeding on the slow descent of leaves, bones, and houses.

And somewhere in the broad green darkness, the great grazers sleep and turn.

When they do, the people notice. Water changes direction. A line of trees opens where none existed. New ground rises wet and steaming from the moss.

By the next season, someone will have planted something there.
