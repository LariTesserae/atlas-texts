What lives there has learned not to stand apart from the basin.

Nothing in the country can afford the old division between creature and environment: skin against weather, mouth against food, burrow against ground. The mineral water enters everything. The heat enters everything. Life survives by becoming a temporary arrangement in the same circulation that makes the chimneys, the salt collars, the wet black channels.

The smallest inhabitants are not visible as individuals. They live in the colored films on the pools, in the warm undersides of crust plates, in the translucent scum that appears overnight on a seep and is gone by afternoon. They are mats of mineral-feeding things, neither plantlike nor quite animal in their behavior. They take sulfur, iron, copper, dissolved stone, and the faint differences in heat between one side of a crack and the other. They make slicks, threads, waxy veils, and granular skins.

At dawn, when condensation is richest, some of these films rise from the ground in faint, damp folds. They look briefly like gray-green cloth caught on the edges of stones. By midday they have flattened again, absorbed into the mud or shrunk into hard little scales. Their life is rapid in expression but slow in intention. A patch may spread no farther than a hand’s width in a century, continually killed at its outer edge and continually rebuilt from within.

The pools have their own inhabitants.

In the clouded water drift pale motes with black centers, small enough that a cupful may contain thousands. They gather around bubbles and ride them upward, then sink again when the bubble breaks. Their bodies tolerate what would strip ordinary flesh away. Some are armored in crystal grains, each generation incorporating whatever mineral is most available: one season of their lives may be copper-green, another chalk-white, another dull yellow. They feed on the films and on each other. When disturbed, they close into hard beads and settle among the sediment, indistinguishable from grit until the water warms again.

Along the rims of the less hostile pools live things like flattened hands made of translucent flesh. They cling beneath salt plates where the water is cool by comparison, extending many fine tendrils into the seep. When rain dilutes a pool, they emerge in greater numbers and make the rim seem fringed with wet glass. They do not move quickly. They wait for currents. They collect the tiny swimming motes, draw them inward, and leave behind a clean, pale trail on the underside of the crust.

The low-bodied animals seen crossing the flats are called, if anything is called at all, seamers.

They are not numerous because their young do not often survive. A seamer is the color of old salt, though its belly and feet are usually stained rust-red or black from the ground. It has a long low trunk, four broad splayed feet, and a head that seems too small until it lifts it to taste the air. Its nostrils can close against vapor. Its eyelids are clear and mineral-hard. Beneath its pale skin lie dark bands of tissue that store water, salts, and heat in carefully separated chambers.

A seamer does not drink in the usual sense. At a pool it lowers its mouth to the mineral skin and lets the fluid pass slowly through combs of cartilage. It takes what water it can, rejects much of the dissolved burden, and swallows the residue only after it has been altered by organs full of symbiotic films. These films live inside the animal as they live outside it: consuming chemical gradients, making small usable richness from poison and heat.

The seamers feed in the warmed cavities under the ridges. There, on rock faces moist with condensation, grow dense black and green films, fattest near the mouths of fumaroles. A seamer will spend hours in such a cavity, motionless except for the slow working of its jaw. Sometimes several gather around the same vent. They do not fight. Their bodies press together in the warm dark, each taking turns at the best patches of growth. Their nearness is not affection exactly, but an old practical intimacy: shared heat, shared warning, shared access to a place that may be poisonous by morning.

Their young are born soft and dark, almost featureless, and spend their first years underground. Only after their feet have toughened and their internal reservoirs have learned the basin’s balance do they come above the crust. A young seamer is often lost in its first dry crossing. The Floor has no shelter. Rain may fall too far away. A steam vent may change its output by a degree or two, enough to burn the lungs. The basin does not kill with drama. It simply continues operating, and the unadapted cannot remain within it.

There are also smaller ground-runners: quick, jointed creatures that emerge after rain from cracks around the salt domes. Their shells are black and polished, their legs pale at the joints. They move in sudden synchronized bursts, racing toward wet places before the water can disappear. They eat the swollen microbial films and leave behind delicate branching tracks. By noon most have returned underground, carrying bright mineral grains on their backs. In deeper tunnels they pile these grains into chambers, not as stores but as architecture. Their nests are built from salts selected for how they hold warmth and moisture. Some old nests become so large that, after the colony dies or moves on, they are mistaken for natural deposits.

The birds belong to the updrafts more than to the land.

They are dark, narrow-winged, and nearly silent. Their feathers have a greasy mineral sheen, making them look wet even in dry air. They do not range far beyond the basin. Outside it, perhaps, the air is too still; perhaps the ground offers no recognizable odor. Here they circle the hot columns for hours, reading variations in vapor the way other birds might read rivers or forests.

They eat whatever the basin yields: dead seamers, exposed runners, stranded pools of gelatinous life after a brief flood. They also peck at mineral films high on the chimneys, especially where insects gather. Their nests are sparse constructions in dry cavities of the bluffs—salt flakes, shed shell, bits of windblown black fiber. The eggs are coated in a chalky layer that resists the damp fumes. When the chicks hatch, they are pale and nearly naked, with eyes that remain closed until they can withstand the glare from the Floor.

But the oldest inhabitants may be the vents themselves.

Not merely the stone structures, though those are alive in their own geological way. Within some of the larger chimneys, behind layers of mineral deposition, dwell colonies that have no clear boundary. A chimney’s inner throat is lined with living tissue: dark velvet in the hottest sections, pale gelatin in the cooler ones, dense threadlike growth in the places where vapor slows. The colony catches minerals from the rising gases and builds them outward. It regulates the opening by growing over it, dissolving itself, hardening, sloughing away. A vent that seems to whistle through one narrow crack may have been shaped by such life over thousands of years.

At night, when the vapor cools, some chimneys show faint internal color. Not light precisely. More a dim alteration in the darkness: green at one mouth, blue-white at another, a reddish pulse deep in a fissure. The glow is the consequence of chemical work, the slow exchange of energies in a place where pressure, mineral, water, and heat are never entirely at rest.

These colonies do not think in the manner of animals, if thinking means a self moving through time and choosing one direction over another. But they respond. A channel shifting toward a chimney may be diverted by sudden new growth. A crack opening near a colony may become lined, narrowed, and eventually sealed. If rainwater changes the acidity of a pool, the colors of its living film alter within days; the animals that feed there alter their paths; the runners appear or fail to appear; the birds change their circling routes.

The basin has no central keeper. No creature oversees its balance.

And yet all its inhabitants are keepers of it.

The seamers crop the films before they choke the warm cavities. The runners carry salts into the dark and return other minerals to the surface. The pool life transforms what rises from below into forms that can be eaten, incorporated, shed, or buried. The chimney colonies regulate the breath of the stone. The birds take death upward and distribute it along the cliffs. Even the smallest living skins, appearing and vanishing on wet ground, help determine whether a crust holds, cracks, or grows.

Nothing there is permanent in the way a tree is permanent, or a herd, or a human settlement. A seamer dies and becomes food, then sediment, then part of a seep. A microbial bloom hardens into a powdery layer and becomes a ledge. A colony seals a vent, and pressure opens another ten yards away. What lives in the basin is continually being translated into the basin’s other forms.

The country is occupied, then, not by a population laid upon an empty ground, but by a single vast chemistry that has learned innumerable ways to persist.

Its animals are its moving parts. Its films are its membranes. Its pools are its slow organs. Its chimneys are its throats.

And beneath all of them, in the heated dark where water enters stone and stone enters water, something older than any individual creature continues the work: not a master, not a god, not even a mind, but the enduring appetite of the earth for balance—forever dissolving, rebuilding, breathing, and making room for life that knows how to breathe with it.
