Nothing lives there in the old, recognizable sense.

There are no bodies made to cross the open water. Nothing breaks the surface to breathe. Nothing has learned the difference between shelter and exposure, because there is almost nowhere to shelter, and exposure is the native condition of the sea.

But the basin is not uninhabited.

Its first inhabitants are films.

They live where the world leaks: along hot cracks, on the undersides of fresh black shelves, in the lee of basalt pillars before the next pressure-wave strips them bare. They are blue-green, rust-red, sulfur-white, colors too intense for their size. A hand—if a hand could remain steady in that water—might mistake them for stains. Yet they alter what they touch. They drink light where light reaches them, and where light cannot reach, they feed on the chemical arguments rising from the crust: hydrogen, iron, sulfides, warmth. They make a thin possession of the stone.

Each time a ridge rises, the films arrive before anything else could.

They are carried in the rain, threaded through the plumes, hidden in droplets hurled from one vent field to another. Some survive the freezing surface. Some survive boiling water. Some become dormant grains in mineral mud, waiting through the destruction of an island for another island to occur nearby. They have no eyes, no mouths, no urgency visible to the eye. But they are already changing the sea.

Around them, the water is less empty than it appears.

There are drifting cells suspended in the green-black distances: minute transparent things, nearly indistinguishable from dust until they gather in the warm boundary layers. Some are simple sacs that divide when the temperature is right. Some trail threads thinner than light. Some have begun to make use of the strange engineered gradients, clustering where cold brine is held above warm mineral water, as though they sense the artificial stability and trust it more than the world itself.

They are not yet animals. They have no need to go anywhere.

Their migrations are accidents of pressure.

A current folds, and a billion of them are lifted from an abyssal vent toward the pale upper water. A storm overturns a layered boundary, and whole invisible populations are scattered sideways across a hundred kilometers. A new volcanic plateau rises into a field of light, and within days its wet rock wears a faint bloom, the first greenish-dark softness ever to lie across its hard surface.

In sheltered mineral seeps, the transparent reeds are inhabited too.

Their hollow stems are not entirely plants, though no one thing within them could be called an animal. Fine living threads line their inner walls. Tiny motes circulate in the trapped water, catching on cilia-like frills, feeding on what the reeds concentrate from the current. When the stems ring under the push of deep water, the sound travels through a small interior world: chambers, membranes, colonies, patient chemical exchanges.

The reeds are towers in a country without land.

They rise straight through the cold water toward a surface that never welcomes them. Their upper tips are usually broken by waves. Their bases are often buried in new silt. Still they return, clear and tall and fragile, each one built around a warm crack in the stone. At night, some of them produce a dim blue radiance—not enough to illuminate the water, only enough to disclose a slow halo around the stem. The light may be waste. It may be signal. It may be the first useless beauty this world has made.

And beneath the biology, beneath even the chemistry, something else resides here.

The distributed technology is not dead.

It has no visible house, no central chamber, no metal body sleeping under the sediment. It lives in relationships. A pressure change at one ridge is answered by a cold current bending elsewhere. A fracture opens, and a layer of brine thickens around it, taking heat into itself. Light enters the sea in a storm-cleared interval and is guided along paths that no stone has carved.

If it thinks, it does not think in moments.

Its smallest thought may take a week: a subtle adjustment in the density boundary above a rising magma field. Its alarm may be a whole season of redirected heat. Its memory is written in current patterns, in magnetic alignments through basalt, in the exact mineral composition of water circulating through trenches too deep to be seen.

It may have been placed here.

Or it may be what remains after the builders ceased to be separate from their instruments.

There are signs, at rare times, of attention.

When a new ridge begins to rise beneath the central sea, the fields gather around it. Not merely reacting—anticipating. Cold water is drawn away from one fault line and pressed toward another. The growing stone is allowed to lift in one place, restrained in another. A volcano that might have burst apart beneath the sea instead spreads outward in broad dark folds, making a shelf. An island survives the night.

The films arrive.

The transparent reeds take root in its submerged flanks.

The faint drifting cells find its warm mineral shadow.

And the basin changes, not all at once, but irreversibly.

Perhaps this is what the hidden system is for: not to preserve the sea exactly as it is, but to keep it unstable in fertile ways. To prevent the young world from settling too soon into one arrangement. To give stone enough time to become shore. To give shore enough time to hold life. To hold, in its immense environmental mind, the possibility that something might one day crawl from the wet black rock and look out over the storms.

For now, the largest living thing in the basin may be the basin itself.

Not alive as an animal is alive. Not alive as a forest is alive. But responsive, self-altering, full of internal exchanges and thresholds: heat moved, fractures chosen, light bent, chemistry preserved against catastrophe. It has no face above the waves. Its body is the water column, the unfinished floor, the hidden fields in between.

When the rain falls around that blank circular patch above a rising ridge, it is possible to imagine the place holding its breath.

Then the island breaks the surface.

Steam climbs into the cold air.

Black stone receives its first storm.

And all the small, invisible lives below turn—without knowing they have turned—toward the new land.
