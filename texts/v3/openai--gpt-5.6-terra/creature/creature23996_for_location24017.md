What lives on the plateau is not a people who conquered it. It is a people who have learned to remain legible to it.

They call themselves, in most of their languages, some variation of *the carried ones*. Not because they are helpless, nor because they believe the land is kind, but because no dwelling, boundary, body, or memory stays wholly fixed. To live there is to be borne a little way each day by things too slow to be called motion.

They are human, though visitors from lower places sometimes hesitate before using the word.

The plateau people are generally spare, compact, and deep-chested, built less for speed than for keeping warmth and breath. Their skin tends toward the dark, mineral shades of the high country—brown, umber, grey-black—though families that have lived longest near the eastern silver mats are often pale beneath their weathered faces, as if dusted with lichen. Their hair is worn close in childhood, then grown or braided according to household custom: tightly bound against the wind, threaded with copper wire, wool, strips of cured leaf-fiber, or small flat beads cut from old roof metal.

Their eyes are their most remarked-upon feature. Not in color, which varies ordinarily, but in habit. Plateau people look past one another frequently. In conversation, they glance to doorframes, rooflines, horizons, the mouths of drains. A person from elsewhere may mistake this for distraction or rudeness. It is neither. The ground can revise a room while a sentence is being spoken. A ridge that has been in the same place all summer may be elsewhere by supper. Attention is a form of courtesy there: to the person before you, certainly, but also to the world that is presently arranging itself around you.

Children are taught measurements before stories.

Not arithmetic alone, though they are good at that. They learn the angle of shadows, the interval between roof-drips, the sound made by a wall settling normally and the sound made when it has decided to leave. They learn to lay a length of cord across a threshold and remember its slackness. They learn the names of distant rock forms as other children might learn the names of uncles: the Split Tooth, the Bent Shoulder, the Fourfold Seam, the Ridge That Is Not Always There.

Only afterward do they receive the stories—stories of cities lifted and carried, of people who trusted straight roads too long, of a woman who followed her own garden for fifty years after it detached from her house and migrated west, of a bellmaker who heard an answer from beneath his workshop and spent the rest of his life attempting to tune the city to the buried reply.

They have no single ruler across the plateau. The distances are too great, the terrain too unwilling, and any authority that tried to draw a permanent border would be ridiculed as an amateur geometer. Each city is governed by assemblies of households, craft-groups, water-keepers, and ground-watchers. In larger settlements, the watchers hold unusual authority—not because they command armies, but because they are trusted to say when the city must abandon something beloved.

A watcher may be a mason, a midwife, a retired porter, a grower of fungus-stone. It is not a hereditary role. Every few years, candidates are tested: they are shown old horizon drawings, false maps, altered measurements, and asked not simply what has changed, but what can safely be ignored. The best watchers are not the most anxious. They are the ones who understand that a city cannot move for every cracked wall or wandering shadow. Survival depends on distinguishing inconvenience from omen.

There are people whose work is entirely devoted to such distinctions.

The road-readers walk between settlements with bundles of stakes, mirror plates, inked slates, and small hollow stones that ring differently when placed on stable and unstable ground. They travel in pairs or groups, always within some chain of bell-hearing. They mark routes that have opened, erase routes that have turned dangerous, and leave messages in the standardized arrangements of cairns: three black stones atop one pale slab means a pass has narrowed; a cord tied through a split stone means water may be heard but not reached; two crossed lengths of reed indicate that the road ahead has become a memory and should not be trusted.

The dead are not buried deeply on the plateau. Deep ground is regarded as occupied territory.

Bodies are placed in low stone chambers, wrapped in reed cloth and layers of waxed leaves. The chambers are built with loose joints, so that when the earth moves, the dead may move without breaking. A family revisits its chambers after every significant shift. If a grave has traveled, they do not say that the dead have been disturbed. They say the dead have gone ahead.

Names are treated similarly. A person has the name given at birth, the name they acquire through household and work, and often a third name that is changed after a major migration, disaster, or encounter with the strange. These later names are not secret, exactly, but they are used with care. To name someone only by who they were before their city moved is to pretend the land has not altered them.

Their religion, if it can be called that, has no gods in the familiar sense. They do not believe the plateau is a person. They are wary of people who claim it speaks in words. But they believe the land possesses inclination, memory, and a patience beyond morality. It is not angry when it folds a district into a new valley. It is not generous when a hidden spring appears beside a famine-struck town. It is simply continuing.

The sacred act is therefore not obedience. It is correspondence.

A roof should correspond to the prevailing wind. A garden should correspond to the seep-lines beneath it. A promise should correspond to what can actually be carried when the ground changes. A map should show not only where a thing is, but how confidently it can be expected to remain there.

Their maps are beautiful and nearly useless to outsiders.

They are made on stitched hide, bark paper, hammered sheets of soft copper, and long woven bands. A map may show a city as a cluster of hearth symbols tied to moveable roads. Valleys are indicated by layers of translucent wash, each one representing a different decade. Some maps include uncertainties as carefully as certainties: a ridge drawn in dotted charcoal, a lake represented by a blank oval, a settlement marked with the phrase *heard by bell, not seen*. The oldest maps are revised rather than replaced. Generations draw over generations until a route becomes a dense dark braid of former possibilities.

The people preserve few monuments. A monument invites the land to contradict it.

Instead they build objects meant to travel: household hearthstones with carrying holes cut into their sides; door lintels engraved with the migrations of a family; public weighing beams, market tables, bell frames, and archive chests designed to be dismantled quickly. Their greatest civic treasures are not statues but methods: the correct proportion of moss-root to ash for preserving food, the order in which a neighborhood should be unbuilt, the songs that keep a work crew lifting at the same rhythm, the old mathematics of bell intervals.

They sing often, though quietly.

Plateau songs are built for communal work and long walking. Many have no fixed ending; the final line depends on the place where the singer happens to stop. Some songs are deliberately altered every year so they do not become too attached to a landscape that will not remain. There are lullabies that teach infants the sounds of weather. There are roof-repair chants whose meter gives instructions for lashing supports. There are mourning songs in which the names of the dead are placed not in verses but in pauses.

Their music has an unusual relation to silence. A person from the cities of the lowlands might think a gathering has ended when the instruments cease. On the plateau, the silence afterward may be the central part. People sit around the hearth, listening to wood cool, rain move through the roof layers, stone settle below the floor. Then someone may say, very softly, “The wall agrees,” and the gathering is complete.

They are not without conflict. A city that has found a reliable seep may refuse water to a neighbor. A migrating district may cross land claimed by an older settlement. Families argue over whether a shift was dangerous enough to justify leaving ancestral halls behind. There are thefts, feuds, hoarded stores, false measurements, and those who manipulate fear for authority. The plateau does not make them wise. It makes the consequences of foolishness difficult to hide.

Some people leave.

They descend through the wet stone belts to lower valleys where animals exist in great numbers and the ground remains where it is placed. Those who return often do so after years away. They bring stories of horses, market crowds, riverboats, bright cloth, orchards heavy with fruit, children who have never been taught to identify a moving ridge. They are received with warmth, curiosity, and a little pity. Most find the lowlands unbearably loud. They say the earth there feels asleep in a bad way.

Others go east.

No city approves of this exactly, but neither do they forbid it. There are always a few who become fascinated by the Still Basin: mapmakers whose drawings grow ever more detailed around its blankness; bereaved parents who have seen a familiar gesture among the reflected figures; young people who believe the basin’s other country must be reachable if only one enters at the right hour, carrying the right thing, or having relinquished the right name.

These travelers are called basinward.

They wear no distinctive clothes, though many cut their hair before leaving, or carry no metal. Some walk only to the shore and return. Some become attendants to the annual delegations, learning the rituals of placement: seeds in the eastern rows, broken tools in the west, names closest to the black surface, maps weighted against the wind. Some remain in the settlements near the basin and grow old looking at a country that may not exist.

A few step out onto the stone after dark and do not come back.

There are also those who come back wrong, though the plateau people do not use that word. They return with a knowledge that cannot be fitted neatly into civic records. A woman may return speaking an unfamiliar dialect but knowing the names of every child born in her city during her absence. A man may bring a branch of white wood that continues to drip clear water for years. Once, an old basinward walker returned carrying a small brass key. No lock on the plateau accepted it, but every bell in three cities rang when it was placed on a table.

Such people are not made priests. They are given homes, food, and the right not to explain themselves.

The other living things of the plateau are not merely background to human life. The low forests of reed-trunks are cultivated, harvested, and apologized to. The moss cushions are combed rather than cut. Fungal stones are kept in family courtyards, watered with saved condensation, and inherited along with tools and names. Some plants are known to respond to certain tones; others curl closed when a nearby seam in the earth is beginning to shift. In many settlements, gardens are watched as closely as horizons. A sudden turn in the leaf-fields may be enough to delay a wedding, move a market, or wake an entire district.

The people do not believe the plants are animals, nor do they pretend they are human. But they know the plants are listening to conditions the people cannot yet perceive. This is enough to warrant respect.

And beneath all of this—beneath streets, reservoirs, old houses incorporated into stone, beneath the careful routes and moving cities—something may live that no one has properly named.

The bells answer too quickly sometimes. Buried water changes course before the land above it has moved. In the oldest ruins, the sounds of former household life occasionally change in response to a listener. A person pauses at a vanished doorway; the faint scrape within pauses too. They knock on bare rock; after a long interval, something knocks back with the exact rhythm, except for one extra beat.

The plateau people do not build a doctrine around this.

They leave a bowl of water near certain seams. They do not sleep in rooms where the old sounds become too clear. They teach children never to answer a voice coming from below unless it knows the name they will have tomorrow.

So what lives there is a woven thing: human settlements, patient plants, water under green skins, old cities retained in stone, and perhaps the immense slow intelligence of a landscape that has not decided whether it is alive.

At dusk, a traveler approaching one of the cities might see little at first: low roofs, smoke pressed nearly flat by wind, dark figures carrying reed bundles between walls. Then the details would emerge. Someone shifts a boundary marker by the width of a hand. A child lies face-down on the pavement, listening to the ground. An elder lifts a kettle from a shared fire. Along the terraces, leaf-blades fold in sequence as cold rain begins to sweep over the plain.

And from a tower too short to challenge the sky, a bell is struck once.

Far away, beyond the curve of ridges and weather, something answers.
