What lives in the low country is not one people, but a set of arrangements that have learned to wear one another’s shapes.

Closest to the streets are the **tenders**: the lamp-box carriers, the hook-bearers, the people one might mistake for the original inhabitants if one saw them only briefly. They are small in number and nearly indistinguishable at a distance. Their coats are made from old sailcloth rubbed with grease and ash. Their boots are wrapped in strips of hide, not for warmth but to soften their steps on hollow paving. They keep their faces hooded against vapor and dripping lime. Many wear little plugs of wax in their ears, leaving only a narrow channel open, because the pipes can make a listener sick if heard too clearly.

They are not born in houses. They are found in them.

A tender is discovered, usually as a child, sleeping in a dry niche behind a boiler wall or beneath a stair that did not exist the previous week. No one asks from where. The adults bring the child a lamp, a hook, a cup, and a length of cord. If the child can recognize the three-part boom of the reservoir gates before the next turning of the water cycle, it is allowed to stay.

They have no names in the ordinary sense. A tender is called by the work they last completed, or the place from which they returned.

*South Sluice.*
*Broken Ladder.*
*Salt Vault.*
*The One Who Heard the Wall.*

These titles change. Some people have had so many that their companions speak to them only in taps.

The tenders do not believe that they maintain the country. Such pride would be dangerous. They believe they negotiate with it.

A leaking roof is not repaired, but persuaded to direct its water elsewhere. A jammed gate is not forced, but reminded of its counterweight. When new masonry blocks a familiar route, the tenders mark the wall with soot, leave a small bowl of clean water at its base, and return later to see whether it has grown an opening. It sometimes has. More often it has not, and the bowl has been incorporated into the brick, its rim visible beneath a smooth new course.

The tenders are afraid of open doors.

Not doors in walls—those are common enough, and most lead to workshops, shafts, chambers of old tools, or stairwells smelling of warm clay. They fear doors that appear where there was no wall before: upright rectangles of darkness, usually framed in wet, unseasoned brick. Behind them there is rarely a room. There may be a downward wind. There may be the sound of somebody breathing through water.

In such cases, the tenders do not investigate. They build a smaller wall in front of the door, leaving a narrow slit at ankle height. Through that slit, once each day, they slide a brick.

Nobody remembers who began this practice. Nobody has stopped.

There are also the **conduit animals**, though the tenders insist they are not animals and do not like them called such. The small pale jointed things in the pipes are known as clinker-mites, or sometimes simply *the counters*. Each has a body like several thumb-sized stones hinged together beneath a translucent shell. They swarm toward warmth, vibration, and mineral deposits. In dry conduits they sleep in heaps, fitted shell-to-shell so tightly that they resemble slag. When steam begins to leak, they wake and travel toward it in a whispering stream.

Their ticking is useful.

A regular tick means mineral scale.
A hurried tick means heat.
No tick at all means the pipe may be occupied.

The tenders sometimes collect the shed shells of the counters and sew them into gloves, kneepads, and the soles of climbing shoes. The material is harder than fired clay but light enough to float in oil. A person wearing counter-shell gloves can put a hand against a hot pipe for a few seconds longer than should be possible.

The long creatures in the reservoirs have a more disputed status. They are called **grippers**, though none have ever been caught and examined. The name comes from the marks they leave: four or five shallow crescents pressed into masonry at the waterline. They are pale below the surface and black above it, their skins seeming to take on the color of whatever water conceals them. They rise only when something falls in.

Not when it splashes.

When it falls.

A dropped tool may vanish before it reaches the bottom. A brick tossed deliberately will float, bobbing in the blackness for hours. The grippers understand accident, or hunger, or perhaps the distinction between an object offered and an object lost.

There are old stories of tenders who descended into drained cisterns and found grippers lying in the mud, folded into impossible shapes, their forelimbs wrapped around old foundation stones as if embracing them. The stories say the creatures are not native to the water. They are trying to climb out of it, very slowly, through centuries of stonework.

Above, in the fog, live the **roof-beasts**.

No one has seen one land.

They are heard passing over tiles and cable lines: a leathery drag, the brief clatter of hooked feet, the settling groan of a roof taking weight. Their wings do not beat often because they are too broad and too stiff; they catch rising drafts of kiln heat and vapor from the reservoirs, moving from district to district on the breath of the city. In rare lightning, their silhouettes have been seen: long bodies, folded wings, heads lowered beneath a bony hood.

They have no visible eyes either.

The tenders leave no food for them. Yet roof-beasts linger over districts where someone has recently died. They circle above the lamp cables, silent except for the soft, repeated clicking of their jaws. By morning, the dead are usually gone—not taken from their beds, but absent in a more careful way. Blankets remain folded around a hollow. A boot remains standing beneath a chair. A lamp-box still burns with no hand near it.

There are things the roof-beasts will not approach.

They will not cross above the newest walls.

And they will not fly over the open basins when the deep three-part boom begins.

Those who live longest in the low country become less like ordinary people. This is not considered a curse. It is weathering.

Their skin takes on the gray-yellow tone of old wax. Their fingertips flatten and harden from handling wet rope. Their teeth grow dark at the edges from mineral water. Their sense of direction changes: they cease to navigate by sight and begin to recognize places by pressure, echo, temperature, and the taste of the air. An old tender can enter a darkened passage and say, from the coldness against their cheek, that there is a cistern two walls away, half-full and rising.

Some can hear the bricks settling.

Some can tell new brick from old by smell.

A few, very few, can speak to the pipes.

This does not mean words pass through the pipes. It means that a person can stand beside a copper line, press their mouth to it, and make a sound so low and controlled that the sound travels into the buried network. Hours later, a gate may lift. A pressure valve may sigh open. Or, in another district, someone may hear the note emerge from a wall and understand that help is coming.

The strongest pipe-speakers are treated with a kind of wary reverence. Their throats are scarred and their chests often malformed by the effort. They do not live long. When they die, their bodies are not buried—there is nowhere to bury anything—but placed in unused sections of large clay pipe, along with a lamp and a bowl of water. The pipe is capped at both ends and marked with three taps.

In later years, people sometimes hear a familiar voice in the pipe-song.

Not speaking.

Just holding one note against the enormous pressure below.

And below the tenders, below the grippers and the warm conduits and the old drowned works, there are the things for which the valley was built.

They are not often called creatures. To call them creatures suggests they have boundaries.

The oldest tenders call them **the Underpressure**, or **the Kept**. Others call them simply **the Below**, as one might call a sea.

They live in the foundation-city: among the immense hollow bricks, in water so cold that metal breaks in it. They do not rise through tunnels in a body. They rise as changes in the constructed world. A new wall where no wall stood. A staircase ending at an unmarked door. A reservoir emptying upward. Fresh brick steaming in the rain.

The country is continually adding weight to itself because weight is what keeps the Below quiet.

Every wall is a hand pressed down.

Every cistern is a sealed eye.

Every channel is part of a pattern too large to be seen from the streets.

The tenders know this, though not in a form they would say aloud. Their work is not merely repair. Their small mallets, their ropes, their bowls beneath drips, their soot marks and cautions and patient brickwork: all are part of the pressure.

They keep the water moving in the right directions.

They listen to the pipes.

They build small walls before new doors.

And when the great deep boom comes—not the ordinary three-part warning, but the one heard perhaps once in a lifetime, a sound so low that lamps tremble and black water rises in its bowls—they gather in the open basins with their lamp-boxes shuttered.

Then, in the dark, every tender lays one hand against the nearest wall.

They wait to feel whether it is still holding.
