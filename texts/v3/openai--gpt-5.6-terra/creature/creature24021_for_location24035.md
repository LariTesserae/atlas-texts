What lives in the basin is not numerous enough to call itself a population without qualification.

There are perhaps several thousand people in the whole city, perhaps fewer. No census is trusted for long. Names are recorded in maintenance ledgers, school rosters, ration allotments, pressure-shift casualty reports; each list gives a different total. People leave for the salt flats and do not return. Others arrive in sealed rail cars from the far side of the pass, pale with travel and unwilling to explain where they came from. Some are born in the upper housing towers and spend their first years believing the brown-violet roof of cloud is the whole sky.

They call themselves, in the old municipal usage, basiners. In private they use district names instead: Court people, Stack people, East Retainers, those of the Wet Rail. Their identities follow water routes more faithfully than streets. A family may live in the same apartment block for generations and still be considered foreign to the next tower over if its condensate comes from a different branch of the system.

They are a narrow-bodied people, not by design but by the accumulated preferences of the place. Broad shoulders catch rain inside capes and make work difficult in pipe galleries. Heavy bodies overheat. The old children’s games reward stillness, balance, and the ability to judge a hot surface through the soles of a boot. Their skin tends toward mineral dryness despite the wetness of the air: pale cracking at knuckles and eyelids, dark glossy patches where constant vapor has changed it. Many have fine rust-colored freckles across their cheeks and throats. Their hair is usually worn close to the head or braided tight beneath hoods, because loose hair acquires a metallic smell and never properly dries.

Their eyes are adapted less in anatomy than in habit. They look downward. They notice the thin differences in shine that distinguish safe pavement from a newly heated slab, ordinary runoff from condenser overflow, a shallow mirror court from a deep one whose black surface has hidden its edge. They can tell, from a tremor in a railing or a change in steam, that a pump has changed phase three streets away. Children learn to read water before they learn maps.

Speech is subdued, but not absent.

In enclosed kitchens, stairwells, maintenance rooms, and the dry rooms of schools, basiners can be loud. They laugh hard. They sing in low, overlapping rounds while sorting mineral cloth or scraping fungal growth from heat plates. They tell stories with a particular fondness for repetition: the same worker walks into the same tunnel three times in three different years; the same lost voice returns from the retaining wall with new instructions each time. Their humor is practical and fatalistic. A person who falls through a maintenance grate is said to have been “promoted to the lower weather.”

Outdoors, conversation becomes gestural. A tap to the breastbone means *I am here.* Two fingers pointed down mean *watch the ground.* An open hand held low and waved slowly means *do not wake the water.* Lovers, when they walk together, often communicate by the pull and release of gloved hands inside the folds of a shared cape.

Their clothing is made for sheen rather than comfort. Reflective capes, hooded work coats, rubberized leggings, layered soles, respirators with ceramic filters. The older garments are patched with mirror-bright strips cut from insulation blankets and obsolete signal fabric. In rain, a group of workers moving across an open court may flash briefly like fish turning beneath water. At home, people favor softness: felted wraps grown from the pale wall-colonies, quilts heavy with mineral wool, slippers with cork-thick bases. Rooms are painted in colors rarely visible outside—amber, coral, faded blue, a green imported as pigment and treated almost reverently.

The city has professions that would seem ceremonial elsewhere.

There are gatekeepers who do little but watch the condensate gates and clear them by hand when the automated teeth jam with mineral growth. There are heat listeners, usually older workers, who kneel beside grates with one palm hovering over the metal and diagnose the underground flow by its pulse. There are reflection wardens assigned to the mirror courts at third siren. They carry poles marked in white bands and never explain what they have seen. There are wall climbers who inspect the basin’s red ribs for fresh seepage, descending through vapor on cables until they become indistinguishable from the stains of runoff.

There are also rainkeepers.

This is not an official title, though everyone knows what it means. Rainkeepers oversee the small acts of receiving: the cleaning of rooftop bowls, the repair of collection lips, the tending of filters, the unclogging of public channels. They argue over rain as farmers might argue over soil. Too much warmth in it, too much iron, a wrong smell from the west, a sluggishness in the first drops. Their records are old and meticulous. Some families possess rain ledgers extending back centuries, pages corrugated by damp and written in inks that have turned green-black.

The city’s children are not sheltered from its machinery. They are taught which tunnels are forbidden, but also how to identify a healthy pump, how to find an emergency dry shelf in a flooding stairwell, how to lie flat and breathe through cloth if mineral mist thickens. At a certain age they are taken to a controlled service level and shown the white culvert creatures moving through the steam. The lesson is not that the creatures are dangerous. It is that they belong there, and the children do not.

The culvert things are among the basin’s oldest residents.

They are called lacebacks, threadlings, drain saints, depending on district. Their translucent limbs tremble constantly in the warm flow. They gather around leak points and mineral deposits, feeding with slow, almost delicate industry. Their bodies shine white in lamplight, then vanish against steam. Workers do not kill them except when they clog a valve in numbers too great to manage. Even then, the remains are collected and rendered into a chalky sealant used around joints and vents. It is considered poor luck to waste them.

Far larger lives may exist below.

No one says this directly in official reports. The underground system is too old, too extensive, and too altered by heat for anyone to claim full knowledge of it. The deep reservoirs produce noises that are attributed to settling plates, fluid pressure, cavitation, thermal expansion. Yet certain maintenance crews refuse particular shifts. Some workers return from the lowest service levels with their boots coated in an oily black residue that does not match any known deposit. There are records of slow impacts beneath mirror courts: not explosions, not quakes, but a single deep pressure applied from below and then withdrawn.

The people who service the deepest conduits wear polished masks without eyeholes. They are called the blind crews, though they can see perfectly well through internal lenses. Their masks are meant to prevent recognition, or so the children say. Adults insist they are protection against glare.

The broad-winged things above the rim are treated with greater seriousness than animals usually receive.

They are not named in the casual way. One does not say, “A bird is circling.” One says, “There is a watcher.” Their arrival empties roof decks and pauses work on exposed gantries. Some people leave shallow bowls of warm condensate on upper platforms, though no one has seen a watcher drink. Their black wings catch no light. Their motion is too smooth, as if they are held up by the heat rising from the basin rather than moving through air. When one turns its unreadable face toward the city, even experienced workers become quiet.

There is a belief—not universally held—that the watchers are the city’s original surveyors, still returning to inspect the work.

The pale colonies on walls and pavement are not regarded as vegetation, though they are tended. People cultivate particular strains in shaded chambers. Some produce insulating mats. Some purify water. Some are used in medicine, pressed into wounds or steeped in bitter teas for lung sickness. One dark velvet species grows only near failing heat exchangers and is therefore valuable as a warning sign. Another, sulfur-yellow and thread-fine, is used to make a paste that glows faintly when spread around leaks.

A few householders keep colonies in ceramic trays by their doors. They speak to them while trimming them, not sentimentally but as one speaks to a boiler or an old animal: with respect for something that must be understood without ever quite becoming familiar.

Among the people, there are those who become less comfortable in ordinary rooms and more at ease in the infrastructure itself. They sleep in pump stations during pressure season, or take meals sitting on warm pipe casings, or volunteer for every night shift until no one can remember where they officially reside. These are often the ones who know the city best. They can find a route through closed service levels by smell. They know which elevator shafts carry cool air, which towers have secret dry landings, which abandoned apartments still receive heat from the central works.

Some eventually disappear into maintenance.

Their tools remain signed out. Their names remain on shift boards. Their lamps are found, once in a while, set carefully beside an access hatch. No funeral is held until a body is found, and bodies are rarely found. Instead, a person’s household receives their filter allotment, their work boots, and a small sealed vial of water from the district reservoir. The vial is kept near a window until its contents evaporate.

At night, the inhabitants emerge into the steam with more of themselves visible.

They visit one another by elevated tubes and warm corridors. They gather in boiler rooms where music can be played loudly enough to be felt through the floor. They trade repaired machine parts, preserved food, old recordings, fragments of dry-world goods brought through the pass: paper books, seeds that will never sprout, photographs of forests. They hold celebrations in the upper floors of towers, above the worst of the vapor, where the windows sometimes show a faint suggestion of stars through breaks in the cloud.

The city’s dead are not buried in the hot ground. There is no suitable earth, and no one wishes to disturb the water channels. The dead are placed in ceramic capsules and installed in the retaining walls, behind small dark plaques. Over time mineral rain polishes the names away. Families visit anyway, touching the blank stone, listening for the faint movement of water behind it.

What lives there, finally, is a people who have made an ethic of maintenance.

They do not expect rescue from the exhausted sky. They do not imagine abundance as a reasonable future. Their greatest public virtue is not courage, though courage is necessary, nor ingenuity, though they possess plenty of it. It is attentiveness: clearing what must remain clear; noticing a changed sound; leaving a gate unblocked; turning east when the ground warms; refusing the beauty of a reflected light when the siren says to leave.

And beneath them, within the wet mineral body of the basin, something older than the city continues to circulate—blind things, heat, pressure, memory, perhaps a life too large and slow to recognize the people above as anything more than a temporary pattern of warmth on its skin.
