What lives in the basin is not one thing.

The people will tell you they live there, and this is true in the ordinary sense. They cook above the floodline. They mend pumps. They sleep behind shutters. They raise children in rooms with tiled thresholds and hooks for hanging damp shoes. They carry groceries over plank walks. They know which bridges flex in hot weather and which drains make a sucking sound before the water begins to rise.

But they do not speak of themselves as the town’s only inhabitants.

There are the **keepers**, first of all: the visible inhabitants, the ones with names recorded in the flood office and mail collected from a rack above the old post counter. They are few, and they seem fewer because they do not spend much time outdoors. Each household keeps its own hours. A light may burn in an upper room for three days, then not appear again for a week. Someone may be heard running a grinder at two in the morning, or singing in a language that seems to have too many vowels for the humid air to carry cleanly.

The keepers tend to be narrow in the shoulders and heavy in the wrists and ankles, as though the basin has taught their bodies where stability matters. Their skin shines easily in the heat. Their hair is cut short or kept wrapped beneath cloth, not for fashion but because nothing dries properly here, and damp hair invites a kind of headache peculiar to the town. Most wear rubber boots even indoors, though older residents prefer sandals made from conveyor belt. Their clothes are patched at the knees, cuffs, and underarms. Metal rusts against them. Fabric mildews. Nothing remains new long enough to become a statement.

They speak softly inside buildings, not out of politeness but because pipes run through every wall. A raised voice can travel into a neighboring house through an old steam line, emerge from an uncapped drain, or disturb the thin membranes in the lower foundations. The town has learned this through consequences no one bothers to explain to strangers.

Children are taught early not to ask what is in the water.

They are taught procedures instead.

If water is still after dark, do not touch it.

If it moves against the slope, go indoors.

If it begins to make rings without rain, find a raised surface and wait.

If a reflection raises its hand before you do, leave by the nearest dry route and do not look down again.

The children make games out of these rules because children make games out of anything. They race one another along the dry crowns of streets. They dare each other to identify pumps by vibration alone. They toss bottle caps into storm drains and listen to hear whether the cap lands in ordinary water, pipewater, or the deeper kind. They have names for all the sounds adults dismiss: the knock beneath the old bakery, the wheezing of the submerged warehouse, the long glassy scrape that sometimes crosses the flooded freight square before the crane turns.

Few children remain when they are grown. Some leave on maintenance barges or service trains that arrive irregularly at the sunken rails. Some go to higher towns and become difficult to recognize when they return: pale with cold, unused to wet air, careless around drains. A few come back after years away because the high country makes them feel exposed. They say the sky is too large there. They say they cannot sleep without hearing a pump below them.

The keepers believe that the basin changes a person’s sense of where a body ends.

In drier places, a person may imagine themselves separate: skin, clothing, house, street, weather. Here, those boundaries soften. Sweat joins rain. Rain joins runoff. Runoff enters culverts, culverts enter the canal, and the canal eventually touches every foundation in town through old channels nobody has fully mapped. The air enters the lungs warm and already breathed by engines, boilers, kitchens, moss, sleeping rooms, flooded cellars. A person is never alone with their own moisture for very long.

This may be why the residents are patient with one another. They understand leakage.

They also understand that something beneath the streets is working alongside them.

Not beneath every street. Not even beneath the deepest water. It is more precise than that.

It lives in the seams.

Where one foundation meets another. Where a drainpipe has been joined to an older drainpipe by a collar of cracked concrete. Where the poured slag of a street gives way to shell fill, and shell fill rests on peat. It occupies the layers that people call infrastructure because “infrastructure” is a useful word for things one depends on without wishing to imagine.

The old people call it the **underflow**.

The flood office calls it “unregulated sub-basin movement,” though no report uses that phrase without becoming vague soon afterward.

Children call it the **other town**.

It is not water exactly. Water is its preferred means of expression. It moves through water because water remembers shape better than soil does. It gathers in stairwells, old lift shafts, pipe trenches, cisterns, elevator pits, loading bays. It enters a puddle and gives it depth disproportionate to its size. A saucer-wide pool beside a curb may reflect not clouds but the underside of a roof many meters below. A black rectangle in a vacant lot may contain the wavering lights of streets that have not existed for fifty years.

The underflow does not speak, though it can repeat.

It learns sounds that pass through its channels. A dropped wrench. A train horn from another decade. The three-note whistle of a kettle. The cough of someone long dead. It can return these sounds from drains and water pipes at hours when no one is prepared to hear them. Sometimes it returns a voice with one word altered. Sometimes it returns only the pauses.

The keepers do not answer voices from plumbing.

That is another rule, rarely posted because anyone born in the basin knows it before they can read.

The underflow seems especially interested in weight. It notices when something heavy is set down. It notices when a building settles, when a truck crosses a bridge, when a person stands for too long in a place where the ground is thin. This is why the signs advise standing on metal or stone when the ground vibrates. Metal and stone have their own resonance. They state their boundaries clearly. Wood, mud, rubber, flesh: these are more easily misunderstood.

No one agrees whether the underflow is hungry.

There are no animal bones found in the basin, not in drains, not in dredged mud, not in the stomachs of fish because there are no fish. This is one of the facts that make outsiders uncomfortable. There should be birds. There should be mosquitoes dense enough to cloud the lamps. There should be rats in the boiler works, beetles in the fruit, frogs in every ditch. But the town has none of these.

The first explanation given is poison.

The second is heat.

The third, spoken only by people who have lived there all their lives, is that animals know when a place has already been occupied.

Humans are different. Humans will occupy a place simply because there is a job there, or a room, or a family obligation, or a debt, or a train connection. Humans can be convinced that a warning sign is merely a warning sign. They can build an entire municipal culture around accommodation.

And perhaps the underflow accommodates them in return.

The pumps are allowed to work.

The foundations are allowed to hold.

The canal gates are allowed to open.

The water rises, but rarely all at once. It takes rooms by degrees. A cellar becomes a storage pond; a ground floor becomes a workshop raised on platforms; an old house becomes pilings with a new house balanced on top of it. The town loses nothing decisively. It merely gives things over in stages.

There are residents who insist this is evidence of mercy.

Others say it is evidence of cultivation.

The crane in the freight square belongs to neither the keepers nor the underflow, exactly. It is older than the present town charter and newer than the deepest water. Its base descends below the square into a foundation that has never been inspected. The machinery is disconnected from the grid, but it turns every evening as reliably as a prayer wheel. The crane’s hook hangs over the water, sometimes empty, sometimes bearing strands of weed, strips of cloth, or a single pale fruit swollen nearly transparent from soaking.

Once every few years, it lifts something.

No one waits to see what.

At the first groan of the gears, shutters close. Walkways clear. The pump crews go quiet. Even the generator seems to find a lower rhythm. By morning the hook is empty again, and the freight square has acquired some small alteration: a new ripple in the paving, a missing rail, an unfamiliar door visible below the waterline.

The town accepts these revisions.

It has always accepted revisions.

There is one more presence, perhaps the strangest because it may be no more than the accumulated behavior of everything else.

At night, when the lamps come on and the basin fills with reflections, the submerged town below the town becomes active.

Its windows brighten where there are no buildings above. Its alleys darken beneath dry streets. Sometimes a reflected figure moves across the water in a place where no person walks. It is never clear whether these are the reflections of the keepers delayed by a few seconds, or the gestures of something below attempting to keep pace with them.

The residents do not call these ghosts.

A ghost, they say, is someone who has left.

These things have not left. They have remained at the level where the town first began: among drowned foundations, old rails, buried machines, compacted shell, peat, warm black water, and the slow pressure rising from beneath.

So the basin is inhabited by people, certainly.

By their houses, which sweat and settle and remember old doorways.

By pumps, which work with the solemnity of hearts.

By a crane that searches each evening for freight no one recalls receiving.

By the underflow, which is patient, imitative, and never entirely asleep.

And by the reflected settlement below, where all the lost rooms remain lit in the water, waiting perhaps for the upper town to become complete enough to join it.
