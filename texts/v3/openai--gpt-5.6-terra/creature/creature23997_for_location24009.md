What lives in the drowned high country is not numerous, and not hurried.

At first it seems that almost nothing lives there at all. The basin has the self-contained silence of a place too recently made, where the water has arrived before the creatures adapted to it. A visitor sees roots, leaves, drowned trunks, the brief white flick of fish. They hear rain, the private waterfalls, the occasional shifting knock from beneath. They may decide the mountains have flooded an empty world.

But the basin is inhabited in layers, and the largest inhabitants are easiest to mistake for parts of it.

There are fish in the root forests, though they do not travel in the bright, restless schools of rivers. They are leaf-shaped and green-shadowed, wide through the body and nearly transparent at the edges. When still, they resemble a leaf caught under a branch. When they turn, the dark water passes visibly through their fins. Their young live inside the curled cups of submerged ferns, feeding on the pale film that grows there; adults descend along the drowned slopes at evening, moving from root shadow to root shadow in deliberate groups of three or four.

Some have evolved—or arrived—without eyes. In the lower ravines, where daylight is only a memory filtered through green water, they feel the basin with long, mobile whiskers. They touch rock, wood, sediment, the flanks of sleeping eels. Their skulls are thin enough that the little pressure-changes made by settling stone can pass through them. They know a landslide before the water clouds. They leave the ravine walls hours beforehand, gathering in root tangles or rising into the open dark, where they hang nearly motionless until the pale sediment begins to bloom below.

The pale eels are older-looking than anything else in the water. They are not truly pale, but the color of wet roots, old bone, and moonlight under leaves. They live in holes where the young mountains have not yet closed their fractures: long cracks beneath the basin, flooded chimneys, collapsed caves filled with mud and drowned wood. Some are small enough to curl in a hand. Others are seen only in portions—a pale loop beneath a shelf, a length of body vanishing between two trunks, a disturbance in the hanging weeds large enough to suggest there was more of it than the eye received.

They do not hunt in any visible way. A dead leaf drifting too low may vanish. A wounded fish may be found resting against a root with a pale eel beside it, both still as branches. In the morning there will be no fish, no eel, only a loosened cloud of silt.

The frogs on the islands are the basin’s brightest things. They are no larger than a thumb joint and occupy the world above the waterline as if it were a separate country. Their colors—yellow, lacquer-red, blue with black rain-speckles—are vivid in the dim forest, but they are almost never seen on open leaves. They live in leaf cups, bromeliad wells, bark folds full of rainwater. Each tiny pool holds its own small weather: larvae, mosquito-thin predators, floating pollen, a skin of reflected sky. The frogs call rarely. Their voices are too soft to cross the open water, more a ticking in wet vegetation than a song.

At night, after a warm rain, they answer one another from separate trees. The calls do not form a chorus. They pass around the islands in pauses, as though each frog is waiting to hear what the water has remembered.

There are insects everywhere, although the air gives little sign of them. The water has claimed many of their forms. Small black beetles live under floating leaves and carry pockets of air like silver beads beneath their bodies. Delicate larvae graze the green threads on drowned branches. Long-legged skaters patrol the still coves, their feet dimpling the surface without breaking it. Where pollen gathers in the narrow passes, translucent things rise through the gold haze: winged adults that live for only a few hours above water before sinking back to it in mating pairs.

The basin has moths too, but they are creatures of mist rather than darkness. They emerge at the first hour after sunset, when the surface is neither fully water nor fully sky. Their wings are dull brown, gray, and algae-green, patterned like bark seen through rain. They gather around the mouths of ravines, not drawn to light but to the cool breath rising from the submerged clefts. By dawn they are gone into crevices in the rock, folding themselves so precisely into the stone that they seem to have become mineral.

In the flooded passes live the glasslings.

No one standing above the water sees them first. They are visible only from within the narrow channels, when sunlight enters between the leaning walls and becomes a column. Then the water appears full of them: countless soft, clear bodies drifting up and down in the beam, each no larger than a grain of rice, each bearing a small dark knot at its center. They are animals, though it takes time to believe it. They have no urgent movement. They expand and contract almost invisibly, rising on warmth and sinking as the water cools. When disturbed, they close around themselves and become nearly impossible to distinguish from suspended bubbles.

The glasslings feed on spores, pollen, and the finest pieces of dead leaf. In turn, almost nothing feeds on them. They are too slight, too flavorless, too dispersed. They simply persist, filling the sunlit water with a patient migration between depth and surface. In certain seasons they become so numerous that the shafts of sunlight seem solid, thickened by living bodies.

There are larger presences in the upper forest.

A kind of heron visits, though it may be better called a ridge-wader. It is dark as volcanic glass and lean enough to disappear when it folds its wings. It does not fly often over the open basin. Instead it moves from island to island at dawn, passing through the lower branches with an awkward, branchlike grace. Its legs are long, its toes broad and pale, made for roots slick with algae. It fishes among the shallows, but it takes only what has already come close to death: stunned fish after a rockfall, eels driven up by rain, insects washed from leaves.

The ridge-wader never calls in daylight. At night, from far across the basin, there may come a single sound like a wet stone struck gently with wood. It is difficult to locate. The answer, if there is one, arrives much later.

On the steepest slopes live small climbing mammals with fur that darkens when wet until they look almost amphibious. They are shy, round-eared, and narrow-faced, feeding on seeds, leaf shoots, beetles, and the soft growths that form where branch meets trunk. During rain they retreat into root hollows above the waterline. During dry hours they descend astonishingly far along the trees, sometimes slipping beneath the surface to cross from one submerged branch to another.

They have learned the shape of the drowned forest better than they know the living one. Young animals play beneath the water in the shallow tangles, holding themselves down with their claws and releasing bubbles through their noses. Their mothers wait above, watching the disturbances among the leaves. A stranger might mistake those bubbles for seepage from the mud.

The old drowned trees themselves are inhabited by colonies of small, blind crabs. Their shells are green-black, covered in the same fine algae as the wood. They live inside hollows softened by water, in the pockets under lifted bark, among roots where fallen leaves collect. They are scavengers, cleaners, gardeners of decay. They carry fragments of leaf and rot deeper into the submerged wood, where fungi thread through the dark interior.

Those fungi are among the basin’s true architects.

Some grow white and delicate along dead branches in the shallows. Others are black, nearly invisible, spreading inside trunks until the trees become hollow chambers. In the drowned forest they do not consume quickly. Nothing here does. A branch can remain intact for decades while its center becomes lace, then mud, then a nursery for roots and eel eggs. At night, in certain coves, small areas of rotting wood give off a faint blue-green light. It is not bright enough to illuminate the water. It merely changes the darkness around it, making each submerged trunk seem to hold a little submerged moon.

And lower still, beneath the dead forest, something lives in the mud.

Not one thing, perhaps. Not an animal in the ordinary sense. The drowned valley floor is covered in deep leaf sediment, mineral dust, pollen, spores, and the slow residues of the mountains breaking themselves apart. Under this soft black layer are pockets of warmth from the young earth. Bacteria thrive there in colors not often seen in air: rust-red, sulfur-yellow, milk-white, violet-gray. They make skins on buried stone and faint clouds in disturbed silt. They feed on what falls and what leaks upward. They turn death into gas, gas into pressure, pressure into the rare hollow knock that rises through the water.

Sometimes, at night, a chain of bubbles emerges from a place with no visible source. It passes up through the drowned branches, enlarging as it rises. At the surface each bubble breaks without sound.

There are stories, if there are people near enough to make stories, of a larger creature that follows those bubbles.

It is said to live below the level where trunks can be seen. It moves through the old road cuts and riverbeds of the valley floor, passing beneath submerged bridges of stone, through the root-choked mouths of former caves. It is never described the same way twice. Some call it a fish with a body like a black ribbon and a head too broad for any fish. Some say it is a colony of eels moving in one shared skin. Some claim it is only a drift of drowned branches caught in a deep current that no one has found.

The most reliable account is also the least useful: when it passes, the water above it becomes briefly clear.

Silt settles. Hanging weeds lean toward a direction no surface wind has made. The fish go still in the roots. Then, from far down, comes a low pressure through the soles of the feet or the hull of a boat, not a sound exactly, but the sense of a door being closed somewhere beneath the world.

Afterward, there may be a new branch floating in a cove, though no tree has fallen. Or a narrow line of warm water may rise and lie across the surface until dawn. Once in a while, something long and pale is seen sliding beneath the mist between two islands. By the time anyone reaches the place, there is only water and the reflection of the mountains.

Perhaps the basin is too young for a great beast. Perhaps it is making one.

For now, its most certain inhabitant is the slow agreement among all these lives: root and eel, fungus and stone, frog and rain-filled leaf, clear drifting thing and dark submerged tree. Nothing in the drowned high country seems to want dominion. Nothing multiplies until it fills every space. Even the plants leave channels between themselves. Even the mountains, still sharp and rising, appear to have paused around the water.

The basin lives as though it has been told to wait.

And so its creatures wait with it: beneath leaves, within wood, under shelves of fresh black rock, in the warm sediment of the lost valley floor—quietly becoming suited to a world that has not yet decided what it is.
