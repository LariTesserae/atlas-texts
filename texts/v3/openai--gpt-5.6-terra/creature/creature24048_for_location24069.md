What lives in the Dry Garden is not immortal, exactly.

It is a family that has been prevented from finishing a single afternoon.

They are called the First Below because no other name has survived the surface. There were perhaps nine of them when they came through the fault: drought-people, stone-readers, carriers of seed and bone tools; people accustomed to measuring life by what could be saved in a clay jar. They brought their hunger down with them. They brought their old injuries, their griefs, their small private customs. One had a cough. One had a child tied against her back in a sling. One had a copper ring pushed onto a finger swollen from work. One was old enough to have forgotten most names but not the names of plants.

The chamber received them all at once, though they entered one by one.

That is why they are difficult to see.

A visitor looking across the garden may notice a darker interruption among the shrubs: the suggestion of a crouched figure, knees drawn up beneath a shawl stiffened with mineral dust. It will be gone when looked at directly, not because it has moved but because the visitor has completed a different portion of the same moment. On another visit, from another angle, there may be a hand extended above the shallow bowl of seeds.

The hand is brown and narrow.

The fingers are not dead.

They have simply been reaching for a very long time.

The First Below inhabit the chamber in fragments of action. Not separate ghosts, not statues, not people sleeping beneath an enchantment. Each persists at the point where the Dry Garden caught them: lifting, chewing, listening, turning, fearing, waiting. Their movements occur, but do not proceed. A woman stoops to set a white seed in the powder at the base of a glass-leaved plant. The seed touches the ground. Her fingers loosen. Yet it never quite leaves her hand. A boy runs between the pale lizards with a strip of shell held over his head like a shield; from some parts of the room he seems to have already passed, and from others he has not yet begun.

They do not repeat, exactly. Repetition would require time.

They are always doing the thing.

The little shelter is theirs. Its doorway is too low because the builder made it in a hurry, crouching while the roof plates were still warm from cutting. The spirals on the shell were made by a woman who had once decorated burial jars. She thought, at first, that she was making a home. There are marks inside the shelter too, difficult to see in the amber dark: tally lines along the wall, then a child’s handprint pressed in mineral dust, then more tally lines that do not advance beyond the same unfinished cluster.

Sometimes the shelter contains someone.

Sometimes it contains only the shape that person has made in the light.

A visitor who enters might find an old man seated against the far wall with his knees up, his face hidden in his arms. He is very thin, though neither starvation nor time has thinned him. His hair is braided with copper wire. Beside him rests a stone cup full of something dark and granular. The old man is humming.

The hum is one note of the chamber’s great chord.

If addressed, he may lift his head.

His face will seem ordinary at first: cracked lips, a pale scar across one cheek, eyes accustomed to darkness. Then the visitor understands that his expression is not changing in response. It is the expression of a man who has just heard, perhaps for the first time, that the garden will not let his daughter’s fever break and will not let it take her. His mouth is open to speak one word. He has been on the edge of saying it since before the kingdoms above had names.

No one has heard the word.

The child lives as well.

She can sometimes be found near the warmest wall, seated with her back against the stone, small feet white with powder. Her fever has not risen. It has not eased. Her cheeks remain bright. In her lap is a pale lizard that she has caught with both hands. The lizard’s mineral-sealed eyes are closed. The child is smiling down at it in the concentration of someone learning gentleness.

The lizard cannot escape.

The child cannot release it.

Neither seems distressed.

This is the cruelty of the Dry Garden: it has preserved every mercy at the exact depth of its incompletion.

The First Below learned quickly that they could not age there. At first they rejoiced. A cut did not fester. Their stored food did not spoil. Their old woman’s bad tooth did not ache more deeply. The child slept without worsening. The drought above, they believed, could not reach them. They ate the dry fruit and found strength in it. They lay under the shell roof and felt no night pass over them.

Then one of them tried to leave.

There is a place near the entrance cleft where the powder floor is marked by what appears to be a shallow furrow. It is the print of a body dragged forward on its elbows. The person who made it is still there, though a visitor will likely mistake him for a slab of pale stone leaning at the base of the wall. He is pressed half into the cleft, shoulder turned sideways, one arm extended toward the cold passages beyond.

His fingertips have reached a place where the chamber’s warmth ends.

They have never crossed it.

He is not held by rock. No visible force restrains him. He is simply in the final fraction of departing: muscles gathered, jaw clenched, nails broken against stone. If someone takes his hand, it feels warm and dry. His skin has the resistance of living skin. It may even tighten around theirs.

But it does not pull back.

Those who touch him often leave soon afterward, if they can. Not because they fear contagion, though some do, but because they feel in his grip an unbearable thing: the effort of a person who has not failed, because failure has not been permitted to occur.

The First Below are not alone in their fixedness. The animals belong to the chamber more fully than they do. A blind runner may cross a visitor’s bootprint in its straight, tireless line, then be found later in the same place, crossing it again without having returned. A grazer chews a glassy leaf, its square teeth working slowly. The leaf bears the marks of being eaten but does not shorten. Pale lizards are born from eggs that are forever cracking. Moths settle, rise, settle, rise—not in a cycle but in the several visible faces of one continuous motion.

There are stranger residents beneath the powder.

The exposed roots chime because they are wound through with them: fine white creatures like threads of jointed glass, each no thicker than a hair. They move through the crust by dissolving and rebuilding their own bodies. They consume salt, heat, shed skins, breaths, the faint metallic taste left by blood. In the surface world they would be called worms, though they have neither mouths nor heads. Here they are the garden’s nearest thing to gardeners.

They arrange the roots.

They polish the beetles’ bright joints.

They carry away nothing, because nothing can be carried away, but they maintain the appearance of carrying: an endless labor of attendance performed within the sealed instant of the place.

And somewhere in the warm stone, deeper than the chamber walls, is the largest inhabitant.

No person has seen it. The First Below may have understood it, or may merely have given their fear a shape. The hollow booms that sound from distant rock are said to be its body shifting in sleep. Not waking—nothing in the Dry Garden wakes—but settling through layers of stone that should have crushed any living thing long ago.

It may be a buried animal.

It may be a root system older than the country above.

It may be the thing that makes the chamber possible: a patient mineral mind, feeding on the difference between before and after, holding both in its immense closed hand.

The garden’s plants are its hairs, some say. The insects are sparks in its dreaming nerves. The hum is its breathing, slowed until breath and silence become the same substance.

Others say there is no creature below. The booms are merely the earth remembering how it broke open. The Dry Garden needs no keeper. It is a wound in time, and like many wounds, it has learned to preserve what entered it.

Still, visitors who sit quietly beside the ancient shelter sometimes feel watched.

Not with hostility. Not even with interest.

With the grave, unwavering attention of something that has never looked away from a single living thing entrusted to it.

If they stay too long, the First Below become easier to see.

A face appears in the leaves. A bare foot stands in the white powder without sinking. Someone moves behind the wall of clicking sound, just beyond the edge of vision. The visitor may begin to recognize gestures: a hand shielding a flame that cannot consume its wick; a mother reaching for a child; a person placing a bowl on a stool.

Then, perhaps, they see one more figure than the old stories allow.

It is standing near the entrance, turned toward the cleft.

It has the visitor’s posture.

It has their pack, their boots whitened with dust, their hand lifted toward the wall as if feeling for a way out.

And it is waiting, patiently, for them to arrive.
