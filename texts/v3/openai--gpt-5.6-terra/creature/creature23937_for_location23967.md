What lives there is not a single people, nor a clean division between citizens, livestock, infrastructure, and weather.

The human inhabitants call themselves, in the broadest sense, the **kept**.

The word does not mean owned. It means maintained in mutual condition. Kept warm. Kept dry enough. Kept informed of the water. Kept outside the feeding range of a channel-mouth. Kept from becoming a new source of rot in a sealed district. In turn, they keep the drains clear, the animal passages open, the membranes fed with mineral dust, the old towers listened to.

A visitor might mistake them, at first, for a population in mourning.

They wear long dark outer garments made from sealed cloth, not because black is customary but because pale materials quickly acquire a visible crust of mineral foam and become difficult to clean. Their collars rise high around the jaw. Most have the small metallic or ceramic seals behind the ears that reduce the bell-throat chord to a tolerable pressure. Their gloves are thin and articulate, with textured palms meant for reading vibration through rails, walls, and fitted street plates. Many wear weighted boots. A person who cannot feel the water moving underfoot is considered poorly equipped, nearly blind.

Their faces are often pale from generations under a lightless ceiling, but not uniformly so. They color themselves instead with temperature: copper warmth pressed into the cheeks before a meeting; blue mineral stains at the temples during mourning; a narrow stripe of reflective green on the throat when one is responsible for a shared building system. Children are marked with a removable red dot beneath the left eye until they are old enough to recognize the flood warnings through their soles.

They do not raise their voices.

This is not merely because the bell-throats would swallow speech. Loudness is considered a kind of false promise. A shout claims that a human throat can matter more than water, machinery, weather, and animals. Important conversations occur with foreheads close together, through collar-to-collar conduction, or in the sound-blank courtyards. Lovers often sit in silence with their boots touching, exchanging small foot pressures in codes learned before they could read.

The kept are not especially solemn, though outsiders would say so. Their humor is dry and physical. A worker returning from an upper-span repair may find their tools arranged in a perfect imitation of a drowned neighborhood. A family may give a child a toy bridge that collapses only when a bell-throat sings a certain note. There are jokes about dry air, which most people have never experienced and regard as either mythical or medically alarming.

They have a highly developed etiquette of obstruction.

One does not block a runoff channel, even briefly. One does not stand still on a marked migration plate. One does not cast a body-shaped shadow over a warm recess, because bell-throats may be nesting there. One does not ask why a particular district was abandoned unless one has already been invited to hear the answer. The city is full of routes that must remain passable for things that are not people.

Many residents know the name, temperament, and seasonal schedule of the animals nearest their homes.

The bell-throats, especially, live almost as neighbors. They are not pets. A bell-throat cannot be owned in the ordinary sense; it nests where it finds warmth, resonance, and the right chemical residue. But households maintain small heat alcoves for them and leave mineral flakes in shallow dishes. In return, the animals’ choruses are used as diagnostic instruments. A sudden thinning of a certain frequency can mean poison in the water. A low wobble in the collective note can indicate a deep geological shift. If an entire block of bell-throats falls silent, everyone nearby begins moving uphill before the official warning arrives.

The oldest people can identify districts by their chord.

They say the eastern retaining terraces sing with too much glass in their lungs. The freight quarter has a bright nervous buzz because its bell-throats sleep near charging systems. The drowned civic levels hum in a way no domestic animal should hum. There are recordings of old choruses, preserved in pressure cylinders, and some families play them beneath the floor during the dark festivals. They are regarded as ancestral voices, though the ancestors in question are white, squat animals with ceramic skin.

Children are taught not to fear the channel creatures, but to respect their incompleteness.

The greater animals below are known by practical names: cilia-mouths, ladderbacks, veil spines, the blind rounds. Nobody agrees on whether these names refer to species, stages of growth, or merely recurring shapes presented by one immense distributed organism under the city. Some biologists insist that the translucent vertebrae rising through a floodgate belong to animals that are effectively immortal, replacing sections of themselves as they pass through mineral-rich water. Others say no individual survives longer than a few months, and that the apparent continuity is an illusion produced by an environment that makes the same forms over and over.

The kept mostly care which ones can lift a bridge.

There are channel wardens whose work consists of feeling through handrails for unfamiliar pressure patterns. They carry no weapons in the heroic sense. They have light-casters, scent dispersers, low-frequency poles, and containers of harvested molt. The dangerous animals are not usually driven away. They are redirected. A warden who kills a cilia-mouth without necessity may be censured more harshly than someone who damages municipal equipment. Dead channel animals are disruptive. They change the water. They attract scavengers. They cause the basin to remember a different chemistry.

Not all inhabitants are born.

Some are assembled.

The construction organisms beyond the dense center are the most obvious examples: low, many-legged machines with mineral grinders for mouths and slow bright seams along their backs. They are not thought of as robots exactly. A robot obeys a command and stops. These things inherit projects. They receive an old foundation pattern, an estimate of water pressure, a list of animal corridors, and a span of decades. Then they go to work. They may alter the plan if the stone alters first.

A person can sometimes meet one at the edge of a new district: a blunt shape crouched in black slurry, working a grain at a time. If greeted, it may turn its light toward them. If asked a question, it may spend several minutes evaluating whether the question affects foundation stability. Most people greet them anyway.

There are smaller maintenance organisms in the city itself: dust-gatherers that crawl through façade seams; pipe eels with transparent bellies full of cleansing enzymes; blind little wheel-shaped carriers that collect shed mineral crust from the walkways. Some were designed. Some descended, in their strange way, from designs. Some were found doing useful work after decades in sealed basements and were incorporated into municipal practice without anyone being able to identify their manufacturer.

The city keeps registries for these things, but the registries are humble documents. They do not say *property*. They say *known habits*, *safe handling*, *last observed repair behavior*, *whether it responds to warmth*.

There are also those who live in the membranes.

At night—or what passes for night in a place that has no dawn—the green-lit skins of certain buildings pulse more quickly. Behind them, dim silhouettes may appear: thin shapes moving upward against gravity, or broad flattened forms passing from one wall section to another. The official explanation is that the membranes contain maintenance cultures, filtration tissues, and distributed sensing systems. This is true.

It is not all that is true.

Residents leave tiny offerings at the oldest membrane towers: a bead of heated water, a clean piece of conductive foil, the first flake of mineral foam gathered from a newborn child’s blanket. No one calls this worship. Yet when a tower is scheduled for deconstruction, people come to stand beneath it and feel its wall one last time. They press their palms to the green surface until it answers with a slow pulse.

The towers are said to have moods.

A tower that leans away from a future collapse is praised for caution. A tower that seals its lower doors before a flood is said to have remembered its tenants. A tower that refuses entry for three days is treated with the wary patience one gives an elderly relative who has heard something no one else can hear.

The city’s dead remain inhabitants too, though not in the way strangers expect.

Burial is difficult in stone that moves and water that carries chemistry everywhere. Cremation wastes heat. Preservation consumes space. So the dead are placed in mineral cradles beneath communal floors, where slow accreting foam seals them into pale, dry chambers. Their names are not carved aboveground. They are encoded into the vibration patterns of the building plates.

In an old residential block, a careful walker can feel the dead beneath the soles of their boots.

Not individually at first. It is more like a difference in the rhythm of the floor: a soft responsive pulse when rainwater pressure changes, a slight hum at the edge of a stairwell. Families visit by standing in the right place during a quiet shift in the bell-throat chorus. They bring warmth in insulated cups. They do not spill it. They hold it over the plate until the warmth sinks away.

Some of the dead become part of the city’s decisions.

Their recorded habits, speech patterns, engineering judgments, and medical histories are retained by local systems. These remnants are consulted in limited ways. An old flood architect may advise on a barrier design through a building’s pressure language. A grandmother’s stored voice may be permitted to tell a child the family evacuation route. The kept are cautious about this. They do not want their dead to become endlessly useful. A person is allowed to recede. After enough years, the stored patterns are softened, reduced to a name, then to a pulse, then to the building’s general memory of having once sheltered someone.

Beyond people, animals, and machines, the basin itself is regarded as inhabited.

Not personified exactly. The kept do not imagine a god under the streets with intentions shaped like theirs. But they know that the black stone responds, anticipates, and sometimes refuses. A foundation can develop a pressure signature that resembles warning. A raw terrace can remain too warm for years, despite every model saying it should cool. Water can begin rising through a district’s joints months before the weather engines register any corresponding surplus.

There are listeners trained for this.

They work alone or in pairs in the lowest safe galleries, lying flat with one cheek against the unfinished stone. Their occupation is called **hearing the lower terms**. They record tiny rearrangements: the passage of trapped water, the settling of mineral foam, the faint internal grinding of the basin making room for itself. The best listeners are not famous. They are deeply trusted. When one tells a neighborhood to move its sleeping spaces to the upper floors, no one asks for proof.

The city has cults, though they are usually disguised as practical societies.

There are the Warm-Hand Houses, which believe every unit of stored heat must pass through at least three living hands before it is used by machinery. There are the Open-Gate people, who argue that the channel animals should be allowed farther into the drowned districts and that the city’s attempts to keep them below are both cruel and geologically foolish. There are membrane devotees who believe the repairing walls are slowly assembling a mind across the basin. There are roof dissenters who insist that beyond the atmospheric retaining engines lies a real sun, a real horizon, and perhaps forests.

Most citizens pity them.

Not because forests are impossible. Because a tree, in the imagination of the basin, has become something indecently extravagant: a thing that drinks water and gives almost none back, a thing that insists on height, light, and private roots.

Still, children draw them.

They draw black trunks with branching crowns. They draw green shapes they have seen only in archive diagrams. They draw leaves falling through an air that is somehow dry enough to allow falling. Their parents keep these pictures in sealed sleeves, along with maps of neighborhoods that no longer exist.

There are visitors, rarely.

They arrive along the high levitation lines or descend through the weather structures with breathing equipment and bright, impractical coats. For a few hours they look upward as if expecting the cloud ceiling to open. They speak too loudly. They step in the wrong places. They ask whether the bell-throats can be removed.

The kept are patient with them.

They explain that removing the bell-throats would not remove the sound. It would merely make audible the things underneath it: the wet pressure in the rock, the work of the weather engines, the distant movement of enormous bodies through submerged chambers, the patient cracking and resealing of a world still in the process of becoming solid.

And perhaps that is what truly lives in the basin.

Not the people alone, nor the ceramic animals, nor the green-lit buildings or construction organisms. What lives there is an agreement that has learned to perpetuate itself.

It has hands. It has mouths. It has cilia and membranes and flooded lungs. It has thousands of narrow human lives moving carefully from warmth to warmth. It has towers that heal, stone that rises, machines that grow foundations for citizens not yet born.

It has no garden.

It has no sunrise.

But under the permanent dark, amid the mineral snow and endless water, it endures with a density and vigilance that resemble life too closely to call it anything else.
