What lives in the Rootward depends on who is asked.

The people of the upper city say, if they speak of it at all, that the Rootward is tended by the low folk: cistern-cleaners, salt-press workers, widows with nowhere else to go, old soldiers whose lungs prefer the cool, children born in the lamp district and frightened by open sky. They imagine a poor little colony clinging to a useful fungus beneath the streets.

The Rootward people do not call themselves low.

They call themselves the **Kept**.

Not because the city keeps them, though it does, in the way a house keeps a broom in the corner until it is needed. Not because they keep the Rootward, though they prune its passages, harvest its seed meal, clear old bridgeways, and teach each generation the safe routes through the dark.

They are Kept because the place has made room for them.

This is not a metaphor to them. A dwelling that has sheltered one family long enough will change around their habits. A sleeping hollow develops shallow ridges where a sleeper’s shoulder rests. A handhold grows smoother and warmer along the path taken by a child learning to walk. In the communal chambers, the root-mass has risen into benches at the same height as the people’s knees. When someone dies, the growth nearest their room often tightens for a season, closing little cracks and swallowing the dust left in corners.

The Kept do not say the Rootward loves them. Love is too quick, too warm, too concerned with one life at a time.

They say it has learned their weight.

They are pale, though not in the sickly manner imagined above. Their skin has the muted, almost translucent cast of onion flesh, dusted at the knuckles and heels with the fine gray powder of the place. Most keep their hair cropped close or bind it in tight cords beneath soft caps. Loose hair catches in fibrous veils and can carry the scent of the deep chambers home for days: mineral, dry, bitter, faintly sweet, like old wood kept in a sealed chest.

Their eyes are not blind. Lamps are useful, and the Kept can read the amber tremble of a flame better than a surface-dweller can. But their vision is a lesser sense below. Children are taught routes before they know their letters. They learn a wall by palm and cheek: this one granular and cool, this one faintly elastic, this one crossed by a pulse every twelve breaths. They can distinguish one root corridor from another by the sound made when a fingernail taps it.

A visitor may hear only darkness shifting.

A Rootward child hears: salt presses to the left, sleeping alcoves ahead, a growth-wall tightening somewhere beyond the bridge, and three unfamiliar people walking too heavily near the old stair.

Their speech has changed to suit the chambers. They do not shout names. They click their tongues against their teeth, knock patterns on the nearest rib-wall, or hum low into their cupped hands. Families have particular rhythms. Work crews have longer ones. A message sent through a corded wall may return many minutes later, softened and distorted by distance, but it returns.

For this reason the Kept are careful with grief.

They believe sharp grief travels. Not a cry, exactly, but the force behind it. A person in mourning is given a place near the outer lamps for several days, where their breathing can rise through the shafts and not trouble the deep growth. No one is forbidden to weep. But no one weeps against a wall.

There are old men and women in the Rootward whose fingertips have become astonishingly sensitive. They are called **listeners**, though they listen with more than their ears. They sit cross-legged beside the thicker cords with their hands spread upon them, sometimes for hours. Their palms are marked with pale whorls, rubbed nearly smooth by a lifetime of contact. They report changes: a new closure near the meal chambers, a thinning in the east folds, an unsettled rhythm under the central bridge.

The city above pays attention to them only when it must.

Before a foundation cracks, before a cistern wall gives way, before a sink opens in a courtyard, the listeners sometimes know. The Rootward’s great body alters its pressure. Its deep knocking changes. The dry dust loosens in the wrong places. A breathing wall begins to draw inward rather than outward.

Then the Kept send someone up.

The messenger climbs into sunlight with a strip of knotted cord or a clay tablet bearing the warning. Usually they are met by an official who listens with the patient face reserved for superstition. Usually the official sends a mason to inspect the place.

Usually the listener has been right.

For this service the Kept receive lamp oil, bronze tools, coarse cloth, salt, and a measure of legal protection. No landlord may claim their chambers. No soldier may enter the Rootward armed with iron spikes. No magistrate may demand more seed meal than the harvest law allows.

The city obeys these rules less from kindness than fear.

There was once a governor who ordered a broad route cut through the root-mass to make an easier underground road between the kiln yards and the eastern cisterns. His crews went down with saws, slaves, and six days of oil. They cut cords thicker than their thighs. They burned through walls that closed behind them. They drove wedges into the pale columns.

On the seventh day, the lamps went out.

No one knows exactly what followed. The surviving workers came back without their tools. Some had blood on their hands, some had dust packed into their mouths, and none could say where the new passage had gone. Within a month, three storehouses above the cut route split from floor to roof. By the next hot season, the governor’s own house had sunk one finger-width on its eastern side.

His descendants still pay for oil at the Rootward entrance.

There are animals there, according to the Kept.

Not animals in the upper sense. Nothing with eyes that flash in the lamp glow, nothing with paws, wings, teeth, or a hunger that can be understood. But sometimes a seed-pod is found where no pod should be, standing upright on the mat like a small clenched hand. Sometimes a dry cleft opens and releases a long rustle that follows a person’s steps for twenty paces before stopping. Sometimes the root floor, under a lone walker, firms itself into a narrow line leading away from danger.

The Kept call these things **manners**.

“The Rootward has manners,” they say.

It makes room. It objects. It remembers a path. It dislikes waste. It does not like iron left in the flesh of it. It accepts bone, ash, hair, dust, and the sweepings of a household, but not blood freely spilled. It will bear a sleeping child safely for hours, yet may raise a hump beneath a thief’s foot and keep him standing there until dawn.

Whether this is true is hard to establish. The Rootward gives no testimony that can be carried into court.

Still, no one laughs at the rules painted by the entrance.

The oldest inhabitants are not necessarily the oldest people.

There is, in a chamber west of the central void, a root pillar called the **Grandmother Post**. It is broad enough that eight adults linking hands cannot circle it. Its surface is layered in pale gray plates, each one thin as a fingernail, and in the folds between them are caught objects from generations: a copper earring, a child’s carved bone bead, a bronze awl, a ceremonial knife rusted nearly to nothing, the heel of a sandal, bits of pottery, one small whetstone worn smooth as a river pebble.

The Kept bring their newborns to it.

They do not press the infants against it. No one presses anyone against a breathing wall. Instead, the child is held nearby while an elder lays two fingers upon the pillar and speaks the child’s name once, quietly.

If the pillar gives a pulse before the name is repeated, the child is said to have been noticed.

Most are noticed.

A few are not.

Those children are not cursed, nor abandoned, nor treated cruelly. They simply tend, in later life, to leave. They become traders, soldiers, scribes, builders of high roofs. They marry into families that live in the windy districts. Their descendants sometimes forget the way down altogether.

But the ones who are noticed often remain.

They grow into the Rootward’s particular kind of person: patient without passivity, watchful without suspicion, difficult to hurry. They have the habit of pausing in doorways. They dislike empty rooms with hard corners. In the upper city they may seem slow-witted, because they wait for a building to settle before answering a question. They sleep badly in houses that do not make noise.

Some return from the surface after decades away and walk through the lamp-dark without a guide.

They put one hand upon the first living wall, and it seems to know them.

And beneath them all—beneath the Kept, the listeners, the lamp-tenders, the meal-grinders, the children learning to count by knocks, the dead folded into the dry root-floor—there is the larger inhabitant.

It may be one body.

It may be a colony so old that individuality has become meaningless.

It may be the buried remainder of a world that once grew upward and, finding stone and city above it, learned to grow inward instead.

The people of the Rootward do not give it a personal name. Names are for things that can be called and might answer.

They call it **Below**.

When the great inhalation rises from the central void, everyone falls quiet. Not from fear exactly. From courtesy.

The lamps lean. The bridge trembles. The pale ropes descending into the black tighten by some immeasurable degree.

Then Below exhales.

The sound passes through the root walls, through the ancient stonework, through the dry foundations of the city. It enters sleeping houses, granaries, shrines, barracks, and palaces alike. It touches every bed from beneath.

And in the Rootward, the Kept put their palms to the living floor and wait until they feel the answering pulse return.
