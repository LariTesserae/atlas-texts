The country begins above the treeline, though there has never been a line between tree and not-tree here. Plants have learned the work of stone, timber, skin, net, chimney, fur, and weather. They do not grow from the ground so much as constitute it. Every ridge is upholstered in old green matter: sod packed deep as masonry, moss piled in wet continental drifts, low black shrubs braided root-to-root across valleys where there may once have been rivers. In some places the vegetation rises into walls, but not forests. These are slow, lopsided ramparts of stem and peat, grown against prevailing winds over tens of thousands of cold seasons. They have no trunks, no clear canopy. Their upper surfaces are pale with lichen and permanently bent by storms.

The land is high and open. It goes on in basins and broad-backed uplands, each feature worn almost past its name. Mountains have been reduced to hunched, patient forms, their flanks shaved down by ice, rain, and the abrasive passage of centuries. The rock that remains is dark, porous, and wet-looking even in frost. Water emerges from every seam. It seeps out of cliffs in curtains; it gathers in shallow, trembling pools among the moss; it shines under the roots in clear channels that vanish again into peat. Nothing here is truly dry. The soil, when exposed, is black and fibrous, smelling of iron, cold tea, and the deep interior of a cellar.

There are lakes, but they are not restful. Their surfaces are crowded with plant film, loose reed mats, floating cushions of blue-green growth, and dark holes that open and close as bubbles come up from below. The water is only visible in interrupted black panes. In rain, which arrives often but never uniformly, the lakes become difficult to distinguish from the land. Entire valleys acquire a thin sheen. The ground reflects the gray sky and seems briefly made of mercury, until a grazing animal moves through it and reveals the shallows.

The cold does not preserve stillness. It makes everything busy.

At every hour there are sounds of drainage, rupture, and labor. Water pours beneath the turf in unseen conduits, making a heavy underground clatter. Peat shelves slump into ravines with soft detonations. Root systems tighten and release. Wind flattens the low vegetation in moving bands, and the bands make a dry whisper against the ceaseless wet noise. Across the high open slopes, long cries carry: birds calling from unseen clefts, herds answering one another through fog, the metallic bellow of something large moving among the pools.

The animals are numerous enough to alter the terrain. Great pale grazers, broad-bodied and many-jointed, move in loose files across the open flats, their mouths combing whole mats of moss from the ground without uprooting them. Their coats are not fur exactly, but layered fibers full of seed and rainwater; small insects and nesting birds live in the folds. They leave behind troughs of compressed vegetation that immediately fill with brown water and become roads for other creatures. Smaller beasts travel those flooded paths: long-backed amphibious things with transparent eyelids, packs of six-legged scavengers that wear lichens like camouflage, flocks of low-flying birds whose wings beat with the sound of canvas snapping.

There are predators too, though they are difficult to see. They are usually mistaken for weather: a moving patch of gray on a hillside, a cloud shadow that climbs against the slope, a wash of black leaves streaming through the reeds. When they feed, the country goes louder rather than quieter. Every animal nearby raises its voice. The ground itself seems to take notice, opening its pores, releasing fog.

The fog is a living participant in the landscape. It gathers in gullies and pulls itself over passes, heavy with spores, water, and pollen. It condenses on every surface, including bodies. A traveler standing still for an hour would acquire a greenish nap around the boots, then across the shoulders, then along the eyelashes. The plants are opportunistic but not violent. They simply assume that anything stationary is available for incorporation. Fallen animals become islands in a day. Standing animals sometimes carry little meadows on their backs. One can find old skulls entirely replaced by flowering structures, the bone gone but the shape retained in stem and leaf.

There are no buildings. There are no roads, no fences, no cuts in the earth that can be called deliberate. Yet the valley possesses an industrial character, an immense production without makers.

The peat beds generate heat far below, digesting old growth into gas. Vents in the upland exhale in regular pulses, and their breath turns small waterwheels of reed and root. In sheltered ravines, mineral water runs through living filters so dense and intricate that they resemble machinery: ranks of fibrous membranes, yellow bladders inflating and deflating, fan-shaped leaves opening in sequence as a current passes. The processes are loud. Steam hisses from ground cracks. Mudpots churn. Root-bundles knock against buried stone in a measured, piston-like rhythm. Once in a while the entire basin gives a low, sustained groan, as if some colossal mechanism beneath the hills has shifted into a new gear.

No one has built this machinery. It has grown because this is what growth does here when given enough time and pressure.

The oldest parts of the landscape are nearly flat. Not smooth—nothing is smooth—but settled. Their chaos has worn itself into repeating arrangements: shallow basin, braided peat, black pond, lichen shelf, wet stone, another basin. These patterns extend for miles, visible from the higher ridges as a dimly gleaming circuit board made of bog and water. The impression is not of design but of a world that has been trying the same solution for so long that chance has begun to look purposeful.

At dusk, which is less a time than a gradual thickening of the cloud, lights appear under the ground. Cold green and dull amber luminescence moves through the marsh in branching networks. Sometimes it follows the paths of water. Sometimes it travels uphill. Herds pause near it. Birds settle around it in rings. The light may be fungal, electrical, chemical, or merely the visible underside of a thought taking place in the peat.

Then the wind changes, and the plants lean all at once toward the same distant direction.

Not bowing. Listening.
