At this altitude the sea has no horizon.

It is held up among mountains, or perhaps the mountains have been worn down until only their chambers remain: a high, drowned country of steps, courts, walls, sluices, and long straight causeways disappearing beneath black-green water. Nothing here was built recently. Nothing here is operated. Yet there is no natural ground to be found.

The floor beneath the water is paved in vast fitted slabs, pale as old bone where the sediment has scoured them clean. They proceed outward in terraces and plazas, descend through drowned gates, cross the mouths of empty-looking aqueducts. Every cliff is a retaining wall. Every ravine is a canal cut between cyclopean blocks. Far off, half glimpsed through the grey water, stand the square profiles of towers whose roofs have fallen away. Some are only vertical stacks of stone rings, dark at their centers. Others lie on their sides across the terraces, slowly being taken apart by pressure, frost, and current.

The place is ancient enough that its makers have become irrelevant. Their work has outlasted purpose and even failure. It has become geology.

Cold water pours constantly through it.

It comes in from above as a weighty, slanted rain, though the rain never seems to strike a surface. The whole sky is water; the whole atmosphere is an immense turbulent suspension. Curtains of pale meltwater descend from unseen high shelves. Darker currents rise from flooded basins. Sudden crossflows seize clouds of silt and draw them along an avenue or through a doorless arch. In one district, water churns continually in a circular public square, revolving around a sunken pillar until it becomes a low, permanent underwater storm. In another, all is nearly still, except for the intermittent heave of the world’s larger pressure.

There are no trees, no weeds in cracks, no moss softening the edges. The old construction remains bare. Its materials have become their own landscape: black basalt brick, white mineral mortar, greenish glassy tile, corroded bronze plates fused into walls, bands of salt crust beneath sheltered ledges. The water has polished every exposed corner into a softened plane. Over uncounted ages it has cut shallow channels in the paving, not deep enough to erase the city-country’s geometry, only to give it a second script: loops, combs, grooves, and branching seams that carry silver threads of disturbed sediment.

In the dimness, animal life moves everywhere.

Not in schools, exactly. There is too much room. The creatures are scattered through the architecture as stars are scattered through a night too cloudy to see clearly. Pale fish, thin-bodied and almost transparent, hold themselves headfirst in the torrents behind parapets. Thick-lipped, blind things browse the mineral films on submerged carvings. Long white crabs emerge from drainage mouths in solemn processions, climb three or four steps, then are swept away by a pulse in the current and begin again. Small black eels braid through joints between the paving stones. They vanish into gaps too narrow to admit anything else.

Along the undersides of bridges hang colonies of soft-bodied animals resembling folded gloves or unopened flowers, though there are no flowers here. When turbulence passes over them, they close all at once. In calmer canals, broad, winged creatures drift from wall to wall without flapping, using the water’s invisible topography as birds use thermals. Their skins flash dull pewter, then disappear in the murk.

There are larger presences too.

Sometimes the water ahead acquires a shape before the animal itself becomes visible: a distortion, a deepening of green, the delayed movement of drifting particles. Then a many-jointed silhouette crosses a distant archway, taller than the arch, patient and unhurried. It does not seem to swim. It is carried, or it allows the current to decide where it will go. For a few moments it passes through the dim light cast down from above, revealing a hide plated in chalky segments and old scars. Then it enters another corridor, and the city closes around its absence.

Sound does not travel here in the expected way. There is no wind, though the water behaves like weather. Most of the time the country is hushed: the close internal silence of snowfields, compounded with the pressure of the deep. A person might hear their own blood as a distant tide. Then a current turns in a sunken chamber and the walls begin to sing. Not a melody, but a low mineral note, sustained through the stone. Gates made of corroded bars knock gently in their housings. A roof slab shifts somewhere below and produces one immense muffled boom, after which everything waits.

The rules of this place are old and simple.

Nothing floats freely for long. The water always finds an arrangement, and then disrupts it.

Nothing can root.

Nothing decays in the warm, living sense. Bodies are cleaned, hollowed, mineralized, and eventually incorporated into the seams of the floor. Shells become paving aggregate. Bones settle in the eddies behind walls and turn white, then grey, then indistinguishable from the stones around them.

There is no true up. The pale glow above may be a surface, or merely the thinnest region of the storm. It lends a little light to the upper terraces: a diluted blue-grey sufficient to outline walls, fish, and falling water. Below the fifth or sixth descending level, the place becomes almost entirely colorless. Yet the drowned structures continue downward with absolute confidence. Stairways lead into darkness. Doorways open under the foundations of other doorways. Great avenues slope away, their paving uninterrupted, as if designed for travelers who did not need air or light.

Now and then, in a far square, an entire mass of water changes direction.

The fauna knows before it happens. Fish leave the open routes. The winged drifters fold themselves beneath lintels. Crabs wedge their legs into cracks. The current pauses, gathering itself in the hollow streets. Then it comes: a blunt, rushing cold that fills an avenue from end to end, lifts loose stones, strips pale animals from walls, drives curtains of silt into the sky-water. It races through the old city with the force of an avalanche, divides at pillars, rejoins in flooded courts, and vanishes through the lower canals.

Afterward, the terraces are slightly changed. A collapsed wall has moved another handspan. A bronze plate is newly exposed. A whole shoal of translucent fish has appeared in a place where none were seen before. The long, bare country resumes its dim composure.

It has endured so long that turbulence is not its enemy. Turbulence is its climate, its season, its only form of time.
