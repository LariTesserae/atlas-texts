Below the winter sea, in a hollow no larger than a public square, there is a city that has never known air.

It occupies a pocket in the black basalt beneath an ice shelf: a round, steep-walled chamber where the sea has entered through several narrow mouths and remains, not still, but slow with circulations. Cold water threads constantly through the place. It comes in as blue pressure through crevices near the floor, rises in translucent columns, turns beneath the roof, and leaves through rents too narrow for anything larger than a fish. The city is therefore always in the act of being rearranged. Nothing is quite eroded; nothing has endured long enough to become ancient. Walls are fresh-faced, edges sharp, sediments only beginning to gather in seams.

Its buildings are not built by hands.

They are made of pale tube colonies, hard-shelled worms, limestone-secreting mats, and thick white fans of organisms that resemble masonry from a distance. They rise in crowded terraces from the chamber floor: little towers, arches, clustered stair-like ridges, buttressed domes, alleyways one must enter sideways. Every surface is densely inhabited. A pillar is a thousand mouths opening and closing; a bridge is a ropey colony binding two shelves together; a roof is the underside of a giant sponge, ribbed and furred with feeding filaments.

There are streets, though no creature has laid them out. The water has chosen them. It moves most insistently between the clustered growths, polishing channels through loose mineral dust and carrying scraps of food along regular routes. Tiny animals gather at intersections. Shrimp clouds drift in the wider lanes. Flat, translucent fish hover under arches, their fins trembling like loose scraps of cloth. Crabs use the lowest corridors, moving in patient lines, stopping at thresholds formed by the shadow of one colony over another. Slender eels inhabit the drains.

The entire chamber is a kind of compressed metropolis, all near-touching parts. There is scarcely an empty span of water. A creature can leave one wall and reach the next without swimming freely, by passing from frond to shell, from shell to sponge, from sponge to the waving arms of some rooted animal. The density makes the place feel sheltered, although it is open to the sea in several directions and subject to its immense weight.

Vegetation, if it can be called that, is scarce. In cracks high on the walls, where a diluted grey light filters down through fissured ice, there are short cold-green veils of algae. They cling flat to stone and barely stir. Elsewhere, threadlike dark growths trail from mineral seeps, too sparse to soften the rock. They are not the city’s character. The city belongs to animals, to mineral exhalations, to the slow labor of bodies turning dissolved matter into architecture.

At the center lies a circular basin, perhaps the footprint of a collapsed bubble in the ancient lava. Warmish water—only a degree or two warmer than the sea—wells from its bottom. The water carries iron, sulfur, and fine black grains. It gives the chamber a muted scent that no lungs will ever recognize: stone broth, metal, salt, and the faint sweetness of decay.

Around this basin the newest districts grow.

They are low and unfinished, only pale nubs and branching crusts so far. Fresh colonies take hold on the dark sediment, forming rudimentary blocks. A few have already been colonized by small blind fish, which enter their holes before the holes have become rooms. Others stand empty for a while, white against the black floor, until the drifting larvae settle on them. Nothing oversees this expansion. Yet it has a rhythm: seep, settle, feed, harden, crowd, collapse, seed again.

Occasionally a larger animal passes above the city: a seal’s distant silhouette under the ice, or a broad white fish with a mouth made for darkness. Its movement changes the water pressure in the chamber. The delicate inhabitants close. Feathery crowns withdraw into tubes. Shrimp vanish behind living walls. For a few moments, the city becomes bare architecture—small towers of chalk and bone, unadorned and vulnerable.

Then the water settles.

The crowns emerge one by one. Fins resume their trembling. Minute claws work over every surface. The streets fill again.

There is almost no sound. No wind reaches this depth. No machinery hums. The sea’s larger violence is excluded by stone and ice. If one could hear with enough patience, there would be only the faintest sediment-tick against shell, the soft click of jointed legs, the tiny contraction of feeding bodies, and beneath it all the pressure of water moving through the chamber’s hidden entrances.

Light enters reluctantly. It is not enough to illuminate the whole place at once. It gathers on the upper walls as a grey-blue dusk, fades among the middle terraces, and fails completely in the lower streets. There, the city is known by touch and current. Some animals carry their own dim signals: a brief cold glimmer from a shrimp’s flank, greenish sparks along the rim of a jelly-bodied creature, a pulse in the throat of a small fish. These lights are not lamps. They do not make the darkness retreat. They merely reveal that something is moving within it.

The rules of the place are simple and strict.

Nothing may remain solitary for long. Any bare surface is an invitation; any crevice becomes a home; any carcass becomes a crossroads of appetite. Growth is construction. Feeding is commerce. Water is law, road, weather, and memory. The city has no builders, no tools, no plans, and yet it is full of neighborhoods: high bright algae-rims, crowded shell markets, sponge roofs, worm-tube courtyards, black mineral suburbs still rising from the floor.

Above it is ice. Around it is stone. Beyond the mouths of the chamber is the great cold sea, immeasurable and nearly lightless.

But in this small hidden hollow, life has become so crowded that even the darkness seems occupied.
