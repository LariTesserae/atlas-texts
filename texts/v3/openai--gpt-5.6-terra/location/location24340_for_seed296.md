The city is built where the mountain has finally stopped pretending it can be climbed.

Above the last forests, above the grazing shelves and the weather stations with their bright little flags, the land rises into an old black massif whose flanks have been worn not into gentleness but into permanence. Its stone is folded, split, and grainy with mineral seams: green-black serpentinite, pale ribs of quartz, rusting iron faces exposed by centuries of freeze and thaw. Nothing here is sharp in a young way. The cliffs have the softened grandeur of bones found in a riverbed. Even the broken places are ancient, each fissure darkened by lichens, each fallen slab settled into its own weather.

At nearly the height of the clouds, a basin lies cupped between these eroded summits. It is broad enough for a town and its works, though no one would call it level. The ground terraces downward in irregular shelves, natural ledges enlarged by retaining walls, gantries, and successive generations of construction. Buildings crowd the slopes shoulder to shoulder: narrow stone dwellings with corrugated roofs; concrete service blocks bolted directly into the rock; old brick pump houses, their chimneys blind; glass-roofed growing halls; freight sheds; substations; workshops whose high windows glow amber through the afternoon mist.

There are streets, but they are never quite free of machinery. Pipes pass overhead in bundled runs, sweating beads of cold water. Conveyor belts cross alleys at second-storey height. Cable lines drag themselves from one ridge to another, vanishing into fog. Every building appears to have been added because there was nowhere else to put it, and then reinforced because it turned out the mountain would not move aside. The settlement has the density of moss on a shaded wall: thick, tenacious, growing into every seam.

The air is cool even in what passes for summer. It smells of wet stone, machine oil, crushed evergreen needles, and the clean metallic trace of snow still lingering in gullies above. Rain comes in brief, practical episodes. A cloud catches on the mountainside, releases a fine silver grain for twenty minutes, and then tears open to reveal a hard bright strip of distant sky. Much of the wetness is not rain at all. Condensation gathers on railings and wires, on the underside of bridges, in the folds of tarpaulins. At night, fog settles through the city and turns every cold pipe into a source of water. It runs down into stone channels cut along the lanes, feeding cisterns, boilers, hydro-turbines, public taps, and the clustered gardens.

Water is too valuable to be allowed to be picturesque. There are no wide lakes here, no cheerful rivers. Instead there are holding tanks beneath courtyards, dark reservoirs in sealed caverns, thin sluices slipping beneath grated walkways. A stream enters the basin from a cleft in the northern wall, white and furious for perhaps forty metres before being divided, filtered, measured, and sent into the pipes. Its original bed continues below the town only as a narrow wet ravine full of ferns and the echo of hidden flow.

The vegetation is lush by high-country standards because it has learned to live in density. It grows where the city has failed to exclude it. Moss climbs the lower walls in thick black-green cushions. Ferns rise from warm exhaust vents. Rowan trees root in old loading bays, their trunks bent sideways beneath steel platforms. There are banks of dwarf willow beside every leak, and hardy grasses stuffing themselves into cracked retaining walls. On the south-facing slopes above the roofs, low forests of crooked spruce and mountain ash form a dark fringe. Their branches are permanently combed by wind in one direction. In protected creases, there are astonishing patches of leaf: broad wet plants, sorrel, alpine celery, carpets of small blue flowers that open whenever the cloud cover thins.

The growing halls are the town’s bright organs. Their panes are patched in several shades of glass, and inside them warm lamps hang over tiers of greens. The windows cloud constantly. From outside, one sees only silhouettes moving among leaves: workers carrying crates, someone kneeling to repair an irrigation hose, a child pressing a hand to the glass. Heat leaks from the halls and makes local weather. In winter, snow withdraws from their walls. In fog, vapor rises from their roof vents and rolls downward through the streets like breath.

The town is industrial, but not triumphant about it. Its engines are old enough to have acquired personalities. Turbine House Three has a low, patient hum that can be felt through the paving stones. The ore lift, which still serves a mine mostly exhausted generations ago, creaks in a particular three-part rhythm before every ascent. A line of electric haulers runs at dawn and dusk between the processing sheds and the mountain rail, their wheels sparking faintly on wet track. The principal factory is no longer a factory in the usual sense. It refines whatever the region still brings down from the high shelves: rare clays, medicinal mosses, mineral salts, scrap from abandoned weather towers, occasionally stone cores extracted from deep fault tunnels. Its chimneys emit little smoke. The fuel is scarce, and the air belongs too much to everyone.

Sound carries strangely in the basin. Most of the time, the city is quiet enough that one can hear water ticking from a gutter onto sheet metal. Doors close softly, as though people have learned not to provoke avalanches, even though the slopes immediately above are old and stable. Then a cable car arrives, and its wheels give a long metallic cry that crosses every roof. A pressure valve opens. A crane rotates. Somewhere within the rock, a pump starts, its vibration travelling through foundations and into bedframes. The settlement is full of work, but its work happens in restrained pulses.

Animals are scarce. The altitude, the crowding, and the weather have kept most creatures at a distance. There are ravens—large, blue-black, and nearly silent—who nest in the inaccessible industrial towers and watch the markets from rooflines. In the evenings they gather around the heat plumes, suspended in the rising air with minimal wingbeats. Small pale moths collect around greenhouse vents. Sometimes a fox comes down from the upper scree, lean and grey, walking the rail bed through town as if it were a natural trail. Its appearance is remarked upon but not pursued. In the wet ravine below the old district, there may be salamanders. No one sees them often enough to be certain, but every spring the maintenance crews find their tiny hand-shaped tracks in the silt.

Beyond the city, the mountain continues in old, enormous systems. There are high plateaus where the rock has been scoured nearly flat, their surfaces pocked with shallow basins holding peat-dark water. There are ridge roads from earlier eras, now partly swallowed by heather and landslide debris, leading to relay stations with no operators. There are abandoned quarry terraces whose walls have become gardens of dripping moss. The whole region seems to be eroding toward a final slow arrangement: cliffs reduced to shoulders, shoulders to slopes, slopes to gravel, gravel to the dark soil accumulating in every sheltered hollow.

Yet nothing is still.

Clouds move constantly through the basin, breaking and reforming against the buildings. Water migrates through stone, pipe, root, and gutter. The hillsides shed pebbles in the night. A retaining wall is always being inspected, a roof patched, a valve turned, a stairway cleared of wet leaves. Plants reclaim one service corridor while another is cut back for a new cable line. The city’s people live inside these negotiations. They build with the expectation that every structure will eventually become part of the mountain’s surface.

After dark, the place does not brighten much. Streetlamps are hooded, their light directed downward onto wet paving. Workshop windows glow in narrow yellow bars. The growing halls shine softly through mist, green and gold. Higher up, red aircraft beacons blink from the weather masts, appearing and disappearing as clouds pass over them.

From the far ridge, the city resembles a cluster of embers lodged in an ancient stone wound: dense, damp, industrious, almost quiet. Behind it, the mountain rises into cloud. Below it, unseen valleys open toward warmer country. And around it, patient in every crevice, the green world waits for water.
