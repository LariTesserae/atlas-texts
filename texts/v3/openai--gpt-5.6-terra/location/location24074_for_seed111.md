At the roofline of the world there is a hollow no larger than a village field, though no village has ever held it. It is cupped between newly lifted ridges of black stone. The mountains around it are not old enough to have softened: their faces are split into blade-thin shelves, raw fractures, and pale seams where the rock has only recently cooled or broken free. Even under the dark, they seem unfinished.

The hollow lies above the weather but not beyond it. Clouds seldom arrive whole. Instead, vapor catches on the cold walls and gathers itself by degrees. Moisture pearls on every exposed edge: on the sharp basalt, on the fur of sleeping animals, on the wiry roots pressing into cracks. A rain may pass through once every few days, a brief soft clatter in the dark, but most water comes as sweat from stone. It slides down in silver threads and vanishes into narrow fissures before reaching any open basin.

There are pools, but they are small and temporary. They gather in stone bowls between the ridges, black as the sky, trembling constantly under the touch of feet, wings, and snouts. Their margins are marked with dark moss, a few gray grasses, low crusts of lichen, and pale fungus that grows where the rock stays wet. Vegetation does not cover this place. It survives in seams. It clings flat against the ground or gathers in sparse, stubborn bristles, as though the wind had combed the land and removed everything soft.

Yet the hollow is crowded.

At first, it seems empty because the darkness has weight. The night here is not merely a lack of sun; it is a blue-black substance filling every crevice. The sky, when it can be seen beyond the surrounding teeth of stone, is close with stars. Their light does not reach the floor of the hollow evenly. It catches only the wet angles, the eyes of animals, the wet tips of noses, the intermittent flash of wings.

Then the place begins to resolve into movement.

There are horned things packed shoulder to shoulder in the lee of the rocks—small, high-backed creatures with shaggy coats darkened by condensation. Their horns curl low and dense around their ears. They do not graze much; there is little to graze. They scrape lichen from stone, chew the roots exposed by frost, and lick mineral water from the walls. Their bodies give off a damp animal heat, and where they cluster, the hollow steams faintly.

Among them move long-legged birds with blunt white faces. They stand in the shallow pools and pick at insects trapped on the surface film, though the insects themselves are strange: slow, wingless, silver-bodied things that emerge from cracks whenever the stone is wet. The birds do not fly often. Their wings are too valuable as blankets. They fold them over themselves and over their chicks, making layered heaps of feather and breathing.

Above, the cliff ledges are thick with other lives. Rock-mice crowd in furred knots. Bats hang inside shallow overhangs, so numerous that the stone looks furred. Pale moths drift between them, larger than a hand, their wings marked like closed eyes. Tiny predators, all teeth and climbing claws, move along the vertical faces in patient routes, testing the cracks. Nothing seems alone. Every patch of shelter is occupied. Every warmer stone has a body pressed against it. Every trickle of water has mouths waiting beneath it.

The sound is immense for such a small place.

It rises not in a single roar but in layers: hoofs ticking on wet stone; claws rasping; a continuous mutter of chewing; bird calls that carry sharply through the thin air; the dry chitter of bats waking and resettling. The horned animals make a low, bell-like complaint to one another, a sound that vibrates in the ribs. At irregular intervals, something enormous calls from beyond the rim—a long, metallic bellow that travels through the mountain rather than across it. The animals freeze when it comes. Even the pools seem to still.

No creature appears to own the hollow, but all of them obey it.

They use the same paths, worn not into soil but into the sheen of damp rock. They gather when the night deepens and loosen only when the eastern sky begins to pale. They do not fight at water unless the water is nearly gone. Predators hunt at the outer edges and rarely enter the center, where too many eyes and horns face outward. The weakest animals sleep in the innermost pockets of warmth; the strong take the cold walls. When rain falls, every creature turns its body toward it, receiving it in fur, feathers, open mouths, folded wings.

There is no sign of a human hand. No cairn, no cut path, no smoke stain, no abandoned tool. Yet some of the animals possess a manner that resembles old craft. The cliff-mice collect shed feathers and press them into cracks around their nests. The horned beasts dislodge stones from certain mineral seams with their horns, returning to the same places until the wall gives. The white-faced birds arrange small bright pebbles around their chicks, not as decoration but perhaps as markers: this hollow is taken, this warmth is guarded, these bodies belong together.

The land itself is still deciding what it will be.

In daylight—when daylight comes at all—it might reveal rust-colored dust on the upper ridges, bluish ice in the deepest cracks, and the green-black shine of wet stone. But for most of its existence the hollow remains in shadow, held under the massive shoulders of the young mountains. Frost gathers and fades. Condensation forms, runs, and forms again. A shelf of stone occasionally gives a sharp report and sheds a scatter of fragments into the dark. The animals pause, listen, and then resume the long work of staying warm.

Nothing here changes quickly. The rocks are young, but their becoming is patient. The water works one drop at a time. The sparse plants lengthen by almost nothing each season. The animals return to the same ledges, pools, and sheltered clefts generation after generation, crowding the small high place with breath and noise.

It is a harsh refuge, but a refuge nonetheless: a dark pocket near the summit of the world, wet at its edges, alive in every available space, and loud enough that the mountain cannot entirely mistake itself for silence.
