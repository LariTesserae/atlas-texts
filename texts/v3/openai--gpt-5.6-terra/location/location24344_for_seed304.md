The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a long afternoon’s walk, where the ground has only recently decided to become land.

All around it, low banks of dark clay rise in uneven shelves. The clay is raw-looking, blue-black when wet and pale at the edges where it has dried and cracked. In several places the banks have collapsed in fresh, clean slices, exposing layers of silt, shell grit, and pale fibrous peat. Water lies immediately under everything. Step off a paved path and the earth gives a little, accepting the heel, then slowly filling the print with cloudy brown water.

There are no broad fields here. There is scarcely space for them. The settlement has been built in the narrow intervals between water: between channels, seepage pools, cistern pits, reed beds, drainage trenches, and the occasional square of ground stubborn enough to bear a house. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, their walls nearly touching across lanes so thin that two people must turn sideways to pass. They are made from what the basin offers—wattle darkened by rain, stacked sod, split alder poles, fired mud brick where the kilns have managed it, and slabs of pale bogwood hauled from the margins.

The roofs are steep despite the low country. Rain is not violent here, usually, but it is frequent, insistent, patient. Water beads on reed thatch and slides along overlapping shale tiles. Every roof has a gutter, every gutter a spout, every spout a jar, barrel, trough, or small carved basin beneath it. Nothing is allowed to fall freely for long. The town is a machine for catching water and persuading it to move elsewhere.

Its streets are not streets in the ordinary sense. Some are plankways laid over sucking mud. Some are ridges of packed gravel bordered by narrow open drains. Others are only sequences of threshold stones: a pale slab before one doorway, two dark planks, a hop over a runnel, then another slab. The whole place is packed with crossings. Little bridges occur every few yards, each made differently—lashed poles, single logs, old boat hulls turned upside down, hand-laid arches of damp brick. A child could learn the town by its bridges alone.

At the center is the Basin, which is not quite a pond and not quite a square. It is a rectangular body of shallow water enclosed on three sides by retaining walls. On the fourth side the wall remains unfinished, or perhaps has repeatedly been washed away. The water level shifts visibly through the day. In dry weather, market stalls stand on the broad stone ledge around it: baskets of smoked eel, clay lamps, bundles of rushes, river mussels, carved spoons, strings of small pale mushrooms grown in cellar trays. After rain, the ledge disappears and the market rearranges itself on raised walkways, as though the town simply has another configuration prepared inside it.

The people build with hand tools and maintain everything constantly. A visitor rarely sees an untouched surface. Someone is always packing mud into a wall seam, clearing weed-choked drains, retying a roof bundle, shaving a warped plank, replacing a broken peg in the boardwalk. Their tools are simple but abundant: stone-headed hammers, bone awls, copper knives worn thin from sharpening, wooden mallets black with use. Even the town’s gates—two heavy frames at the only firm road leading north—are held together by wedges, lashings, and cleverly fitted joints rather than grand hardware.

There is something young about the basin beyond its buildings. The surrounding ground is still moving in minute ways. New rivulets appear after rain. Banks slump. A pool may be opaque and empty one week and threaded with silver minnows the next. At the southern edge, a narrow tongue of pale mineral earth has pushed up through the mud within living memory, forming a crooked little rise. The town has already claimed it. A row of storehouses stands there, raised on short stone feet, their foundations looking faintly provisional against the newly exposed ground.

Vegetation is sparse, but what grows does so fiercely in the wet margins. Reed clumps scratch at the sky. Flat, waxy leaves crowd the rims of stagnant pools. Tufts of sedge thrust up between planks. A few bent willows lean over the channels, their roots exposed like hands gripping the banks. There are no forests nearby, only distant low lines of scrub visible beyond the basin. Wood is valuable. Every beam has been reused, every branch with a useful bend retained. Driftwood is collected with the seriousness other towns reserve for coin.

Animal life is mostly heard or inferred rather than seen. Frogs click under the boards at dusk. Eels leave faint, delicate markings in the mud where the water has withdrawn. Occasionally a marsh heron lands on a roof ridge and stands there with the solemnity of an official. Rats are present, though no one speaks of them. Dogs are uncommon; their paws sink too deeply in the wet lanes, and they dislike the shifting boards. Cats do better, moving from sill to roof to stacked woodpile without touching the ground.

The air is cool enough that breath occasionally shows in the morning, especially above the open drains. It smells of wet clay, smoke, damp straw, and the mineral tang released when fresh earth slumps into water. Indoors, there is the sweeter smell of peat fires. Most windows are small and covered with oiled cloth, thin hide, or shutters pierced in decorative patterns. When the sun appears, it is ordinary sunlight—white, mild, unceremonious—but the town receives it in fragments: a bright pane on standing water, a line along a slick roof, a glint in a gutter, a sudden illumination of steam rising from a drain.

It is mostly quiet. Not silent: never silent. Water makes a thousand restrained sounds—the slow pouring of roof runoff, a channel ticking beneath a grate, droplets falling from eaves long after the rain has stopped. There is the creak of wet timber underfoot, the muted knock of mallet on peg, the whisper of reeds rubbing together. Voices stay low in the tight lanes, perhaps because sound carries strangely here. A call at one end of the town may travel through the drainage channels and emerge, softened and directionless, from beneath another house.

At night, the Basin reflects lamps in broken ribbons. The lamps are squat bowls of oil or rendered marsh fat, set in niches along the paths. Their light does not push far into the dark. It clings to walls, gleams on water, catches on the slick backs of roof tiles. The unlit spaces between buildings feel occupied—not by anything hostile, exactly, but by dampness, stored tools, sleeping people, old boats, covered jars, and all the innumerable small necessities for keeping a settlement afloat on ground that has not yet fully hardened.

The town has laws about water, because it must. No household may block a drain without permission from the ditch-keepers. Rain barrels are marked with a family sign and measured during dry spells. Anything found floating in a public channel belongs first to the person who retrieves it, unless it is a body, a child’s toy, or a marked tool. New construction must leave a hand’s breadth between wall and watercourse, though everyone quietly violates this rule when space grows scarce. The oldest and sternest prohibition is against filling the Basin.

No one entirely remembers why. Some say it is the town’s drinking reserve. Some say it is a sinkhole disguised as a pond, connected to an underground river that would drown the whole basin if sealed. Some say the first houses were built around it because, in the earliest days, it was the only patch of water that did not freeze.

On still mornings, before the work begins, the Basin sometimes gives off a low, almost inaudible bubbling from its unfinished side. The sound is too regular to be quite natural. Then the water trembles, and the reflections of the tightly packed town break apart, reform, and continue as if nothing has happened.
