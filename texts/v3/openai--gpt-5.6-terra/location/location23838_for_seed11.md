The high country has been worn down so long that it no longer resembles a mountain range. It is a roof of stone, immense and nearly level from a distance, lifting above the lower world in pale shelves and softened black ridges. Its edges fall away abruptly in places, not as fresh cliffs but as the exposed grain of something ancient: layer after layer of volcanic rock, red shale, compressed green mineral seams, all rubbed smooth by weather that has had ages to work.

Nothing here looks young except the plants.

They gather wherever the land admits them—along cracks in the stone, at the bottoms of shallow basins, under overhangs where overnight condensation drips steadily from dark ceilings. A person can walk for half a day through open, bare expanses of wind-polished pavement and then come upon a ravine entirely hidden by foliage: broad-leaved trees, ropes of vine, thick ferns growing from walls too steep to climb. The ravines are warm. Mist rises from them in the mornings, though there are few true springs. Much of the water comes sideways, carried in storms, caught in moss, pooled in leaves, stored in porous rock.

Rain is intermittent and never gentle. For weeks the plateau may be dry enough that the ground rings beneath a heel. Then a bank of cloud catches on the high rim and breaks apart over the land. The rain arrives in grey curtains, traveling visibly across the open stone. It fills every seam at once. Dry channels become white rivers, tumbling toward the escarpments. The forested cuts roar. Whole mats of vegetation shake and shed torrents from their leaves.

The sound is the country’s first fact.

Even under a clear sky, wind moves across the high flats with a force that makes speech unreliable. It strikes the stone shelves and is divided into different voices: a low organ-note in the narrow canyons, sharp whistles at the holes in the rock, a deep continuous grinding where loose pebbles are pushed slowly over exposed plates. In storm weather, the land becomes deafening. Water hammers in unseen channels. Thunder rolls back and forth between the distant walls until it seems less like a sound in the air than a pressure inside the bones. Trees in the ravines clash their crowns together. Vines snap. Rocks, loosened from old slopes, fall one by one with immense intervals between them.

The plateau appears empty because it is empty. Its distances are too broad for the life it supports. A lone tree might stand in the middle of a basin, its roots drinking from a pocket of permanent damp far below. A cluster of tall cycads may mark a buried fracture line. Flocks are absent; herds are absent. Now and then, high against the washed-out sky, a long-winged scavenger circles without seeming to beat its wings. Small bright lizards inhabit the warm walls around the wet ravines, and there are frogs whose calls are nearly impossible to hear under the water and wind. Their existence is inferred from motion, from a sudden green body disappearing beneath a leaf.

The people who cross this country do not live widely across it. They keep to a few sheltered places, usually where a ravine opens onto a flat ledge with enough soil for roots and enough stone for walls. Their houses are low, rounded structures of stacked slab and woven reed, built beneath boulders or behind natural rises. From afar they resemble debris left by the weather. Smoke vents through narrow holes and is taken immediately by the wind.

There are no roads in the usual sense. There are remembered routes: lines of cairns, scratched symbols at the bases of cliffs, little windbreaks made from three or four stones. Travelers carry water in stitched hides and polished gourds, but they also know where to find it—the cold dripping mouths beneath overhangs, the tree hollows that stay wet after rain, the dark rock that sweats at dawn. Their tools are simple and carefully maintained: bone awls, stone blades set into wooden handles, copper hooks traded up from lower valleys, heavy mallets for breaking the thin mineral crusts that form around old seepage basins.

At several points on the high flats stand pillars no one living claims to have raised. They are not grand monuments. Most are only waist-high, made from stones fitted so closely that grass grows over their joints. Some have been swallowed by roots. Others lean at improbable angles yet do not fall. During storms, the pillars sing. Wind enters holes deliberately bored through their centers, and each makes a different note: a mournful flute sound, a bark, a metallic hum. When the entire plateau is under cloud, these scattered pillars call to one another over enormous distances.

It is said the old builders used them to speak.

It is also said the land speaks through them now, though it is never clear whether this is belief or observation. The people of the ravines do not camp near a singing pillar if the note changes during the night. They do not cut the trees that grow closest to one. And after the great rains, when fresh streams have written temporary maps across the flats, they walk out at first light to see which pillars remain exposed and which have been covered in washed stone. This tells them, somehow, where the storms will go next.

The sky is ordinary in color—blue when it is open, white and bruised when weather gathers—but it feels close because of the elevation. Clouds move below some of the outer escarpments. Sunlight is clean and hard on the bare stone, while the ravines retain a green twilight even at noon. At evening, warm air rises from the vegetated cuts and meets the colder currents sweeping over the plateau. The result is a constant unmaking of fog: strips of mist form, tear apart, reform in another place. Whole groves briefly emerge from cloud, dripping and luminous, then vanish again.

The land has settled into no final calm despite its age. Its rocks are ancient, its contours reduced nearly to their last durable shapes, but weather keeps working at the surface with restless hands. Water appears, disappears, and appears elsewhere. Vegetation advances wherever it can and is scoured back by flood, wind, or the collapse of a wall. A stone that has sat undisturbed for a thousand years may be moved tomorrow by a rainstorm lasting less than an hour.

To stand there is to feel both things at once: the vast patience of the place, and its unbearable activity. There is almost no one in sight. There may be no animal, no house, no smoke, nothing but green fissures in old stone and the far drop of the world beyond the rim.

Yet the air is full of motion. The ground is full of hidden water. And the silence, when it comes between gusts and thunder, feels less like peace than the held breath of something enormous.
