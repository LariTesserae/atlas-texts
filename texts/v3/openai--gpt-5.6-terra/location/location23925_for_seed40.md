At this altitude, the brightness is not sunlight exactly. It is a pressure in the air.

The valley has been manufactured from horizon to horizon: a basin of poured pale material, ribbed with retaining walls and cut through by service corridors, freight lines, heat pipes, and slow canals of condensation. There is no ground beneath the construction that anyone can reach. What looks like soil at a distance is ground rubber, crushed brick, cooled slag, dark compost manufactured in sealed municipal vats and spread in narrow beds for the animals. The mountains around the basin are mountains only in outline. Their faces are tiered warehouses, ventilation towers, clinker silos, stacked substations, and immense inclined panels whose purposes have been forgotten by all but the maintenance guilds.

Everything is newly sharp.

The outer walls have crisp corners. Drainage channels run in hard blue-gray lines. The high slopes are scored with fresh-cut stairways and conveyor trenches, not yet softened by rust or dust. Even the cracks look recent: bright seams in the white aggregate, showing raw metal mesh underneath. The whole place seems less built than extruded, pushed steadily upward by a factory somewhere below the basin. At night—or what passes for night, in the brief dimming between shifts—the distant ridges give off a deep, almost inaudible structural ticking, as if the valley is cooling by fractions.

It is crowded, though not noisy.

Dense neighborhoods cling to the walls in terraces, their dwellings packed close beneath mirrored awnings. The roofs overlap like scales. Between them, pedestrian bridges cross six or seven levels at once, enclosed in clear plastic tubes clouded by breath and damp. Laundry hangs from cable frames, but the cloth is all gray, blue, silver, and industrial orange; bright colors are reserved for warning symbols, official doors, and the collars of certain animals. There are markets in the old loading bays, schools in former turbine halls, small shrines built around obsolete control panels. The people move through it all with the deliberate economy of a place where every sound carries farther than it ought to.

Machinery is everywhere, but it works with astonishing restraint. Tall cranes make a single adjustment over the course of an hour. Freight elevators descend without a hum, their counterweights hidden in black shafts. Vast brush-wheeled sweepers crawl along the main avenues, gathering mineral dust and feathers and scraps of packaging into their bellies. Pump houses breathe steam through latticed vents. A line of locomotives waits for days under a roof of glass, all their lamps burning, all their engines warm, without departing.

The quiet is not absence. It is compression.

There are tiny noises: a chain finding tension, water condensing in a gutter, the papery clatter of thousands of wings settling on a cable. From an open workshop comes the occasional careful blow of a hammer. The valley has learned to keep its volume low. Loud work happens below, in the levels no longer marked on public maps.

Moisture arrives irregularly. Some weeks the air is dry enough to sting the eyes and leave a silver crust on lips. Then a weather engine on the western rim releases a bank of cold cloud, and rain falls only in selected districts: clean vertical rain over the condenser gardens, mist over the animal yards, a controlled drizzle on the slopes where dust must be suppressed. Water gathers in shallow basins along the transit platforms. It runs through brass gratings and vanishes. In the morning, condensation pearls on every rail, every handhold, every wire. The city-valley shines as though varnished.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. There are strips of lichen engineered to grip concrete, flat black mosses in the shadowed drainage gullies, and thin gardens of reedlike plants growing from nutrient troughs outside the filtration stations. Their leaves are narrow, metallic, and always moving slightly in the high air. A few trees have been attempted: clipped, columnar forms rooted in enormous ceramic planters. They are regarded with affection and suspicion. Their bark flakes in sheets of pale polymer; their fruit is not eaten, but collected by children and traded for small machine parts.

The animals thrive where the plants do not.

Pigeons are innumerable, though they are larger than ordinary pigeons and have pale bands of reflective scales at the throat. They roost in the warm seams of the buildings and rise in sheets whenever a maintenance siren flashes—not sounds, flashes. Foxes move along the cable corridors after dimming, lean animals with soot-dark paws and bright tag-rings around their ears. There are goats in the high terraces, bred to eat insulation fungus and the roots of invasive moss. Their bells have been removed generations ago. In the lower canals live long white fish, blind but vigorous, feeding on flakes from the water-treatment membranes.

And there are things called chimney swifts, though they do not resemble birds. They are soft, many-winged creatures, gray as wet paper, that nest inside the warm exhaust stacks. In flight they fold and unfold so slowly that they appear to drift upward against gravity. Their nests are protected by ordinance. No one agrees whether they came from outside, or whether the valley made them in response to its own need for living things.

The rules here have accumulated around such uncertainties.

No one may enter an unlit corridor alone.

No one is permitted to name an unfinished building after a person, because unfinished buildings sometimes continue to grow and carry the name with them.

If rain begins indoors, residents are required to open all internal doors and wait for the water to choose a route. Redirecting it is considered dangerous. The water is not always water once it has crossed three thresholds.

At the highest elevation, beyond the final housing terraces, the construction ceases to pretend it is a city. There are broad white plains of roof and platform, intersected by black expansion joints. Brightness pools there, almost solid. The air is cold enough that breath appears immediately and hangs in a person’s wake. Herds of goats move between vent towers. Above them, enormous factory chimneys stand empty, their mouths ringed with frost.

Beyond the chimneys are the new hills.

They rose over the last seventeen years, gradually lifting from the flat rooflands in folded, geometric ridges. Engineers say they are pressure accumulations in the underlying support structure. Priests say the basin has begun to remember the mountains it replaced. The hills are faced in smooth ceramic panels, but their slopes buckle by millimeters each season. Fresh creases appear. Long flights of stairs, once level, develop a tilt. Rainwater runs uphill along certain seams.

Nobody panics about this. The changes are too slow, too beautiful, and too bureaucratically anticipated. Surveyors visit with bright poles and take measurements. New handrails are installed. A market relocates one terrace downhill. People make jokes about property values on the moving slopes.

On very clear days, standing at the upper edge of the valley, one can see the entire made world laid out below: white roofs, wet channels, rail loops, stacked dwellings, animal yards, gardens in troughs, clouds released by machinery, and the gleaming threads of bridges. It looks complete. It looks permanent.

Then one notices that the farthest ridge is not in the same place it was yesterday.

Not much. The width of a hand. Perhaps less.

But it has moved.
