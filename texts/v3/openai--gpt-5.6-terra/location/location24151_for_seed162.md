The high country has only recently decided to be land.

Its mountains are still lifting. Not in the slow, dignified manner of old ranges, softened by centuries and named by people, but visibly: whole black escarpments rise through the cloud layer in long, shuddering breaths. At dawn—though dawn is difficult to distinguish beneath the permanent bronze overcast—the ground sometimes gives a gentle upward lurch, and a new seam of warm stone appears across a valley where there was only moss the night before. The peaks are sharp enough to look unfinished. Their flanks are vitrified in places, glossy with rapidly cooled mineral flow, and elsewhere packed with raw, pale rubble that has not yet learned how to become soil.

The elevation is immense. Much of the landscape stands above the ordinary weather, but not above moisture. Clouds do not arrive here from elsewhere so much as condense around the mountains’ shoulders. They catch on ridges, gather in the folds of basalt, and linger as dim, wet vapor. Rain falls only occasionally, in brief vertical threads, but every surface sweats. Under overhangs, clear drops grow slowly from stone, swelling for hours before the tremors shake them loose. In cracks and volcanic hollows, pools form—warm, mineral-rich, faintly luminous under the dark sky. There are no rivers of any settled kind. Water enters the world in pockets, runs a few miles through a fresh ravine, disappears into a steaming fissure, and perhaps returns as cloud from some gorge a hundred leagues away.

The light is low and strangely distributed. The sky is not black, not gray, but the color of old iron submerged in tea. A dense, high atmosphere reduces the sun to a wide pallor behind cloud. There are no hard shadows. Even at the brightest hour, every distant ridge seems seen through deep water.

Yet the land is warm.

Heat comes from below: broad zones of breathing earth, volcanic chambers close to the surface, beds of dark stone that remain warm after the clouds have cooled everything else. The air in some valleys is almost tropical. Ferns grow beside mineral chimneys. Leaves as broad as sails spread from beds of red-brown humus gathered in faults. Vines climb newly exposed cliffs with impossible speed, fastening themselves into cracks before the rock can collapse. Vegetation is not continuous enough to make a forest, but wherever water and shelter coincide it becomes excessive, urgent, dense as a crowd.

There are thickets of needle-leafed trees whose trunks are soft and fibrous, filled with hot vapor. When a slope shifts, they bend rather than break, then root again wherever they fall. There are cushions of amber moss, swollen with condensed cloudwater, that cover entire ledges and release thin, fragrant steam when stepped upon. Along the mineral pools grow black reeds with silver undersides, always trembling though the air is still. Their roots are webbed through the mud in great conductive mats. During electrical weather they carry a blue current from one pool to another, and their seed-heads flash silently in sequence across the valley.

The landscape has industry, but no builders.

Nothing has been erected. No road cuts the hills. No pylons, no roofs, no discarded tools, no walls stand against the wind. Still, the country works with the exhausting rhythm of a factory.

In the warm basins, colonies of immense shelled organisms knead mud and ore through their bodies. They are low, round, and ridged like mobile furnaces. Their mouths open beneath them, taking in stones, fungi, roots, carrion, and mineral water; their backs release clean plates of dark metal-like shell. Hundreds may move through a basin in parallel, leaving behind compacted terraces that gleam faintly in the dimness. Their movement is nearly noiseless. Only a subtle grinding felt through the soles of the feet marks their passage.

On cliffs, white larvae live in the heated cracks and extrude long strands of ceramic material. The strands harden in the air, spanning gaps, binding loose rock together, or dangling in curtains from ridge to ridge. These are not bridges, though animals use them. They are the accidental exhaust of digestion. Whole slopes are draped in their pale filaments, and when the mountain trembles, the filaments sing below the threshold of hearing: a pressure in the teeth, a throb in the sternum.

Far above, where the cloud ceiling catches against the youngest summits, immense winged creatures circle in the dark. They are neither birds nor bats, though their silhouettes suggest both. Their wings have many joints and translucent membranes threaded with reddish heat. They rarely cry. They glide through the dim layers, feeding on airborne spores, electrical charge, and perhaps the fine mineral dust thrown up by the rising land. In groups they can alter the weather. Their wings beat moisture out of a cloud bank; their passage through charged vapor can turn a misty afternoon into a storm of silent blue branching light.

Fauna is everywhere, though it does not announce itself.

Small, many-legged grazers move through the moss in waves, each one carrying a tiny garden of fungus on its back. Pale climbing animals nest in the vine mats, their faces hidden behind folding frills. Long-bodied predators lie motionless in hot pools, mistaken for roots until their eyes open beneath the surface. In the upper screes, packs of angular, hopping things hunt among the fresh talus, eating the soft-bodied burrowers that live under warm stones. Their feet are padded with a black, glassy substance; when they leap from boulder to boulder, there is no clatter.

Even the airborne life is crowded. Flecks drift through the mist, some seeds, some insects, some organisms too small to resolve until they collect on the skin. Their presence makes the air seem thick, populated. In certain valleys, every breath carries spores that give dreams to sleeping animals. These dreams are not private. A herd resting beneath the same overhang may wake at once and begin walking in the same direction, as if following a route remembered by the ground.

The silence is not peace.

The country is mostly quiet because its violence is too large, too deep, or too gradual to make ordinary sound. Mountains rise with the hush of pressure. Clouds boil without thunder. Whole forests slide down fresh slopes with only the faint tearing of roots. The loudest common noise is water: a drop releasing from a ceiling of stone, a warm stream ticking over black pebbles, the soft patter of condensation falling through leaves.

Then, at irregular intervals, the world changes.

A ridge may turn inside out.

This happens most often after several days of oppressive stillness. The sky lowers. The warm pools cease steaming. Animals gather in dense knots under ledges or descend into crevices. The ground becomes subtly reflective, as though a sheet of dark water lay beneath the soil. Then an entire mountainside opens along a line that had not been there. Rock folds outward, not breaking but behaving like muscle. From within comes a terrain that cannot quite be understood: sheets of green-black stone moving like liquid; root systems thousands of feet thick; bright cavities full of drifting lights; a wind blowing upward, carrying the smell of rain from a place that may be years away.

For a few minutes, gravity loses confidence around the opening. Stones roll uphill. Pools rise in beads from their basins and hang beneath the clouds. Flocks of winged creatures circle without flapping, all facing the same impossible direction. Plants along the fissure flower at once, producing fleshy blossoms that open like eyes and close before anyone could call the event a season.

Afterward, the mountain is different. A new spur stands where a valley was. A lake may have become a field of steaming salt. An old grove may be found hanging vertically from a cliff, its roots growing into open air, each leaf turned toward the ground as though that were still where the sun lived.

Nothing here is permanent enough to be ruined. Nothing has been built, so nothing can be abandoned. The landscape is always in its first age.

To travel through it would be to accept that maps are only weather reports from the past. One could follow a line of warm pools upward for days, moving through dripping fern country and fields of raw obsidian, then find the route cut off by a mountain born overnight. One could shelter in a grove and wake to discover that the grove has migrated, its soft-trunked trees dragging themselves slowly across the slope in search of a hotter seam. One could stand in the dim air above a vast basin and see, through gaps in cloud, hundreds of miles of living terrain processing itself: growth feeding heat, heat feeding motion, motion breaking stone, broken stone becoming soil, soil taken at once by roots and mouths.

The place does not seem hostile. It does not seem welcoming either.

It is simply too busy becoming to notice who passes through it.
