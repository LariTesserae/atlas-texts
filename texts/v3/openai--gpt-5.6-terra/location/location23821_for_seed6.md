At the height where clouds usually break apart, there is a drowned works.

It occupies the crown of a narrow mountain ridge: not a city, not quite a factory, but a compact, complete made place pressed into a basin no larger than a small harbor. The mountain beneath it is invisible. Perhaps it was cut away long ago. Perhaps the structure itself is the mountain now. Every surface is constructed: riveted iron terraces, pale ceramic retaining walls, lattices of greened copper, concrete ribs, black glass reservoirs, service gantries with their lower halves submerged. Water fills the whole arrangement almost to its rooflines.

Nothing here has been worn smooth. The place is young, or has only recently been opened to the sky. Its edges are keen. Staircases descend abruptly into clear blue-green depths. Fresh welds shine beneath the slow-moving water. The corners of sluice houses and pump towers stand hard against the clouds, each one too precise to feel ancient, even when curtains of moss have begun to gather along their seams.

The water is not a lake in the ordinary sense. It has rooms in it.

It lies between walls, inside galleries, along the undersides of bridges. It rises through circular shafts in old turbine halls. It hangs as thick vertical panes inside certain open frames, held there by a pressure system no visitor can see. A walkway may begin in ordinary damp air and, three steps later, continue through a weightless wall of water, the handrails vanishing into it. Beyond the watery threshold are chambers where the light bends and trembles, and trees grow with their roots in flooded machinery.

Vegetation has taken every allowance the construction gives it. Broad-leaved vines spill from ventilation grilles. Ferns roof the maintenance corridors. Reedlike plants emerge from overflow channels, their stems polished by current. In the flooded courts, warm-water lilies open in the stillness, each leaf dark and immaculate, their roots threaded through steel mesh below. There are trees, too—small, dense-crowned things with waxy leaves—growing from engineered pockets in the upper platforms. Their branches are packed so closely among pipes and signal masts that from a distance the works seems to wear a green fur.

The warmth is surprising at this elevation. Steam escapes from thin relief valves and coils among the treetops. It gathers beneath the metal ceilings of covered decks, condenses, and falls back into the reservoir in patient drops. The air smells of wet stone, hot metal, leaf mold, and the faint mineral tang of electrical insulation. Condensation beads on every railing. The whole place feels recently rained upon, though the rain, if it comes, has nowhere to land that is not already full.

There are no birds in the towers. No insects stitch the air over the water. No fish disturb the submerged streets.

The absence is not dramatic at first. The plants make the place seem alive enough. Leaves shift in currents of warm exhaust. Root systems steadily prise apart grout lines. Algae paint the underwater walls in soft emerald gradients. Yet no small movement answers the eye. A fallen leaf may rest on the water for days before drifting into a grille. The lilies open and close without visitors. Fruit forms in the high planters, ripens, drops, and floats pale and untouched through the lower halls.

The works is densely occupied, but not by inhabitants. Every inch has a purpose. Pipes run beside pipes. Catwalks cross catwalks. Old control cabinets have been built into retaining walls; retaining walls conceal cables; cables enter glassy black columns that descend into the flood. There are gauges everywhere, their faces dry behind thick circular windows. Most needles hold steady. Some tremble almost invisibly.

The rules of the place are gentle but absolute.

Water cannot be fully excluded. A sealed door may keep it out for a while, but eventually a bead will appear at a hinge, then a thread, then a silent, vertical seam of water crossing the floor. Conversely, water cannot freely escape either. It is retained by the architecture’s intention: square basins hold it, curved basins hold it, and even broken-open chambers continue to contain it as though remembering their former walls. Pools may stand high above the surrounding decks with no visible barrier, their surfaces level and quiet.

Plants obey the water more readily than gravity. Roots seek submerged conduits, climbing toward them through air. Vines often grow downward from flooded ceilings. Where a pipe carries heated water, leaves gather around it in layered collars, making the pipes appear to have sprouted green insulation. Nothing grows outside the built world, because there is no outside soil. Still, the vegetation is so lush that it has begun manufacturing its own ground: mulch caught in gratework, moss packed into bolt recesses, black loam accumulating in the bowls of abandoned machinery.

The machinery remains active, though no one has been seen tending it.

Deep below, pumps turn at a rate too slow to hear. The water rises a finger’s width through one sector over the course of a week, then lowers elsewhere. Certain lamps brighten toward noon and dim toward evening, even under cloud cover. Elevator cages occasionally travel through flooded shafts, their cables pulling them upward without splash or wake. At night, a row of industrial windows may glow far beneath the water, rectangular and yellow, revealing nothing but suspended leaves and the smooth sides of drowned equipment.

Sound is kept low here, as if the complex has been designed not to disturb the water. There is the quiet tick of cooling metal. The minute plink of condensation. A distant, padded throb from a pump chamber. In some passages, water presses against glass with a nearly inaudible soft roar, like a sea heard through sleep. But there is no crash, no flood-rush, no wind loud enough to overcome the hush.

In clear weather, the surrounding world appears only in fragments beyond the upper parapets: peaks beneath cloud, a vast white weather system rolling below, a brightness on the horizon. The drowned works sits above it all, warm and wet and crowded with green, a small manufactured watershed lifted into the cold altitudes.

It does not seem ruined. Ruin implies abandonment by a purpose.

This place has simply entered the phase for which it was made: a high reservoir of rooms and gardens, engines and flooded passages, where water is not an accident but the principal resident, and everything else—steel, concrete, leaf, light—has learned to live pressed closely around it.
