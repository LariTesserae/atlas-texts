The city sits high in a basin that is not quite a valley and not quite a crater: a broad, shallow bowl among young black ridges, where the stone has only recently learned the shape of weather. The mountains around it are angular, their slopes broken into slabs and needles, their cuts too clean to seem ancient. In several places the ridgelines end abruptly, as if they rose from the earth and then stopped at a command.

Nothing in this country continues changing.

Rain hangs in the air but does not fall. It gathers beneath eaves, along cables of woven grass, in the cupped leaves of shrubs, and remains there: perfect clear beads that never grow large enough to drop. Mist lies in the low channels between terraces, a pale, knee-high wool, neither thinning in daylight nor deepening after dark. The city’s cisterns are always half-full. Their water is cold, dark, and motionless even when scooped; a rippling surface becomes smooth before the hand has fully withdrawn.

The people call this condition mercy. They have no word for drought, no real memory of flood. Their gardens do not need seasons. Every plant stands at the exact point between bud and decline.

Vegetation gathers wherever stone gives it an edge. The basin floor is mostly pale gravel and hard-packed volcanic dust, open enough that one can see from district to district. But green has claimed the margins: low silver-leaved trees in long gullies, broad dark moss along retaining walls, thickets of fern growing from the cracks in the black rock. Tall blue reeds surround the still pools. Their stems are stiff as carved bone, and each carries a little skin of unfallen rain along its upper joints.

The city is extensive but not crowded. It has been built as a spread of precincts across the basin, with generous gaps of garden, animal-ground, quarry, and empty court between them. From above, its neighborhoods resemble islands arranged around dry channels.

Most buildings are made from stone pried from the nearby slopes: basalt blocks, gray tuff, and a soft porous red rock that can be cut with bronze blades and polished with sand. The walls are thick. Their corners are rounded not by erosion but by generations of hands rubbing past them. Roofs are low-pitched and layered with slate, bark, and woven reed mats, designed less to shed rain than to catch the perpetual condensation. Every house has gutters, though the gutters almost never run. They lead into narrow ceramic jars, then into communal cistern courts.

There are higher structures near the basin’s center: watchtowers, grain halls, aviaries, shrines, and the old water houses. None reaches very far into the sky. At this elevation, the sky feels low already—an immense cool ceiling, blue-white by day, star-crowded by night. The towers are built wide and deliberate, with external stairs and platforms wrapped in rope railings. On many of their roofs grow gardens of lichen, herbs, and dwarf fruit trees, held in beds of black soil.

The city’s technology is old in the hands and ingenious in the arrangement. Wheels exist, but are scarce; the streets are too stepped and uneven for carts except on the few broad causeways crossing the basin floor. Goods move on sledges, shoulder poles, pack animals, and shallow wooden trays dragged over smooth stone. Copper and bronze are used for knives, adzes, needles, and the ceremonial bells that ring only when touched, because there is no wind strong enough to stir them. Most construction is achieved with wedges, levers, stone mauls, braided cord, and patient labor.

There are no mills. Nothing turns.

Instead, the city has lifting frames, counterweighted gates, stone presses operated by teams, and treadle looms. All mechanisms are direct, visible, and human-powered. The inhabitants favor things that can be stopped exactly where they are. A door is not simply shut; it is set into its proper unmoving place. A jar is not filled to the brim; it is filled to the line that has always been sufficient.

Animals thrive in the empty spaces between districts.

Long-haired goats browse the green seams of the upper terraces. Their horns grow in flattened spirals, and their coats collect bright droplets of condensation until they look dusted in glass. Small mountain deer feed in the reed marshes, nibbling without ever visibly reducing the plants. Burrowing animals—pale marmots, black-faced rock rabbits, thick-tailed mouse-like things—live among the retaining walls and old quarry piles. They are tame enough to cross streets in daylight, though they do so quietly, with the solemnity of citizens.

Birds are everywhere, more numerous than people in some quarters. Dark swifts nest under roof tiles and circle the towers in slow, repeatable paths. Broad-winged ravens ride the updrafts from the hot cracks in the young mountains; though the land is cool, some fissures still breathe a faint mineral warmth. In the gardens, small green-and-amber birds hang from reeds and drink the suspended drops of water one bead at a time.

There are larger creatures beyond the walls. Herds of shaggy, long-necked grazers move among the high slopes, though “move” may be too strong a word. They can be seen in the same places for days: one silhouetted on a black ridge, one drinking from a pool, one with its head bent into a patch of moss. When the city’s hunters return with meat, they speak not of chasing but of approaching. Hunting here is a matter of finding the moment that has already made room for you.

The people are not silent, but the place encourages quiet. Sound does not travel far across the basin; it seems to settle into the damp air and the porous stone. A conversation at one end of a market court becomes a soft blur before it reaches the fountain at the other end. Footsteps are muted by dust, moss, wool, and woven reed. The ordinary sounds are intimate: the tap of a stone chisel, a goat’s teeth on reeds, the dry brush of fabric against a doorway, someone rinsing a bowl in a water trough that never splashes.

The market itself is spread through several long courts rather than concentrated in one square. There are racks of root vegetables that do not shrivel, baskets of bitter berries, smoked fish brought up from the lower country, salt cakes, pottery, hides, woven capes, and obsidian tools sharp enough to reflect an eye. Vendors sit beneath awnings striped in undyed wool and faded mineral reds. Prices are marked with little stacks of pebbles, shell disks, or knotted cords. Bargaining exists, but it has a ritual quality: no one is hurried; no fruit spoils; no afternoon presses toward evening in any meaningful way.

Yet there is daylight and darkness. The sun crosses the basin in its familiar course. Shadows pivot around towers, slip across courtyards, and climb walls. Stars appear. Fires glow in windows. But the changes do not accumulate. A flame burns without consuming its fuel. A harvested garden bed returns not by growth but because, at some unknowable interval, it is simply once again full. A child’s footprint remains on a dusty stair until stepped over, and then the dust resumes its old unmarked surface.

The city has rules built around this stability.

No one is permitted to dismantle the oldest retaining walls. Not because they are sacred alone, but because each stone has become part of the basin’s agreement with itself. New buildings may be erected only in designated empty ground, and the builders must leave a hand’s-width of air between new work and old work. This prevents structures from “taking hold,” as the citizens say. Too much contact between things is believed to invite a kind of permanence more absolute than the ordinary kind.

They bury their dead in high chambers cut into the soft red rock. Bodies do not decay. For this reason, burial places are sealed with fitted stone plugs and marked only by a shallow groove in the cliff. The dead are not gone; they are simply kept apart from the daily arrangement of the city.

At the center of the basin lies the oldest court, paved in black glassy slabs. A low circular pool occupies its middle. Its surface reflects the sky with unnerving accuracy, though no breeze, bird, or falling leaf ever troubles it. Around the pool stand twelve stone posts, each capped with a bronze bowl. The bowls hold rainwater gathered long ago, or perhaps still gathering now. The water in them is said to reflect not the sky above the city but the world as it might be if time were allowed to proceed.

Almost nobody looks into them for long.

Beyond the city, the country opens in terraces and dark uplands. There are young lava fields where the rock remains folded in ropes and whorls, sharp enough to cut leather. There are hanging meadows crowded with low flowers, each bloom open forever at the same degree. There are narrow ravines full of damp green shade, and high passes where the cold is clean and thin enough to taste. In the distance, peaks rise through bands of cloud that never entirely form or disappear.

It is a living place, but not a growing one. It is wet, but never raining. It is young, but never aging. Its streets hold the marks of innumerable feet without wearing down; its gardens remain abundant without advancing; its animals breed, feed, sleep, and die according to patterns no outsider can easily observe.

The people endure no urgency except the urgency they make for themselves. They carve, weave, trade, sing softly, raise children, tend animals, maintain walls, and walk the same long paths between neighborhoods. They have learned that a life need not move forward to become deep.

From the northern ridge, the whole basin can be seen at once: the scattered dark roofs, the green seams, the pale roads, the thin smoke that rises straight and remains suspended over cooking courts. The city appears less built than placed—carefully set among stone, water, moss, and quiet creatures—like a collection of useful objects arranged on the floor of a vast, cool room.
