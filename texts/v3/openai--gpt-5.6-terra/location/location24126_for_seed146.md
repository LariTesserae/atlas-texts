Beneath a crust of ordinary stone lies a country with no horizon in the usual sense. It is not a cave, though those who enter first call it one. A cave implies enclosure: a ceiling, walls, an eventual end. This place is instead an underworld spread sideways for distances that make maps useless. Its roof is a continent of black basalt, somewhere overhead in the dark. Its floor descends in shelves and basins, then rises again in abrupt mineral ridges. Entire mountain ranges have grown downward here, their peaks hanging from the ceiling, their fluted slopes stopping just short of the ground.

The air is warm and dry enough to chap the lips. It tastes faintly of copper, hot stone, and the bitter dust of ground shells. There is no water—not a drip, not a damp seam, not even the cool mineral smell that precedes a hidden pool. The local people, if people is the word, tell of water in the same tone surface-dwellers use for extinct gods. They know it as a theory: a thing that falls, gathers, reflects light. Their tools have never had a use for it.

The terrain is young. Nothing has been softened. Every broken surface is raw, every ridge sharp as a newly struck blade. The floor is made of interlocking plates of dark red stone, glassy black extrusions, and pale mineral foam that has hardened in clustered bubbles. Broad faults divide the plain into steps several body-lengths high. Their faces shine in the darkness, not from moisture but from thin deposits of mica and salt-metal. In places, the earth seems to have cooled too quickly: frozen ripples of molten rock retain the appearance of movement without moving at all.

There are no plants. No roots pry at cracks, no moss darkens shaded walls, no pollen drifts through the air. Yet the place is crowded with life.

At first this abundance is invisible. A traveler sees only stone, and hears almost nothing beyond the faint rasp of their own breath. Then their lamp goes out, or their eyes become accustomed to the dim radiance leaking from the walls, and presences resolve all around them.

They occupy every ledge, crevice, pillar, and exposed seam.

There are pale, jointed grazers that feed on crystalline deposits, their mouths fitted with nested rings of grinding teeth. They move in long, deliberate files across the floor, following mineral bands no human eye can detect. Their hides are armored in overlapping tiles of chalk and dark horn. Their feet make no sound on the rock. There are low, many-limbed things, warm as kiln bricks, pressed in heaps beneath overhangs. They derive energy from heat and trace chemicals rising through the stone, and their bodies are veined with dim orange channels, as though each contains a buried ember.

Higher up, on the hanging mountains, cling thin-bodied predators with membranes stretched between their limbs. They do not fly. There is no wind here for flight, and they have no need to hurry. They descend only when prey has reached the exact place it will reach. Their patience appears supernatural, but it is merely native to a world where almost nothing changes.

The smallest animals are the strangest. Grain-sized burrowers live inside the porous mineral foam. They digest sulfurous gases and leave behind intricate tracery: lattices, spirals, repeating angles, and occasionally patterns that resemble script. Whole slopes are covered in their work. From a distance these slopes resemble carved city walls; from close by they are living colonies, the accumulated refuse and architecture of countless soft, blind mouths.

Nothing grows, exactly. Nothing flowers, decays, migrates, or visibly reproduces. Life here seems to maintain itself by substitution. An old creature does not die in the quick, wet way of surface life. Its body gradually mineralizes, becoming still and dense, and a smaller creature emerges from a seam in its carapace or beneath one of its plates. The distinction between animal, shell, nest, tool, and stone is often impossible to make.

This gives the underworld a peculiar quiet density. Thousands of creatures may be within arm’s reach, but all are economizing motion. An entire herd can stand around a warm fissure without shifting for days. Predators remain attached to walls, their heads lowered, for years. Small scavengers circle a dead body so slowly that their paths can only be recognized by comparing the dust patterns from one season of travel to the next.

There are seasons, perhaps, but no one has witnessed them. The temperature is always warm. The air pressure is always the same. The deep mineral light never brightens or fades. There are no drafts, no storms, no distant thunder. A thrown pebble clatters extravagantly, almost obscenely, and its echoes travel away through the chambers until they are swallowed by scale.

Light comes from several sources, none comforting. Long cracks in the ceiling emit a dull violet-black glow, like the afterimage of lightning seen through closed eyelids. Some walls contain broad seams of blue mineral that shine without heat. The light does not illuminate so much as outline: a ridge edge, the curve of a shell, the polished eye of an animal. Most distances remain hidden. The land is therefore vast not because one can see it, but because it refuses to disclose its boundaries. A distant shape may be a boulder, a settlement, a herd, or the folded leg of something larger than a house.

Here and there, scattered across the plain and embedded in the rock shelves, are built things.

They are sparse enough that one can walk for a week without finding one, but each has the gravity of a landmark. A low dome made from fitted plates of volcanic glass. A narrow bridge of bone-white stone spanning a fault. Rings of stakes driven into the ground, their points capped with copper that has oxidized into a dry green powder. Cairns arranged not upward but horizontally, forming lines that lead into blackness. Small ovens, still warm despite having no fuel. Pits lined with polished scales.

The builders use crude tools: hammered copper, sharpened obsidian, mallets of dense shell, cords braided from animal tendon. Their workmanship is simple but exact. They do not build high; the ceiling makes heights feel presumptuous. They build close to the earth, sometimes partly inside it. Their shelters resemble the shells of local animals, though whether the resemblance is imitation or shared ancestry is unclear.

The inhabitants themselves are rarely seen. There are marks of their passage: handprints pressed into soft mineral paste and baked hard, stone blades wedged upright beside trails, rows of tiny carved disks placed at the mouths of fissures. Some structures contain seats, but no beds. Some contain bowls, though there is nothing liquid to put in them. In the largest settlements, thousands of little chambers open from a central warm shaft; each chamber holds a single tool, a shed skin, a collection of teeth, or a shaped stone that may be an idol.

Their rule is inscribed in several places, always in the same plain geometric forms. Scholars from the surface have translated it imperfectly:

**Do not wake the heat.  
Do not call downward.  
Do not make the dark answer.**

No one knows what waking the heat entails. Fires are used only in sealed ovens, and never for light. Voices are subdued. Even children, if there are children, are taught to communicate by tapping a fingertip against stone: one tap for food, two for danger, three for absence. Loud speech is not forbidden in the manner of a taboo. It is simply regarded as an irresponsible manipulation of the landscape, like breaking a dam.

Sometimes, in the far reaches of the underplain, there are formations that appear to be listening.

They rise from the floor as clusters of tall black rods, each as thick as a tree trunk and faceted like a crystal. They are warm to the touch. No animal feeds on them or shelters beside them. The local people leave offerings there: ground shell, copper scraps, carefully broken tools. The rods never react. Yet no one strikes them, and no one walks between them.

The world has remained like this for longer than its inhabitants can measure. Its fresh edges do not weather. Its tracks persist in dust. A tool dropped beside a trail may be found in the same place generations later, lightly coated in mineral bloom. The life of the underplain does not battle time; it inhabits an arrangement in which time has little leverage.

That is its deepest strangeness. The land feels newly made, but not temporary. Its stone looks as though it cooled yesterday. Its creatures move as though they have always known exactly where to stand. The darkness is not empty. It is dense with patient bodies, fixed customs, warm breath, hidden teeth, and an immense silence carefully maintained beneath the world.
