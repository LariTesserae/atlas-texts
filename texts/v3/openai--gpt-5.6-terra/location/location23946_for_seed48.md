The city sits almost level with the sea, though the sea is not visible from most streets. It is behind the levees, beyond the freight yards and the long black backs of storage tanks. The ground has no confidence in itself. It sweats through cracks in concrete. Brine rises in the inspection pits. After rain, shallow water remains in every low place and never quite evaporates, even in the heat.

There are no trees.

Nothing grows from the pavement margins, no grass pushes between curbstones, no weed softens the walls. The soil is too salty, or too hot, or has been paved and repaved too many times for a root to find a beginning. The few open lots are fields of pale mud, broken pallets, collapsed chain-link fencing, and drying mineral crust. In summer the crust wrinkles white around the edges of puddles. At noon it can glare, but most days the light is subdued by a low ceiling of cloud and industrial haze. Everything is rendered in wet gray, rust red, oil-black, and the dull yellow of old sodium lamps.

The city is large enough to have districts, but too empty to feel crowded. Warehouses stand with whole loading bays sealed shut. A municipal pumping station operates beside a six-lane road where traffic comes only in brief, purposeful convoys: tankers, fuel lorries, buses carrying shift workers, flatbeds with corroded machinery strapped under tarps. Rail lines cross the flats on raised embankments. Their signals burn through the dimness—red, green, amber—over stretches of track that seem to go nowhere.

At the center is the refinery, or perhaps several refineries grown together over a century. Its pipes pass above streets in silver bundles. Its towers are softened by steam. Cooling water runs constantly through concrete channels, warm enough that fog forms where it meets rainwater. The plant is not loud from a distance. Its noise is held inside its walls: a far, continuous furnace-breath, the occasional release of pressure, the click of metal contracting after a storm. The pumps are the deepest sound in the city, but they are so steady that people cease to hear them.

The animals have taken possession of the silence.

They are everywhere, and they behave as though the industrial landscape was made for them. Gulls occupy rooftops by the thousands, standing shoulder to shoulder along parapets and ventilation hoods. In the early morning they cover the flat warehouse roofs like a shifting layer of ash. Cormorants gather on pipe racks above the drainage canals, wings spread in the humid air. Pigeons live inside the rafters of empty distribution halls, where their cooing is amplified into a soft, architectural murmur.

Closer to the ground, the water is alive. Crabs move through storm drains and along the blackened margins of culverts. Small fish flash in runoff channels where no fish ought to survive. Frogs collect in flooded underpasses after rain, their calls low and numerous, less like singing than the vibration of a machine idling somewhere underground. Rats travel in the shadowed service corridors between warehouses. At night, feral dogs move in loose, quiet groups across the truck lots, stepping around puddles that reflect the refinery lights.

In the marshier fringe beyond the city walls, where the levees sag and the drainage gates open toward tidal flats, birds become almost impossible to count. Herons wait beside warm effluent streams. Egrets stand in the shallows among plastic bottles and lengths of floating insulation. Clouds of mosquitoes rise from the ditches, but they are carried away by the wet wind before they can gather around a person for long. The predators are well-fed: kestrels on the electrical pylons, cats under loading docks, snakes in the stone ballast beside the rail line.

Human life persists in scattered concentrations. There are apartment blocks on slightly higher ground, their lower floors stained by old floodwater. Laundry hangs indoors because there is nowhere clean to hang it outside. Shops occupy the ground levels: a pharmacy, two cafés, a tire repair yard, a place selling work boots and electrical fittings. In the evenings, factory workers emerge in reflective jackets and move toward buses under covered walkways. Their voices carry briefly across the open concrete, then disappear. The city is not abandoned. It is simply built for more people than remain in it, and for a louder kind of work than it now performs.

Its rules are practical and old.

Doors at ground level are raised above the pavement. Electrical cabinets are mounted high. Every neighborhood knows the flood siren and the routes to the upper floors of public buildings. Nothing is stored directly on the ground. Rust is assumed. Moisture is assumed. Pumps are inspected more faithfully than streets. The water will enter eventually; construction merely decides where it is allowed to wait.

By night, the heat remains in the concrete and steel. The sky has no stars through the haze, only a faint violet brightness over the refinery and the intermittent sweep of aircraft lights. Steam drifts across empty avenues. Somewhere beyond a warehouse wall, gulls settle with a thousand small scrapes of feet and feathers. A train moves slowly through the yard, almost silently until its couplings touch and the sound travels across the flat city: one deep metallic knock, then another, then stillness again.

The place is wet, hot, low, worked-over, and enduring. It has been weathered into its condition. It does not look ruined so much as adjusted: a city that has accepted the water beneath it, the animals above and within it, and the long industrial labor of keeping dry ground dry enough for one more day.
