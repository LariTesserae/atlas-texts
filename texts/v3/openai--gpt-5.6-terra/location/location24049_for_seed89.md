At the top of the mountain, where the air ought to be thin and cold, there is a warm hollow in the rock.

It is not large. A person could walk its perimeter in an hour, if there were a person to walk it, and if the ground permitted a straight path. The hollow lies beneath a rim of black volcanic stone, an old crater whose walls have weathered inward until they are scalloped, ribbed, and crowded with ledges. The sky is mostly withheld. At midday, a narrow gray strip may be visible above; at night, which is nearly always, the basin holds darkness like water.

The mountain sweats.

Moisture gathers on the undersides of overhangs, beads in seams, runs blackly down the faces of cliffs, and falls in individual heavy drops onto the floor. There is no broad lake. Water is too dispersed for that: a thousand wet places instead of one. Shallow pans lie between slabs of stone. Fissures breathe steam faintly. A thread of warm runoff appears after rain, traces a shining route through a ravine, and vanishes underground by morning. The ground is damp enough to smell alive—mineral, fungal, animal, and sweetly rotten—without ever becoming soft or green.

Vegetation has not taken possession here. It survives in hard little claims. Dark moss makes cushions beneath perpetual drips. Pale grasses grow out of cracks and bend flat under passing bodies. There are clumps of leathery leaves, glossy with condensation, and pale-rooted vines that cling to the crater wall, never rising more than a few handspans. In sheltered pockets, low flowers open without color, their petals a waxy white that seems to produce its own faintness in the dark. They last only a night or two before being eaten.

The animals make the place dense.

They are in the rock, beneath it, across it, hanging from it, pressed shoulder to shoulder in every available fold. Herds of compact, sure-footed grazers move among the ledges: creatures with broad padded feet, blunt horns, and dense wool the color of ash. They do not travel quickly. Nothing here needs to hurry. Their paths have been worn for generations into the crater floor, smooth channels between boulders, wet and black at the bottom. Each herd seems to know the shape of the darkness by hoof and muzzle.

Above them, pale winged things crowd under shelves of stone. Their wings are leathery, but the membranes are patterned with fine translucent veins that catch even the smallest spill of moonlight. When startled, they rise all at once and transform the hollow into a storm of bodies. Their calls are not delicate chirps. They bark, knock, shriek, and issue deep bell-like throbs from inflated throats. The sound strikes the crater walls and returns in layers. A single animal’s voice can become a crowd; a crowd can become weather.

There are burrowers, too—round-bodied, blind or nearly blind, their noses wet and mobile. They open the damp soil around the moss beds and draw out roots, grubs, and fallen fruit. Small predators follow them: long-backed things with bright reflective eyes, moving low along the channels of stone. They do not hunt constantly. In this enclosed abundance, even hunger has a settled rhythm. A kill is heard rather than seen: a sudden burst of wingbeats, a protesting scream, the scuffle of feet, then the larger continuing noise of everyone else refusing to be disturbed.

At the center of the hollow lies a field of broken pillars.

They are natural columns, not ruins. Once they were molten stone; now they stand exposed, separated by long erosion into hexagonal stumps and leaning stacks. Their tops are rounded by centuries of rain and hoof traffic. Some are only knee-high, others rise like dark trees without branches. Between them are close passages, warm pools, nests, sleeping shelves, hidden tunnels, and root tangles. The field is so densely inhabited that it seems to breathe. Steam rises from cracks around the pillars. Animals climb them, sleep against them, rub their hides on their rough sides, lay eggs in their shaded bases.

This is the nearest thing in the world to a village, though no hand built it.

There are customs, not structures. The horned grazers give way at certain dripping stones. The winged creatures leave one narrow cleft undisturbed during the season when the blind burrowers bear young. At intervals, perhaps every few weeks, a great old scavenger comes down from the high outer slopes: featherless, almost reptilian, with a hooked beak and a neck heavy with folds. At its arrival the basin changes key. The small animals disappear. The herd masses together. The winged things fall silent except for a low communal ticking. The scavenger feeds from the dead caught in crevices, cleans the bones, and leaves without challenging the living.

The mountain has long ago arranged these lives around its patience.

Rain comes seldom, but when it comes, it comes as a warm slanting veil through the open throat of the crater. It rattles on stone, fills every depression, and drives the animals into tighter and tighter clusters. The basin becomes deafening then. Water runs down the walls in silver cords. Moss swells. The sparse plants unfold. The creatures drink not from streams but from every surface: from their own fur, from the rock, from pools in hoofprints. For a few hours, the darkness has depth. It glimmers with wet backs, shining eyes, and the trembling membranes of wings.

Then the rain passes. The runoff finds the old channels. Steam lifts. Drops continue to fall from the cliffs for days afterward, one at a time, with the solemn regularity of a slow clock.

Nothing in the hollow appears newly made. Every claw-mark has older claw-marks beneath it. Every trail follows a trail that was already there. The stone is not barren; it is worn into usefulness. The animals are not wild in the sense of disorderly. They are numerous enough, loud enough, close enough, that the crater seems to possess one many-bodied mind: sleeping in shifts, feeding by habit, responding to water, scent, moon, and the distant pressures of weather climbing the mountain.

Above the rim, the high world may be dry, cold, and empty.

Inside, in this small warm darkness, life has gathered until there is scarcely room for silence.
