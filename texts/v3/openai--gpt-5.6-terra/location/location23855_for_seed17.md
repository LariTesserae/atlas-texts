At this altitude the sea has no shore.

It occupies a basin so immense that its curvature is lost in weather. The water lies under a pale, ordinary sky—blue in the brief noon, iron-colored toward evening—but nothing about its behavior is ordinary. It has been lifted into the mountains and held there, a continental body of black-cold water caught among fresh volcanic ridges. The ridges are young enough to cut the clouds: basalt teeth, glassy slopes, sheer faces still clean of soil. No roots pry at their cracks. No grass softens their ledges. Snow gathers only in the protected seams, a thin white powder that never seems to increase or melt.

The sea is not rough in the usual sense. Its surface has been fixed into a permanent weather pattern: miles of dark swells standing in procession, their crests whitening and collapsing with identical force, at identical intervals, forever. Each wave breaks; each broken wave is replaced by the same break. Far offshore, the water rises in long walls and folds down in sheets, as if the ocean is continuously being poured from an invisible height.

The sound fills everything.

It is not simply surf. It is the impact of whole watersheds striking themselves. A sustained, crushing roar comes off the water and enters the stone, making the basalt hum beneath a hand or boot. Speech exists only as a movement of lips. A person near the open shore can feel the noise in the teeth, the ribs, the soft interior of the skull. Higher up, where the wind strips the ridges bare, the roar becomes a single level tone—almost calm in its uniformity, almost mechanical.

And below it, beneath the obvious sound, are others: the long metallic groan of submerged structures; the repetitive knock of pistons; chains tightening against current; deep bells ringing from under miles of water.

There are platforms out there.

They appear only when the low cloud thins: isolated industrial towers anchored to drowned elevations, each one a small dark geometry against the heaving plain. Some are drilling rigs, though no flame burns from them. Some are elevator pylons or crane gantries, their arms held above the water in permanent arrested gestures. A few have narrow windows lit with weak yellow light. Most are unlit. Rust runs over their sides in orange veins, though the cold has kept the corrosion slow and clean. Cable bundles descend from them into black depths; pipelines vanish under the waves in ruler-straight lines.

No ships travel between the stations. There is no harbor, no settlement visible, no smoke beyond the mist that the sea makes for itself. Yet the machinery continues. At intervals—always the same intervals—an engine begins beneath the water. Its vibration travels across the standing swells, reaches the cliffs, and dies without fading, simply ending when the cycle ends.

The landscape has become accustomed to this rhythm. Or perhaps it was built around it.

On the high black shores, there are sparse installations: a switch house bolted to a ridge; a ladder descending into surf; a concrete shelter with its door welded shut; marker poles tipped with red lamps. The lamps burn even in daylight. Their red glow is subdued by the wide, normal sun, but at night they become the only signs that human intention ever reached this place.

The ground around them is naked and sharp. Volcanic ash forms gray drifts in hollows. Fresh fractures expose blue-black stone, pale minerals, and occasional seams of ice. There is no moss, no lichen, no weed in the lee of a wall. Even the places where rainwater gathers remain bare, as if the world has not yet learned how to grow a surface.

But the water is crowded with life.

It is a cold abundance, concentrated below the reach of sight. Schools of silver-bodied animals pass under the waves in densities that turn whole patches of sea bright, then dark, then bright again. Their movements are too coordinated to resemble fish at first glance: thousands pivot at once, making luminous grids in the water. Larger shapes follow them. Long pale backs rise beyond the breakers, venting vapor that is immediately torn flat by the wind. There are seals—or things close enough to seals—resting on the lowest rock shelves, thick-skinned and nearly white, their eyes black and unwinking. They do not call. Nothing living here seems able to compete with the sea.

Sometimes a column of creatures moves vertically through the water beside a platform, an ascending storm of bodies. For a few minutes the ocean around the structure flickers with internal light: blue-green organs, blinking in shared timing. Then the lights go out below the surface and the station returns to being a dark, solitary machine.

Birds are scarce in the air, though not absent. A few enormous black-winged scavengers circle the ridges, using the updrafts that rise from the warmer machinery vents. They rarely flap. From far away they look like scraps of furnace soot caught against the sky. Their nests, if they have nests, are hidden in inaccessible cuts in the rock.

The cold is not dramatic. It does not sting so much as remove. It removes smell, color, flexibility from the fingers, patience from exposed skin. Moisture freezes on metal in a pearly crust. Spray hardens on the seaward faces of the cliffs, building thin translucent armor over the rock. Yet the sea never freezes. Its vast movement, its submerged heat, its industrially regulated circulation keep it liquid from horizon to horizon.

Nothing changes here, though everything moves.

The waves continually fall. The platforms continually shudder. The animals continually stream through the depths. Water becomes spray, spray becomes ice, ice returns to water. The clouds pass over the basin in long low ranks, but their passage seems only a pattern repeated across a fixed field. A person could mark a particular white breaker far offshore, wait an hour, and see what feels like the same breaker lift in the same place, throwing the same white edge into the same gray air.

There is no sense of a future storm, no easing after weather, no season preparing to turn. The young mountains remain young. Their edges do not round. The unoccupied buildings do not collapse. The red warning lamps do not fail. The deep engines do not wear down.

At night the sea becomes nearly invisible except for its foam, which lays pale bands across the darkness, and for the rare underwater migrations shining through the black. The sky above is clear enough for stars. Below, the ocean contains a second, colder constellation: lights moving in multitudes under an unbroken roar.

It is an empty place only in the way a cathedral is empty—vast enough that each small presence is made solitary by space, and each sound becomes larger than its source.
