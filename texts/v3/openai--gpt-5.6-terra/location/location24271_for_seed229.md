The place is called the High Dry Garden, though no one agrees whether it is a garden or a ruin.

It occupies the bowl of an extinct volcano high above the cloudline, a hollow perhaps half a mile across. Its enclosing walls are black-green stone, polished in places by centuries of abrasive wind. The rim is broken in three places, but nothing enters through those gaps except dust, migrating creatures, and the long, hot breath of the upper air. From inside, the world beyond is usually invisible. The garden is filled with a permanent dimness—not darkness, but a coppery, wind-stirred shade made by the dense haze of mineral spores and floating seed-filaments suspended beneath the crater lip.

There is no water anywhere.

No pools hide under leaves. No mud darkens the hollows. Nothing drips. The stones are dry to their cores, split with pale veins of salt and glass. If one digs, one finds only warm powder, the fossil shells of roots, and layers of old windblown pollen compacted into something nearly edible. Yet the crater is lush.

Its vegetation does not drink. It feeds on the air.

The upper winds carry dust from distant salt flats and vapors from volcanic vents far below. At night—though night is only a slight deepening of the rust-colored gloom—the plants open their thousands of tiny reflective mouths and strain the atmosphere. They catch metallic dust, static charge, and the fine exhaust of lightning storms that pass over the mountain. Their leaves are not soft. They are layered, flexible mineral films: blue-green, translucent, sometimes edged in black crystal. They clatter rather than rustle.

Low forests of bell-shaped growths stand scattered across the crater floor. Each “tree” rises only ten or fifteen feet, with a thick, fibrous trunk anchored in a circular mound of pale root-stone. Their crowns resemble clusters of enormous closed flowers, except that their petals are hard, faceted, and continually opening and shutting in response to the wind. When gusts sweep through, the whole grove flexes and rings. The sound is immense: a choir of struck bottles, bronze sheets, animal throats, and distant chimes.

Between them grow carpets of red wiregrass. It curls over the ground in coarse, springy loops, never quite touching the soil. When the wind is strong, the grass lifts into moving sheets and slides across the surface in slow, crawling waves. It leaves no roots behind. By morning it may have gathered around a different stone, a different hut, a different corpse.

There are shrubs with white, featherless fronds that turn like weather vanes. There are purple columns of fungus-like material, though they are dry and cold and exhale a sharp odor of struck flint. There are broad dishes of orange skin spread among the rocks, collecting sunlight by day and moonlight by night; when disturbed, they fold inward with a sound like a sail snapping.

All of it thrives in the absence of water, and all of it seems slightly desperate. Growth is fast here, but not gentle. Leaves burst from stalks with dry popping sounds. Seed bulbs ripen in hours, then split open explosively, flinging glittering kernels against the crater walls. A mature bell-tree may shed an entire crown during a single storm and stand naked by dawn, already knitting new crystalline petals from its upper joints.

The fauna is everywhere.

Small creatures move through the wiregrass in schools: six-legged, smooth-backed things no larger than fists, colored the gray of ash. They have broad mouths lined with grinding plates and feed on the mineral coatings of plants. When alarmed, they inflate their bodies with hot air and roll across the crater floor like tumbleweeds, clicking furiously.

Larger grazers—if grazing is the word—wander among the bell-trees. They are narrow animals with long, jointed limbs and translucent chest cavities. One can see their internal organs glimmering as they process dust and pollen. Their hides are covered in overlapping scales made of dried resin, amber, and shed leaf-fragments. They travel in loose, widely spaced herds, never close enough to touch. Each makes a deep resonant call every few minutes, as if checking that the others remain somewhere in the haze.

The calls answer from all directions.

Above, winged things circle beneath the crater rim. They have no feathers, only broad membranes stretched between fingerlike bones, and their wings make a continuous beating roar. Their nests hang from cliff ledges in clusters of woven grass, bone, and polished mineral. The young are louder than the adults: pale, blind, enormous-headed chicks that scream whenever wind alters direction. Their screams are answered by the shriek of the vegetation, by insect swarms vibrating inside hollow trunks, by the booming throat-sacs of the ground animals.

Silence has no foothold in the High Dry Garden.

Even when the wind stills, the place ticks, cracks, whistles, scratches, grinds. Seeds roll across stone. Roots tighten underground. Lichen-like films release static with tiny blue sparks. Creatures communicate in calls, rattles, percussion, and flashes of reflected light. The crater has the concentrated noise of a great forest compressed into a small room.

Scattered among the living growth are signs that people once kept the garden, or perhaps still do.

There are low shelters built from black slabs leaned together in rough cones. Their interiors are lined with braided wiregrass and the pale hide of the six-legged rollers. The roofs are weighted with stones, but every roof bears wind-chimes: bones, seedpods, shards of old ceramic, cut shells from creatures that no longer live here. The chimes are not decoration. They tell the inhabitants which direction the dangerous winds are coming from.

Near the center of the crater stands a squat tower, perhaps twenty feet high, constructed from fitted volcanic blocks without mortar. Its exterior has been carved with spirals, animal tracks, and rows of tiny handprints. At its top is a basket of branching copper, green with age. During storms it catches lightning. The charge runs down hidden rods into the ground, where it feeds a ring of unusually bright vegetation: blue bushes that glow faintly in the dim air and produce hard, sweet nodules used as food.

The people who tend the place, if they are present, are difficult to see. They wear cloaks made of reflective leaf-scales and move only when the wind moves. Their tools are simple: stone blades, bone awls, wooden mallets, nets braided from dry roots. Yet they understand the garden’s invisible rules better than any outsider could.

They never cut a bell-tree while it is ringing.

They never sleep in the center of a wiregrass migration.

They never follow a rolling creature downhill, because downhill is where the crater’s old vents breathe.

And when the sky begins to pulse green behind the haze, they climb into their stone shelters and seal the entrances with mats of resin cloth. This is when the upper storms arrive: dry storms without rain, storms of charged dust and green lightning. The plants close their mineral mouths. The animals press flat against the ground or wedge themselves beneath roots. The air becomes bright enough to show every bone and branch in silhouette.

Then the whole small world convulses.

Lightning walks from bell-tree to bell-tree. The tower hums like a struck bell. The red wiregrass rises in twisting columns and lashes itself against the stones. Winged creatures batter the crater walls, crying in ecstatic terror. Dust flows through the broken rim in pale rivers, filling the bowl, stripping old leaves away, laying new minerals over everything.

By morning, the storm is gone.

Nothing is wet.

The crater smells of hot stone, resin, powdered metal, and the faint sweetness of newly opened growth. Several trees may be shattered. A shelter may have lost its roof. Entire herds may have moved elsewhere, leaving their calls behind as an echo in the cliffs. But the garden is already busy repairing itself. Fresh leaves unfold. Small animals emerge from cracks. The dry orange dishes reopen to the dim sky. Somewhere inside the haze, a person strikes a stone against a copper rod, once, twice, three times, and the wind-chimes begin answering all around the basin.

The High Dry Garden endures by never becoming still.
