The city occupies the upper basin of an old salt mountain, where the ground has been hollowed by heat and water for so long that it no longer looks cut or ruined. It looks accustomed to itself.

At this elevation the sky is painfully near. It is a white-blue vault in the day, bleached almost blank around the sun, and the light falls with such force that every wet surface becomes a mirror. There are no trees, no grass in cracks, no green softening the edges of things. The basin is made of pale mineral shelves, rust-colored stone, old concrete, black piping, and broad fields of crusted salt. Yet water is everywhere.

It rises from the ground in warm seams. It gathers in shallow, perfectly still pans among the rock. It runs down walls in narrow dark threads. It hangs in the air beneath the city’s overpasses, condensing on railings and cable bundles, swelling at their undersides until a drop releases and falls for a long time without a sound anyone can hear. The water smells faintly metallic, with gypsum, sulfur, and the mineral taste of a coin held on the tongue.

The basin is high enough that clouds come through it rather than over it. In the afternoons, clouds drift into the streets at axle height: vast clean bodies of vapor, hot at the edges and cool inside. They catch on the upper gantries and factory chimneys, wrapping the industrial quarter in moving white cloth. When they break, they leave rain that does not strike the ground so much as appear upon it. Pavements darken. Salt wakes. Every dry hollow acquires a thin, steaming skin of water.

The city is sparse for its size. It has broad distances between structures, as if it was planned by people who expected the ground itself to expand and contract. Low apartment blocks stand on pylons along the more stable shelves. Their shaded undersides are blue with damp. There are pumping stations shaped like blunt fortresses, service towers with exterior ladders, refineries built around old thermal vents, and long pipe corridors that cross open basins on concrete stilts. The pipes are painted in fading colors: yellow for heat, white for condensate, red for brine, black for things no public map admits exist.

Nothing is crowded. Even the central market is a sequence of isolated roofs beside a drainage channel, with bright awnings stretched between poles and meters of empty wet pavement around each stall. The city has room to breathe, though it does not breathe easily. Its industry depends on the basin’s patient processes: water drawn from deep porous rock, heat borrowed from below, minerals precipitated from brine, vapor collected from the air. Machines do not conquer the landscape here. They wait beside it with buckets, coils, filters, and turbines.

Many of the oldest buildings have been slowly altered by the climate. Their lower stories are eaten into shallow caves by warm water. Their foundations wear rounded skirts of deposited salt. Steel doors bloom orange and then become plated in white crystals. Windows, where they remain, are filmed with mineral dust on the outside and condensation on the inside, so that occupants live behind a double weather.

The fauna has adapted to the absence of plants so completely that the city’s people no longer think of it as unusual.

Along the water pans, pale six-legged skimmers move in small flocks. They resemble crabs made from folded porcelain, each no larger than a hand. Their feet do not pierce the water’s skin; they travel over its surface tension, sifting microscopic mineral films and colonies of heat-loving organisms. When the sun is highest, they gather in the shadow cast by a pipe or bollard. At dusk they spread across the pans in patterned lines, leaving delicate disturbances that close immediately behind them.

There are larger animals in the drainageworks: long-bodied siphoners with translucent ribs and blind, lamp-like organs along their throats. They live on bacterial mats in the warm dark runoff channels, grazing the soft colorless growth that forms wherever brine, heat, and metal meet. Their bodies are usually invisible beneath the water. One only notices the faint moving phosphorescence beneath a culvert grate, or the slow opening and closing of the water’s surface in a place where there should be no current.

Above, heat kites circle in the rising air. Their wings are thin membranes stiffened by black jointed rods. They do not flap. They climb the columns of hot vapor issuing from the refinery stacks and thermal fissures, turning once, twice, then slipping out over the salt flats. From below they look like scraps of burnt paper caught in a draft. They feed on the tiny airborne creatures that congregate in moist air: spores, drifting larvae, mineral-feeding motes too small to see except when the sunlight strikes them in a shaft beneath the cloud.

The most common animals are the wall-lizards, though they are not lizards by any strict old-world measure. They have glossy, damp-looking hides and flattened bodies that cling to vertical stone. By day they retreat into the cooler, wet interiors of masonry. At night, when the city’s lamps come on and the pipes begin to tick as their temperatures change, hundreds emerge to drink the condensation from rail joints and to hunt the pale flying insects clustering around light.

The city is full of life, but it is a life that has learned discretion.

This is why it is so quiet.

There is no leaf-rustle. No wind in branches. No loud birds arguing from rooftops. The factories are designed for quiet work: insulated turbines, deep-set generators, electric freight carts that move along rubberized tracks. The great pumps operate far below hearing, their vibration translated through the soles of one’s boots rather than the air. A person standing alone on an avenue may hear only a bead of water falling from a cable, the dry skitter of a skimmer crossing salt, the faint crackle of crystals forming at the edge of a channel.

Even rain is muted. It falls warm and fine onto warm stone.

At noon, brightness removes detail from the distance. The basin becomes an arrangement of white glare, dark structural lines, and hovering towers. The far margins are visible only as ochre escarpments, worn into layered shelves by ancient floods and slow mineral dissolution. Beyond them lie more high basins, perhaps more settlements, perhaps merely industrial extraction sites marked by a thin vertical haze. Roads leave the city in straight, exposed causeways. Along them, every few kilometers, are water houses: square buildings with cistern roofs, emergency shade galleries, and tall reflective panels meant to signal travelers who have become stranded in cloud.

No one walks far during the brightest hours. The rules of the place are written into its public architecture.

Every building offers a condensation rail. Every plaza has a shaded basin with filtered water, even if it is only knee-deep and too mineral-rich to drink unprocessed. Exterior stairs are ribbed so wet soles cannot slip. Doors open inward, because sudden cloudbursts can turn an alley into a flowing channel in minutes. Ground-level apartments keep their possessions above a painted flood line. Children learn which white crusts are harmless and which will burn skin. They learn not to touch the blue-green seepage around old power conduits, not to step into clear water without checking it for siphoner eggs, and never to shelter directly under a brine pipe during a pressure release.

At sunset, the entire basin changes color.

The hard daylight loosens. The salt flats turn pink, then violet. Steam rises from every dark wet surface. The upper city catches gold along its railings, antennae, and water tanks, while the lower drainage districts sink into a luminous blue haze. Lights appear one by one: not many, not brilliant, but deliberate. Lamps beneath pedestrian bridges. Green indicators on pump houses. Red warning beacons on vapor towers. Thin rows of orange windows in residential blocks.

Then the heat kites descend, folding themselves along high ledges like scorched metal tools. The skimmers gather at the broadest water pans. The wall-lizards begin their silent climb toward the lamps. Somewhere under the city, hidden pumps alter the pressure of the basin’s water, redirecting warm flow into reservoirs before the night clouds arrive.

From a distance, the city might seem abandoned: too open, too pale, too still, built in a place no ordinary life should endure.

But close by, it reveals itself as a careful compact between stone, heat, water, and appetite. Nothing here grows upward. Nothing flowers. Nothing shades the sun. Life spreads instead across surfaces, through pipes, beneath mineral skins, in vapor, in runoff, in the thin interval between a hot world and the water it cannot stop making.
