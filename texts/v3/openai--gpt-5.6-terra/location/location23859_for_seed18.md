At the highest point of the weatherworks, where the mountain ought to have ended, there is a garden no larger than a village square.

It is called a garden only because things grow there.

The place is a constructed shelf bolted into the pale upper body of the world: an oval deck of dark, riveted metal, perhaps two hundred paces long, hung between two enormous cooling stacks. Nothing below it is visible. The stacks rise from cloud and disappear into brighter cloud, their sides armored in old plates the color of wet iron. Cables as thick as tree trunks disappear upward through hoists, pulleys, and housings. Every surface is manufactured. The soil is manufactured. The rocks are manufactured. Even the dripping water has come through pipes, gauges, filters, and patient machines somewhere inside the mountain of works.

Yet it is lush.

The central deck has been given over to a thick, uncontrolled growth of heat-loving vegetation. Vines climb the support lattices and thread themselves through warning rails. Broad leaves, waxy and green-black, spread over the hatches. Ferns crowd in the seams between steel plates, their roots drinking the mineral condensation that gathers there. There are tall flowering stalks with hollow, crimson stems that knock softly against each other in the windless places. Moss has claimed the warm engine casings beneath the deck, making them look upholstered. Yellow fruit grows in clusters around valve mouths, nourished by warm vapor and trace metals. Its skins are tough and pitted like old boiler paint.

The whole place smells of wet leaves, hot copper, oil, and the mineral sharpness of rain before it falls.

Water is scarce enough to be noticed. There is no stream, no pond, no obvious source. Instead, droplets collect on the undersides of pipes, swell for a long time, then fall onto leaves with an audible tick. Every few hours, perhaps every few days—the local clocks are unreliable—a bank of mist rolls through the gap between the stacks. It is not weather exactly. It comes in at a measured pressure, whiter than cloud, smelling faintly of rust. It beads on every rail and leaf. The plants open themselves to receive it. So do the animals.

Most of the animals are small, but there are many of them.

Winged things occupy the upper scaffold, where heat and condensation meet. They resemble swifts from a distance, until they land: their feathers are made of overlapping translucent plates, and their feet end in delicate gripping hooks. They nest in insulation bundles. Their nests are warm enough to steam. When the mist comes, they spread their wings and let water collect in their plates, then shake it into the mouths of their young.

There are long-bodied grazers in the undergrowth, no larger than cats, with velvety hides and faces that resemble folded flowers. They feed on moss, fungal foam, and the soft white bloom that grows around leaking seals. They move in silence, except for the occasional dry clatter of their jointed tails. Beneath the deck, in the darkness around the drainage grates, something larger shifts slowly. Only its breathing is heard: a long intake through metal ducts, then an exhale that makes the ferns bow.

The animals do not seem frightened by machinery. They have adapted to its rhythms so completely that they use it as weather.

This is necessary, because the machinery is deafening.

The stacks are not loud in a simple way. They contain many sounds at once: the immense bass note of air being drawn upward; the shuddering hammer of pistons far below; whistles that rise and fall across frequencies too high to locate; the constant frenzy of belts, fans, and gear teeth; the far-off impact of something being stamped, again and again, at intervals of several minutes. The deck trembles beneath the feet. Loose bolts dance in shallow trays. Leaves vibrate visibly. Conversations cannot be held except by putting one’s mouth to another person’s ear, and even then words arrive incomplete.

Yet the vegetation has made a second soundscape inside the first.

Drips strike leaves. Seedpods rattle. Insects with brass-colored shells rasp their wings against warm panels. Vines, when the deck shifts, pull against one another with a creaking like rigging. The crimson hollow plants make low fluting tones when exhaust passes through them. In the few narrow alcoves shielded by thick growth, the industrial roar is reduced to a bodily pressure, and one can hear the small creatures feeding.

There is almost no one here.

The emptiness is striking because the place seems designed for labor. There are catwalks, signal lamps, inspection windows, numbered handrails, lockers, lifting cranes, tool hooks, and a control booth with six chairs facing a wall of dead or sleeping gauges. A brass plaque by the entrance lists seventy-three approved maintenance roles. The last date on the inspection board is so old that the digits have been worn smooth by damp.

No workers appear.

Still, the factory continues its work with slow, relentless confidence. A crane travels half the length of the deck over the course of an afternoon. A gate opens somewhere in the cloud and stays open for an hour, releasing a low torrent of warm air. The cooling stacks periodically shed sheets of condensed water down their inner walls, a rain that cannot reach the garden but is heard as an immense whisper. Nothing here is hurried. The engines are ancient enough to have found their own pace; the garden is ancient enough to have grown around that pace. Neither seems to be winning.

The extreme height changes ordinary light. In clear moments, the garden is lit by a sun that seems too near and too white, caught in all the wet leaves and steel edges. Clouds pass below in level, illuminated fields. Above, the sky is not dark but extremely blue, with the hard clean look of enamel. When the cloud closes around the stacks, daylight becomes diffuse and greenish. The plants glow from within their wet surfaces. The deck feels less like an exposed summit than the interior of some vast breathing vessel.

There are rules, though they are not posted.

Nothing thrown from the railing falls in a way that can be watched. It enters cloud and is gone immediately.

The mist is safe to drink only from leaves, never from the pipes. Water taken directly from a pipe causes the mouth to taste of pennies for days and makes one dream of turning gears.

The animals may be fed fruit but not touched. If touched, they stiffen, unfold their flower-faces, and emit a note that brings every warning light on the deck to life.

No one cuts the vines away from the red emergency cables. The cables have been inaccessible for generations, buried in green coils and pale blossoms. Yet when a storm reaches the upper works, those hidden cables pulse faintly red through the leaves, and every creature in the garden goes still.

Most importantly: when the stacks stop singing, leave.

They have stopped only once in living memory, according to a message scratched into the metal door of the control booth. The writer did not describe what happened afterward. They only left a final instruction beneath the date:

**Do not wait to see what the garden does in silence.**
