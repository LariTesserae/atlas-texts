At this height, night is not an absence of daylight so much as a material of its own: a deep blue-black cold laid across the world in broad, soft weights. The mountains are not sharp enough to resemble the clean teeth of ordinary ranges. They have been worn into old shoulders and immense backs, folded one behind another beneath a sky so near and star-dense that the constellations seem caught in the upper ridges.

There are no roads. No lights fixed by human hands. No walls, towers, cables, tracks, or ruins. Yet the place has an industrial character—not because anything has been built here, but because the mountain is full of processes so regular, so immense, that it feels like a factory operating without workers.

Along the high valleys, glaciers move in the dark.

They do not look like white rivers; in this scant light they are black glass banded with dull grey, their surfaces furred with wind-hardened snow and dark colonies of algae. Beneath them, deep under the ice, water works through channels with a compressed, mechanical force. It grinds stone to flour. It turns buried boulders in slow chambers. It forces air through cracks in the bedrock until the whole valley sometimes gives a faint, almost inaudible exhalation.

The sound is too low to be called a roar. Most of the time it is only pressure: a hush in the soles of the feet, a distant mineral vibration, an occasional knock from inside the ice. When a crevasse opens somewhere beyond sight, it gives one abrupt report, like a sheet of iron struck beneath a great deal of cloth. Then quiet resumes, complete enough that the small sounds become large: droplets forming on a moss frond, the dry brush of feathers, the careful placing of hooves on shale.

The land is unexpectedly green.

Not in broad warm forests, but in dense, cold architectures of life. Every sheltered fold holds growth. Moss gathers on the north sides of stones in cushions as thick as animal pelts. Low shrubs spread through the valleys, their leaves small, waxed, and black-green. Lichen covers whole slopes in maps of silver, rust, saffron, and pale luminous blue. In cracks where meltwater vanishes, ferns rise improbably from the rock. Their fronds are dark enough to disappear until rain catches on them.

Rain comes often, but never generously. It is a fine, cold precipitation carried sideways by the wind, passing through one valley while the next remains dry and starry. At dawn, if dawn occurs at all—the mountains make their own long nights—condensation pearls along every stem and stone. The vegetation drinks from cloud, fog, frost, and the breath of the ground. Water does not gather here easily. It appears in hidden seams: a black tarn under a cliff, a thread of runoff beneath a snowbank, a cold pool in the hollow of a boulder. It is always moving elsewhere, underground or under ice.

The animals know these routes.

There are herds of pale, long-limbed grazers crossing the high meadows in silence, their coats carrying burrs of ice. They feed on lichens and tough flowering plants that open only for a few dim hours at a time. Their eyes reflect starlight with a greenish glint. They do not form tight groups. The land is too open, and each creature keeps a great measure of darkness around itself.

Higher still, among the windswept shelves, heavy horned things stand in clusters beneath overhangs. Their horns sweep backward like polished roots. Their breath rises and immediately becomes fog, drifting away from their faces in slow banners. They scrape at snow with broad hooves, uncovering mats of red-brown moss.

Predators move through this country with almost no sign. A cat-shaped animal, larger than any familiar mountain cat, follows the glacier margins where the ice meets stone. Its fur is grey as wet slate and grows long around the feet, masking its tracks. It waits beside melt holes, not for fish but for birds: white-winged cliff-nesters that descend to drink at the water’s black surface. In the upper air there are immense owls, thin-bodied and quiet-winged, turning between stars. Their calls are not heard often. When one does call, it sounds less like a cry than a brief wooden creak from an old beam.

There are insects too, though the cold seems to forbid them. They gather in the thermal breaths that emerge from fissures, clouds of tiny dark bodies humming at a pitch nearly beneath hearing. The fissures are warm only by comparison. They release the heat of buried rock, the breath of microbial colonies, and the stored pressure of the mountain’s interior. Around them grow miniature jungles: liverwort, grass, fungus with translucent caps, small flowers whose petals are nearly black. At night, some of the fungi produce a weak blue-green glow. From a distance these patches resemble scattered indicator lamps on the floor of a vast, abandoned engine room.

That is the strange law of the place: nothing made is needed for machinery to exist.

The slopes convey material downhill. Ice compresses snow into blue crystal, then drags it over stone. Water sorts the crushed mineral into fans and braided channels. Roots pry open fractures. Fungi dissolve metals from the rock. Herds transport seeds from valley to valley in their coats. Predators cull the old and weak. Ravens, fat and glossy, distribute what remains. Every process has its interval and its place. None is hurried, though much is always happening.

Even the darkness is active.

Clouds pass low enough to snag on ridges, then unravel in the valleys. Frost travels across meadows in thin advancing lines. Snow slides now and then from an unseen shelf, first a soft whisper, then a muted rolling beneath the ground. At the glacier’s edge, the ice rises and settles by fractions, making trenches collapse and new pools form. Whole hillsides creep imperceptibly toward the valley, held together by roots, frozen soil, and the accumulated restraint of centuries.

The terrain bears the marks of great age without appearing dead or exhausted. Cirques hold their old bowls. Valleys have the broad, settled shape of places long worked by ice. Ridges are weathered down to stable forms, their exposed faces dark with mineral varnish and patterned with pale lichen. Nothing is raw. Nothing seems newly broken except the occasional fresh scar of snow or rockfall. The mountains have been doing this for so long that their violence has become orderly.

In a hollow beneath an overhanging cliff, there may be a pool perfectly still beneath a thin skin of ice. Roots hang down from the moss above it. A few blind white insects drift in the water. Something broad and slow moves below the surface, barely disturbing it: an amphibious creature with external gills like dark red leaves. It has spent its entire life in that one cold reservoir, fed by droplets from the ceiling and the small bodies washed in from the mountain.

Outside, the valley opens for miles.

There is no settlement in it, no smoke, no evidence that anyone has named the peaks or measured the distances between them. Yet it feels occupied—not crowded, never that, but attentively inhabited. Every ledge has a sleeper, a nest, a burrow, a runnel, a patch of growth working patiently in the cold. Vast distances separate these lives. Between them lies stone, ice, dark air, and the long mechanical labor of the world.

Above the ridge, a dim band of cloudlight appears, perhaps moonlight diffused through weather, perhaps the beginning of a sun that will not quite clear the mountains. For a moment the glaciers acquire a low internal sheen. Wet leaves flash. The horns of grazing animals become visible across a far slope. Blue fungus glows in the cracks. Then cloud closes again, and the landscape returns to its deep, industrious dark—silent, living, and continuously in motion.
