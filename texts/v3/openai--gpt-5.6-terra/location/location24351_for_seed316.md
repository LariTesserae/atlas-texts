The low country has no horizon in the ordinary sense. It has a rim of weather.

Everything lies nearly level beneath it: a continent-wide basin, older than mountains, pressed down and kneaded smooth by epochs of rain. The ground is not a surface so much as a packed arrangement of surfaces—black mud, shell pavement, drowned stone, mats of mineral crust, pools skinned with ash-colored algae, ribbed banks where some enormous flood once stopped and forgot to leave. There is scarcely an empty handspan anywhere. Water fills every depression; mud fills every crack; roots, bones, salt, peat, clay, and the small hard houses of insects occupy what the water does not. Even the air seems crowded, heavy with vapor and the close wet warmth of decomposition.

The land is ancient enough that its original shape has been erased several times. Hills have sagged into long swells. Rivers have worn themselves broad and indecisive, divided into hundreds of channels, rejoining, disappearing under reedless flats, rising again miles away from where they entered. No stream is clean. Each carries black silt, yellow foam, splinters of pale wood, whole fields of floating peat, and a constant slow inventory of the basin’s ruins. In places the water is only ankle-deep but moves with the force and noise of a cataract, because the rules of slope are unreliable here. A channel may roar uphill. A sheet of rainwater may pile itself against an invisible boundary, gathering vertically in a translucent wall before collapsing across the flats.

The sounds are unbearable, though almost nothing alive appears to make them.

There is water everywhere, striking water: rain hammering pools, runoff rattling in stone hollows, tides forcing their way through rootless channels, boils opening and shutting in the mud. The basin breathes through vents beneath its saturated skin. They emit a bass note so deep it is felt in the teeth and in the water inside the body. Over this comes a continuous shriek of wind dragging grains of salt and dry reed stems across the flats, a hiss like an ocean being poured through a keyhole. Thunder does not arrive from above. It travels along the ground, rolling under the mud in long black pulses, lifting puddles into trembling lenses.

Speech is useless here. Even silence is loud: silence is the roar heard when the storm briefly removes all distinct noises and leaves only pressure.

The light is perpetually late in arriving. Clouds cover the basin in shelves, swollen and bruised green at their undersides. Daylight filters through water-rich air and comes down gray-gold, weak enough that distance dissolves quickly. Yet the clouds are not stable. They buckle, open, and reseal with startling speed. In one direction there may be a dim amber glare as if sunset is happening behind a thousand miles of rain; in another, an almost nocturnal blue where lightning flashes repeatedly but never seems to strike. Some flashes remain suspended in the clouds as branching white roots. They drift with the weather, illuminating the flats for minutes at a time.

There are no trees, except in the most stubborn definition of tree. Sparse columns rise here and there from the marsh, widely spaced and black, without branches or leaves. They may be petrified trunks. They may be chimneys made by a long-dead forest. Water spills from their crowns in steady curtains. At their bases grow low plants adapted to endurance rather than abundance: gray fans of sedge, waxy bladderwort, mats of copper-green moss, threadlike flowering stems that bloom only after lightning. Vegetation does not cover the land; it grips it in isolated, difficult knots. Between these knots, the world is open and exposed—mud, water, mineral skin, and the dense, restless sky.

The few animals are difficult to observe. A large bird may cross the dimness, but it moves without wingbeats, as though borne forward by the air’s own violence. At the edge of a flooded channel, something pale occasionally turns beneath the surface: an eel, perhaps, or a root seeking a new place to anchor. There are tiny clicking creatures in the mud that withdraw before they can be seen. Once in a while, a herd of low-backed animals appears on a distant levee—black silhouettes, shoulder-deep in rain—then vanishes because the levee itself slowly folds flat into the floodwater.

Nothing remains long enough to be confidently named.

The turbulence is not merely weather. The basin continually remakes its own details. A pool overflows, and the overflow cuts a channel; the channel opens a sink; the sink exposes a buried stone plain; the stone plain cracks in the warm rain and becomes gravel; the gravel is covered by peat before the next hour has passed. Entire islands form from floating vegetation and sediment, travel for miles, collide, and compact into new ground. The inhabitants of the few scattered structures understand this, if there are inhabitants.

Their buildings stand on the rare firm ridges: small, low huts of woven mud and shell, roofed in overlapping slabs of dried peat. Most lean at angles that should be fatal but have somehow become permanent. Some are empty, their doorways filled with water. Some appear to have grown out of the ground, their walls layered like termite mounds or coral. There are no roads between them. A line of stakes may lead into a marsh and stop abruptly where the land has changed its mind. On certain stone rises, old circular foundations can be found, polished smooth by rain, with no evidence that any wall ever stood upon them.

The people—if people still inhabit the basin—leave little more than these marks. Tools are nearly absent. The place does not reward possession. Anything made is softened, rusted, submerged, dissolved, carried off, or incorporated into the endlessly crowded body of the land. A basket becomes a nest for peat. A blade becomes a brown line under mineral crust. A house becomes a bank, then a shoal, then a memory embedded in the sound of moving water.

There are laws here, though they are not comforting laws.

Nothing falls in a straight line. Rain arrives diagonally, from several directions at once, and sometimes rises from the pools in thin streams before descending again somewhere else. Footprints fill not from the edges but from the bottom, as if water has been waiting beneath the mud for the impression to appear. Distances can be crossed only if the land permits continuity: walk toward a black tower for an hour and find it no nearer, then turn away and discover it standing directly behind you. Maps become wet before they are unfolded. Compasses spin, not because of metal beneath the ground, but because north is distributed through the basin in shifting patches.

The oldest locals, if encountered, do not point. They listen. They place a palm on the ground, feel the submerged thunder, and wait for the basin to indicate which direction is temporarily real.

Despite its violence, the place is not young. It is not a beginning. It is what remains after beginnings have spent themselves. The world here has eroded past its proud forms and entered a more difficult permanence: not stability, but recurrence. Rain returns. Mud returns. Channels return in new places with old voices. The water remembers every shape it has held and keeps trying them again.

At dusk—which is only a slight deepening of the dimness—the whole plain seems to rise and breathe. Mist thickens above the warm water. The scattered black pillars vanish from their bases upward. Lightning glows inside the clouds without sound for a few seconds, and in those seconds the deafening basin seems to reveal its true scale: a wet, ancient mechanism extending beyond sight, densely occupied by water and weather, forever grinding its features down, forever making another surface on which to begin.
