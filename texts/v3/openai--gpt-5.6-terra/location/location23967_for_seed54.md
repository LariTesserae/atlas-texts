The city occupies a basin above the snowline, though snow is not the right word for what gathers there. It is pale mineral foam, a slow precipitate from the cloud layer, settling in the seams of the streets and against the foundations of towers. It does not melt. Water does.

Everything is wet.

The ground is an unfinished black stone, still deciding whether it is bedrock, pavement, or a skin. It rises in broad, low folds beneath the city’s structures, occasionally opening into shallow basins full of dark water. The water is everywhere without ever appearing as a river: behind retaining walls, inside the transparent underlayers of walkways, dripping from bridge undersides, beading on cables, pushed upward through cracks by the pressure of a saturated world beneath. In certain districts, the streets are made of closely fitted hexagonal plates that gently tremble as water passes below them. A person can feel the current through the soles of their boots.

The basin is high enough that the air is thin, cold enough that every exposed surface wears a rim of rime, and dark enough that distance is mostly inferred from sound.

There is no dawn here. Above the city hangs a permanent roof of cloud, not wholly natural and not wholly engineered, layered for kilometers in shades of charcoal and deep slate. It reflects nearly no outside light. The sky is a ceiling that sweats. At intervals it opens its internal veins: a distant silent brightening, like lightning trapped behind thick cloth, then darkness again.

The city has made its own lesser night visible.

A blue-white glow leaks from drainage channels. Soft red points burn along the edges of elevated routes. Whole buildings possess no windows, only vertical membranes of dim green luminescence that pulse slowly, perhaps with power, perhaps with the tide in the rock. The light is insufficient to make objects clear. It gives them outlines. Towers appear as masses; bridges as black arcs; crowds as motion inside a metallic haze.

The built portions are dense, but they are not cleanly separated from terrain. Construction has not conquered the basin so much as grown within its available folds. Foundations enter the stone like roots, except there are no roots, no moss, no blades of grass tucked into crevices. No leaf has ever moved in the wind here. There are no trees breaking the skyline because there is scarcely a skyline to break.

In place of vegetation, the walls bear machine-films: gray, close-fitted lattices that drink condensation and direct it into capillary grooves. They are not alive by any biological measure, but they repair themselves with patient persistence. A cracked façade will, over a week, arrange bright dust around its wound. Over a month the dust will join into plates. Over a year the seam will be invisible. The city is always being constructed, though never dramatically. It accretes. It heals. It adjusts its own weight as the wet ground shifts beneath it.

Some of the oldest towers are half submerged in newer streets.

The fauna makes the place impossible to mistake for abandoned.

They inhabit every level, in numbers that initially seem unbearable. Pale cliff-bats cling beneath transit vaults, their folded bodies packed so tightly together that they resemble the insulation of the ceiling. On the water, broad-backed skimmers move in fleets through the flooded lower plazas, their skin matte black, their many jointed legs tasting the surface tension. They do not splash. They distribute their weight over so many points that they travel across ponds as if across polished stone.

There are greater animals in the deeper channels.

Their shapes are seldom seen fully. A procession of wet, translucent vertebrae may rise past a floodgate and sink again. A round mouth, lined not with teeth but light-sensitive cilia, sometimes presses against the underside of a glass footbridge. The bridge will darken itself in response. Citizens do not react much. They have learned the schedules of these things, or at least learned which vibrations mean danger.

Most numerous are the bell-throats.

They are squat, white animals with low bodies and skin like glazed ceramic. They nest in every warm mechanical recess: exhaust alcoves, charging hollows, the sheltered backs of public staircases. Their throats contain resonant chambers. In the dark they call continuously, not melodically but structurally, each voice taking a narrow frequency and holding it. Thousands together form an immense standing chord that fills the basin day and night.

The noise is so loud that the city has been designed around it.

Buildings have inward-facing courtyards lined with sound-eating black fabric. Street signs speak through bone-conduction rails rather than speakers. Public notices appear as pressure patterns beneath the feet: three soft pulses for a descending lift, a steady vibration for incoming floodwater, a rapid rattle for predators in the channels. People wear collar seals that tune the worst of the sound out of their hearing, but even so the bell-throats are felt in the ribs, in the teeth, in the surface of the water in a cup.

Their chorus changes slowly, almost imperceptibly, as though the entire city were resting on the chest of a sleeping giant.

Other sounds thread through it. The high clicks of winged scavengers in the tower gaps. The deep hydraulic complaint of locks opening somewhere below. The constant soft ringing of ice forming on metal and then releasing. Water falling through shafts that have no bottom visible from street level. A train passing without wheels, its levitation field making the bridge plates hum in reply.

The trains run at a dignified pace. Nothing here appears hurried. Even the automated freight carriers drift along their routes as if moving through deep water. Their hulls are narrow and faceted, fitted with arrays of instruments that peer into stone, weather, and animal traffic. In a more brightly lit world they might be called advanced. Here, their capabilities are noticed only in small mercies: a walkway warming just before ice makes it dangerous; a flood barrier rising before the first dark swell arrives; a tower subtly leaning itself away from a slope that will collapse fifty years hence.

The technology is not displayed. It is embedded.

The upper city consists of narrow causeways and clustered habitation blocks, their surfaces dark with condensation. The lower city is mostly inaccessible, a drowned network of older foundations, service tunnels, and public chambers lit by the blue emission of water-treatment systems. At the boundaries between them are markets held under heavy awnings. The stalls sell heat, sound filters, sealed cloth, mineral salts, animal-safe transit permits, tools that can identify structural weakness by touch.

No one sells food grown from the ground. There is no ground food. Protein comes from cultivated vats, harvested foam colonies, the managed molting of certain domesticated channel animals. The city’s residents have developed customs against waste with the seriousness of religion. Water is never spilled deliberately. Warmth is passed hand to hand. When an animal dies in a transit shaft, the entire route may close until the body can be removed, not from sentiment alone but because decomposition changes the underground chemistry and draws worse things upward.

Beyond the dense center, the basin opens into terraces of raw stone.

They are not empty. In the darkness, one sees the low glimmer of survey stakes and the slow crawling lights of construction organisms: squat machines that soften mineral, shift it grain by grain, and lay down new foundation patterns. They work in silence except for a faint grinding. Their projects take decades. A future neighborhood is indicated only by a field of black pylons, each sweating silver water into a trench. Farther off are ridge lines under formation, broad heaved scars of young geology. The world has not finished hardening here. Every few years a new vent opens, breathing cold vapor instead of fire. Every few decades, a whole block is raised by a meter, gently enough that most residents notice only when their old stair count no longer matches.

At the rim of the basin, immense retaining structures climb toward the cloud ceiling. They are not walls precisely. They are weather engines, or perhaps dams for the atmosphere. Their ribs vanish upward into blackness, catching moisture and sending it downward in controlled, ceaseless threads. Behind them, one can hear water moving at a scale too great to picture.

The city survives because it has accepted that it is part of a wet, cold mechanism.

Its people do not speak of conquering the basin. They speak of keeping terms with it: with the pressure beneath the streets, with the animals in the floodways, with the slow-born stone, with the dark sky that gives no light but gives water without end.

From a high walkway, looking over the roofs, the city seems less like an object built on land than an elaborate reef assembled in a black inland sea. Its lights are sparse. Its voices are swallowed by the animal chorus. Its towers lean close together for warmth and stability. Water runs down every surface, gathers in every hollow, and returns to the hidden depths.

Nothing grows.

Yet nothing is still.
