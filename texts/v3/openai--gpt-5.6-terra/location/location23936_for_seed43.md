Below the surface, beneath a low hill that has long ago forgotten it has anything underneath, there is a made place called the Underwork.

It is not a cavern. Nothing in it was hollowed from stone, because there is no stone. The walls, floors, arches, drains, ledges, and ceilings are all assembled: fired brick softened by generations of vapor; timber blackened but still bearing adze marks; old clay tiles; lime plaster; wicker packed into partitions; ropes, hooks, shelves, gates, canals, and innumerable patches. The place is about the size of a small hamlet laid sideways and folded inward. One can walk from one end to the other in an hour, if one is not delayed by animals, people, dripping pipes, or the frequent need to duck.

It has been standing so long that its construction has become a kind of weather.

The floor slopes almost imperceptibly toward a central basin. Every wall does as well, in its own manner: bricks bulge where damp has entered them; plaster rounds off at knee height where bodies have brushed past; the wooden posts are thin where hands have rested. The corners are never sharp. They have been rubbed, chipped, re-mortared, nibbled, scoured by baskets, paws, sandals, and the continual narrow streamlets that appear after rain above.

Rain does not fall in the Underwork, not properly. It arrives as a change in sound.

First there is a pattering somewhere high in the lattice of ceiling beams. Then the clay cups tied beneath known leaks begin to tick. A few minutes later, every gutter and narrow ceramic channel becomes busy. Water slips down the walls in shining threads, gathers in greenish mortar seams, leaps from broken spouts, and makes the central basin breathe with circles. The inhabitants know the rain by its routes. A western rain fills the eel troughs. A hard north rain pours through the old kiln shafts and turns the stairwell into a ribbon of brown water. A soft, long rain makes droplets swell from the entire ceiling at once, until it seems the Underwork is sweating.

Most days it is merely damp.

The air is warm, close, and full of smells that never entirely separate: wet clay, sour grain mash, smoke from oil lamps, animal fur, fish scales, crushed herbs, old wood, limestone plaster, and the mineral smell of water held too long in a covered place. There are no winds, only currents. The upper passages breathe cool air down from cracks in the surface. The boiler alleys, where charcoal braziers and cookstoves are clustered, exhale heat. At night, when most lamps are extinguished, a slow warm draught follows the drainage channels and stirs the hanging strips of cloth.

The light is sufficient but never broad.

Some chambers receive daylight through shafts capped with horn panes or woven grilles. At noon, pale columns descend through dust and steam, revealing every suspended thing: ropes, drying nets, strings of onions, strips of leather, bird cages, pots hanging by their handles. Elsewhere, light comes from shallow oil lamps fitted into wall niches. Their flames are protected by pierced clay covers, so the corridors glitter with hundreds of small red-gold points. In the basin hall, polished scraps of shell and beaten tin have been set into the ceiling vault. They catch lampfire and make a wavering imitation of a night sky.

No patch of the Underwork is empty. Its density is its safety. Its passages are lined with storage jars and sleeping platforms. Its unused stairs hold coops. Its former drains have become worm beds. The dead spaces between retaining walls are stuffed with charcoal, bones, rejected pottery, and nests. A person sitting down alone in any corner will quickly discover that they are sharing it with beetles, a cat, two hens, a clutch of blind white crickets, and possibly a child sent to retrieve something from a shelf.

Animals are everywhere, and few of them are truly wild.

Small goats live on the higher dry walkways, their hooves clicking on tile, their long ears brushing the walls. They eat peelings, fungus cakes, woven reed mats left unwatched, and occasionally the plaster. Hens roost in the warm rafters, descending at dawn in a flutter of feathers and complaints. Ducks occupy the basin’s shallow edge and have learned to shove aside floating lids with their bills. Rats run in plain sight along the roof beams, fat and glossy from grain stores, watched with professional resignation by a population of rangy cats. The cats themselves sleep wherever there is warmth: on kiln bricks, over pipe junctions, across the shoulders of seated workers.

The most valued animals are the tunnel swallows.

They nest in clay tubes mounted beneath the ceilings and feed on the cloud of midges that rises wherever standing water has been left uncovered. Their wings produce a continual dry flicker in the dimness. When they begin circling low, people know a storm has found the surface. When they vanish into their tubes before midday, it means smoke is trapped in the lower rooms and someone must open the east vent.

There are also pale fish in the deepest water channels—stout, blind things with soft whiskers. They are trapped with basket funnels, stewed with pepperleaf, and returned by the handful to the channels after every meal. Nobody agrees whether this practice is practical or ceremonial. The old people say the fish are what keep the water remembering its way downhill.

Vegetation survives only where it is given a task.

Moss grows in deliberate seams, pressed into the dampest brickwork to keep mortar from crumbling too quickly. Ferns live in broken pots under the light shafts. Narrow beds of bitter herbs occupy ledges beside the warm drains: pepperleaf, fever mint, a blue-stemmed plant used to sour milk, and a greasy pale fungus cultivated on trays of rotting reed. There are no trees, no meadows, no unclaimed tangle. Every green thing is counted, watered, cut, traded, or cursed when it spreads beyond its appointed line.

Even the mold has boundaries. A white bloom in the grain rooms is scraped and burned. A black bloom near the cisterns is harvested for dye. The orange mold that grows beneath the southern stair is left untouched, because it is believed to repel biting insects, and because, after several generations, no one can remember whether anyone actually tested it.

The Underwork is loud enough that silence feels alarming.

Water taps and runs. Pots knock. Someone is always chopping roots on a board. Mortars pound grain and salt. Ducks argue in the basin. Goats call from their raised pens. Swallows scratch inside their tubes. A hundred voices pass through the passages—calling prices, scolding children, singing while they work, asking who has seen a missing spoon, a missing cat, a missing grandfather.

Sound does not travel cleanly here. It bends around the curved brickwork and descends stairs. A conversation in a side room can seem to come from directly behind a listener. Hammering from the repair galleries becomes a huge, slow heartbeat throughout the settlement. At night, the water channels amplify every drip until the Underwork is full of tiny footsteps.

There is a bell made from a cracked bronze cooking pot suspended over the central basin. It is struck for flood, fire, the sighting of a surface patrol, and births that have gone badly. Its note is ugly, flat, and impossible to ignore. When it sounds, shutters come up, animals are driven higher, the basin gates are checked, and everyone who can carry a bucket begins carrying one.

For this is a place with rules, though most are not written.

No one may block a drain, even temporarily. No lamp may be left at floor level, where goats and children can reach it. Nothing edible may be washed in the north runoff. The basin belongs to everyone and therefore to no one: one may draw water, float nets, bathe infants, clean blood from tools, or release fish, but one may not claim its center. If a wall begins to bulge, the nearest household must mark it with red clay and report it before sleeping. If the tunnel swallows are silent, fires are put out until the air clears.

The deepest rule is older than the people now living there: nothing is discarded whole.

A broken jar becomes drainage sherds. A cracked beam becomes pegs, then charcoal. Worn sandals become patches for pipe wraps. Bones become needles, handles, broth, and finally powder for the lime mortar. Even the soft bricks that crumble from the oldest walls are ground, mixed with ash, and pressed into new bricks. The Underwork renews itself from its own injuries. Its materials circulate slowly through it like blood.

In the center, where all the slopes meet, is the basin hall. It is the only room large enough for everyone to stand together. The ceiling rises in a squat dome made of thousands of hand-set bricks, each stained differently by smoke, moisture, and age. Around the basin are market tables, repair benches, a shrine of river-polished objects brought down from the surface generations ago, and a ring of communal ovens. The ovens are almost always warm. Their walls have become glossy with soot and grease; their mouths open like dark, patient faces.

Above the basin hangs a wooden wheel, once part of a lifting machine. Now ropes run from it in every direction: to baskets, lantern racks, drying frames, the basin gate, a child’s swing, and a net of small clay pots that ring softly whenever the whole place shudders.

Sometimes the ground does shudder. Not violently. Not enough to break things. It is more like a deep animal shifting its weight somewhere beyond the constructed dark. Then the lamps tremble, the ducks stop talking, dust loosens from the vault, and everyone waits.

The Underwork has endured such tremors for so long that its builders have made room for them. Every arch has a little give. Every shelf is tied, not nailed. The channels have overflow lips. The walls are thick where they must resist and layered where they must bend. The settlement does not conquer the pressure of earth above it. It bargains with it daily.

By evening, surface light fades from the shafts, leaving only the lamps and cooking fires. Heat gathers under the low ceilings. The animals settle noisily. Water continues making its small determined journeys through channels made by hands that have been dust for centuries. In the upper passages, people returning from above bring down baskets of dry grass, fruit skins, scavenged boards, and news of weather.

Then the place becomes most itself: bright in fragments, wet at the edges, crowded with breath, work, hunger, feathers, smoke, and the enormous patience of repaired things.
