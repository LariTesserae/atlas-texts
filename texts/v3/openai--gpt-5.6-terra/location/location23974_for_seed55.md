There is no shore.

The water begins above the world and continues through it, filling every hall, field, passage, vessel, and hollow. It is cold water, old water, black except where it gathers a little grey light from distances too great to be useful. The whole place lies low: not on an abyssal floor exactly, but across a drowned plain so broad that its horizons have their own weather. Far off, clouds of silt lift and settle in slow fronts. In some regions the water is clear enough to see a mile through the blue-dark; in others it is stained green by gardens, or brown with the dust of crumbling walls.

Nothing here is natural ground.

The plain is paved from edge to edge, although almost no pavement remains recognizable as pavement. It was made from fitted slabs of dark stone, fired clay blocks, woven mats of root-fiber packed with mud, and immense rafts of timber laid one upon another. Over ages, seams opened. Edges softened. Corners disappeared beneath a continuous velvet of growth. The builders’ work has not been destroyed so much as worn into another condition: every structure has acquired the gentle, exhausted contours of river stones.

The old roads are wide enough to be seen from far above as pale lanes between dense growths. They no longer lead cleanly anywhere. Their surfaces buckle gradually into low ridges and shallow troughs. A traveler might follow one for days, passing the roofs of submerged dwellings, stone cisterns, shrines, retaining walls, grain stores, towers, watergates, and long communal platforms. All are open to the water. Doors hang ajar or have long since swollen into their frames. Windows have become square mouths crowded with weed.

The architecture is primitive in its means and enormous in its patience. The place was built by hands with stone adzes, bone scrapers, wooden wedges, clay kilns, rope, baskets, and fire. No iron ribs hide within the walls. No machines hum under the floors. Yet every visible thing was planned, carried, shaped, fitted, lashed, stacked, or grown into place.

Some buildings are made of cyclopean fieldstone, their blocks fitted without mortar so precisely that the joints have survived the centuries. Some are of fired brick, now rounded as if kneaded by the water. Others were constructed from whole tree trunks driven upright into the drowned soil, their tops bound together to support platforms. Many of those forests of posts still stand. They form submerged colonnades stretching beyond sight, black vertical ranks among which the gardens have thickened.

Vegetation has become the country’s soft anatomy.

Long ribbons of dark green kelp grow from rooflines and parapets, streaming in the faint deep current. Mosses coat stone, timber, pottery, and old carved faces alike. Pale pondweed fills courtyards. Fine red-brown fronds emerge from cracks in the paving, making every street resemble a furred path. There are groves, too: not trees as they would appear above water, but drowned forests whose trunks rise through the submerged city, branches netted in algae and lichen. Their roots have worked into foundations and emptied chambers, not violently but completely. A wall that has leaned for a thousand years rests now against a living trunk. A house may be held together by roots where its original beams have dissolved.

In the deeper districts, gardens survive as architecture. Low walls still enclose rectangular beds. Stone channels guide the water through them. Flights of steps descend into pools thick with luminous green weed. The plants are not luminous in the bright, magical sense; they only retain what little light reaches them, holding it in their tissues until their leaves seem a shade lighter than the surrounding dark. To move through one of these gardens is to cross a dimly visible geometry: terraces, seed basins, raised beds, trellis frames, all softened under growth.

There are no fish among the stems.

No insects stir in the drowned upper rooms. No shell clicks on the stones. No birds cross the surface, if there is a surface. Nothing hunts, flees, feeds, nests, breeds, or calls. The vegetation occupies every unattended place, but it does so with the deep quiet of a world without animals. Its fronds turn. Its mats expand. Its roots enter cracks. That is enough.

The silence is not absolute. It has texture.

Water presses against everything with a low, nearly inaudible weight. A loosened roof tile occasionally shifts and settles. Wood, saturated beyond any hope of drying, gives a soft internal creak. On cold days—though day is difficult to distinguish here—the broad timber structures contract a little, and a sound passes through their beams like a distant throat clearing. In some districts there is a faint clatter of hanging ceramic charms touching one another in the current. Elsewhere, ropes of old reed fiber rub against stone posts: a patient, fibrous whisper.

The largest sound is weather.

It comes not as wind but as pressure. A slow-moving cold front can cross the drowned plain over several weeks, changing the water’s clarity, bending whole forests of weed in one direction, carrying a gradual haze of mineral dust through roofless plazas. When it arrives, the walls darken. The plants fold their blades close to their stems. Fine silt rolls down the old avenues, following the paths that carts and bare feet once followed. Then the current weakens, the silt settles, and the city returns to stillness.

At the center of this world are the reservoir districts: vast basins, embanked with hand-laid stone, now indistinguishable from inland seas. Their retaining walls run for miles beneath the water, low and heavy, furred with green. Gates made from overlapping logs still divide one basin from another. Most have failed, but some remain wedged in place, and the water beyond them can be unexpectedly clearer, colder, or greener. One may pass through a narrow gate and find the entire color of the world changed.

The builders understood water, though not enough to keep it out forever.

They made channels to carry floodwater away, raised dwellings on platforms, cut spillways into the low hills, and built stepped walls against the lakes. They excavated ponds and lined them with clay. They gathered rain from roofs into cisterns. Their tools were simple, but their labor was vast. Every embankment suggests generations. Every wall contains the accumulated decisions of people who expected the land to remain land.

Eventually, perhaps, the water rose faster than repairs could be made. Or the great gates failed during a season of storms. Or the world had always been below some outer sea, held dry only by human maintenance. The reason is no longer present in any dramatic form. There are no broken ships embedded in walls, no sudden heaps of belongings, no skeletons in doorways. The flood is too old for catastrophe. It has been absorbed into the shape of things.

Even the highest constructions are underwater.

Their tops reach into a faint, silver-black zone where the water seems thinner. There are bell towers with no bells, watch platforms, granary mounds, signal cairns made from stacked stone, and the remains of long bridges. Their uppermost beams support drapes of weed that trail down hundreds of feet. Looking upward from the streets, one sees these dark forms converging into obscurity, like the roots of a forest suspended overhead.

There is very little light. What exists arrives diluted through leagues of water and weather, reduced to a color rather than illumination. It outlines a wall’s upper edge. It catches a broad leaf turning in the current. It lays a dull pewter sheen on the backs of old paving stones. Most of the landscape must be known by touch, by the resistance of plants, by the change in temperature near a stone face, by the echo of a hand-knock against a wall.

Yet the darkness does not make the place hostile. It makes it settled.

Nothing is in a hurry. A collapsed arch will take another century to lower itself fully into the road. A garden wall will lean one finger-width farther before the next cold season. Roots will fill a chamber grain by grain with soft black soil. The vegetation will cover a carved inscription, then recede from one letter, then cover it again. The built world and the growing world have reached a truce so old that neither can be separated from the other.

In the deepest avenues, the paving is almost perfectly smooth. Erosion has taken every harshness away. Stone thresholds have become shallow lips. Staircases are not climbed but drifted over, their steps reduced to soft underwater terraces. Statues, where they remain, have no faces; their heads are rounded, their hands melted into their sides. The city has not become ruin in the usual sense. It has become terrain.

And because it is so vast, terrain is what it feels like.

A person could travel for months through its drowned provinces: across the reed-roof quarters and their dark post forests; through brick districts laid out in broad grids; along old causeways where gardens grow waist-deep in the water; over immense communal floors whose mosaics are hidden beneath green felt; between reservoir walls as broad as hills. There would always be another horizon of constructed shapes, another low ridge that proved to be a roof, another valley that had once been a market, another forest of pillars supporting a vanished town.

The world has been made completely, and then given back to water.

It remains, cold and quiet, beneath its weight.
