The city occupies a shelf beneath a pale ridge, though from a distance it does not look like a city. It looks like a dark green growth lodged in the mountain’s folds: a dense swelling of stems, roofs, roots, and wet leaves gathered around a basin where snowmelt cannot decide whether to become a lake.

The shelf is high enough that the air is thin and always on the verge of freezing. Clouds drag themselves through the upper streets. They catch in the latticework of branches and hang there, unraveling slowly into beads. Water gathers everywhere: along the ribs of leaves, in cupped fungi, under mats of moss, at the polished roots that make the city’s pavement. It does not rain often in the ordinary way. Instead the whole place condenses. Walls sweat. Bridges drip. Mist enters through every crack and becomes a faint silver skin on the interior surfaces.

Nothing here has been cut into shape.

The houses are made from plants trained by patience, pressure, grafting, and generations of selective tending. Broad-rooted trees grow in tight spirals, their trunks split and braided around chambers large enough for families. Their branches have been bent into low, many-layered roofs; beneath the outer leaves are dense felted strata of fiber, pale wood, old bark, and living moss. Windows are gaps encouraged between boughs. Doors are living curtains of reed and vine, heavy with wetness, which part when pushed aside and close themselves gradually afterward.

The city is small—perhaps a hundred dwellings, perhaps fewer—but there is almost no emptiness in it. Every space is occupied. If there is a wall, something climbs it. If there is a gutter, something roots in it. If a roof has sagged, ferns have made a garden in the hollow. The narrow lanes are not really lanes but negotiated gaps between supports: root corridors, channels of flattened liverwort, stairways formed by overlapping shelf fungi. One must turn sideways to pass in places. The pathways are soft underfoot and dark with absorbed water, yet their deep layers are astonishingly firm, compacted by years of feet and root growth.

At the center stands the cistern-grove.

It is a cluster of ancient-looking plants, though the settlement itself is young and the mountain shelf has only recently been exposed by a retreating glacier. The plants were brought as seedlings or spores, perhaps, and have been encouraged into maturity with remarkable speed. Their trunks braid over a deep hollow in the ground where water collects beneath a membrane of roots. The grove holds the city together physically: its roots spread under every district, binding the new soil against washout, lifting terraces, drinking excess meltwater, releasing it gradually through pale threadlike feeder-stems. The people tend it with no metal, no engines, no artificial lamps. Their tools are bone, horn, stone, woven root, sharpened wood. Much of the work is not done by tools at all. It is done by tying, weighting, singing near seedlings, placing wet moss where a branch ought to take root.

The city has terraces, but they are not stone terraces. Each is a thick horizontal mattress of living growth held in place by root walls. On them grow cold herbs, dense leafy vegetables, blue-green bulbs with waxy skins, and a grainlike grass that ripens almost black under the dim sky. In the brief bright season, every spare ledge becomes a field. In the long cold season, the plants do not die so much as draw inward. Their leaves darken; their stems grow hard and close; heat from decay and respiration remains trapped in the packed interiors of the dwellings.

The mountain beyond is raw.

Its upper slopes were recently freed from ice and retain the scraped, sharp look of exposed bone. Black rock teeth rise through snowbanks. Fresh channels, cut by meltwater, gleam with slick ochre clay. Every thaw alters something: a narrow wall slumps, a ribbon of gravel spills across a lower garden, a new spring appears from beneath the hillside. The settlement does not resist this landscape by imposing permanence. Its edges move. A house that begins to lean is braced with living poles, then slowly abandoned as its roots are redirected into a new structure farther from the unstable ground. Paths close. New paths open. Whole courtyards can become ponds in a wet year, then be reclaimed by reeds and floating mats.

There are no straight lines except those made briefly by water.

In the dimness, the city’s colors are subdued but various: deep green nearly black; the gray-white fuzz of lichen; wet brown bark; translucent yellow leaves underlit by cloud; occasional bruised purple berries. The cold brings a blue cast to everything. At dawn, if dawn can be called the slow whitening of the mist, frost gathers along the high nets of vine and turns the city into a web of dull glass. By midday the frost melts, drops through the layers, and disappears into the floor.

The quiet is not empty.

There is the constant small sound of water moving through plant matter: dripping from a roof edge, ticking into a basin, traveling inside hollow stems. Leaves rub against one another when the mountain wind reaches down. Roots creak in the walls. Soft rainless showers fall from the canopy whenever a branch shifts under its own weight. The settlement has the muffled acoustics of a forest after snow: voices do not travel far, footfalls vanish into moss, and even busy work seems private.

Fauna is scarce at this elevation and in such density of human-tended growth. A few white moths sleep beneath leaves, waking only on the mildest nights. Tiny dark frogs inhabit the cistern-grove, their bodies cold and still as pebbles until the thaw. There are small birds, but they are heard more often than seen: brief dry chirps from the outer thorn hedges, then silence. On the ridge, sometimes, a long-legged mountain animal appears in the fog, antlered or branched in a way that makes it difficult to tell where its body ends and the surrounding brush begins. It does not enter the city. The city is too crowded, too wet, too thoroughly claimed by roots.

Its boundaries are living boundaries.

A thick ring of thornwood surrounds the shelf, woven through with climbing shrubs and low trees that bend inward in the wind. Beyond it, the mountain drops away into a white ravine. There are only three passages through the barrier. Each is narrow, roofed by branches, and marked with bundles of colored moss. No gates need be built. The thornwood opens for those who know where to press, which cords to untie, which young branches may safely be bent aside. Left untended, it would seal itself within a few seasons.

At night, there is no fire visible from outside. Light is precious and contained. Within the homes, pale fat-lamps or sheltered embers throw a weak amber glow through leaf-veined walls. From the ridge, the city is nearly invisible: only a few warm spots breathing faintly within the green-black mass, quickly obscured by cloud.

It is not an old city, even where its trunks look old. It is a city growing fast because the mountain has only just made room for it. Every year brings more water, more soil, more exposed shelf, more roots reaching into fresh crevices. It feels provisional in the way a nest feels provisional: built for the present season, repaired constantly, certain of its own return.

The people who live there do not speak of buildings as finished things. A dwelling is “still taking hold,” “thickening,” “learning the winter,” “making room.” A street is “open this year.” The cistern-grove is not a monument but an appetite. The city is not laid upon the mountain.

It is being taught, root by root, to remain.
