The land lies low and broad beneath a night that has not cooled it.

There is no horizon in the usual sense. The ground does not rise enough to show a distant outline, and the darkness closes around every open space before it can become a view. Low swales, shallow channels, old flood basins, and firm hummocks pass into one another under a press of vegetation. The country has been shaped by water for a long time, though water is not presently abundant. It survives in the blackness beneath roots, in films over clay, in the damp seams of fallen wood, and in the warm breath rising from leaf litter. Rain comes hard and briefly here, then departs; afterward the whole plain holds its memory for days.

The soil is dark, soft at the surface, and packed over deeper layers of old silt. In places it gives underfoot with a wet sigh. In others, it is dry enough to crack around the roots, its cracks immediately inhabited by pale insects, seedlings, fungus, and the fine searching threads of plants. Nothing remains bare for long. A fallen branch is not an object on the ground so much as a temporary district: bark peeling, ants entering, beetles chewing galleries, moss taking hold in the damp underside, vines throwing their first loops over it before the wood has finished falling still.

The vegetation is lush, but not spacious. It is a crowded lowland growth: reeds around old pools, broad-leafed shrubs, thorned runners, palms, grass tall enough to hide an animal’s entire passage, and trees whose lower limbs disappear into curtains of climbers. Every trunk carries another life. Lichens and small ferns occupy the bark. Orchids cling in pockets of accumulated dust. Vines lie across vines. A tree may be healthy, hollow, flowering, colonized, and slowly strangled all at once.

The heat remains physical after sunset. It fills the throat. It gathers under leaves and inside the still chambers of brush. The air smells of warm green matter, bruised herbs, mud, animal fur, and the faint mineral odor released when damp earth begins to dry again. Condensation beads along grass blades and on the smooth surfaces of large leaves, though there is no coolness in it. The moisture is merely another form of heat.

Animal life is everywhere, so densely distributed that the darkness seems alive before any creature is seen. Small bodies rustle in the ground cover. Lizards sleep under bark. Frogs sit in the remaining wet places with their mouths open to the damp air. Moths collect in pale drifts wherever a patch of sky offers even the weakest reflected light. Bats stitch unseen paths beneath the canopy. Birds settle, wake, settle again. Snakes move with the patience of roots. Larger mammals pass through the reeds without breaking them visibly, leaving only a changing density of darkness and, afterward, a thread of scent.

Yet the place is mostly quiet.

It is not silent. There is a low ongoing work beneath the silence: chewing, scraping, the tiny dry percussion of insects in wood, a periodic drip from leaves, the soft collapse of soil at the edge of a burrow. Far off, an animal gives one short call, and another answers after so long an interval that the reply seems less like conversation than confirmation. A wing brushes a leaf. Something splashes once in a ditch hidden by sedges. Then the land resumes its held breath.

What might be called industry happens without buildings.

Under the soil, termites process fallen timber into chambers, galleries, and fine earth. Ants move fragments of leaf in steady lines through the dark, cutting, hauling, storing, tending their cultivated fungus in sealed rooms. Beetles reduce trunks to powder. Fungi soften what the beetles cannot. Roots draw the released material upward again. The work is organized, continuous, and almost invisible: a manufacturing of soil, a recycling system with no smokestacks, no walls, no machinery except jaws, enzymes, weather, and time.

Even the larger animals participate in it. Hooves compress paths that become drainage lines. Burrowing creatures turn layers of soil that floodwater later rearranges. Birds carry seeds between the scattered openings. Predators keep trails from belonging too completely to any one herbivore. Nothing here is pristine in the sense of untouched; everything is touched constantly, but by living hands, mouths, feet, and weather rather than tools.

The terrain has reached a durable restlessness. Old erosion has softened every edge. Channels are shallow and established, their banks woven tight by roots. The heavier rains will cut new little scars, topple a few trees, fill some depressions, and leave others dry. But the low plain does not transform quickly. It absorbs disturbance. A fallen tree becomes a bridge, then a mound, then a line of richer vegetation, then only a difference in the grass.

Above the canopy, the sky is dark and thick, perhaps clouded, perhaps simply moonless. In a rare break, a small scatter of stars appears, dull through heat haze. Their light does not travel far. It catches the wet back of a leaf, the eye of a frog, the thread of a spider’s web, then is swallowed by foliage.

The landscape does not present itself as wilderness in the grand, empty sense. It is too occupied for that. It is a packed warm country of low ground and persistent life, with scarcely a vacant handspan anywhere. Its great spaces are not visible clearings but systems extending outward through darkness: wet channels linked to roots, roots linked to burrows, burrows linked to feeding trails, trails linked to distant patches of denser forest. The whole plain continues beyond sight, busy in minute ways, patient in large ones, making itself over and over without ever appearing to move.
