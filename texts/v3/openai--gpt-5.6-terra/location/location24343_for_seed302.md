At this height the world has been stripped down to its durable elements.

There are no trees, no grass, no moss softening the stone. No green anywhere. The slopes are made of pale mineral ribs, black seams of wet rock, shelves of old ice, and fields of shattered crystal that lock together so tightly that the ground appears paved by geological pressure. Every available surface is occupied: by boulders fitted against boulders, by salt-white plates, by blunt columns of limestone, by packed banks of granular ice, by dark wet hollows too narrow to enter. The valley is open overhead but crowded underfoot, a vast bowl filled to its rim with matter.

It is bright enough to hurt the eyes.

The sky, when visible, is a hard, luminous white-blue, bleached by altitude. It gives no impression of a moving weather system. Light lies evenly over everything: in the gullies, under overhangs, along the near-vertical walls of the basin. The wet stone reflects it in cold flashes. Some cliffs are gray and chalky, others nearly blue-black, glossy from permanent seepage. Here and there, broad faces of translucent mineral seem lit from within, as though the daylight has entered the mountain and become trapped behind its skin.

Water is everywhere, though there is no river in the ordinary sense.

It condenses on the undersides of ledges and falls in steady threads. It glides down walls in thin, overlapping sheets. It collects in clefts between rocks, disappears, and returns twenty yards away in a fan of white spray. The ground is not muddy—there is no soil—but saturated. The stone sweats. A person kneeling beside a crack might see clear water welling upward through a bed of angular fragments, each fragment polished by ages of contact with the others.

The basin seems fixed in this state: neither drying nor flooding, neither freezing fully nor thawing. Ice remains in the shade, transparent and old, while water runs beside it without reducing it. Mineral crusts grow around the water’s edges but do not visibly expand. The channels have their shapes, their routes, their exact volumes; they have had them for so long that water and stone appear to have agreed on them.

The sound makes this difficult to appreciate.

It is deafening, constant, and without drama. Not the sudden roar of a waterfall after a storm, but the total voice of a terrain whose every crack is conducting water and air. Thousands of little falls strike hidden shelves. Runoff hammers through blind tunnels below the ground. Wind, compressed between walls, gives a low unbroken note like breath blown across the mouth of an enormous bottle. In some places the rock chambers amplify this into a metallic bellow. In others, the sound becomes a fine hiss, so dense it resembles silence only from very far away.

Speech would vanish almost immediately. Footsteps would not be heard. Even the impact of a falling stone would be swallowed by the larger, unending noise.

There are few animals. The absence is conspicuous, though not theatrical. Occasionally a pale, broad-winged bird passes high across the white sky, so high that its shadow never reaches the basin. It does not call. There may be small blind creatures in the water-filled fissures, colorless things adapted to cold mineral darkness, but nothing announces their presence. No insects circle the seepage. No grazing trails cross the slopes. Nothing has eaten the landscape, because there is nothing here to eat.

Yet it is not dead.

The rocks are covered in textures that suggest a slow, non-vegetal liveliness: mats of pale chemical bloom around vents, amber films on submerged stone, dark velvety mineral deposits that curl along waterlines. They are not plants. They have no leaves, roots, or stems. They seem instead to be the visible habits of the water itself—compounds precipitating, dissolving, and precipitating again in a cycle too slow to witness. In shallow pools, delicate lattices form beneath the surface, white as bone and sharp as frost. They do not drift. They hold their shapes against the current.

The land has no horizon in the gentle sense. Beyond each crowded slope lies another wall, another pale ravine, another fan of rubble shining under the same severe light. The basin opens regionally: one could travel through connected stone valleys for days, perhaps following the noise from one throat of the mountain to another. But there would be no true path. The terrain has settled into a dense, mature arrangement, long weathered and balanced, with every possible route pinched between wet faces and unstable-looking—but actually ancient—blocks.

Nothing is newly fallen. Nothing is about to happen.

A slab leaning over a chasm may look ready to collapse, but it has leaned there for centuries. A frozen cataract appears caught mid-descent, yet water has been passing beneath it all that time. The bright clouds do not seem to travel. The sound never rises, never fades, never pauses for breath.

It is a high country of permanent aftermath: a place that feels as if some immense event has already occurred, long before any observer arrived, and has since become the ordinary condition of the world.
