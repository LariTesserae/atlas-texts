The basin is no larger than a market square, though it takes time to understand where its edges are. It lies at the foot of an old retaining wall of black stone, or perhaps in the hollow left by some vanished building. The ground is low enough that water has nowhere decisive to go. It gathers in every seam.

Nothing grows here in the usual sense. There are no grasses leaning in the damp, no weeds worrying at the masonry, no green at all. The surfaces are mineral, metal, shell, soot, and wetness. Yet the place is crowded with life.

Rain has recently passed, or is always in the act of passing. Drops fall at long intervals from overhead pipes and broken gutters, making individual silver reports in the pooled ground. The wall sweats continuously. Its mortar has failed in places, exposing brick beneath, each brick furred not with moss but with a pale chalky bloom that could be salt, fungus, or the slow exhalation of the wall itself. Water runs down in thin, deliberate threads and disappears among the paving stones.

The paving is old and uneven, compressed close together by the confines of the basin. Flat iron plates alternate with yellow-grey cobbles, cracked ceramic tiles, sections of ribbed concrete, and the grates of drains that no longer lead anywhere reliable. Most of the gaps are filled with black water. Beneath its surface lies a dense sediment of filings, shell fragments, smooth bits of glass, dead wires, coal dust, and tiny white stones washed down from streets higher up.

There are structures scattered around the wet floor, though calling them structures gives them more confidence than they possess. A narrow pump house leans against one wall, built of riveted sheet metal and patched with mismatched doors. Its windows are clouded from within. A rust-red winch hangs above a sump, its chain descending into water and vanishing. Across the basin stands a small electrical cabinet on stilts, painted in warning stripes that have weathered to the color of old teeth. A few lamps are mounted under corrugated awnings, but only one is working. It burns with a weak amber filament, making the rain visible whenever it falls through its cone.

The machinery has not entirely stopped. Somewhere inside the pump house, a motor gives a low, cyclical shudder: three seconds of effort, a pause, then another effort. The sound is not loud. It is felt more than heard through the wet soles of boots and through the thin metal skins of the little buildings. At irregular intervals, a valve coughs. Warm vapor leaves a pipe near the ground and drifts over the water, flattening itself into the basin air.

The coolness is intimate rather than severe. It gets into sleeves and collars. Everything touched is damp. The air smells of iron, wet stone, oil diluted almost beyond recognition, and the faint clean sharpness of electricity. In the deepest corners there is another odor: briny, animal, like tide pools beneath a pier.

The animals account for it.

They are everywhere once the eye adjusts. Small pale things move in the drainage channels: many-legged scavengers with translucent plates over their backs, folding and unfolding themselves through the water. Their bodies resemble shrimp, though they have no obvious heads and carry pinpricks of blue luminescence under their shells. They gather in dense trembling knots wherever warm runoff enters the basin.

Larger creatures cling to the walls. They look at first like discarded gloves soaked through, but they lift themselves on hooked limbs and slowly traverse the brickwork. Their skin is grey and slick, seamed with dark mineral ridges. They feed by pressing their undersides to wet stone, rasping away invisible films. When disturbed, they close into hard little bundles, so convincingly stony that the wall seems to acquire a fresh scatter of pebbles.

In the water are the most numerous beings: narrow, blind swimmers with whiskered faces and transparent fins. They thread through pipes, emerge from flooded cracks, circle the warm sump, and vanish again. They are not fish exactly. Their bones—or whatever serves as bones—gleam faintly through their bodies. At night, or in the perpetual dimness that passes for night here, their schools make pale moving clouds beneath the black surface.

There are predators too. One lives under the pump-house steps. It is roughly the size of a cat, but built low and broad, with six splayed feet and a glossy hide like wet coal. It waits motionless with its mouth open in the water. When the small swimmers pass, its throat flickers. It does not chase. Nothing here spends more energy than it has to.

The basin has rules, though no sign explains them.

Water is allowed to enter, but it must leave changed. By the time it reaches the lowest drain it has passed through rust, heat, animal bodies, lime, old oil, and the patient filtration of the wall-clingers. It comes out clearer than it entered, though no one knows where it goes afterward.

Nothing with roots survives here. Seeds carried in by wind or boot heel soften, split, and are quietly consumed by the chemistry of the ground. Even wood loses itself quickly: handles blacken, boards become sponge-like, then collapse into fibrous brown paste. Metal fares better, but only if it is already old. New steel reddens rapidly. Old iron develops a black skin and seems almost protected by age.

The animals obey the water level. When rain is heavy, the basin rises by inches and every living thing rearranges itself. The wall creatures climb. The transparent swimmers appear in gutters at ankle height. The coal-black predators occupy thresholds and stairwells. Tiny plated scavengers emerge by the thousands and make dark rippling mats across the paving. When the pumps succeed in lowering the water, the entire population withdraws with eerie discipline into drains, cracks, pipes, and damp cavities in the stone.

The one functioning lamp does not attract insects. There are no flying things. Instead, whenever its current flickers, the creatures in the nearest pools answer with a brief blue brightening beneath the surface, as if the water remembers another kind of light.

Beyond the retaining wall there may be streets, warehouses, a river, perhaps an industrial district that has forgotten this low little place. Their existence is suggested by occasional vibrations, distant wheel-noise, a muffled horn, the dim orange glow that leaks through a high crack in the masonry. But the basin feels complete without them: a cramped wet chamber at the bottom of the city’s circulation, neither natural nor fully made, busy with quiet lives and old machines.

Standing there, one gets the impression that the pump is not trying to empty the basin.

It is trying to keep it from becoming something larger.
