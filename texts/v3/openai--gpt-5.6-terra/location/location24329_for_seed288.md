The city lies beneath a country that has forgotten it.

There is no entrance anyone uses now. The old roads descend through broad cuts in limestone and disappear under shelves of rock, but at their lower ends they have been softened by centuries of dust, root growth, and the passage of animals. The underground valley beyond is not a cave in the narrow sense. It is a whole sunk landscape: a basin roofed by stone, several miles across, its ceiling rising and dipping like a dark, petrified sky. In places it is so high that its upper vault cannot be seen. In others it comes low over old districts and presses the air into cool, mineral-smelling lanes.

Everything here has been dry for longer than memory.

There are no pools, no streams under the paving, no seepage on the walls. The stone is pale and old, fissured into rounded surfaces by an erosion that occurred before the basin was sealed. Its cliffs have the worn, patient appearance of exposed mountains, though no rain has touched them for an age. Fine gray dust gathers in their folds. Salt blooms faintly in cracks. The ground crunches underfoot with flakes of gypsum, old shell-like mineral deposits, and the brittle litter of leaves.

Yet the city is green.

Vegetation grows where it can find the smallest reserve of shade, soil, and breath. Great mats of dark fern spread along the bases of buildings. Their fronds are leathery, almost black in the dimness, and they gather the mineral haze that drifts down from the ceiling. Pale grasses rise from the joints of old streets, sparse at first, then thick in plazas where windblown dust has collected for centuries. There are shrubs in courtyards, their leaves small and waxed against the dryness; there are low trees as well, with white trunks and narrow crowns, rooted deeply in collapsed rooms.

The trees do not need much water. Nothing here does. They live slowly on vapor breathed out by stone, on moisture carried in the bodies of insects and animals, on condensation that occurs only during the coldest hours near the deepest floor. Their roots find ancient clay beds beneath the streets. Their leaves open wide only in the dark and fold themselves tight when the faint overhead glow strengthens.

That glow comes from the ceiling.

Across the vault are broad fields of luminous fungus, not brilliant enough to banish darkness, but sufficient to make the city visible in gradients: blue-gray avenues, black arches, copper-colored roofs, silver edges on leaves. The fungi grow in long bands where cracks cross the stone. From a distance they resemble a permanently clouded night sky. Their light is old and weak, and it does not flicker. It simply persists.

The buildings are numerous but widely spaced. This was never a cramped city. It was planned for space, for air, for processions, for the keeping of animals. Low houses of fitted stone stand along avenues broad enough for herds. Their roofs are flattened, and many have fallen inward, becoming square gardens. There are storage halls, colonnades, market courts, stair towers carved directly into the surrounding rock. At the center of the basin stands a civic terrace: several levels of broad steps, worn smooth, leading to a ring of roofless chambers. Pillars remain there, thick and plain. No inscription survives on them. The builders did not decorate much. Their work was heavy, durable, and practical—stone walls fitted without mortar, drains that now carry only dust, doorframes rounded by innumerable hands.

There are no machines. No wires, no lamps, no forgotten engines waiting to be understood. The city is urban in the oldest sense: a great number of lives organized around streets, thresholds, shared enclosures, animal yards, food stores, burial rooms, and places for gathering. Its technology is masonry, rope, clay, bone, woven fiber, and habit.

Some of its inhabitants may still be human, though from a distance it is difficult to tell.

Figures sometimes move between the pillars or along the raised walkways. They carry nothing that shines. They wear layered skins and woven mantles, pale with dust. Their settlement occupies only a portion of the old city, principally the warmward side where the roof descends and the fungal light is thickest. Smoke never rises; fuel is too precious, and flame consumes air. Meals are dried, fermented, ground, or eaten raw. The people speak close to one another when they must speak, but the basin is not a quiet place in which speech carries.

It is deafening.

The noise begins as a continuous roof-high vibration, a dense, living roar. Tens of thousands of bats crowd the upper cavities and narrow chimneys of the ceiling. Their wings make a leather-dry surf of sound. They chirp, scrape, quarrel, settle, rise again. In the trees and ruined houses, insects rasp continuously: hard-shelled clickers in the walls, cave crickets beneath paving slabs, broad-winged moths beating themselves against the glowing fungus. Flocks of white cave birds nest in ledges above the central terraces, their calls sharp enough to strike the stone and return in layers.

There are larger animals too. Blind deer move through the fern gardens in loose, cautious groups, their antlers pale and wide. Thick-furred burrowing cattle sleep in abandoned halls and emerge at dusk to crop the grasses. Packs of long-bodied foxes hunt among the collapsed dwellings. Everywhere, lizards warm themselves against fungus-lit walls; beetles turn dry dung and leaf litter into dust; pale spiders string nets between statues and broken lintels. The ecosystem wastes almost nothing.

The sound becomes greatest at the basin’s far end, where an enormous collapse has opened a series of sloping galleries into lower country. The galleries act as throats. Air moves through them steadily, slowly, without weather, drawn by the different temperatures of the deep chambers below. It is not a gusting wind. It is a permanent exhalation, cold and dry, carrying spores and the smell of animals. It makes every hanging vine tremble. It whistles through window openings. It finds old pipes, cracked culverts, hollow statues, and turns the abandoned city into an instrument.

Some streets hum in a single low note. Some towers moan. In the old aqueduct—now empty, its channels full of roots—the air produces a sound like distant chanting. The inhabitants know these noises as landmarks. They call a district not by its vanished official name but by what it sounds like: the Hissing Market, the Bell Steps, the Long Throat, the Court of Insects.

Nothing changes quickly here.

A wall may settle by the width of a fingernail in a generation. A tree may take a hundred years to shade a single doorway. The dust on an avenue records footprints for days, perhaps weeks, before the slow air smooths them away. Buildings do not collapse in dramatic storms; they loosen grain by grain, their stones gradually returning to the basin floor. The city is being absorbed with extraordinary patience by the country beneath it.

And still it is inhabited. Still paths remain clear. Still animals are led through the old gates and people gather on the terrace when the glow over the ceiling dims into its nightward phase.

At those times the basin appears larger than it is. The far neighborhoods vanish into dim green haze. The roof becomes invisible. The old streets run outward between ferns and silent façades until they seem to enter a separate world: not a buried city, not a cave, but a cool and ancient plain made of stone, breathing slowly in the dark.
