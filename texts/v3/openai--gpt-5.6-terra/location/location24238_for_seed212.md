The lowlands have no ground in the ordinary sense. What might once have been soil is buried beneath a continuous civic mass of vegetation: buttressed trunks pressed together like tenement walls, braided roots opening into corridors, leaf-skins stretched across gaps as awnings, bridges, roofs, and sometimes whole streets. Nothing has been built here, because there is no need to build. The plants have achieved density, complication, and enclosure on their own.

The place is called, by no one, a city only because it has neighborhoods.

In the eastern reaches, the vegetation rises in crowded vertical stacks. Broad reed-columns, each as thick as a house, grow from mats of glossy root-knuckle. Their hollow interiors are open at both ends, and warm air blows through them with the force and modulation of organs. Around them climb lattices of pale vines, making stairlike ribbing where their tendrils have stiffened and fused. Above, plates of foliage overlap in angled terraces. Some bear pools in their cupped surfaces, shallow and brown-green, trembling whenever the air convulses. These reservoirs spill unpredictably. Rain here is not weather so much as a local accident: a sudden pour from a hundred leaves three stories overhead, then dryness again, steam lifting from the struck surfaces.

Everywhere, things are growing too quickly for stability.

A green wall that was flat in the morning has buckled outward by noon. A passage closes when a root swells through its center. A ceiling of fern-fronds lowers itself, slowly but irreversibly, until it becomes a floor. New shoots spear upward through old ones, splitting them with wet cracks. The city continually revises its own circulation. Corridors become wells; wells fill with tangled stems and become platforms; platforms fold into dense thickets. There are no ruins, exactly, but there are abandoned geometries: clearings shaped like rooms, vine arches that lead into solid wood, avenues whose original alignment can be seen only in the repeated curve of trunks.

The air is warm, bright, and almost constantly moving.

Sunlight enters through a broken canopy in ordinary gold-white shafts, but the shafts do not fall straight. They bend around leaf clusters, divide into ribbons, gather again beneath translucent foliage. In certain districts, whole layers of plant tissue have grown thin enough to glow. Their chlorophyll is pale, near yellow, and the low sun turns them into luminous walls. Behind them, darker trunks shift in silhouettes, although there is nothing alive in the sense of animal life. The movement belongs to growth, pressure, transpiration, and wind. It is difficult not to feel watched by it anyway.

No birds cross the open spaces. No insects whir in the flowers. There are no calls, no paws, no small bodies darting from cover.

Yet the place is deafening.

The sound comes from the plants and from the weather trapped among them. Vast leaves clap against one another in hot gusts. Seedpods, inflated to the size of barrels, burst one after another with concussion-thuds that travel through the rootwork. Fibrous towers groan as they twist toward light. Water runs in hidden channels beneath the vegetation and booms when it enters hollow cavities. Vines under tension snap with rifle-cracks. There is a constant rasp of stems rubbing, millions of dry green surfaces sawing together. During the strongest surges, the entire lowland produces a layered roar: wind, sap-pressure, falling water, splitting husks, and the deep wooden complaint of the living substrate shifting under its own weight.

Conversation would be impossible here, if there were anyone to speak.

There are civic-looking places in the vegetation, expanses that suggest plazas or markets though they contain no inhabitants. One is a basin of compressed moss, perhaps half a mile across, surrounded by tall fans of blue-green leaves. It remains open only because the plants around it bend away from its center. At intervals, their leaf-stems contract in sequence, making the entire rim rise and fall like the breathing edge of a mouth. The basin collects mist in the early hours. By midday the mist has condensed into beads on every surface, and the beads begin to roll downhill, gathering into threadlike streams. In the afternoon, the sun dries everything almost completely. The basin becomes springy, hot, and fragrant with bruised green matter.

Beyond it lies a district of fresh formations: raw, sharp, young structures where the lowlands are still inventing themselves. Ivory roots emerge from the plant-mass in high arches, smooth and damp-looking despite the heat. Their tips are pointed, almost mineral. They plunge back down in rows, making vaulted tunnels. Some have thrust aside older growth and exposed layers beneath: black peat, pale fungal lace, red fibers, pockets of dry crumbly earth. The exposed cuts have the look of an excavation, but no hand has made them. The landscape excavates itself whenever it grows.

Reality is less dependable in these younger zones.

Distance does not hold steady. A line of trunks may appear to recede toward a horizon, then reveal itself as a dense loop when approached. A bridge of roots can lead across a ravine that was not present from its far side. Light sometimes arrives before the opening that produces it. Water falls upward briefly along the undersides of leaves, collecting in silver cords before vanishing into the canopy. These events do not seem supernatural to the vegetation. Tendrils continue to curl. Leaves continue to turn their faces toward whichever direction has become sun.

The low elevation gives the whole region a heavy, enclosed heat. Even at its most open, it feels pressed beneath the accumulated green volume of the world. The horizon is not sky but a far-standing wall of canopy, intermittently broken by steam and bright gaps. The ground—or what passes for ground—quivers with the tiny adjustments of roots. Its surfaces are yielding: mats of moss, woven rhizomes, old leaves compacted into resilient brown layers, slick membranes stretched over cavities full of warm air.

There are paths, but they are not promises.

Some are made by the repeated collapse of plants in the same direction, a long seam of flattened stems leading between larger growths. Some are polished by rainwater that comes only after storms and disappears almost immediately into thirsty fibers. Others are simply absences, maintained by the force of two enormous root systems pushing against each other and refusing to overlap. These paths may persist for weeks or minutes. Their edges are always reaching inward.

At night, the city does not quiet. The canopy closes many of its pale surfaces, and the spaces below become dense with dark green heat. But some flowers open only then: huge, blind blossoms with interiors of luminous cream, their petals unfolding with audible wet pops. They give off no perfume for animals. Their scent is functional only to the plants themselves, a chemical weather spreading through the corridors. The rootways respond. Leaves turn. Whole facades of vine subtly tighten and release. Somewhere far off, a trunk splits from top to bottom with a thunderous report, and for a moment a new vertical avenue opens, full of moonlit mist.

By morning it may already be full again.

This is not a forest that contains a city. It is a city without citizens, whose buildings are thirsty, whose streets grow over, whose towers flower, whose plumbing is rain and condensation, whose foundations are still moving. It is busy beyond endurance. It is fertile beyond purpose. Every surface is involved in becoming another surface.

And beneath the noise—beneath the cracking, roaring, dripping, breathing, tearing volume of it—there is a slower pressure: the entire lowland leaning toward a shape it has not yet discovered.
