The town occupies a high hollow where the mountain has only recently decided to become a mountain.

Its ridges are still raw: dark stone folded up through the turf in narrow blades, clean-edged and almost black when wet. The slopes do not descend so much as stop, forming terraces and little shelves crowded with moss, low ash trees, and immense ferns whose fronds unfurl in the cold air like green smoke. Beyond the hollow, the land falls away abruptly into cloud. It is difficult to see the valley below, and perhaps there is no valley at all—only a long pale weather filling the space beneath.

Rain comes often, but rarely properly. It arrives as a bead on the underside of a leaf, a film on lichen, a cold shine between the stones. At dawn, every roof drips though no one recalls rain during the night. Water gathers in shallow basins hollowed in the new rock, where it lies dark and still beneath floating grasses. There are no streams large enough to name. Instead, moisture travels secretly: down root channels, through cracked walls, along the hairy stems of plants. The whole place feels damp from within.

The settlement is built around one of these stone shelves, no larger than a market square. Its houses are low and carefully wedged among boulders, constructed from stacked slabs, bent alder poles, turf, and pale hides stretched tight over rib frames. There are perhaps thirty dwellings, but they are scattered widely, each with a cleared patch of ground and a little smoke-hole that usually emits no smoke. Paths of crushed white gravel connect them, fading into grass, then appearing again somewhere unlikely. The town does not seem planned until one notices that every doorway faces the same central rock.

That rock is neither a shrine nor a council hall, though it serves as both when needed. It rises only twice the height of a person. A seam in its side opens into a narrow interior chamber, smooth and damp as the inside of a shell. The inhabitants keep their sharpest tools there: chipped obsidian knives, bone awls, stone-headed hammers, polished antler hooks. They do not lock the chamber. Nothing in the hollow is stolen, because the animals would notice.

Animals are everywhere.

They do not crowd the eye at first. The town appears sparse, almost empty: a person crossing a path in a woolen cloak; a roof huddled beneath wet ferns; an old ladder leaning against a wall. But then movement resolves in every layer of the place. Small white-backed deer stand silently among the houses, chewing roof-thatch with grave concentration. Broad-winged moths cling beneath eaves, their wings patterned like maps of unfamiliar coastlines. Long-legged birds walk through the herb beds, lifting each foot with ceremonial care. Fox-sized creatures with six jointed limbs sleep in the warm ash pits, waking only when someone speaks too loudly.

The most numerous are the lantern hares.

They emerge in the dim hours before morning, though morning here is only a slight dilution of gray. They are large, almost dog-sized, with long pale ears and fur that holds an internal greenish light. Not enough to illuminate the ground, but enough to make them visible in the fern thickets: a hundred low lamps moving without sound. They graze on moss and fungus, and in their wake the plants close their leaves. The townspeople collect the hairs caught on brambles, spin them into cord, and use the cord to mark paths during fog.

No one hunts the lantern hares. This is an old rule, older than the settlement. The people say the hares know where the rock is still growing.

At intervals—weeks, sometimes years—the hollow gives a small shudder. Cups tremble in their niches. Water rises in the stone basins. A new crease opens in a slope, or a tooth of black rock pushes through the grass behind someone’s house. The people respond with no alarm. They move their fences. They lift their houses a little, stone by stone, if a wall has become misaligned. Their tools are made for alteration rather than permanence. Nothing is nailed that cannot be untied; nothing is mortared that cannot be restacked.

The air is cool enough that breath hangs close to the mouth. It smells of wet granite, crushed fern stalk, animal fur, and the faint mineral odor released when fresh stone breaks. Fires burn low. Wood is scarce on the high shelf, so fuel comes from dried peat, from shed antlers, from a dense brown fungus cultivated in lidded pits. The fires make little crackle. In the quiet, their blue cores can be heard more than their flames: a tiny inward ticking, like distant ice.

Sound is sparse and strangely absorbed. Wind does not whistle through the rocks; it brushes them. Rain does not patter; it gathers and falls one drop at a time from great height. Animals communicate with soft movements: the pulse of wings, paws in moss, the minute rasp of teeth on stems. Human voices are low by custom. Not reverent exactly. Careful.

There is a belief that the mountain is listening itself into being.

Above the town, on the highest raw ridge, grows a grove of silver-barked trees. Their trunks are thin, their crowns sparse, but their roots have entered the new fractures of the stone and forced them wide. At night—or what passes for night, when the clouds lower until they seem to rest on the roofs—the trees exhale a dim bluish mist. This mist flows downhill in strands. It collects around doorsteps, through animal burrows, across the central path. By morning it has vanished, leaving droplets arranged in rings on the gravel.

Sometimes the rings contain tiny tracks no one recognizes.

The people do not sweep them away. They step around them until the next moisture comes.

The hollow is active in every hidden way. Ferns open and close according to the pressure in the rock. Moths hatch from crevices in the walls. Deer shed velvet antlers that sprout pale fungi before they touch the ground. The lantern hares migrate in slow circles around the settlement, following a route that changes each season by the width of a hand. Beneath the paths, roots, water, insects, and mineral veins negotiate incessantly.

Yet from a distance, the place would look nearly motionless: a few low structures in a green notch high above cloud, black stone newly exposed among the grass, a small settlement holding its breath.

It is not a place made to conquer the mountain. It is a place that has learned to live in the brief, quiet intervals between the mountain’s thoughts.
