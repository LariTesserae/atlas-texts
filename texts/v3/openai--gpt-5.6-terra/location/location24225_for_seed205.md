The upland is called a forest only because there is no better word for a country made chiefly of growing things. It rises across a whole shelf of the world, high enough that the horizon is often level with the undersides of clouds, yet hot enough for heat to lie visibly above the ground in the long clear hours. Its vegetation does not form a continuous green roof. It stands apart in enormous presences: solitary trunk-columns, groves spaced miles apart, root ridges crossing empty red distances, vast pitcher-plants whose open mouths hold their own weather.

Between them is space—bright, wind-scoured, and immense.

The soil is not really soil. In most places it is an old woven mat of roots, pith, shed leaves, fungal plates, and compressed fibrous dust, built up over ages until it has become a landform. It rolls in low tawny swells. When cut, it shows rings of different centuries: pale dry cordage, black compost, amber sap deposits, nests of tiny white roots, layers rich with the shells of insects. The ground gives slightly under a person’s weight. In places it is warm, because the plant-mass below is still digesting itself.

There are no ordinary trees here. There are tower reeds with trunks broader than village wells, their segmented stems ribbed in bronze-green plates. There are spreading crown-ferns that begin at ground level and rise in stacked shelves for hundreds of feet, every shelf carrying its own mosses, nesting birds, and dripping curtains of aerial roots. There are hard-leaved shrubs, small only by comparison, whose leaves are broad as doors and edged with rows of harmless-looking glass teeth. At a distance, the whole country seems scattered: isolated dark shapes standing in an ocean of sun-baked ochre. Underfoot, however, nothing is empty. Every grain is seed, root, husk, spore, egg, or the waiting tissue of some plant that has not yet decided what shape it will take.

Moisture arrives in intervals. For days the air can be dry enough to crack the lips and bleach dead leaves white. Then, usually toward evening, wind climbs from some lower unseen basin and meets the heat trapped across the plateau. Rain does not fall evenly. It selects things. A single grove may be drenched while the plain around it remains dusty; a trunk may stream with water on one side while the other side is dry. The great plants harvest these local rains with elaborate surfaces: cupped leaves, furred bark, coils of root that funnel runoff into underground cisterns.

At dawn there is condensation everywhere. The grassless open spaces sparkle with low beads of water caught in root hairs. The animals come out then, before the sun takes it back.

There are too many animals to mistake the place for stillness. The fauna has learned the geography of the plants so thoroughly that the vegetation seems to move in animal shapes. Vast pale browsing herds drift between the reed-towers, each beast high-shouldered and many-legged, its back thick with symbiotic flowering moss. They travel in loose lines miles long, never clustered tightly, because the land provides abundance in points rather than carpets. Long-necked leaf-cutters climb the outer trunks of crown-ferns, bracing themselves with hooked tails as they strip only the oldest leaves. Their calls are deep wooden knocks, passed from one tree to another until the entire plateau seems to be resonating.

In the shade around the water plants live bright, frantic smaller creatures: lacquered beetles, needle-beaked drinkers, sluglike animals with fans of iridescent breathing frills, thin mammals that sleep rolled inside unfurled leaves. Flocks of ribbon-winged birds travel low through the open spaces. They do not flap much. They catch warm rising air, fold and snap their wings, and emit cracking noises like sailcloth in a storm.

Predators are not hidden by the land; they are announced by it. The most feared are the climbing cats, striped in the dull gold and green of old stems. They are almost soundless themselves, but the trees react to them. Leaves tighten. Pods close. The long aerial roots pull upward in slow coils. An attentive traveler can watch a distant grove become subtly less hospitable and know that something is moving within it.

The noise is constant and enormous. It begins before one can identify its sources. Insects run their legs across hollow reeds, producing a shimmer like hot wires. Burrowing animals drum their skulls against the root floor. Herds breathe in rough synchronized gusts. High in the tower plants, colonies of bark-apes hammer open seed chambers with stones, their strikes echoing down the hollow interiors. Every afternoon, the swollen pods of the thunder vine split in sequence across the plain—one report, then another, then thousands—casting seed fluff into the air with a sound like far-off artillery.

When storms gather, the country becomes deafening. The wind catches the hollow crowns, turns them into pipes, and plays different notes according to the amount of water inside each trunk. Whole regions sing. The music is not beautiful in a gentle sense. It is blunt, muscular, resonant enough to be felt in the ribs. Local people navigate by it. A grove with an empty root-cistern moans in a low voice; one rich with water produces a sharp, rising whistle. Travelers can hear rain falling over the next ridge long before they see it, not as a patter but as a change in the forest’s chord.

There are people here, though at first the land seems too large to contain them. Their settlements are sparse: a cluster of platforms lashed around the base of a living tower, a line of shade screens beside a seasonal seep, a solitary watch hut perched on the spine of a root ridge. They build lightly, largely because heavy construction is unnecessary and because the plants resent it. Cut wood softens quickly in this climate unless smoked, oiled, or hung above the ground. Stone is rare. Metal exists only as inherited scraps—dull blades, hammered rings, little hooks treasured across generations.

Most tools are made of plant material shaped by heat, pressure, and patience: knives of sharpened thorn-glass, bowls grown from bound gourds, ropes braided from living root strands, shutters of overlapping wax leaves. Some communities cultivate particular trees into structures, persuading young trunks to grow around a central platform until the house has walls that are alive, breathable, and self-repairing. A dwelling may need twenty years to become habitable. Its residents think of themselves less as owners than as the current occupants of a slow animal.

The people do not clear fields. The plateau has taught them that clearing is an invitation to the wrong kinds of growth. Instead they tend routes. They prune passageways through root mats, mark safe condensation hollows, coax edible creepers across sun-facing slopes, and maintain agreements with the larger plants. Certain trunks are tapped only on moonless nights. Certain seed cones may be harvested, but never their first opening of the season. Certain groves are left entirely untouched, because they are believed to remember footsteps.

This belief may be more practical than mystical. Some plants do respond to repeated disturbance. Paths walked too frequently develop stiff fibers that rise under the feet and make the way painful. Camps left too long become engulfed in rapid root growth. But a trail maintained carefully—swept, sung over, watered with the first cup drawn from a cistern—can remain open for generations, a narrow gray vein across a living continent.

The strangeness of the plateau lies partly in its scale and partly in its timing. Plants here are not slow in the familiar way. They have long lives, but their decisions can be immediate. A thirsty grove can turn all its leaves within an hour. A damaged reed-tower may send roots several yards in a night, seeking firmer ground. Once each year, when heat and condensation meet in the right proportion, the whole upland undergoes a flowering that is visible from the clouds. The solitary structures of vegetation ignite in color: red crowns, violet ladders, yellow feathered canopies, blue flowers the size of boats. Animals arrive in masses from regions beyond the plateau. The air becomes thick with wings, pollen, mating calls, and the wet scent of opened sap.

Then the season ends. Petals fall. The tremendous color rots into the root mat. The migrating animals leave in thinning waves. The rain becomes uncertain again, and the upland returns to its spaciousness: hot, green, resonant, and old.

From a high ridge, one can see the land extending far past the reach of sight. Here and there rise the immense dark forms of trees or plant-towers, each surrounded by its own population, shadow, moisture, and sound. Between them, the open country shines under ordinary daylight, almost barren-looking. But even in the apparent emptiness, things are moving: a herd in a distant dust plume, a black migration of birds tracing the ground, a root swell slowly crossing the plain, a line of people walking from one living shelter to another.

Nothing is really separate. The spaces are only the pauses between roots.
