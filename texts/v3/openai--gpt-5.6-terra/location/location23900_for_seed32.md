It is a small place, though it does not feel empty enough to be called small.

It might be a courtyard, or the interior of a collapsed bell, or a city reduced to a single room and then rebuilt until no particle of the original ground remains. There is no soil. There is no bedrock visible beneath the work. Every surface has been placed there: fitted stone, baked clay, pale brick, tiles rubbed round by a weather that does not quite occur. The floor is a dense mosaic of fragments—slabs, plugs, broken columns laid sideways, pieces of stair, old kiln-mouths, lintels with no doors beneath them. All are mortared together so completely that the place has become one artificial mineral mass.

The walls begin immediately. There is scarcely a clear pace of ground anywhere.

They rise in crowded terraces around a central depression no wider than a table. Each wall carries smaller structures on it: niches blocked by stones; shallow archways leading nowhere; shelves bearing stacks of dry bowls; rows of closed shutters made of fired wood or blackened ceramic. Narrow stairs ascend three steps and end against other stairs. Low bridges cross gaps too thin to fall through. Chimneys emerge from roofs that have been built beneath other roofs. The architecture has no grandeur, only insistence. Nothing was ever demolished. New making occurred in the available cracks.

The whole enclosure is bright.

Light comes from above through an irregular aperture crisscrossed by beams, latticework, hanging tiles, and the undersides of yet more rooms. The sky, where it can be seen, is a hard milk-blue. Cool daylight enters in blades and patches, striking white salts along the mortar lines, polished corners of stone, the pale glazed faces of old bricks. There are no shadows deep enough to disappear into. Even the smallest seams seem illuminated, as though the materials retain daylight and give it slowly back.

No water has passed here for a very long time. The drainage grooves are dry and filled with dust as fine as flour. Cistern mouths are capped with fitted stone, their rims sealed in lime. A few old fountains remain: carved basins, animal-headed spouts, bas-relief rivers winding around their sides. Their pipes are dry, and their bowls contain only wind-borne powder, bits of shell-like plaster, and the husks of seeds that never found enough softness to open.

Yet there is vegetation.

It survives as a kind of refusal. Thin gray grasses grow from hairline faults in the brickwork, each tuft rigid and sparse, its blades almost metallic. Small cushions of lichen occupy cool shaded joints; they are green only at their centers, with broad outer rings of silver. A vine, long dead except for one living stem, moves across a wall in a careful horizontal line, holding itself to the masonry with dry pale roots. In places, low plants resembling moss have colonized old mortar, though they are not wet. They seem to feed on mineral dust, on shade, on the brief condensation of cold mornings that vanishes before it can be called dew.

There are no animals. No insects thread the light. No birds roost in the rafters. Nothing scurries beneath the piles of tile.

The loudness comes from the place itself.

Air passes through thousands of gaps at once. It whistles in bottle-necked vents, hums in buried pipes, rasps over the mouths of empty jars. The sound is constant and layered: a held chord of breath, clay, stone, and confined sky. Some openings give a low organ tone. Others produce sharp fluting notes. In the central depression, where several culverts meet under a grating, the wind makes a dull roaring sound like a distant crowd.

This noise never rises into a storm and never falls silent. It has found the exact pressure at which all of the little instruments of the courtyard speak together.

Nothing here is actively decaying. The erosion has already happened. Every exposed edge has been softened; every inscription has been reduced to its durable strokes; every painted surface has faded to its essential mineral colors—iron red, smoke black, chalk white, the faint blue of old glaze. Cracks are present but not spreading. Dust gathers but does not deepen. The dry plants bend in the wind but remain the same length year after year.

The builders are absent, if they were ever people. Their work suggests hands rather than machines: uneven brick courses, thumbprints preserved in plaster, repairs made with whatever was nearest. But the density of the construction suggests generations who could not bear to leave even a pocket unfilled. The world outside may be vast, low, and cold, a plain of salt or stone beneath an empty sky. Here, however, every available volume has been claimed by walls, vessels, beams, roots, echoing ducts, and light.

There are rules to the enclosure.

Nothing may be removed; that is why every broken thing remains and becomes a foundation for another thing. Nothing may fall; collapsed material catches on lower structures and is incorporated into the architecture before it reaches the floor. Nothing may grow large; roots turn aside at a certain depth, stems dry at a certain height, and even the vine follows the old masonry lines rather than seeking the sun.

Most of all, nothing changes its purpose entirely. A fountain remains a fountain after its water is gone. A stair remains a stair though it climbs nowhere. A door remains a door even when its opening has been packed solid with stone.

At the center, under the loud grate, lies a small circular patch of pale tiles. They are cleaner than the surrounding ground, worn smooth by no feet. The tiles form a pattern of concentric rings, dry ripples around a drain that has never held water in living memory. A single gray grass blade rises from the central seam.

It trembles continuously in the wind.

It does not bend.
