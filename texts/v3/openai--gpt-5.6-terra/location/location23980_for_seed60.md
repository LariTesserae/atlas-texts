The place is called a marsh only by people passing through it too quickly.

From above, it appears to be a low green compression in the country: an irregular basin no larger than a village, dark at its center and bright at its rim. But there is scarcely any visible ground. The whole basin has filled itself over centuries. Roots have knitted through old silt; reeds have fallen, softened, and become soil without ever fully ceasing to be reeds. Moss has climbed over the resulting ground and grown thick enough to carry new plants. Every surface is either leaf, stem, pollen-dusted water, wet wood, or the black fibrous peat made from all those things waiting patiently underneath.

Walking into it is less like entering a field than entering a body.

There are narrow routes, but they have not been laid. They are the places where the vegetation has agreed, temporarily, to leave a person-sized absence. They bend between walls of horsetail, water-loving grasses, broad taro-like leaves, and climbing vines which make slow decisions overhead. At shoulder height, the paths brush the traveler from both sides. Underfoot lies a springy mat of root and moss, warm in the daylight, with water rising through it when pressed. In a few places the mat shivers, and a brown eye of water opens among the plants. It closes again with a quiet suck.

The basin is old and settled. Nothing here looks newly broken. Its banks are low, weathered slopes of pale clay, padded in fern and creeping fig. Rain has carved them into folds so soft they resemble upholstery. The water does not run dramatically; it accumulates, lingers, sweats from stone, gathers in the cups of leaves, travels by hidden seepage beneath the mats. Old rainfall is still present everywhere. It clings in the creases of bark, pearls along the undersides of vines, and condenses at dawn on the galvanized metal objects scattered through the vegetation.

Those objects are the traces of an industry that has not entirely departed.

A squat pump house stands near the eastern bank, its brick walls submerged up to the lower windows in green-black water. The roof has sagged under a burden of flowering creepers. Inside, an electric motor continues to turn. No one has maintained it in living memory, yet the armature moves with a steady, exhausted dignity, drawing water through pipes swallowed by roots. The pipes lead nowhere useful now. Some surface far away as iron ridges under the moss; some emerge vertically, open-mouthed, among the reeds, breathing damp air in and out with the pressure changes of the underground channels.

There are other remnants: a steel catwalk ending abruptly in a curtain of leaves; a line of ceramic insulators strung from post to crooked post, each one furred with moss; a rust-red winch whose drum has become the center of a small tree. In the north corner of the basin, half concealed by giant leaves, a narrow conveyor belt runs at a pace so slow it is almost geological. It carries no cargo except leaves, sediment, fallen seed pods, and occasionally a ribbon of water. The belt disappears into a corrugated shed. From the shed comes the damp, methodical clatter of machinery processing something nobody can see.

Perhaps it is processing the marsh itself.

The sound is larger than the place should permit. Pumps throb under the root mat. Belts rasp. Loose sheet metal clicks in the warm wind. Water falls from a height of only a few inches but does so everywhere, making a constant fine applause. The plants contribute their own noises: broad leaves knocking together, seed cases ticking open, stems creaking under the weight of climbing growth. At noon, a deep electrical hum seems to inhabit the air. It may come from buried cables. It may be the collective vibration of insects too small to notice.

There are very few animals. A pale marsh heron is sometimes seen standing under the industrial catwalk, so still that its reflection looks more alive than it does. Small frogs call from hidden pockets of water, though no frog is ever easily found. Once in a while, something long and silver slips through a flooded channel and vanishes under the vegetation. The place belongs overwhelmingly to plants, machines, water, and the old patient mineral ground.

Some of the plants have learned the machinery’s habits. Vines turn their tendrils toward warm generator casings. Moss grows in concentric bands around vibrating pipes. A yellow flower opens only beside vents where the underground pumps exhale heated air. Its petals are faintly translucent and, in the late afternoon, glow as though lit from inside.

At the basin’s center rises a structure that might once have been a water tower. Its concrete cylinder has been cracked open from within by roots. A banyan-like mass has occupied it, pouring down through window slots and broken seams in ropes of reddish root. The tower no longer holds water; it holds an entire suspended garden. Ferns spill from its upper rim. A fig grows in the old hatchway. In the hollow of the tank, rainwater has collected around a black, motionless mechanism. When wind crosses the basin, the tower’s roots shift against the concrete with a low wooden groan.

The strange rule of the place is that nothing is removed.

Anything left here becomes incorporated. A dropped glove becomes a pocket of mold, then a nest of roots. An abandoned tool acquires a green handle of moss and is slowly levered deeper into the peat. Even the paths are only borrowed; if unused for a season, they seal with grass and vine, their remembered route preserved only as a slightly softer weave beneath the growth.

The people who visit know not to cut plants unless they mean to make a permanent exchange. A stem cut at the edge of a path will often cause another to bend into the gap somewhere else. Water diverted from one pool rises through a different patch of moss by morning. The basin is not hostile, exactly. It simply maintains its fullness.

In ordinary daylight, it is lush, humid, almost domestic in scale: a room made from green things, wet with its own respiration. But when evening comes, the industrial lights wake among the vegetation. Small bulbs behind cracked glass glow amber in the pump house. A signal lamp at the top of the ruined tower begins to turn, its red beam sweeping slowly across leaves and black water. Beneath the roots, the old engines deepen their pulse.

Then the marsh seems not abandoned but occupied by a long, quiet shift change—plants taking over from people, water taking over from plants, machinery continuing its obscure work beneath them all.
