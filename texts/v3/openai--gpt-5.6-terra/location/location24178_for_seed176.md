At the height where maps stop using contour lines and begin using warnings, there is a small shelf of land caught in the flank of a young mountain.

It is not a broad summit. It is more nearly a wound that has chosen to become habitable: a basin no larger than a village green, tilted toward a precipice, enclosed on three sides by black volcanic walls. The rock is new enough to look unfinished. Its edges are sharp, glazed in places, and ribbed with pale mineral veins that run through it like cooling cracks in iron. Whole faces of the cliff are wet. Water emerges not from springs exactly, but from the stone itself—beading in the porous black surfaces, gathering in bright threads, falling in a thousand separate drips.

The basin is warm despite its altitude.

Clouds arrive below it, pressed against the mountain in a white, slowly rising sea. Their upper surfaces sometimes flood over the rim, and then the place disappears for hours in luminous vapor. The air carries the smell of wet leaves, hot metal, peat, and the faint sulfurous breath of the earth. It is never quite raining, but nothing ever dries. Moss swells in seams of rock. Ferns, oversized and lacquered with moisture, fill every patch of soil. Vines climb the old lava spires and hang back down from them in ropes, their leaves dark enough to seem blue until sunlight touches them.

There are pools everywhere: shallow, clear, trembling pools in the hollows of lava; brown pools under mats of roots; narrow channels slipping through grass; a larger reservoir held behind a low wall of fitted basalt at the center of the shelf. This reservoir is not natural. Someone has made it, though not recently and not carelessly. Its rim is built from dark stone blocks, each softened by lichen, with iron straps running between them. A lattice of pipes draws water from it and carries it downhill into the machinery scattered through the vegetation.

The machinery is the loudest living thing here.

A waterwheel turns beneath a roof of corrugated metal, enclosed in a narrow cleft where warm runoff is forced through a gate. It does not spin quickly. Nothing in the basin does anything quickly. But it is massive, and each slow revolution gives the mountain a deep wooden groan. Belts rise from it toward a squat engine house on stilts. The belts vanish through slots in the walls, emerge elsewhere, and pass over great exposed wheels furred with moss. There are pistons that breathe steam in measured intervals. There are dynamos in weatherproof cases, their little lamps burning amber under eaves. There are cables strung from one rock tower to another, disappearing into cloud, humming softly with a power that seems less electrical than geological.

At intervals, a relay somewhere on the far wall clacks loudly: one hard metallic strike, followed by a ringing echo that travels around the basin and returns altered by water and leaves.

The purpose of the works is not obvious. No smoke rises from a factory chimney. No road reaches this place. There is a narrow iron track, but it begins at a rusted platform near the precipice and ends twenty meters later at a locked shed half hidden by tree ferns. Beyond it, the ground drops away into cloud. The track may once have connected to an aerial lift, or it may be waiting for one to be built. A single cable car hangs far below the shelf, nearly invisible in mist, fixed in place among the cables like an abandoned seedpod.

Scattered around the basin are signs of human keeping rather than settlement: a tool cupboard, a rain shelter with one bench, a small hut roofed in oxidized red tin, a pair of boots left on a pipe rack, their leather green with mildew. There is a station marker painted in white letters on black enamel, though the lettering has peeled beyond reading. In the hut are calendars from different years, all pinned beside one another. The latest date is decades old. Yet the lamps are lit. The water gates are adjusted. Grease shines fresh along the teeth of the largest gear.

Perhaps the station tends itself.

Perhaps someone comes only when the clouds are low enough to hide their approach.

Life has filled the gaps left by industry, but not conquered it. The wheelhouse roof supports a garden of moss and small orchids. Roots have entered the cracks in the reservoir wall without breaking it. A bright orange fungus grows around the warm exhaust pipes in thick shelves, opening and closing its gills when steam passes. In one corner of the basin, a stand of bamboo-like reeds leans together so densely that rain trapped in their leaves continues dripping long after the sky has cleared.

Animals are scarce, almost startling when they appear. A pale bird with long legs picks its way along the reservoir edge, probing the water for insects. It has no call anyone has heard; it merely lifts its head whenever a relay strikes, listening. Occasionally, at dusk, a small dark shape moves among the ferns: perhaps a mountain fox, perhaps something smaller and more cautious. Its tracks vanish quickly in wet ground.

Most of the motion belongs to water, cloud, and mechanisms.

Water slides down. Moss thickens. The reservoir rises by a finger’s width during a day of mist, then lowers by the same amount through the iron channels. A piston advances, withdraws, advances again. In the valley far below, weather may race from sun to storm in an hour, but here the climate has a patient character. Changes arrive as accumulations: a slope becoming greener over a season, a pipe acquiring a new skin of rust, a fresh crack appearing in the volcanic wall after a distant tremor.

The mountain is still growing in some sense. Warmth rises through its bones. At night, when the clouds fall away, the basin opens to an impossible depth of stars. The lights of the station make only a small amber cluster against the black rock. Beyond the outer edge of the shelf is a vertical dark so complete that the steady machinery begins to sound like it is holding the place in existence.

There is no fence around the precipice. There is no sign saying where it is safe to stand.

The only rule the place offers is quieter than a warning: keep to the wet ground, follow the pipes, do not step where the moss is too smooth, and if you hear the waterwheel stop, listen carefully for what begins beneath the rock.
