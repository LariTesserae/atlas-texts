The low country has been built rather than raised.

Nothing here grows from the ground, because there is no ground in the old sense. The entire valley is laid in courses: dark brick, fired clay slabs, crushed shell mortar, blackened beams, riveted sheets of soft greenish metal. The horizon is not a line of hills but a crowded succession of constructed slopes—banks of retaining walls, stepped roofs, leaning sluices, kiln mounds, aqueducts with their arches packed too closely together. Every surface has an intention, though most intentions have been forgotten.

It is a place made by hands using tools simple enough to understand: wedges, hammers, adzes, rope pulleys, clay moulds, saws with uneven teeth. Yet the quantity of work is overwhelming. Whole ridges have been assembled from brick. Ditches lead into cisterns; cisterns overflow into channels; channels disappear beneath neighborhoods of low vaults. There are stairways that rise into darkness and end at blank walls. There are gates with no accompanying fence, doors set high in towers with no ladders beneath them, immense clay pipes laid across courtyards like the bones of a buried animal.

The country has no open wilderness. Its openness comes from scale alone: the maze continues for miles. A traveler may stand on a broad embankment and see, beyond the nearest roofs, another basin full of furnaces and reservoirs, then another arrangement of walls, sheds, ramps, towers, cranes, drying racks, and black water. Each district has a different construction language. Some are all round mud-brick cisterns, sweating with condensation. Some are timber frames tarred against the rain, their planks swollen and splitting. Some are made of pale blocks fitted so tightly that water runs over them in silver threads without finding a seam.

The air is cool, metallic, and wet enough to taste. Rain is not frequent, but it is always recently departed or nearly arriving. Moisture gathers in the undersides of bridges. It beads on iron hooks. It lies in dark seams between paving stones. In places, condensation falls steadily from ceilings, not as a shower but as patient individual drops: a perpetual tapping that can fill a bowl over the course of a day.

There are pools everywhere, though few large ones. Water persists in troughs, wheel-pits, roof gutters, abandoned vats, stone basins made to receive something other than rain. It is usually black, reflecting lamps and chimney mouths. When disturbed it shows colors underneath—rust-red, blue-grey, milk-white with lime. Narrow runnels hurry along the edges of passageways, guided by thumb-sized channels cut into the brick. Children of some vanished workforce must once have scratched little dams across these streams; the remnants remain, tiny walls of tile and pebble, still redirecting the water.

There is no vegetation. No grass softens a stair. No moss climbs a shaded wall. No vine finds a crack. The dampness produces only stains: pale mineral blooms, powdery salt, greasy black films, furry-looking deposits that are not quite fungus and not quite soot. These deposits gather in the corners of rooms and around drains. They sometimes pulse faintly when struck by water, giving off a dull blue glimmer that vanishes before one can decide whether it was light or merely the eye adjusting.

At night—or what passes for night, since the sky is almost always obscured—the settlement is loud.

It does not sleep. Water wheels groan in chambers below the streets. Counterweights slide in shafts. Bellows breathe behind kiln walls. Somewhere, always, a hammer strikes metal: not quickly, not with industry’s rhythm, but at long solemn intervals, as if a person is repairing one small thing in a building too large to repair. Wooden shutters bang under gusts moving through the upper galleries. Ropes creak. Loose tiles chatter. Water drops into deep cisterns with sounds too resonant for their size.

The loudest sound is the pipes.

The place is threaded with pipes of clay, copper, hollowed stone, and stitched leather. Some carry water, some hot vapor, some only wind. They moan across the valley as pressure changes. A long, low note may begin beneath one quarter and pass through walls, towers, and culverts until it seems the entire constructed landscape is singing through clenched teeth. Smaller pipes answer it in whistles and sobs. People who live here—if people do—know to listen for these notes. They are warnings. A high pipe-song means a cistern is rising. A broken stutter means a wall has shifted. A deep three-part boom means the old reservoir gates are opening somewhere upstream.

There are animals, but only rarely seen.

Small blind things inhabit the warm conduits: pale jointed creatures with stone-hard backs, feeding on whatever mineral residue collects in the bends. They make a dry ticking sound when they move through pipework. In the deeper water, shadows sometimes roll beneath the surface of reservoirs: long-bodied, smooth, almost fishlike, but with forelimbs that grip the masonry when they surface. Their eyes are absent or sealed over. Their mouths open toward vibration rather than scent.

Above the roofs, occasionally, something crosses the fog. Not a bird. It has too much weight and too few wingbeats. It passes between chimneys with a leathery clatter, then disappears into the upper darkness. No nests have been found. No one knows where it rests.

The structures themselves seem young in certain places, despite their immense age. Whole new slopes of brick are being added by methods no one witnesses. At the edge of the valley, raw walls rise from broad foundations, their surfaces wet and unweathered, with tool marks still sharp. They have no doors yet. They contain no rooms visible from outside. Every month, perhaps every week, another course appears.

The new masonry is darker than the old. It steams faintly in the cool air.

Old sections are not collapsing much. Erosion has barely begun its work here. Corners are still corners; inscriptions remain legible under soot; stair treads retain the shallow concavity of feet rather than rain. The danger is not ruin but accumulation. New channels cross old ones. Fresh walls block familiar routes. A courtyard can become a reservoir overnight if a sluice is redirected. A building may wake with a second floor it did not possess the day before.

The inhabitants, if encountered, carry lamp boxes made of horn and cloudy glass. Their light is weak, yellow, and close to the hand. They walk with simple iron hooks, mallets, lengths of rope, and chisels tucked into belts. Their clothes are waxed cloth and layered hide, always dark with damp. They do not speak above the machinery. Instead they knock messages through walls: two taps to ask for passage, three to mark a leak, a long scrape to say that the way ahead is no longer the way ahead.

In the open basins between districts, the sky appears as a dull black lid. Lamps are hung from cables at irregular heights, so that the darkness is patterned with isolated amber circles. Rain passing through these circles is briefly visible—thin bright threads, then nothing.

The place smells of wet clay, old smoke, iron filings, mineral water, and the faint sourness of enclosed spaces. Its atmosphere is not abandoned, exactly. Too much is moving. Gates rise and fall. Water finds its appointed route. Heat travels through walls. Someone continues to build where no builder can be seen.

And beneath all of it, under the lowest paved drains and the oldest cistern floors, there is said to be another constructed layer: a foundation-city of immense hollow bricks, filled with cool water and darkness. The valley above was not built upon it.

It was built to keep it closed.
