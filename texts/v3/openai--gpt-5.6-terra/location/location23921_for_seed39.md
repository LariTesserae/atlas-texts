The high country has not yet decided what shape it will keep.

It is a broad alpine basin lifted above the weather of the lower world, though weather still finds it: low cloud spilling over ridges, hard rain turning briefly to sleet, white breath gathering in the hollows. The ground is young in a visible way. Dark stone lies fractured in plates and fresh angular blocks, scarcely softened by soil. Whole slopes seem to have been exposed yesterday. Pale gravel fans extend from the feet of cliffs, not yet carved into gullies; they are only beginning to learn where the water will run.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a great river. It beads on black rock, slides beneath loose shale, fills shallow turfless pans, and surfaces in long, trembling seams through the valley floor. In the morning the basin is silvered with standing water. By afternoon, under the weak, obscured light, it becomes a map of dull pewter pools joined by narrow channels. The soil, where there is any, is thin and saturated—a cold, dark paste around stones. A person walking here would hear the soft suction of each footfall and, beneath it, the tiny continual ticking of water entering cracks.

Vegetation keeps close to the ground. There are no trees. There are scarcely even shrubs, except for a few low, wind-combed patches of heath along protected shelves. Moss darkens the lee sides of boulders. Lichen makes small continents over exposed granite: ash-gray, sulfur-yellow, black-green. In wetter pockets, grass rises in thin stiff ranks and sedges make brittle bronze rings around pools. The plants do not cover the landscape so much as test it, advancing a few inches at a time into raw mineral country.

Above the basin, the mountains are immense but unfinished-looking. Their upper faces are bare, striated, and wet, with snow lodged in creases too deep for the sun to reach. The ridgelines are often lost in cloud. When they clear, they show fresh scarps and wide flanks of shattered rock, as if the peaks are still being assembled from material brought upward by forces beneath the ground. Here and there a slope has failed recently: a new pale wound, a rubble tongue spreading into the valley. Nothing is ancient enough to be rounded. Even the water has sharp edges.

Yet the place is crowded with life, if not with things.

Birds occupy the air at every scale. Small dark birds work the saturated ground among stones, flicking through sedge and moss for larvae. Larger birds circle the cliff bands, appearing and vanishing in cloud. Waterfowl gather in the broader pools, their bodies low and deliberate on the gray water. In the evening, when the basin dims toward blue-black, their calls travel astonishingly far: a scattered clatter, a hollow honk, the brief whistle of wings passing overhead. The emptiness makes each sound seem placed rather than made.

The ground animals are less obvious but numerous. Hare tracks stitch between boulders. Small rodents rustle beneath mats of grass. Herds of mountain ungulates cross the higher shelves in slow files, pale shapes against wet stone, finding grazing where there seems to be none. Their paths are beginning to mark the landscape more clearly than streams do: narrow dark traverses through scree, trodden strips among the mosses, muddy crossings at the edges of pools. Predators follow these routes at a distance. Nothing about the basin is tame, but nothing is fantastical either. Hunger, cold, shelter, mating, migration: the plain old machinery of a hard high place.

There is an industrial character to the region, though no hand has built anything there.

The mountain works constantly. Water is pumped upward by wind and cloud, condensed against cold rock, released as rain, snow, and fog. Frost enters every seam at night; by day it loosens stone from the walls. The cliffs feed the scree. The scree feeds the fans. The fans settle, fail, are wetted, and settle again. Beneath the valley floor, invisible groundwater moves through gravel with the low persistence of machinery. In certain narrow gulches, snowmelt forces itself between slabs and produces a steady metallic clink as stones knock together under the current.

The sound of this industry is mostly too quiet to notice at first. Dripping. A distant trickle. The dry skitter of a pebble descending a slope. Wind stroking through stiff grass. The intermittent cough of a bird hidden in fog. Only occasionally does the country speak loudly: a sudden crack from an upper snowfield, a small rockfall rattling down a ravine, thunder muted behind the cloud ceiling. Then quiet reforms almost immediately, vast and attentive.

Light rarely arrives cleanly. The sky is usually a low lid of cloud, bright enough to reveal distances but not enough to give warmth. Wet stone reflects it in dim planes. Pools hold a gray glow. Snowfields seem faintly luminous, even at midday. In breaks between storms, the sun comes through as a cold white opening and sets every rivulet shining at once. Then the basin looks briefly electrified: thousands of wet threads running over black ground, every droplet flashing, the animals’ coats outlined in silver. But the light closes again, and the mountains return to their subdued colors—charcoal, iron, old bone, peat, tarnished green.

There are no roads, fences, towers, or ruins to suggest an alternative order. No mark persists unless weather and animal movement permit it. The rules are simple and severe. Water takes the lowest available route, until frost changes the route. Soil survives only where stone shelters it. Plants remain low or are removed by wind. Animals travel where the ground can carry them and gather where the brief summer offers food. Every open surface is provisional.

The basin is not a wilderness preserved from development. It feels earlier than that distinction. It is a landscape still entering its own future: wet, cold, exposed, and busy with processes that have not yet produced anything stable.
