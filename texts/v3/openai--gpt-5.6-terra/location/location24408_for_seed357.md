The country is built entirely of plants, though no one living would call it a garden.

It begins high above the cloud floor, on a broad volcanic shelf where the air is hot enough to make distance shimmer and wet enough that every surface darkens with moisture. The plateau extends for days in every direction: not empty, exactly, but spacious. Vast intervals open between its structures. A person could stand in one place and see a dozen miles of the country’s green construction—towers, walls, embankments, stepped reservoirs, bridges—without seeing another moving creature.

There are no creatures.

The plants make the place by themselves.

Their roots have packed and lifted the highland into usable forms. Great mat-forming species have braided the ground into thick, springy pavement, layered with pale leaf-sheaths that seal rainwater beneath. Where the plateau would otherwise fracture, root-columns have been raised under it, reinforced with woody fiber, lacquered sap, and sheets of mineral-bearing bark. The land is not natural bedrock with vegetation growing over it. There is no bare ground anywhere. Every slope, parapet, avenue, spillway, and terrace is plant tissue in one state or another: green, hardened, hollow, flowering, drowned, dead but still structural.

The architecture is elaborate without being deliberate.

At the edge of the shelf, immense buttress roots form arcs like flying supports. They hold up overhanging gardens that look from afar like fortified cities. Their outer skins are gray-white, scaled, and cracked by heat; from every crack emerge red wet shoots, translucent as candle wax. Beneath the arches is open air and a dizzying drop through layers of vapor. Clouds rise from lower ravines and strike the underside of the living shelf. They condense there. Water gathers along the root-veins, swells in clear beads, and falls in steady curtains.

Nothing in this place is dry for long.

Rain arrives in sudden hot gusts, not as a weather event moving over the plateau but as something repeatedly born from it. The vegetation exhales vapor all morning. By afternoon, clouds accumulate between the tall structures. Their shadows pass over the open green expanses. Then a pressure change ripples through the leaves, all at once: tens of thousands of broad blades turn their pale undersides outward, and rain begins to come down nearly vertically, thick and warm.

The rain strikes leaf roofs, drumlike pods, hollow trunks, the taut membranes of reservoir banks. The whole highland becomes loud. Water has a language here: its heavy applause on foliage; its rush through trunk-channels; the sudden flat slap of a living wall unfolding to redirect a flood; the deep, throatlike gulp of cistern plants taking in more than they can hold.

There are rivers, though they do not cut channels in soil. They move through designed-looking green troughs, each one a vast folded leaf with a stiff raised midrib. Some run for miles across the plateau before tipping into a rootwell. Others disappear under translucent coverings, visible only as dark, hurried shapes beneath veined membranes. Water is always changing routes. A swollen reservoir folds its retaining petals down, releasing a sheet of water across an abandoned plaza of mossy plates. A dry-looking avenue suddenly opens along its center and becomes a stream. A tall wall, overloaded by rain, collapses gently—not breaking, but unzipping into hundreds of long wet ribbons that lie flat and take root where they fall.

The place is young. Its forms still have the bright, provisional violence of recent growth.

Many towers are incomplete. They rise in tight spirals from the plateau, fifty or sixty feet tall, then end abruptly in clusters of curled shoots. Their lower sections have already become rigid and architectural, furred in lichenlike epiphytes; their tops are soft and continuously rearranging themselves in the heat. Other structures have failed spectacularly. There are fresh green landslides where a whole slope of woven stems has shifted downhill, exposing its pale inner fibers. There are suspended bridges whose far ends have rooted too high, leaving them tilted in long, impossible ramps. There are reservoirs held behind transparent skins, bulging with rainwater, each carrying a distorted reflection of the sky.

The open spaces between the great constructions are not bare. They are fields of low vegetation, close-cropped by no animal and unwalked by any person. Their sparseness comes from another logic. The fields are covered by a single tough, blue-green ground plant that grows in polygonal pads, each pad separated by narrow black seams full of water. From a height, the plateau resembles an enormous tiled floor, wet and gently breathing.

At intervals, one of the pads lifts.

It does not rise far—perhaps the height of a house—but it changes the entire field around it. Hidden stems tense beneath the surface. Water drains away from one set of seams and floods another. A low ridge becomes a shallow basin; a basin becomes a convex hill. Across the landscape, these movements travel slowly but constantly, as if the high plateau were being rebuilt from below by thoughts too large to perceive at once.

The tallest living structures are called, if names are needed, rain chimneys. They are not trees. Their trunks are square in cross-section, made from four fused walls of fibrous tissue, and their interiors are open to the sky. Rain falls into them and echoes downward for a long time. Their upper rims split into fronds as broad as sails, which catch wind and steer mist into the hollow centers. At their bases, water emerges from dozens of pores and runs away in silver threads over the paved root-ground.

When wind reaches the plateau, it cannot move cleanly through it. It is divided by towers, caught under leaf eaves, compressed through root arches, released across broad wet courts. The wind makes the entire country vocal. Great leaves knock against one another like wet flags. Seed chambers boom from within. Tall hollow stems issue tones that overlap into a droning chord. In the stormiest hours, the rain chimneys howl. Their calls travel across the open spaces and return from distant walls, so that one cannot tell whether the landscape is being battered by weather or singing to it.

There is light enough to see everything clearly when the rain pauses. The sky is a washed white-blue, high and enormous. Sunlight lies on the wet vegetation in broad ordinary bands, making the young shoots almost painfully bright. Steam rises from the black water seams. Far away, beyond the last terrace, the country falls off into a succession of green escarpments and cloud-choked gaps.

At dusk, the plants do not become still.

Their flowers open in the heat held by the day. Some are immense, cup-shaped, and full of collected rain; others emerge from the sides of walls in rows, like lamps made of flesh, though they give no light. Their pale interiors reflect the remaining sky. Leaves close over channels. Reservoir skins tighten. Root buttresses emit slow creaks as their inner water pressure changes.

No birds settle on the towers. No insects stitch the air over the pools. No small life troubles the rainwater.

There are only the constructed plants, their endless weather, and the high wet emptiness between them.

It is a landscape that seems to have inherited the idea of a city without inheriting its inhabitants: avenues leading nowhere, terraces overlooking valleys, walls enclosing nothing, bridges spanning vapor, cisterns gathering water for no thirst. Yet it is not abandoned. It is busy beyond measure. Every wall is growing. Every field is shifting. Every spillway is deciding whether to hold or release. The whole elevated country is occupied by its own making.
