The place is called the High Kiln, though there is no fire in it.

It occupies the summit of a mountain that is itself no longer a mountain in the usual sense. Whatever stone once rose here has been entirely enclosed, faced over, replaced, and forgotten beneath construction. The High Kiln is a small world compressed into a summit basin: perhaps no wider than a village square, though it has the inward complexity of a city. Every surface is made. There is no soil between the structures, no exposed bedrock, no seam where the built thing gives way to the natural world.

Its floor is fitted from dark ceramic blocks, smooth under centuries of heat and footless abrasion. The blocks are not large enough to be called paving stones, nor small enough to be tiles. Each is irregular, shaped by hand with a primitive precision: thumb-width hollows, knife-marks, hammered corners. They interlock so closely that no dust can settle in their joints.

No dust settles anywhere.

The High Kiln is packed with low constructions: chambers, towers, ducts, blind shrines, stacked wall-plates, ladders that ascend only a body-length before meeting ceilings, little vaulted mouths opening onto nothing. The place has been built over and over by generations who apparently valued enclosure above all else. A corridor turns through a mass of clay-brick arches, becomes a stair, becomes the roof of another corridor. Some roofs are paved walkways; others support fields of inverted bowls. Copper rods, long since blackened, run from wall to wall in careful bundles. Stone wheels are fixed in niches, their spokes fused into their bearings by age. Doors of heavy wood have become nearly charcoal-hard in the warm air, yet have not rotted, because there is no moisture to invite softness.

Everything is hand-made, but nothing is crude.

The bricks are baked from some mineral mud hauled from far below before the summit was sealed. The mortar is pale and glassy. The beams are fitted with wooden pegs and corded bindings, though the cords have mineralized into stiff gray tendons. There are bronze bells everywhere, thousands of them: some the size of a clenched hand, some as wide as a cistern, most hidden within walls or suspended in cavities above the narrow ways.

They are the source of the noise.

The High Kiln roars continuously.

It is not a wind. There is almost no air movement in the cramped passages, only a dry, warm pressure pressing against the skin. It is not machinery, because nothing moves. No wheel turns. No hammer falls. No chain advances a link. Yet every bell is sounding, every bronze throat held in the same unending vibration. The noise is too complete to be called ringing. It is a single immense chord made of innumerable tones: low stone-deep booming beneath a metallic shriek; a trembling middle register that seems to fill the teeth; bright, thin notes threading through the arches like wires.

At first the sound seems to come from outside the body. Soon it appears inside the ribs, behind the eyes, in the cavities of the skull. Speech cannot survive in it. A person could shout directly into another person’s face and only see the shape of the words vanish.

There are no people now.

There are no animals either—no birds clinging to the upper vents, no insects in cracks, no pale scavengers under the floors. There is nothing that feeds, breeds, hunts, grazes, or flees. The High Kiln has no water, not even the remembered dampness of a cellar. Its air is too dry for mold. Its warm stone is too dry for lichen. In shaded creases, one occasionally sees a gray velvet-like crust, but on close inspection it is not living matter. It is a slow bloom of salts forced from old brick by heat, crystallizing in elaborate soft-looking fans.

The crystals do not grow. They have already grown.

Nothing here is in progress.

The warmth is steady and peculiar. It does not rise from below or arrive from sunlight. The summit lies high enough that the outer world, glimpsed through a few shaft-like apertures, appears as a dark cold sky and distant black ridges. But within the construction the temperature is that of skin-warmed pottery. Stone floors are warm. Bronze rails are warm. The sealed interiors of the bells are warm. The heat has no source because, perhaps, its source is no longer relevant. It simply belongs to the place, as color belongs to a mineral.

Light is sparse. It comes from narrow chimney-slits near the uppermost walls, where the sky hangs as a dull iron-gray strip. Most illumination comes from objects embedded throughout the masonry: thumb-sized wafers of translucent stone, cloudy amber, blue-white, or deep red. They do not flicker. They have been shining for so long that their light feels ancient, exhausted, yet unwavering. They are set at knee height, at shoulder height, under lintels, inside the mouths of bells. Their dim colors pool across the black ceramic floor and are broken by the tight geometry of the walls.

The place is never truly dark because there is nowhere for darkness to gather. There is too much built matter, too many surfaces close together. Every niche holds a small mineral glow; every turn catches a thin reflection from bronze or glazed brick. The High Kiln is dim in the way a closed eye is dim: reddish, crowded, internal.

At its center stands the oldest structure, a tower only three stories high but so densely surrounded that it can be seen only from certain corridors. It is made of pale, hand-dressed blocks, each incised with lines that may be writing or measurements. The marks repeat with meticulous variation: circles nested in squares, sets of three vertical cuts, long branching grooves like maps of dried rivers. There is no entrance at ground level. A ladder leads to a hatch halfway up, but the ladder is blocked by a curtain of hanging bells, each bell touching the next.

Nothing swings. Nothing needs to.

The bells press against one another and sing.

Above the tower, the summit has been roofed with a lattice of black beams and ceramic plates. The roof is not complete. Through its gaps, one can see the altitude: a depthless sky, stars sometimes visible even in daylike hours, and the blunt profile of adjacent peaks. No rain enters. No rain has entered for an age beyond counting. The outer slopes may have long ago become fields of salt, naked mineral, or silent ruins of their own. From the High Kiln, there is no way to know. The apertures are too narrow, and the sound makes observation feel impossible, as though vision itself is being shaken apart.

The old builders seem to have understood this limitation. They made their world inward-facing.

There are seats pressed into walls, but they are too close together for comfort. There are shelves filled with identical clay vessels, every vessel sealed. There are hearths without ash, cistern-shaped basins without water, worktables bearing stone chisels whose edges have not changed. Some workshops contain half-finished objects: a bronze mask without eyeholes; a drum frame stretched not with skin but with a hammered sheet of copper; a block of reddish stone shaped almost into a human hand.

Almost, but never quite.

The High Kiln is not a ruin. Ruins imply collapse, weather, reclamation, some visible argument between what was intended and what came after. This place has passed beyond ruin. It has reached completion by persistence. The cords have hardened, the doors have sealed, the mineral lamps have held their weak colors, and the bells have found the one chord they can sustain forever.

Its ancientness is not expressed by decay. It is expressed by perfect accommodation.

Every object has accepted every other object. Every wall bears its load. Every beam has settled into its socket. Every crack has filled with crystalline deposit. The heat is balanced. The air is balanced. The light is balanced. The sound is balanced, though it is deafening.

There is no morning in the High Kiln, no evening, no weather, no season. The same warmth touches the same black floor. The same amber wafers glow in the same alcoves. The same bronze bells ring with no visible hand to strike them.

And in the packed central tower, behind the curtain of bells, something perhaps remains sealed: not alive, not dead, not sleeping. Only kept.

The High Kiln does not wait for anyone to open it. It has no need of witnesses. It was built to make a sound that could survive the disappearance of all listeners, and it has succeeded.
