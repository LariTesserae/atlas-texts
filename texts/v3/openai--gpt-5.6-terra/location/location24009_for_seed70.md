The high country has drowned without becoming a sea.

It is a broad, elevated basin among young mountains, filled nearly to the rims with clear warm water. The old valley floor lies far below, invisible under green depth, but its shape remains legible in the slopes: steep shelves of dark stone, fresh-cut gullies, raw pale scars where rain and gravity have only begun their long work. The surrounding ridges are not old enough to be gentle. They rise in abrupt black spines, some breaking the surface as narrow islands, some passing beneath it in blue-green walls.

From above, the place might resemble a lake. Inside it, it feels more like weather that has settled and deepened.

The water is everywhere, not violently moving but fully present: lifting leaves, filling ravines, resting against cliffs, carrying the heat of daylight down through the forest. Its surface lies high among the mountains, smooth for long distances, interrupted only by the green crowns of trees and the occasional ridge of volcanic rock. In the morning it reflects cloud, but by noon the reflection gives way to depth. A person looking down from a wet ledge would see submerged trunks descending into wavering light, then ferny understory, then darkness where the basin drops away.

Vegetation has not retreated from the flood. It has adjusted with a luxuriance that makes the drowning seem almost intentional.

Forests grow along the upper slopes, rooted where the ground still rises above the waterline. Their roots spread outward into the shallows in tangled, pale systems, clasping stones and each other. Trees lean over the basin with leaves thick and waxy, broad enough to catch rain and glossy enough to shed it. Some have lower branches permanently submerged; these branches are furred with algae, draped in long green threads that stream only when one is close enough to see the minute current.

Below the surface, the drowned forest persists in a second form. Vines loosen from their former paths and float in slow braids. Ferns occupy ledges in the shallows, their fronds opening underwater as naturally as in air. Dense gardens of water-leaf, moss, and translucent weed cover every stable surface. The vegetation does not form a neat boundary between land and water. It blurs it. It makes coves into green chambers and fissures into vertical wetlands. In the narrow places between rock walls, the water is crowded with life so thoroughly that a swimmer would have to part the landscape by hand.

At this elevation, the air above the water is warm but thinner than lowland air. Cloudbanks catch on the mountain rims and dissolve into mist. Rain comes often, though usually gently: a fine, soft fall that dimples the basin for an hour and then passes. The rain feeds hundreds of threadlike cascades. They descend from unseen upland springs, silvering the young rock faces, vanishing into curtains of leaves. None are large enough to roar. Their sound is private: a close, continuous trickle heard only beside them.

Most of the basin is quiet.

The surface sometimes clicks as a seedpod opens. Water insects stitch brief lines across sheltered coves. Leaves touch leaves. In the submerged woods there is the faint dry sound of stems rubbing together, softened by water until it seems less a noise than a pressure in the ear. Now and then, from a depth where the slopes meet the drowned valley floor, a single hollow knock rises. It may be stone settling. It may be a trunk shifting in mud. The sound is too infrequent to become familiar.

Animals are scarce, as if the basin is still too new, or too complete in itself to require many of them. Small fish appear in flashes among root tangles, then are gone. A few pale eels keep to the lower ravines, their bodies almost indistinguishable from submerged vines. On the islands, one may find tiny bright frogs hidden in leaf cups, or the shed skin of a snake caught in the roots of a waterside tree. Birds rarely cross the open water. When they do, they are usually alone: a dark-winged shape traveling from one ridge to another without calling.

The world seems to observe a rule of patience here.

Nothing quickly decays. Fallen leaves drift downward and settle in layers over the drowned ground. Branches soften but remain whole. Rockfalls occur, but the broken stones keep their sharpness for years beneath the water, unworn by any strong current. The mountains are still making themselves: cliffs cracking along fresh seams, slopes steep with loose rubble, ravines waiting to become valleys. Yet all this forming happens beneath a calm surface, slowed and muted. A new scar on a wall may take a season to reveal itself, first as a faint discoloration in the water, then as a fan of pale sediment among the roots below.

There are places where the basin narrows into flooded passes. The mountains lean close there, their walls descending on either side until the water between them becomes dark even at midday. In these channels, suspended pollen gathers in golden clouds. Sunlight enters in long pillars and catches countless particles: silt, spores, fragments of leaf, small transparent creatures too slight to name. They rise and fall so slowly that the water seems to be breathing.

Beyond the passes are other basins, other drowned shelves, perhaps connected deep below by channels choked with stone and roots. The landscape extends farther than the eye can confirm. Every opening leads to another shoulder of mountain, another stand of wet forest, another long plane of water held impossibly high among the ridges.

At evening, the warmth remains in the stone. The water darkens from green to blue, and the islands become black masses of leaves. Mist begins low, first gathering around root systems and in the mouths of ravines, then spreading across the surface in thin, level veils. It does not hide the land so much as make it seem submerged a second time.

By night, the whole basin becomes a quiet depth under the stars: forest above, forest below, young mountains holding a warm inland ocean between their unfinished bones.
