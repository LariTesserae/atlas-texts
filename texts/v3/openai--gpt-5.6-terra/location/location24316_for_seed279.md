The low country begins without a horizon.

Not because it is flat—though it is nearly flat—but because every distance is occupied. Ridges of pale mineral rise and overlap, each only a little higher than the next, their sides packed close together like the folded skin of a great cooling thing. Between them lie channels full of dark water. The channels do not run cleanly. They branch, stop, return beneath thin crusts, seep through porous stone, and gather again in black, wavering pools. There is water everywhere, but little of it moves quickly enough to be called a stream.

The ground has been made recently, or is still being made. It has the unfinished look of land after a sea has withdrawn from it reluctantly. Fresh edges show along the ridges: chalk-white breaks, blue-grey fractures, faces of glassy black rock sharp enough to catch the dim light. In places the earth is not earth at all, but a hard skin over wetness. A person could step from one dry shelf to another and hear a soft hollow note beneath each foot, as if the country were roofed over with stone.

Nothing grows.

There are no grasses around the pools, no reeds leaning into the water, no lichens softening the exposed faces. The absence is not clean or empty. It is crowded by other textures: salt flowers crystallized in shallow basins, blisters of mineral foam, sheets of translucent deposit hanging from ledges like wet parchment. Fine black filaments sometimes drift through cracks in the stone, but they are not roots. They shrink from touch and dissolve when brought into the open air, leaving a metallic taste behind.

The dense ridges confine the air. Cool mist hangs in all the narrow places, and the world seems permanently after rain, though rain is seldom seen falling. Water condenses from the low ceiling of cloud, gathers on every projection, and ticks down in patient drops. The rocks weep from seams. Stony walls sweat. Even the old structures scattered across the valley shine with wetness.

These structures are few enough to feel accidental, but each has the stubbornness of a monument. A square hut of fitted slabs stands half-sunk beside one of the broader pools. Farther out, an arched causeway crosses a watercourse that has shifted away from it, leaving its lower stones marooned in a field of grey mud. Here and there, upright posts emerge from the ground: not timber, not metal, but columns of a yellowish stone too regular to be natural. Some are wrapped in mineral accretions until they resemble figures with bowed heads. There are no roads joining any of them. No tools, no lights, no wires, no evidence that the structures have purpose now or ever did.

The loudness comes from the water and the stone.

All across the basin, trapped water knocks beneath crusts: a deep, irregular booming, too slow to establish a rhythm. Drops strike hollows with amplified plinks. Air presses through fissures and produces long organ notes, some so low they are felt in the ribs before they are heard. Where mineral shelves overhang the channels, thin sheets of water fall continuously, breaking into a hard silver hiss. The whole place sounds inhabited by machinery, but there is no machinery—only pressure, cavities, and the slow settling of a young landscape.

Occasionally there is another sound: a wet slap in a pool, followed by silence.

The rare animals do not show themselves readily. In the deepest water, pale shapes may pass below the surface—long bodies, perhaps, or merely ribbons of sediment drawn by some submerged current. On the ridges, narrow jointed things sometimes cling to the undersides of stone lips, their bodies almost identical to the deposits around them. They have no visible eyes. When the air grows colder, they release a brief clicking chorus, then become still again.

Nothing in this country wastes motion. The pools rise by a finger’s breadth over days. Mineral banks advance around them in frail terraces. A ridge may split open in the night with a sound like a cannon shot, yet by morning the fracture is merely another black line taking in water. The old structures lean a little farther each season. Their foundations disappear grain by grain into the wet ground.

Above, the sky is a continuous lid of grey-green cloud. Day arrives not as brightness but as a thinning of darkness. The wet rock takes on muted colors: bone, ash, bruised violet, dull copper, the occasional seam of green-black glass. At evening, these colors do not fade so much as sink into the water.

It is a place with rules that can be learned only slowly.

Do not trust a surface that reflects the sky; it may be water, or it may be a stone membrane stretched over a flooded hollow.

Do not follow the loudest sound; the ground beneath it is usually thin.

Do not shelter in the old buildings when the basin begins to hum. Their walls are sound, but their floors remember the channels that existed before them.

And if a pool becomes perfectly still—no dripping reflected on it, no tremor from the surrounding earth—walk around it. In this landscape, stillness is not the absence of movement. It is movement gathering itself far below.
