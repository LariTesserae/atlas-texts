The sea has not covered the land so much as entered into an agreement with it.

At low elevation, where a river delta might once have spread itself into reeds and silt, there is now a warm, unsettled expanse of water under a ceiling of weather. The horizon is close in some directions and infinitely postponed in others. Rain falls in vertical sheets, but the sheets do not always reach the surface. Some stop halfway down and gather into translucent walls, gently billowing. Others rise from the water in reverse, pulled toward bruised openings in the cloud deck. Between them, the air is wet enough to swim through.

Nothing is dry. Not the black roots protruding from the inundation, not the concrete footings of old utility sheds, not the rust-scaled housings of pumps and turbines. Even the few structures built above the waterline—narrow platforms, power pylons, a stranded operations tower with its windows glowing a dim green—have acquired submerged qualities. Barnacles do not grow here, because there are no animals at all, but mineral frills and pale water-moss mantle every surface. The buildings seem to perspire. Their rivets bloom with orange corrosion, then darken, then shine again as waves pass through the rain.

The place is young. Its islands have only recently been made.

They rise as sharp-backed ridges of gray sediment and fresh basalt, pushed up from the drowned plain in long crooked seams. Their edges are too clean, their slopes unsoftened by time. A person standing on one would find the ground granular and unstable beneath their feet: warm black sand, glassy fragments, mats of root fibers, raw clay with water sluicing through it from nowhere. In an hour, a ridge can shear open and let a channel through. By dawn, the channel may be sealed by a new shelf of mineral foam. Maps are useful only as records of what has already disappeared.

Vegetation has arrived with alarming confidence.

Mangrove-like forests stand in the shallows, though their trunks are not entirely wood. They are wrapped columns of bark, salt crystal, old cable insulation, and dense green tissue that seems to have learned how to grow around whatever it encounters. Their roots form crowded vaulted systems beneath the water, black lattices packed so tightly that waves strike them and become lace. Broad leaves overlap above the flood, lacquered and immense, catching dim industrial light in pools of oily emerald. Vines run from tree to derelict crane, from crane to drowned warehouse roof, from roof to the steel ribs of a half-built floodgate. Ferns occupy the interiors of electrical cabinets. Pale flowers open in the rain, their petals trembling to frequencies too low to hear separately.

There are no birds perched on the gantries. No insects whine among the leaves. No fish disturb the luminous silt below.

The absence is not empty. It is dense.

The water is crowded with movements that are not life: ropes flexing in currents, leaves turning their undersides upward, bubbles traveling sideways, drowned machinery restarting in intervals. A conveyor belt extends from a low factory block and vanishes beneath the water; every few minutes it shudders into motion, delivering a slow procession of smooth dark stones into a flooded loading bay. A line of turbines, half-submerged along a former canal, turns furiously in water that appears almost still. Their function is unclear. Perhaps they are pumping the sea somewhere else. Perhaps they are generating the storm. Perhaps the storm is running them.

The sound makes speculation difficult.

It is not merely rain. Rain drums on leaves, on corrugated roofs, on the open water, on steel walkways and sheets of exposed rock; millions of impacts combine into a roar with no center. Beneath it comes the deep hydraulic bellow of pumps, the metallic shriek of strained cables, generators thudding behind concrete walls, sirens that activate briefly and are swallowed by everything else. Every so often, a newly formed island fractures with a noise like a mountain-sized plate being dropped into a furnace. The sound travels through water, ground, metal, teeth. Speech becomes a private gesture. Even thought seems to need to shout.

Light is sparse and mechanical.

The sky is a layered dark: green-gray storm cloud beneath a heavier purple-darkness, with occasional white distortions where lightning has struck but failed to finish happening. In these flashes, the landscape is revealed not as one floodplain but as several versions laid atop each other. A stand of trees appears farther inland than it was. A tower is upright, then on its side, then reduced to an outline of cables in the water. The great rain walls show brief interiors—stairs descending within them, lit corridors, geological strata, an ocean horizon facing the wrong way.

Most illumination comes from the infrastructure. Sodium lamps remain lit along submerged roads, their orange bulbs glowing under green water. Blue arc lights burn within flooded substations. The operations tower blinks its dull red beacon through curtains of rain, though its top floor is visibly full of water. Long strips of white factory light shine from beneath the mangrove canopy, reflected and broken across every moving surface.

In the deeper channels, the water gives off its own reluctant luminance. It gathers around new faults in the ground, around exposed rebar, around the roots of the largest trees. The glow has no source and no rhythm. It simply develops in places where pressure, metal, fresh stone, and vegetation have become too entangled to distinguish.

The rules here are practical until they are not.

Water obeys gravity most of the time, but only locally. A stream can flow downhill around a pumphouse and then climb its outer wall, threading itself through a broken window. Rain accumulates on the undersides of leaves before falling upward into the branches. Rust advances most quickly on objects that are remembered as being made of steel; in places where the past is uncertain, iron remains bright and newly machined. Plants grow toward electricity as readily as toward light. Engines continue to operate after their fuel tanks have washed away, provided their exhaust pipes remain underwater.

Nothing living moves through the flood except the vegetation, and it moves slowly enough to be mistaken for architecture. Roots lift platforms. Vines tighten around handrails. Leaf canopies shift over entire channels, closing one route and revealing another. In the course of a night, a drowned industrial estate may become a garden supported by girders; by the next afternoon, the garden may have split apart, its roots drifting through open rooms where control panels still blink.

Far out across the floodplain, beyond the factory lights and the dense green islands, there are silhouettes of larger works: refinery stacks, a seawall under construction, cranes with their booms raised into cloud. They are intermittently visible through the rain. At times they seem distant, appropriately regional and immense. At times, between one thunderclap and the next, they appear just beyond the nearest trees, huge enough to eclipse the horizon.

No one is seen maintaining them.

Still, valves open. Floodgates lift. Warning lights turn from red to amber to red again. A loudspeaker somewhere beneath the water issues a calm evacuation announcement in a language distorted past recognition. The message repeats, pauses, repeats, while warm water climbs another step, another root, another ladder rung.

The landscape does not feel abandoned. It feels occupied by processes that have outlived the need for witnesses.
