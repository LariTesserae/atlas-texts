The city is hidden in the hollow of a stone that, from outside, looks no larger than a sleeping animal.

Its entrance is a narrow fracture in a low, sun-paled ridge. Nothing marks it except the heat leaking out, carrying a smell of crushed green stems and hot dust. One must stoop to enter. Beyond the crack, however, the rock opens into a chamber of impossible breadth—not truly large, perhaps, but made to feel immeasurable by its emptiness. The ceiling rises in a shallow dome, white with mineral bloom, and at its highest point is a single round shaft through which the sun enters.

All day the shaft pours light into the city.

It is a brutal, clean light. It strikes walls of packed clay, chalky stone, woven reed, and bone-white plaster. It glints from crude copper hooks and polished shell tools. It turns every leaf hard-edged and green-black. There are no lamps, almost no fires. The sun is enough. The city has arranged itself around it for so many generations that even its shadows seem deliberate: long narrow alleys of shade, shaded sleeping shelves cut into the wall, awnings braided from dry grass, pollen cloth, and the skins of some vanished animal.

There is no water anywhere.

Not a basin, not a well, not a pipe carrying a hidden trickle. The people of the hollow have words for water only as a superstition, a cold sound said to have existed in the upper world before the stone sealed itself. Their gardens do not drink. Their plants draw nourishment from heat, minerals, breath, and the faint vapors exhaled by the city itself.

The vegetation grows in terraced pockets along the chamber walls and in long hanging belts across the open space. It is lush, but not soft. Broad leaves rise like paddles of dark jade from powdery soil. Vines descend in stiff ropes, their surfaces furry with silver dust. Low shrubs produce translucent pods full of sharp-smelling gas; when split, these pods release a buoyant mist that drifts upward toward the sunshaft. The most valuable plants are the throat-palms, whose ribbed leaves open at night and gather the chamber’s cooled air into dense, edible crystals. At dawn, families climb their ladders and scrape the crystals into clay jars before they evaporate.

This is how the city survives: not by drinking, but by keeping moisture from ever becoming water.

Nothing is allowed to condense freely. Every surface is designed to breathe it back into the air. Roofs are perforated. Walls are roughened to catch vapor and pass it inward to root mats. The dead are not buried, because burial would waste their stored dampness; they are wrapped in leaf-fiber and placed in high drying galleries, where the gardens may slowly receive them. Even speech is regulated during the brightest hours. People cover their mouths with woven veils, conserving breath as carefully as grain.

Yet the place is deafening.

The sound comes from the city’s lungs.

All along the chamber floor stand tall wind organs: towers of clay pipe, stone throat, and stretched membrane. Heated air rises from sunlit streets and rushes upward through them. Every tower bellows, whistles, moans, rattles, or shrieks according to the day’s temperature. Some give a single low note that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Others chatter rapidly, like a flock of birds trapped behind a wall. The largest organs, built around ancient fissures in the stone, produce a roar so constant that the inhabitants communicate by hand signs, shoulder touches, colored cords, and flashes of polished mica.

The noise is not merely endured. It is infrastructure.

The wind organs drive fans. The fans ventilate the root terraces. The roots filter dust, capture breath, and feed the city’s strange green abundance. Certain pipes are tuned to repel the pale cave insects that would otherwise strip the gardens. Others signal the movement of heat through the stone: a rising howl means the upper crack has opened in the night; a dull, grinding cough means the air below is becoming stale. Every citizen knows the voices of the towers. Children learn them before they learn the names of their relatives.

There are many structures, but few people.

The city was built for a population that no longer exists—or perhaps never existed all at once. Whole neighborhoods are empty: stacks of little mud-brick rooms clinging to the walls, bridges connecting balconies with no doors open onto them, plazas floored in pale tile where no one gathers. Their emptiness is not abandonment exactly. The rooms are swept. The bridges are mended. Clay wind-chimes are kept hanging beneath eaves, though their sound is swallowed by the larger roar.

The people maintain space as an inheritance.

They live spread apart, each household occupying its own tower, terrace, or wall-quarter. Between them are expanses of garden, sunlit dust, echoing workshops, and unused civic constructions: a council bowl with a hundred seats, a market arcade of a thousand stalls, a processional avenue wide enough for a crowd that would take the whole remaining population to fill. The city feels less like a settlement than the carefully preserved shell of one.

Its tools are simple. Stone blades, copper needles, wooden levers, fired-clay gears, woven belts, hand-turned fans. Yet everything is intricately adapted. A basket may be a seed store, a filter, a musical warning device, and a cradle for a particular kind of vine. A knife is shaped to cut stems without releasing their precious vapor. Ladders are made from the hollow stalks of a garden plant and sing under weight; by their pitch, a climber can tell whether the stalks are drying out.

Fauna is scarce. There are no beasts of burden, no herds, no familiar birds.

A few long-legged dust moths inhabit the highest levels, gathering around the sunshaft whenever its light tilts across the dome. Their wings are transparent except for veins of gold, and their passage causes brief flickers to travel over the walls like reflections from an absent sea. There are also root-eels: blind, dry-bodied creatures that wriggle through the mineral soil beneath the terraces, feeding on dead fibers and leaving behind richer dust. They are protected by law. To kill one is considered an attack on the city’s foundation.

The only other animal is rarely seen. It lives in the inaccessible black folds behind the wind organs, where hot air enters from deeper rock. People call it the Bellows-Walker. No one agrees on its shape. Some say it has too many jointed legs. Some say it is only a moving knot of warm darkness. Occasionally, amid the continuous noise, a new note appears: a wet, resonant boom, though nothing here is wet. Then the tower workers lower their eyes and wait for it to pass.

Above, beyond the crack, lies the exposed land: low hills of salt-colored rock, wind-cut and lifeless beneath an immense white sky. The outer world is dry enough to flay paint from stone. No tree can grow there. No soil holds. The city’s entrance is usually concealed by a sliding screen of mineral plates, because sunlight outside is too fierce and the air too empty even for the hollow’s resilient plants.

But once each year, when the sun is at its harshest and nearly vertical overhead, the city opens.

The upper fracture is widened by hand. Teams with copper picks and antler wedges clear the old dust. The shaft becomes a blazing column. The wind organs go mad. Their roar becomes so loud that the walls tremble and every hanging vine streams upward. For a few hours, the city exhales into the desert: old air, plant-spores, heat, songs no human throat can make.

Then, from hidden folds in the gardens, thousands of seed-pods split at once.

Their silver down rises through the light and out into the barren world.

Most will burn. Most will drift until they become nothing. But a few may settle in another crack of rock, another small pocket where breath and mineral and shade can gather. Perhaps they will make another garden. Perhaps another hollow city will begin: loud, green, waterless, and patient beneath the stone.
