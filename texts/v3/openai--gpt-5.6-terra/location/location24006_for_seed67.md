The low country begins where the last service escarpments descend into vapor.

There is no soil. There has never been soil here. The ground is made of fitted plates, poured mineral composites, ribbed ironstone, and long terraces of pale ceramic that disappear into the haze. Everything has been assembled: the shallow basins, the banks between them, the channels carrying warm water, the low black pylons that punctuate the horizon. Even the apparent mud is manufactured—an ocher suspension of crushed refractory, biological waste, lubricants, and fine metallic dust, held in designated flats behind retaining lips.

At first it resembles a marsh.

Then one notices that every reed-shaped thing is a conduit, every hummock a capped valve-house, every pool edged with numbered bolts. The landscape has borrowed the geometry of a wetland without permitting anything so untidy as growth. Broad expanses of water lie half a meter below the walking decks, brown-green and nearly still. Their surfaces carry a thin iridescent film that rearranges only when an animal enters or leaves. Warm condensate beads constantly on the underside of gantries. It drops at long intervals: one clear note, then another somewhere far off, making the whole basin sound as though it is thinking.

The country is immense. It does not end at the horizon so much as become another version of itself: more processing lagoons, more elevated rail lines, more square-bodied pumping towers, more pale spillways crossing wet flats. Its weather is generated internally. Above the central works, columns of heat lift into the normal blue-white day and flatten into cloudbanks. The clouds drift little. They have occupied their places for so long that the shadows of whole districts are known by schedule, though the schedules are never written down. In the afternoons, rain falls over the condenser plains in a fine warm mist, not violently enough to wash anything clean.

The light is ordinary in the unsettling way that makes the place believable. Sunlight comes down through humid air, turns the water surfaces white, finds every rust-red seam and polished wet edge. There are no dramatic alien colors, no permanent darkness, no cosmic spectacle. The strangeness comes from how completely the familiar has been fabricated. A bird’s silhouette crosses a sunlit channel and lands on a girder. The girder is shaped like a branch because somewhere, at some point in the construction of this world, somebody decided a branch was a useful thing to imitate.

There are animals everywhere.

They fill the canals, crowd beneath the decks, occupy maintenance cavities, perch in the mouths of ventilation ducts, sleep in the warmth around cable trunks. Yet the place is mostly quiet. The animals have adapted—or been adapted—to live without advertisement. Millions move, feed, breed, and die with an almost ceremonial restraint.

The most common are the waders: tall, jointed creatures with shell-white legs and blunt, dark heads, walking the shallows in dense silent ranks. Their feet are broad plates, soft at the edges, and they stir sediment from the tank bottoms. They feed on filter colonies that gather in the water’s warm margins. When one lifts its head, water runs down the grooves in its neck and falls without sound.

There are slick-backed canal beasts as well, low and many-limbed, moving in the brown water between submerged grates. Their hides resemble rubberized hose, but their eyes are small and wet and animal. They surface in groups beneath floating inspection platforms, breathe once, and sink. Along the upper structures cling pale mothlike things with wings too heavy for flight. Their wings are heat membranes; at dawn they open them toward sun-warmed wall faces, and by noon they fold together and hang motionless, like scraps of damp cloth.

The most numerous creatures are almost impossible to see as individuals. They occupy every warm seam: bead-sized armored grazers, translucent threadworms in drain channels, soft-bodied mites that consume scale from pipe joints. They are a living maintenance layer. They keep water passages clear, digest mineral crusts, carry spores of engineered bacteria from basin to basin. Nothing green grows. No leaf interrupts the hard horizons. But life is abundant in the wet heat, tuned to metal, vapor, and waste.

The animals are not quite wild, though no one seems to command them. Each species has a place in the system, and the system has stood long enough for purpose to become instinct. Waders avoid red-marked overflow channels. Canal beasts gather beneath pump stations during pressure changes. The winged membranes migrate annually—though “annually” may be inaccurate, because the weather scarcely changes and the sky is always much the same—to the cooling fields beyond the western haze.

There are rules embedded in the landscape.

No water is allowed to remain unmeasured. Every pond has a depth gauge rising from it, often so stained and bird-covered that it looks ancient. No surface may be left unoccupied for long; the empty concrete is claimed by nesting animals, service sheds, drain teeth, or arrays of shallow collection trays. Heat must descend before it can be released. Waste must pass through at least three kinds of body. Anything that breaks is not removed from the world but sealed beneath another layer of construction.

This makes the country feel young and old at once.

Its visible surfaces are young: sharp curbs, fresh welds, unrounded spillway edges, straight channels that have not yet learned to wander. There is little erosion because there is little permission for erosion. Where water tries to undermine a retaining wall, a secondary wall has already been built. Where mineral salts bloom, they are contained in designed white shelves. Where a flood might have cut a natural course, it instead fills a grid of emergency reservoirs, each identical, each rimmed with black access ladders.

But beneath these clean, recent layers are older systems, and beneath those, older still. The basin is continually being rebuilt in place. A road may rest on the roof of an abandoned factory. A pool may be the flooded interior of a transit station. Sometimes, through a service grille, one can see lower levels full of water and slow animal movement: dark chambers extending below the lowlands, populated by things that have never seen the sun.

The industrial machinery is present everywhere but rarely active in any obvious sense. Conveyors run with no visible cargo. Far pumping engines turn at a pace almost too slow to detect. Exhaust towers breathe heat without smoke. A freight line crosses the flats on concrete stilts, and its cars remain parked for decades, or perhaps forever, their sides streaked with condensation and marked by pale nests.

The quiet is not the silence of abandonment. It is the quiet of complete operation.

There is a low broad pressure in the air, the sound of water being moved across impossible distances. There are occasional ticks from cooling metal, the soft clatter of shell-feet on grating, the dry whisper of wing membranes rubbing together. In certain culverts, enormous bodies turn in the dark and make a sound like wet fabric being folded. But there are no alarms. No shouting workers. No roar of active furnaces. The place has passed beyond the noisy phase of industry. It has become self-maintaining, self-fed, and so stable that its processes are almost geological.

The air is warm enough that skin is always damp. It smells of rain on concrete, hot stone, algae that are not algae, animal breath, and the faint electrical tang of insulated machinery. The low elevation makes the atmosphere heavy. Clouds gather close to the roofs of towers. The horizon is seldom crisp; it dissolves into gray moisture and the soft vertical repetition of structures.

Travelers who come here learn not to trust distance. A tower that looks an hour away may require three days of walking around drainage precincts and fenced breeding channels. A narrow line in the haze may be an elevated aqueduct wide enough to carry a city. The animal populations make every route crowded. Paths are not empty avenues but negotiated corridors, with waders standing shoulder to shoulder in the warm runoff and small hard-backed creatures flowing across the pavement like a second, living shadow.

Still, nothing hurries.

The rain falls in its appointed districts. The pumps move the same water through the same buried arteries. The animals repeat their circuits. Sunlight crosses the tiled basins. Condensation collects on the rails and drops into the channels. A new retaining wall may be built over an old one, but the change is too slow to witness. In this vast made marsh, permanence is not stillness. It is repetition so exact, and so deeply inhabited, that even decay has been assigned a function.
