At the height where most mountains begin to shed their names, there is a hollow city built around no road, no river, and no apparent purpose.

It occupies a bowl in the stone: not large, perhaps the size of a compact town, though the enclosing walls of the basin make it feel contained like a room. The rim rises in layers of ancient grey rock, soft-edged from ages of weather. Nothing here is sharp. The cliffs have been worn into folded shelves, gullies, low buttresses, and broad shallow faces that resemble the bones of some enormous animal after every hard angle has been rubbed away.

Within the hollow stand buildings.

They are real buildings, but not in the expected sense. There are walls of stacked slate, doorways, stairways, narrow bridges spanning cracks in the bedrock. There are little squares, colonnades, drainage channels, and terraces set one above another. Yet no tool marks are visible anywhere. Each block seems to have been found rather than cut: flat stones fitted with a patience that could only belong to hands, but to hands with no iron, no machines, no need for hurry. Roofs are mostly gone. Some structures have become only a sequence of low walls amid moss; some remain nearly intact, their small black entrances opening into cold interiors.

No smoke rises from them. No voice answers from inside.

The place is empty in the spacious way a high valley is empty. A person crossing one of its squares would feel exposed, too distinct against the pale ground. The streets are wide enough for gatherings, processions, markets, or migrations, but grass and wet lichen have come up through their cracks. In places, the paving itself has settled and tilted, forming shallow steps that catch rainwater.

Water is never abundant here, but it is everywhere in traces.

Mist gathers every morning along the lowest parts of the basin. It pours from crevices in the upper stone, threads through windows, lays itself across stairways, then thins under the afternoon light. A few springs seep from the walls—clear, cold, mineral-tasting water that runs in narrow silver lines through channels older than the buildings’ collapse. Rain comes often enough to darken everything, then vanishes into the porous mountain. Under overhangs, droplets persist for days, swelling at the same points and falling with a slow, deliberate sound.

The vegetation is concentrated where the damp remains.

Velvet moss covers north-facing walls and the interiors of empty rooms. Low shrubs fill protected courtyards: dark green, dense, wax-leaved, their branches bent permanently by snow and wind. Ferns grow in the wet seams beside the watercourses, though their fronds are small and leathery. There are pale grasses on the open terraces, and on the basin floor stand a number of crooked trees, not many but intensely alive. Their trunks are silver-brown and fissured; their crowns begin low, spreading sideways rather than upward. Their leaves are deep green during the brief season of thaw and turn black at the edges after frost.

The city seems to have been made around these trees, or perhaps the trees have reclaimed the old plan so thoroughly that the distinction is useless.

At noon the light is ordinary, almost disappointingly clear. The sky above the bowl is a hard alpine blue, and sun reaches the streets in angled slabs. Stones brighten to bone-white, ash-grey, blue-grey. The shadows of arches are crisp. Nothing glows or burns or announces itself as magical.

But in this brightness, the proportions become difficult.

A doorway will appear human-sized from across a court and turn out to be barely wide enough for a fox. A stairway will climb toward a terrace only to end at a blank wall, while another stairway—hidden behind an ivy-coated cistern—continues downward into an empty chamber under the ground. Some of the standing walls are pierced by openings that do not align with any room. A bridge may cross a ravine at waist height, though the ravine itself is only knee-deep, as if it had once held a deeper terrain that has since been worn away.

The oldest parts of the settlement are nearly level with the stone. Their foundations have entered the basin floor. They seem less ruined than absorbed.

Sound gives the place its life.

Wind arrives over the rim with the force of a descending thing. It hits the hollow and breaks into currents, moving through alleys, up stairwells, under bridges, through roofless chambers. Every opening has a voice. Low circular wells hum. Narrow slots in the walls whistle in thin, piercing intervals. Loose rows of slate clatter together like teeth. Rain, when it comes, becomes loud enough to erase distance: drumming on flat stone roofs, splashing from ledges, rushing through the carved drains, falling into hidden pools beneath the streets.

At certain hours, the whole basin seems occupied by a choir of unseen breath.

There are animals, but so few that each sighting carries weight. Ravens nest high in a collapsed tower and cross the sky with their blunt, echoing calls. Tiny mountain hares appear at dusk along the grassy walls, then vanish between stones. Sometimes a pale goat-like creature stands on a ledge above the city, motionless for so long it may be mistaken for weathered rock. It has no visible horns. Its coat hangs in wet ropes after rain. If it sees a visitor, it does not flee. It simply waits until fog passes between them, and then is gone.

No large predators live in the hollow. Or none leave tracks that can be understood.

The rules of the place are quiet and dependable. Moisture travels downward, but moss grows upward, climbing toward the coldest, highest stones. Snow settles only on roofs and ledges that no longer shelter anyone; the open streets remain strangely clear, even in winter. Seeds dropped in the central square do not sprout there. They roll, by wind or inclination, into the old channels and grow only where water has been given a path.

And nothing decays quickly.

A fallen branch may lie beside a stair for decades, bleached but still whole. An abandoned nest remains tucked beneath an arch long after its makers have disappeared. Stone erodes, steadily and without drama, but the settlement does not collapse in storms. It is being reduced grain by grain, softened back into the mountain. Each year a little more wall becomes slope, a little more step becomes streambed, a little more house becomes a pocket of soil where roots can hold.

There may once have been inhabitants. Their absence is not tragic in the ordinary way, because the city does not feel interrupted. It feels completed.

It has reached the portion of its life in which it no longer needs to be used in order to exist. Rain has inherited the gutters. Wind has inherited the towers. Moss has inherited the courtyards. The few animals have inherited the thresholds. And the mountain, patient beyond all civic memory, is taking the structures back into its long, cold body.
