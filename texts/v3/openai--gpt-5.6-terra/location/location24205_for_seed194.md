Under the oldest floor of the city there is a place the masons call the Rootward.

No map records it properly. A few narrow service shafts descend through the brickwork beneath the public cisterns, beneath the kiln yards, beneath the old refuse vaults and the foundations of houses whose upper stories have been rebuilt six or seven times. The shafts end not in caves but in a room of roots: a low, cool precinct perhaps the size of a market square, though its boundaries are impossible to see in the dark.

The city above is built from mineral things—mud brick, salt mortar, fired tile, dark hand-cut stone—and all of it is dry. Dryness is the first law below as well. There is no seepage along the walls, no wet shine, no underground stream. The soil has forgotten water so completely that it lies in pale layers like crushed bone. Dust hangs in the air but does not drift far. It settles on every surface and is immediately taken up by the living floor.

For the Rootward is almost entirely vegetation.

Not greenery, exactly. Nothing here needs sunlight, and the people who tend the descent say that leaves are a surface habit, a vanity of the upper world. The growth below has no leaves. It has fans, furred plates, knuckled cords, pale columns, and broad folds of flesh-firm vegetable tissue that rise from the ground and return to it. It feeds on warmth stored in the earth, on mineral salts, on the faint exhalations of stone and people. Its colors are colors that do not require looking at: ash-white, purple-black, the brown-red of old iron, a dim green felt more than seen.

The floor is a single continuous mat of it. One must walk on living material. It yields under a bare foot with a cool, fibrous resilience, then slowly restores the print after the walker has passed. In places the mat has lifted into low walls, ribs, gates, and clustered hollow trunks. The city has learned to use these forms rather than eradicate them. Rough passages are cut between the growths. Footbridges of braided reed and bonewood span trenches where the root-mass opens into dry black clefts. Small dwellings have been fitted into its larger hollows: not built so much as lined, scraped, and furnished with woven panels.

There are no animals in the Rootward. No rats among the granaries. No insects. No small white cave-things scattering beneath overturned pots. The city’s people find this absence unsettling at first. They bring no dogs down here. They leave food uncovered without fear of nibbling mouths.

Yet it is not silent enough to feel empty.

The place is loud.

All through the dark, the vegetation makes its patient industry heard. Great root cords contract against stone with a noise like a ship’s hull straining in winter ice. Dry pods split somewhere beyond the reach of lamps: a sharp crack, then a prolonged rattling fall of hard seeds across the floor. Fibrous veils scrape one another in broad slow sheets. From deep underfoot comes a repetitive drumming, not rapid, but immense in its confidence: one knock every few breaths, then another, as if a buried worker were driving stakes into the foundations of the world.

The city’s own noises filter down and change. Cartwheels become distant thunder in the shafts. A forge becomes a red, hollow hum. The bells at the gate towers arrive as low pulses through the masonry and cause the root-walls to shiver. When many people walk above, the Rootward murmurs in response. Not speech. Not precisely an echo. More a bodily recognition.

The inhabitants have arranged a small district here. It is an underground town nested in a living one.

At the entrance stand shelves of oil lamps, though the lamps do very little against the depth of the dark. Their flames make small amber pockets, enough to reveal a hand, a threshold, the woven cord tied around a guide-post. Beyond that, darkness keeps its substance. The locals navigate by touch and sound. Every route has a different texture beneath the palm: smooth fungal-bark on the way to the salt presses, braided root on the way to the sleeping chambers, flaked mineral crust on the way to the old stair. Every turning has its acoustic shape. A narrow throat amplifies footsteps into claps. A low chamber swallows voices. The place beside the long rib-wall produces a faint whistle whenever someone exhales.

The built work is primitive but careful. Stone has been shaped with bronze chisels, bone picks, and patient rubbing stones. Pegs are hammered into the root-mass and hold lattices of dried stalk. Clay lamps are set into niches. A few old copper bowls have greened almost black in the cool air. There are benches, storage racks, sleeping alcoves, a small shrine made of three stacked slabs, and a communal grinding floor where people reduce the Rootward’s hard seed-capsules into meal.

This meal is bitter, gray, and nourishing. It tastes of chalk, mushrooms, and cold smoke. It is one of the few things the underground growth gives willingly. The city gathers it only at certain times, after the loud splitting season. To harvest too much is forbidden.

There are rules painted in raised pitch along the entrance walls, meant to be read by fingertips:

**Do not cut a cord thicker than your wrist.**  
**Do not sleep against a breathing wall.**  
**Do not answer sounds that call from beyond the lamps.**  
**Carry your dust home.**  
**If the floor rises beneath you, stand still.**

The last rule is the most important.

The Rootward changes, but on a scale that makes change difficult to witness. A person may descend every day for a year and swear nothing has moved. Then, one winter, the path to the meal chambers is gone beneath a new white buttress. A doorway that served three generations opens onto solid growth. A root column slowly enters an old dwelling and divides its bed in two. The people do not fight this. Their buildings are light, repairable, woven to be moved. The stone portions of the district are ancient and rounded, their corners worn away not by water but by centuries of rubbing roots and human hands.

The land itself is low and nearly flat, pressed between layers of city foundation and deep mineral shelf. There are no slopes except the ramps made by people. No horizon. No sky. The sense of vastness comes from emptiness: the great black spaces between lamp clusters, the distance implied by a sound that takes too long to return, the knowledge that beyond each wall of vegetation lies another chamber, and beyond that perhaps nothing but dry earth and more living root.

Some say the Rootward began as a single buried seed found beneath the first settlement stones. Others say it was always there, older than the city, older than the dry plain above, a surviving piece of an age when the ground itself grew rather than merely endured. The oldest caretakers do not argue. They say it is not a plant in the ordinary sense. It does not rise toward light because it belongs to a world before light had authority.

Its age is visible in the way it has settled into itself. Nothing in the Rootward hurries. The great structures are worn smooth, their surfaces layered with old scars and healed cuts. Dead portions do not rot; they harden into a light woody stone, becoming part of the architecture. A collapsed chamber may, over decades, become a solid root dome. An abandoned ladder is eventually swallowed, its rungs emerging years later in another wall, polished and clean.

The coolness never varies much. It is not the cold of winter, but the cool of deep storage: grain cellar, sealed jar, shadow beneath a boulder. Even during the hottest days above, the Rootward keeps the same grave, mineral breath. People come down to sit in it when fever runs through the crowded upper streets. They do not come for healing exactly. They come because the dark is broad enough to hold discomfort without reflecting it back.

There is one central chamber where the vegetation parts around a void. No one has measured it. A bridge crosses the opening, built of dark wood and replaced plank by plank over centuries. Beneath it lies a hollow so deep that dropped stones are never heard to land. The root-mass descends into it in enormous pale ropes.

From that void comes the loudest sound: a slow, cavernous inhalation, followed by a shuddering exhale through countless unseen fibers. The bridge trembles with each cycle. The lamps bend their flames though there is no wind.

The people say the Rootward is breathing.

They say the city stands safely because it breathes below them.

And sometimes, in the middle of the night, when every house above is dark and even the watch bells have paused between hours, a dull sound rises through the foundations—one vast wooden knock from the buried world—and the sleepers turn in their beds, not waking, as if some old root far beneath the streets has remembered them all.
