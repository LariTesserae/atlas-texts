The plateau has no horizon in the ordinary sense. It has a rim of brightness: a white-gold distance where the ground rises so gradually that it seems to become the sky, and the sky, in turn, is a hard pale dome with a faint copper stain at its center. The land is high enough that the air feels thinned, though it is not thin in the way mountain air is thin. It is thin because most of what would make it air has burned away or been ground into dust over ages.

Nothing grows here.

There is no soil. The surface is packed mineral powder fused by heat into a crust, then cracked, lifted, and re-fused a thousand times. It resembles old ceramic flooring on an impossible scale: pale gray fields, ochre expanses, black glass seams, plates of red stone curled at their edges. Wind does not much move the dust. The dust has been baked heavy. It lies in shallow drifts in the lee of ridges and around the bases of isolated monoliths, each drift edged with a dark, metallic glaze.

The plateau is not flat. It is a terrain of abandoned processes.

Long trenches run through it, too regular to be rivers and too old to be roads. Their walls are layered with slag colors—blue-black, rust-red, sulfur-yellow—and their floors emit a low orange radiance from cracks far below. Great circular basins open among the ridges, dozens of kilometers across. In each one, the center has subsided around a standing column of dark mineral, ribbed and ringed as though it had been extruded slowly from beneath the world. The columns exhale heat, not smoke. The heat rises in visible sheets and folds the distant terrain into wavering replicas of itself.

There are no buildings, no towers, no pipes, no recognizable machinery.

Yet the land is industrial.

The ground works. Beneath the crust, immense pressures sort minerals with patient precision. Iron migrates along magnetic lines. Salt crystals grow in arrays around faults and then are crushed into powder by the closing stone. Gas pockets ignite in deep chambers, turning with a sound like a huge engine rotating at the far end of the world. Across the plateau, narrow black seams periodically brighten, carry a red glow for several minutes, and go dark again. They are not cables. They are not lava, exactly. They are the exposed circulation of a geology that has learned production.

Every few hours—or every few years; time is difficult to judge here—one of the circular basins gives a single heavy cough. A sheet of mineral vapor climbs into the air, hardens as it rises, and falls in a broad ring of tiny black needles. These needles accumulate in dunes, where they chime constantly against one another. The sound is loud, though the landscape appears still: a million fine impacts, a dry silver rattling, the persistent clatter of materials being processed.

The creatures make the place louder.

At first they are mistaken for stones, because nearly all of them are armored, low-slung, and colored like the ground. They cover the plateau in patterns so sparse that an observer can stand for minutes thinking the plain is empty, then notice movement in every direction. A ridge heaves. A field of dark pebbles separates into thousands of individuals. A dust drift exposes eyes—many eyes, bright and heat-sensitive, opening in layers.

They are large and small at once. There are locomotive-sized grazers that crawl on rows of hooked legs, their backs armored with plates of fused silica. They travel from vent to vent, lowering wide, shovel-like mouths into hot cracks. They feed on vapor, on oxidizing metals, on whatever chemical energy the ground delivers. Their interiors tick and clang. When they move, their bodies sound less like animals than freight moving through a distant factory: pistons, chutes, grates, the occasional shriek of stressed metal.

Around them swarm smaller forms: beetle-like things made of interlocking black shells, pale six-winged kites that float on thermal currents, burrowing spindle animals that vanish into the crust and leave behind thin fans of powdered glass. The smallest are nearly invisible, a shimmer moving across hot stone. They gather where mineral vapor condenses and strip it away grain by grain.

There are no plants, but there are feeding grounds. The black seams are grazed down to dullness. The pale mineral blooms around vents are harvested by clustered, many-limbed scavengers. On the cooler north faces of the monoliths, dense mats of something neither moss nor lichen form temporary silver pelts. They are not green. They are a web of crystal membranes, feeding on temperature differences, spreading over stone in the relative cool of dusk and collapsing into brittle flakes by midday. Whole herds converge on them when they appear. The eating is noisy: a universal scraping, thousands of hard mouths abrading the mineral film from the rock.

The fauna is overwhelming not because it crowds the eye, but because it occupies every function the absent vegetation and absent water might have occupied. Nothing is wasted. A dead crawler does not remain dead-looking for long. Its shell attracts cutters, then smaller dissolvers, then dense humming clouds of heat-feeding mites. Within an hour, the carcass is an articulated skeleton of polished internal struts. Within a day, even those are drawn below the surface by burrowers. The land reclaims its elements with an efficiency so complete it feels ceremonial.

And all of it moves slowly.

The great animals progress at the pace of glaciers. Their migrations are measured in generations. A herd may spend a decade traveling from one basin to another, following a deep subterranean warmth that no surface instrument could trace. The glass-winged kites circle for days without flapping. The mineral membranes take a century to alter the color of a cliff. Trenches widen by a finger-width in a human lifetime.

But the slow motion does not make the world quiet. It makes its noises immense and sustained. The plateau groans. Cracks report like artillery, then echo for a long time among the basins. Herds communicate through subsonic pulses that are felt in the ribs before they are heard: a vast, regular thudding beneath the ground. Over it all lies the clink and scrape of innumerable shells, the hiss of vents, the dry ringing of falling mineral needles.

At normal daylight the sky is bright enough to bleach shadows, though the sun itself is rarely visible. It hangs behind a permanent pale veil, perhaps dust, perhaps something in the upper atmosphere that has become too old to settle. At noon, every black surface is painful to touch. The ground radiates heat upward in broad layers. The animals’ undersides glow faintly where they lift their armored bodies from the crust.

Toward evening, the brightness does not fade naturally. It breaks apart.

First, the distant horizon acquires a second horizon beneath it, inverted and trembling. Then the basins appear deeper than they can be, their floors descending into a dark blue distance. Columns split into multiple versions of themselves: one solid, one shimmering in heat, one standing upside down in the sky. Herds travel across the air in mirror formation. Their sounds arrive before their movements, or continue after the creatures have passed.

No one here seems troubled by this.

A crawler approaches a fissure that is visibly not in the same place from one moment to the next. It waits, perfectly still, until the fissure settles into agreement with itself. Then it lowers its mouth and feeds. A cloud of winged creatures rises from a basin and becomes, for several minutes, a black weather system far above the plateau; its shadow crosses the land in the wrong direction. The smaller animals stop moving whenever this happens, their shells folding tight against the ground, as if obeying an ancient rule.

There are many such rules.

Do not cross a ringing field while the stones are singing in unison.  
Do not follow a herd into a basin whose central column has no shadow.  
When the sky shows the underside of the plateau, stand on a black seam; the seams remember which way is down.  
If a dead shell speaks with the voice of a living creature, leave it unopened.  
If the ground becomes smooth enough to reflect you, do not look for long. The reflection will eventually finish moving after you stop.

The landscape has existed in this state for a very long time. It is not a young catastrophe. Its violence has settled into habits. Heat has found its channels. The crust has learned where it may split. The creatures have evolved bodies for pressure, glare, dry chemistry, and the occasional failure of distance. Even the strange parts have weathered. Reality breaks here with the familiarity of seasonal weather.

And across all that high, empty country, between the red ridges and the luminous trenches, the herds continue their almost imperceptible migrations: black, armored, innumerable, loud as industry, patient as stone.
