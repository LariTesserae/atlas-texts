At a high place under water, there is a basin no wider than a village square.

It is not a natural basin. Nothing here is natural, though the distinction has become difficult to feel. The floor is fitted from broad, black slabs whose seams have been worn round by centuries of water. The walls rise close on every side: a drowned court, perhaps, or the interior of some immense stone vessel. They lean inward slightly, not enough to threaten collapse, only enough to make the place feel held. Above, there is no visible surface. The water continues into darkness, dense and warm around the skin.

The court was made with patient, simple means. The blocks bear chisel marks under their softened coats of mineral deposit. Ropes of hammered copper, green-black and furred with salts, run from wall to wall. Pegs of bone or fired clay fasten little structures to the masonry: racks, cages, hinged shutters, ladders made from short lengths of stone drilled and lashed together. Everything is handmade. Everything has been repaired so often that original design has disappeared beneath additions.

No weed rises from a crack. No moss clouds the joints. There are no roots searching for mortar, no leaves drifting down from an imagined shore. The water is clean of green life. Its only softness comes from animals.

They occupy every level.

Pale, transparent fish sleep almost motionless beneath the arches, their fins moving with such slight deliberation that they seem suspended in glass. Bands of armored creatures, no larger than a hand, feed along the walls with stone-scraping mouths. Blind eels curl in the mouths of drains. Crabs with flat, coin-like bodies stack themselves in the warm hollows around the copper ties. At the bottom, where the slabs dip toward a central bowl, there are dense colonies of shell-bearing animals, each shell shaped like a tiny roof or doorway. When they open, they show a moist white interior, then shut again with a sound too small to hear.

The largest inhabitants are the slow ones: broad-backed swimmers, dark as the court itself, moving from chamber to chamber through openings low in the walls. They resemble seals in the way they carry their weight through water, but their heads are smooth, round, and without visible eyes. Their forelimbs end in many fingered fans. They do not hunt. They pass their fans over stone, copper, shell, and one another, tasting the world by touch. Sometimes one settles in the center of the basin and remains there for days, while the small animals gather in the folds of its skin.

There is little sound. Water presses against the ears until silence feels material. A strap shifts against a peg. A shell closes. Somewhere beyond the wall, a very slow current passes through a narrow passage and makes a breathless, hollow note. It may take an hour to become audible and another hour to fade.

The court is high, though there is no sky to establish it. That knowledge comes from pressure, from the peculiar cold that gathers in the upper masonry, and from the rare fall of pale grit through the dark water. The whole complex has been built into the crown of a submerged mountain or tower. Below it lies a vast depth. Above it lies more water, held somehow over the world in an unbroken roof.

The builders understood this arrangement without explaining it. They made their rooms small and dense because open water is dangerous, or empty, or sacred. A person moving through the place would travel by handholds. Every corridor has a cord. Every doorway has a ring set into the lintel. Every chamber has a low resting shelf, polished by generations of bodies. The rules of construction are clear even now:

Never make a flat ceiling; the trapped air, if any comes, must have somewhere to go.  
Never block a drain; the deep water remembers its routes.  
Never strike stone after the dark hour; vibrations attract the large animals below.  
Never carve a face with open eyes; eyes invite the water inward.

Most of the old carvings have eroded into anonymous shallow lines. On the walls are rows of figures with bowed heads and closed eyelids, their hands raised not in prayer but in the gesture used for feeling one’s way through dark water. Their makers are gone. No bones remain in the court; perhaps bodies were given to the depth, perhaps the builders changed into something else, or perhaps there were never builders in the biological sense—only a long sequence of hands working through the warm black flood.

At intervals, a dimness less complete than the surrounding dark descends from above. It is not light exactly. It is a dilution of darkness, a gray pressure passing down through the water. The animals respond at once. The transparent fish turn their bodies toward it. The crabs withdraw into cracks. The broad-backed swimmers leave the central bowl and press themselves against the walls.

Then, far overhead, something immense moves.

Not close enough to be seen. Not fast enough to make a wake. Its passage alters the water’s weight by the smallest degree. Dust lifts from the slabs. Copper cords tremble. For a few minutes the entire ancient court seems to inhale.

Afterward, all settles again.

The grit descends. The shell-creatures reopen. A blind eel unknots itself from a drain. The dark returns to its usual depth. In the warm water, among the chisel-softened stones and the little handmade repairs, the drowned high place continues its nearly motionless life.
