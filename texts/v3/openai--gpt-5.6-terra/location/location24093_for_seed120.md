At the top of the world there is a room no larger than a cistern.

It has no door. It is reached, if it can be said to be reached, through a crack in the upper air: a crevice between two slabs of weather, where thunder has been pressed thin by altitude. Climbers who find it do not emerge onto a summit. They crawl into a hot, wet enclosure assembled from thousands of hand-made things—tiles, baskets, beaten mud-bricks, cups, ladders, roof-beams, knots of reed, little shutters, fragments of wall—packed so tightly that the place seems less built than composted.

Nothing here is natural ground. Even the floor is a floor laid over another floor, generations of fired clay and woven mats and flattened seed husks, all swollen dark with moisture. The walls are not really walls, either. They are vertical accumulations: stacked doors, plastered shelves, water jars set mouth-to-base, woven screens, miniature retaining walls, and crude carved stones fitted into the gaps. Every available surface has been claimed by construction. A nail-sized ledge may support a clay gutter. A gutter may feed a cup. The cup may overflow into a mossy seam that has been carefully braced with tiny sticks.

The entire high place sweats.

Water beads along the undersides of rafters, gathers in the eyes of carved wooden figures, and runs in narrow courses down tile channels. It is warm water, almost body-temperature, and it smells faintly mineral, green, and smoky. It comes from nowhere visible. The air arriving through the high crack is cold enough to sting, but when it enters the room it turns feverish; vapor condenses at once upon the dense architecture, drumming softly for a moment before joining the larger sound.

There are no streams. There is only circulation.

Water rises through wicks of braided fiber. It seeps from clay joints. It drips from upside-down bowls. It travels along gutters scratched into roof tiles, vanishes beneath matting, reappears in a wall, and falls again into a basin that is not quite deep enough to contain its own reflection. The basin is full of floating leaves, but the leaves are made from thin hammered copper, green with age. Moss grows over them anyway.

Vegetation has entered every compromise the builders ever made with space. Ferns unroll from the mouths of jars. Broad, waxy leaves force themselves through cracks in plaster and turn the dark room into a greenhouse. Vines run upward along rope handrails, then find no further wall and continue across the ceiling. Their roots do not reach soil; they inhabit woven packing, rotten cord, spongey mortar, and the soft interiors of old bricks. There are tiny flowering plants in the seams of stair treads, orchids no bigger than knuckles, their petals wet and red as fresh lacquer. A single banana-like leaf rises through the center of the enclosure, too large for it, folded again and again against the roof until it has become a green tower.

The plants grow quickly, visibly. A tendril will test a new gap while someone watches. A leaf opens with a small leathery sigh. Roots take hold in the damp fibers of a wall and shift a whole panel of plaster by the width of a fingernail.

But the place does not erode.

It cannot.

Nothing has had time to wear down. Every object is new and ancient at once: newly shaped, still holding the thumbprints of the hands that made it, yet already layered beneath replacements and repairs. The bricks are soft at their edges, not from age, but because they are still becoming bricks. Clay is forever being pressed into molds somewhere behind the visible room. Roof tiles push slowly from walls as if the chamber is growing scales. Fresh cord extrudes from wicker bundles. Little wooden pegs appear in clusters under shelves, pale and damp, and harden by evening.

The room is under construction without builders.

Sometimes, in the dimness, a crude stone mallet lifts itself and taps three times against a wedge. Sometimes a reed mat tightens its own weave. A row of unfired bricks quietly inches forward beneath a planter, looking for its place in the structure. No motion is hurried, but all motion is constant. The whole enclosure works around its occupants with the intimate insistence of a nest.

The light is poor. It comes from the crack above in brief blue-gray pulses, as though daylight were being strained through miles of storm. It also comes from wet fungi under the steps, from small lamps burning fatty oil in wall niches, and from an uncertain green luminescence trapped in the leaves. Faces—if there are faces—are never fully visible here. The carved guardians along the walls have broad, simple features cut with flint or shell: closed eyes, square mouths, brows like rooflines. In some flashes of lightning they seem unfinished. In others they seem to be the ones doing the building.

The noise makes the room feel larger than it is.

Rain ticks on tile overhead. Water falls through hundreds of minute channels. Ropes creak under loads no one can identify. The hot leaves slap and shiver. Somewhere inside a wall, clay is kneaded with a slow, wet grinding sound. Thunder, squeezed through the opening above, arrives as a prolonged metallic bellow that makes every cup and nail and clay vessel sing in response. The chamber hums with these answers. It is loud enough that speech would be useless, though occasionally a person-shaped shadow leans from a narrow passage and seems to shout instructions into the din.

There are almost no animals. A few pale insects travel the wet rafters, each carrying a bead of water larger than its body. Once in a while a thin, translucent lizard appears between bricks and drinks from a gutter. It has no eyes, only mirrored patches of skin, and it vanishes whenever looked at directly. The true fauna of the place may be the constructions themselves: the ladders that settle their feet; the baskets that open and close in the damp; the roof tiles which creep into overlap when storms intensify.

Outside is an impossible drop. Through slits in the packed walls one can glimpse cloud, far below and far above, turning around the peak in slow white rivers. The room is perched at an altitude where the sky ought to be clean, cold, and empty. Instead it is humid enough to breathe like a mouth. Heat rises from the chamber and meets thin air at the opening, producing a perpetual fog that spills out into space. From a distance, perhaps, the mountain would seem to be wearing a small wet crown.

No one knows why this pocket exists. It may be a shrine assembled by people who had not yet learned how to make a world and so practiced inside a single room. It may be a seed of architecture lodged in the summit, trying to germinate. It may be that the mountain itself has begun making shelter, using the simplest possible tools: mud, fiber, heat, water, repetition.

Whatever its purpose, it is not complete.

A new stair is always appearing beneath the old stair. A new wall is quietly taking shape behind the wall one leans against. The enclosure is densely, lovingly overcrowded with its own first attempts. It has not yet discovered emptiness, distance, or decay.

Above it, the crack in the sky narrows and opens, narrows and opens.

Each time it opens, another handful of rain enters. Each time it narrows, the room exhales steam, leaf-scent, lamp smoke, and the loud, patient sound of a world being made too close to itself.
