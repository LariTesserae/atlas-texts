At first there appears to be nothing but blackness and heat.

The land is high enough that the air is thin, though it does not feel cold. Warm currents rise continuously from the ground, smelling of iron, wet stone, and a faint mineral sweetness, as if the whole plateau has recently been washed but never fully dried. The darkness is not the darkness of night exactly. There is a sky overhead, immense and open, but it is permanently overcast by layers of airborne dust and dark vapor. Somewhere beyond those layers, a sun may exist. Its presence is known only by the slow warming and cooling of the rocks.

This is a continent of elevated basins: broad, nearly level expanses of dark shale, salt-colored clay, and old volcanic material, interrupted by escarpments that descend into pits too wide to see across. The ground has been worked over for ages by wind, intermittent rain, and the subtle movement of warm water beneath the crust. It is not rugged in a youthful way. Everything has been reduced, settled, made durable. Ridges have soft shoulders. Gullies are shallow and old. Great fields of stone lie in quiet, organized drifts, their slabs aligned by prevailing winds or ancient floods.

There are no buildings.

No roads cross the plain. No pylons, fences, mines, vessels, or abandoned works suggest human use. Yet the place has the atmosphere of industry.

From cracks in the ground come deep pulses: pistonlike thumps, distant turbine hums, long metallic groans that travel through the stone rather than the air. In some basins, slow belts of dark sediment move across the floor, carrying pebbles, shells, and mineral nodules in steady parallel streams. They look manufactured until one kneels close enough to see that the belts are composed of innumerable small creatures—blind, plated bodies interlocked edge to edge, each pushing against the next. They travel in files for days, perhaps years, processing the grit beneath them. Where they pass, the bare rock is left polished and scored.

Other animals inhabit the vents.

They are abundant, though the land remains spacious because each species keeps its distance. Tall heat-feeding forms stand around fumaroles in loose circles, their narrow bodies draped in soot-dark membranes. At intervals they unfold those membranes to expose dim internal radiance: dull orange, green, or the blue-white of an electrical arc. They do not illuminate much. The world swallows light quickly. But from far away, on a clear stretch of plateau, a cluster of them can resemble a factory burning behind a ridge.

Smaller life gathers wherever condensation forms. Water is scarce but reliable in its habits. During the coldest portion of the dark cycle, moisture beads on the undersides of overhangs and collects in stone hollows. Thin rain sometimes falls—not from clouds, but from the vapor sheets drifting low across the highlands. It arrives as a fine warm drizzle that lasts an hour and leaves the ground smelling sharply alive.

Around these damp places grows the vegetation: sparse stands of stiff black reeds, mats of silver lichen, low cushions of waxy leaves that close when touched. Nothing makes a meadow. Nothing softens the whole world. Plants occur in selected pockets, clustered around seepage lines or the mouths of vents, their roots sunk into warmed fractures. Some have reflective skins and catch what little light filters down from the sky. Others are almost invisible until they move in the wind, revealing rows of pale undersides.

The fauna comes for them.

There are grazing herds that move by sound, not sight: broad-backed animals with resonant chambers in their chests, calling to one another in tones too low to be heard clearly. Their passage can be felt through the soles of the feet. They wander the plateaus slowly, consuming mineral-rich lichen and strips of vent-fed growth. Behind them come long-legged scavengers with hinged faces, careful and patient. At the edges of the herd range, predators wait in shallow cuts in the terrain, nearly indistinguishable from the rocks until their insulated hides flex.

Despite this abundance, the place is mostly quiet.

There is no constant chorus. Calls are infrequent and practical: a single pipe-note from a ravine, a dry clicking exchange near a water hollow, the heavy wingbeat of something moving overhead through unseen vapor. The louder sounds are geological or biological in a way that makes little difference. A vent releases pressure with a hiss like steam. A migratory mass crosses a field of brittle stone and produces a rattling like loose machinery. Beneath it all, the deep ground works.

The industrial character of the world is not a legacy of construction. It is a condition of life. Organisms process ore, filter vapor, cultivate heat, and move sediments in patient systems that have no planners. Vast colonies beneath the surface draw mineral slurry through porous rock and expel it in regular pulses. Their waste hardens into terraces, chimneys, and dark towers. Entire slopes are built this way, not by hands but by generations of unseen bodies responding to pressure, temperature, and chemical scent.

There are rules in this landscape.

Warmth rises; life follows it.  
Water appears only where stone has agreed to hold it.  
Light is a resource so slight that no creature wastes it.  
Open ground belongs to the wind, but cracks belong to the living.  
Nothing stays in one place entirely: the herds migrate, the vapor banks drift, the sediment belts crawl, the vents open and seal.

Even the darkness is active. It gathers in hollows, thickens around the bases of cliffs, pools beneath the vapor canopy. In the deepest basins, blackness has texture, full of motes and slow-moving phosphorescent bodies. A traveler might see a faint line of lights descend a slope and assume it was a convoy of vehicles. It would be a procession of burrowing animals carrying heat in the sacs beneath their skins, moving from one exhausted vent field to another.

The high plateau extends beyond any useful horizon. Its weather belongs to itself. Storms form in distant basins and move silently through the dark, announced only by changes in the pressure of the ground and the behavior of the fauna. Then the warm rain comes, briefly, and all at once: droplets ticking on shale, dust becoming slick clay, dormant lichen unfolding, creatures emerging from cracks by the thousands.

For a little while the land shines.

Not brightly. Never brightly. But every wet surface takes on a dull sheen; mineral crusts gleam; the membranes of the vent-animals catch their internal light; beads of water tremble along black reeds. The vast, unbuilt country seems almost engineered in those moments—an immense working floor beneath a roof of cloud, operating without witness, without purpose beyond its own continuation.
