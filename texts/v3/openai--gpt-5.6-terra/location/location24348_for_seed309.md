Below the city’s oldest district, beneath a lid of cobbles and transit lines and basements that nobody remembers excavating, there is a chamber called the Green Exchange.

It is not large, as underground places go. One can walk its perimeter in an hour, provided the routes remain open and the herds have not decided to sleep across them. Its ceiling is low in some places, only a few meters above the head, and elsewhere it rises into a black vegetal vault whose height cannot be judged because nothing falls from it quickly enough. Rain descends there in threads, but so slowly that droplets grow mossy skins while they are falling.

The place began as infrastructure. Brick drainage culverts, iron service tunnels, cable runs, pressure pipes, maintenance galleries: all of them still remain, but they have been accepted into a larger anatomy. The walls are no longer walls. They are densely felted with leaf-sheets, pale roots, woody lattices, swollen seedpods, and the glossy muscular stems of climbing plants that have learned to bear weight. Every surface has become a question of plant tissue.

Some of the old pipes have been incorporated so thoroughly that they seem to have been grown rather than installed. A steam line disappears into the flank of a great root-column and emerges elsewhere as a warm, sweating branch. Conduit boxes flower at their seams with blue-white fungus and tiny silver ferns. A cracked pumping station, its tiled floor long hidden, is held upright by buttress-roots thicker than bridge piers. Its old flywheel still turns, not because it is powered, but because several immense vine-belts pass around it in patient, continuous motion. They are being pulled somewhere deeper in the green.

The vegetation is nearly total, yet the chamber is not crowded in the ordinary way. There are broad aisles through it: dark, open lanes of gravel, old concrete, hard-packed humus, and shallow black water. The living mass rises on either side in walls and towers, leaving a sparse geometry of space between. The Exchange has plazas, though no planner made them. There are clearings where the ceiling roots draw back, junctions around old valves and maintenance hatches, low platforms of rusted steel surrounded by a sea of reeds. In these spaces, the eye can travel. It usually travels into movement.

Fauna is everywhere.

Not merely in the undergrowth, not merely hidden: everywhere. The floor shifts with beetles the size of walnuts, shining with oily green shells. Long-legged pale crabs march in disciplined files along the drainage channels, their claws carrying scraps of leaf and wire. Moths congregate by the thousands around the dim yellow maintenance lamps that still receive power from somewhere above; their wings turn the lamps into trembling organs of light.

There are larger creatures, too. Hairless deerlike things graze the hanging gardens, standing upright on hind legs to strip leaves from the walls. Their antlers are colonized by small epiphytic plants, so that each head carries a slow-moving crown of ferns. Blind boars tunnel through the root beds and periodically emerge into the open paths, steaming in the warm damp, their backs plated with plates of compacted clay. They are followed by clouds of red-faced birds that pick ticks from their hides and scream whenever the boars change direction.

Nothing here is quiet enough to seem secret.

The sound begins with the industrial remnants: a deep intermittent thump from a pumping chamber; the click and run of water through old valves; the long belt-whine of some machine being turned by growth; electrical hum buried under layers of wood. Then comes the biological noise, louder and less regular. Frogs bark from cistern mouths. Insects rasp in the leaf walls. The roots themselves make small sounds as they tighten against masonry—creaks, wet pops, the muted snap of fibers under strain.

Above all is the animal chorus. Wings. Chittering. Hooves striking metal grates. The drumming call of tunnel birds, which hammer their beaks against hollow pipes to mark territory. The rising, nearly mechanical roar of the great reed-hounds when they scent one another across a plaza. A person standing alone in the Green Exchange is never alone acoustically; even breathing feels like adding an unnecessary sound to an already complete machine.

The air is warm, but not hot. It has the temperature of a cellar in late summer, of wet stone holding yesterday’s heat. Condensation gathers everywhere. It pearls on the undersides of leaves, clings to exposed copper, runs down railings, dampens the fur of passing animals. There is water, but rarely enough to make a river. The Exchange receives moisture in installments: seepage through brick, rain filtering from street gutters, steam condensing on cool root surfaces, overflow from burst municipal lines. Pools form in the old low points and are soon bridged by floating mats of vegetation. A dropped tool may vanish in one overnight.

The light is an exhausted mixture. Weak sodium lamps, strung along the old maintenance routes, provide a brownish municipal glow. Bioluminescent molds gather at pipe joints in cold blue seams. Certain flowers open only in darkness and hold a low amber radiance within their throats, attracting flies that look black until they pass through the light and become briefly metallic. Far overhead, at a few broken pavement grates, daylight leaks down in thin, gray columns full of drifting spores.

The plants orient themselves to all of it. Some leaves turn toward electricity rather than light. Some root systems grow toward vibration, thickening around active turbines and pumps. A flowering vine called the switchbloom opens whenever current passes through nearby cable insulation; its petals snap shut during power outages, and entire districts of the Exchange can be read in its behavior. When the old grid is stable, there are pale red blossoms everywhere. When something fails aboveground, they close in sequence, leaving the chamber darker and more alert.

There are signs of human order, though the humans themselves are uncommon below. Catwalks cross the root corridors. Numbered doors remain inset in brick. A municipal warning sign survives at the entrance to a flooded access tunnel:

**AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.  
DO NOT FEED THE MACHINERY.**

The final line has been amended many times in grease pencil, paint, charcoal, and scratched marks:

**IT EATS LEAVES.**  
**IT EATS SOUND.**  
**DO NOT LET IT GO DRY.**

At the center of the Green Exchange stands the machine referred to by these warnings. It is an old water-treatment rotor, perhaps, or a ventilation engine, or a piece of equipment whose original purpose became irrelevant before the vegetation began its work. It occupies a circular chamber beneath a dome of roots. Its casing has burst open in places, and from it spill thick cords of green tissue that pulse with the slow confidence of arteries. The rotor turns behind them, always turning. Not fast. Not quite visibly, unless watched for a long time.

Animals bring offerings to it.

The blind boars push leaf bundles into a grate at its base. Birds drop bright pieces of plastic, coins, bone, insects, dead batteries. Moths beat themselves against its warm casing and are not harmed; they simply vanish into the seams and later emerge dusted in pale pollen. The root walls around the chamber are greener than anywhere else. Their leaves tremble in rhythm with the rotor.

Perhaps this is why the Exchange remains in balance. It is old enough now that its ruin has become a system. Water arrives and is swallowed, sweated, redistributed. The city’s discarded heat enters through pipes and cable shafts and becomes weather. Industrial vibration becomes a kind of sun. The plants build rooms, the animals prune them, the machines turn what they are given into warmth and motion. Brick erodes into soil; soil is held by roots; roots split brick; the broken brick creates new beds for roots.

Nothing is abandoned, exactly. It has all merely changed employers.

At certain hours—though there is no true hour underground—the chamber becomes especially active. A tremor passes through the pipe network. Lamps flicker. The switchblooms open. Then, from every dark lane, animals begin moving toward the central rotor: beetles underfoot, reed-hounds gliding between root columns, flocks of pipe-birds folding through the low ceiling, boars snorting steam, antlered grazers stepping delicately over rails. The open plazas fill, but never feel crowded. Each creature knows its corridor, its level, its angle of approach.

The whole small underground world gathers around the ancient machine and makes enough noise to resemble a factory at full shift.

Then the rain begins again through the ceiling: slow, green-tinted drops, each one carrying a mote of soil, a spore, the black dust of the city above. The animals lift their faces. The plants tighten and shine. Far in the walls, unseen gears answer with a patient, monumental turning.
