The city begins above the weather.

It is built along a broken highland where the world has lifted itself into thinness: a continent-sized escarpment, pale and folded, rising through layers of cloud that never become rain. Below, far below, there may once have been a sea. The old maps incised on gate lintels call it the Low Blue, though no living person has seen blue water. In the valleys beneath the city, clouds collect in stagnant white basins and spill over ridges like slow smoke. They do not wet the stone. They merely pass through it, leaving a mineral brightness in the cracks.

At this altitude, air is not absent, but it has become selective. It enters the lungs reluctantly. Breath is visible only at dawn and then only in certain streets, where the old masonry causes the cold to gather. The inhabitants—few enough that an entire district may appear abandoned for a season—move carefully between stairways, terraces, covered bridges, and narrow courts. Their city is too large for them, or perhaps they are too late for it. The avenues cross open space for miles. Great retaining walls hold up empty neighborhoods. There are plazas broad enough for armies, but grass has never found purchase in their joints.

Nothing grows in the ordinary sense.

There are no trees, no weeds, no lichen softening the walls. The roofs are bare stone, their tiles blanched by high light and powdered by the slow abrasion of airborne crystal. In shaded cistern chambers, where there is no water but a little coolness, a velvet of gray mineral fungus gathers. It has no leaves and no recognizable roots. It forms in low fans around old drains and shrine niches, and when touched, it closes its surface into a hard, seamless skin. Some people collect it for its faint warmth. Others leave it untouched, believing it is the first stage of the city remembering how to become a mountain.

The ground is made of many materials, all dry:

black volcanic glass fractured into shining plates;

pumice pale as old bone;

red strata exposed in the sides of streets;

walls of sandstone layered with salt;

squares paved in a dark, dense rock that rings almost silently underfoot.

The silence is not complete. It is crowded with minute things. Powder moves across the paving in long, low threads. Dust whistles through keyholes. High overhead, stone grains strike the city’s upper parapets with a sound like someone slowly turning pages. In the deepest alleys, where the walls are close together, there is sometimes the quiet clicking of cooling masonry.

No one hears birds.

There are creatures, though rarely seen. At night, pale angular animals emerge from cracks in the high retaining walls. They have too many joints and bodies that appear, from one direction, as flat cutouts against the stone. They feed on salt bloom, mineral fungus, and whatever gathers in the mouths of vents. Their feet leave no tracks, but their passage can be inferred from lines of newly polished rock. Above the towers, black shapes sometimes wheel in the dimness. Whether they are birds, scraps of shade, or pieces of the sky loosening in the wind is disputed. They make no calls. When one lands, it folds itself around a chimney or statue and becomes impossible to distinguish from either.

The city has no machines. Its engineering is older and more patient than machinery: counterweighted stone doors, gravity-fed shafts, wind baffles, heat wells, canals designed for water that no longer comes. Great wheels remain in dry aqueduct halls, their paddles suspended over empty channels. They are fixed in place, yet now and then a wheel will have turned by morning. There is no evidence of force being applied. The residents do not repair such things. Repairs imply that an object has become wrong; here, things are allowed to decide their own condition.

The architecture rises in tiers along the cliffs. At lower levels, carved facades emerge directly from the mountain, their doors black and square. Above them stand tenements of stacked stone balconies, now mostly hollow. At the highest elevation are ceremonial structures: observatories, colonnades, halls with roofless domes. Their builders were concerned with the heavens, though the heavens here are difficult to observe. The sky is often the color of tarnished silver. Light arrives obliquely, diluted through dust and high mist, and shadows do not always agree on where to fall.

In some streets, the shadows point uphill.

In others, they gather beneath arches regardless of the sun, thickening into a cool darkness that has weight enough to slow a person’s hand. A thrown pebble may vanish before reaching such a patch. The people avoid these places, not from terror but custom. A child is taught which stairways go only downward even while they rise, which courtyards return an echo from the previous day, which doorways should never be crossed in a pair.

The old city was planned around these irregularities. It may even have been founded because of them.

At the center stands the Empty Reservoir: a circular basin larger than a lake, cut into a plateau of white rock. Its floor is smooth and dry, divided into geometric fields by low walls. There is no water line anywhere on its sides. Yet the basin is treated as a harbor. Stone piers extend into it. Mooring rings are set along its rim. Towers resembling lighthouses overlook the vacant expanse. On certain cool evenings, an entire fleet of translucent structures appears below: hulls, masts, sails hanging without wind. They make no sound and cast no reflection. By dawn they are gone, but the city’s people sweep the piers afterward, gathering fine grit as though ships have docked.

The weather is active despite its dryness. Cold fronts descend from the upper ridges in curtains of gray dust. Heat lifts from fractures in the earth and distorts neighborhoods for hours, so that distant towers waver and bend into unfamiliar shapes. A storm may take three days to cross a valley, not because it is large but because the valley repeats itself within the storm. Lightning is rare. When it comes, it travels horizontally through the clouds below, illuminating the underside of the city’s bridges.

The most dangerous weather is the inversion wind.

It comes without pressure, without a rising sound. At first, loose objects begin to tremble: beads of glass in old windows, chipped tiles, spoons left on thresholds. Then the wind moves upward from the void below. Dust lifts from the streets, not into spirals but into coherent sheets, as if the whole city is being carefully unwrapped. For an hour or less, voices cannot travel downward. Fires bend toward the sky. The black birds—or shadows, or whatever they are—cling to walls. And in the open plaza near the eastern gate, footprints sometimes appear one after another in the dust, leading from an absent traveler toward the city.

By the time the inversion ends, the streets are clean.

Erosion has worked on everything for ages, but it is not destroying the city so much as editing it. Corners become soft. Faces carved in stone lose their eyes, then their mouths, then all identity, until they resemble weathered cliffs. Staircases shorten as their lower steps blend back into the slope. Whole buildings settle into the escarpment with the dignity of old animals lying down. The city’s builders used no mortar in some districts, only precision and weight; those walls remain almost perfect. Other structures are held together by salts that bloom and fail, producing a slow fall of white powder from ceilings.

There are laws, though they are not written in one place.

Do not carry dust from the Empty Reservoir into a dwelling.

Do not name a street while standing in it.

If a window shows a landscape that is not outside, close the shutter without looking again.

If you hear running water, remain still until the sound stops.

The last law is observed most faithfully. No one alive has heard water for more than a few seconds. The sound comes from sealed wells, abandoned bathhouses, dry fountain mouths, sometimes from beneath the paving stones. It is said that if the sound is followed, it leads not downward but outward—to a coastline on the far side of the sky, where the city is only a reflection in a sea that has not yet occurred.

At dusk, lamps are lit with oil rendered from the rare wall-creatures and from deep-rooted mineral wax. Their flames are small, bluish, and incapable of brightening a room completely. In the inhabited quarters, one sees them as scattered points along balconies and stairs: a constellation arranged across the mountain. People speak quietly near them. They trade preserved fungus, salt, polished glass, old cloth, and maps of routes that change less often than the streets. There is no market clamor. Transactions happen by nods, by carefully weighed handfuls, by objects placed between two people and left there until morning.

Beyond the city, the high country extends in a maze of ridges and dry basins. There are roadways, milestones, collapsed watch stations, and terraces that once held some crop no one can identify. The land is empty enough that a person can be seen crossing it for hours. Yet distance is unreliable. A traveler may walk all day toward a dark hill and arrive at the same archway from which they departed. Another may step across a narrow ravine and return decades later, carrying no memory of the intervening time but with stones in their pockets from a place the city has never built.

Still, life persists in its sparse, strange manner. Mineral fungus folds itself shut in the cisterns. Pale wall-animals emerge under the cold stars. Dust travels from ridge to ridge, carrying the powdered remains of every roof, statue, and threshold. The city continues to settle, to empty, to rearrange its silences.

And every morning, from the highest terraces, the people look down into the cloud-filled world below.

Sometimes the clouds part.

Sometimes there is nothing beneath them at all.
