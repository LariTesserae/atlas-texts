The land lies low enough that water never quite leaves it.

It is not a swamp in the closed, tangled sense, nor a river valley with banks and a clear direction. It is a broad, old plain of mudstone, shell-limestone, and dark clay, nearly level from horizon to horizon, but folded into shallow basins by ages of rain. Water occupies every lower thought of the ground. It lies in long reflective sheets between rises no taller than a person, in brown-green pools beneath undercut shelves of rock, in hidden channels that announce themselves only by a line of brighter reeds or the steady motion of insects above them.

The stone is extremely old and tired-looking. Once it may have stood in cliffs or ridges, but rain has made it humble. It emerges in low ledges, rounded knobs, and broad plates cracked into pale polygons. Its edges have been softened until they resemble bone weathered in a river. In places, rainwater seeps straight through the porous rock and appears halfway down a slope in curtains of mineral-stained dripping. The wet faces shine black, green, ocher, and a chalky white where the sun has dried them.

There are no trees except perhaps a few far apart along the deepest watercourses: low, wide-crowned things with roots exposed like knuckles in the mud. Most vegetation keeps close to water or clings to fissures. There are mats of sedge, sparse reed stands, thorny shrubs with dusty leaves, patches of liverwort on continuously wet stone, and short coarse grass on the higher ribs of ground. Between them are expanses of bare clay, salt-pale earth, and open rock. The landscape has room to breathe. A creature can see a long way here, provided heat haze and the glare off water do not interfere.

The warmth is heavy rather than fierce. The sun rises into a sky already pale with moisture, and by midday the air has a wet, mineral weight. Sweat does not vanish; it stays on skin. Rain is frequent but not dramatic. Clouds gather in large gray rafts, release a dense straight-down shower, and then open again. The ground drinks little. Water beads on stone, fills every crack, slides slowly downhill through courses established before any living thing now present was born.

At ordinary daylight, the pools are bright enough to hurt the eye. They hold blue sky when the sky is clear, silver cloud when it is not, and in the late afternoon they become sheets of tarnished copper. The low terrain gives the light nowhere to stop. It travels across the mudflats and over the slick stone, catching on wet backs, wings, eyes, and the surfaces of leaves.

Life is numerous, even though it is thinly distributed.

The water is crowded. Small fish turn beneath the shallows in quick bright groups. Eels or eel-like animals thread the reed shadows. Frogs call from unseen pockets in the grass, each with a voice different from the next: wooden knocks, bottle-like booms, chirps, wet rattles. Long-legged wading birds move through the margins with deliberate steps, pausing as if listening through their feet. Larger birds circle above the open pools and descend suddenly, folding themselves into the glare.

Every exposed patch of mud bears tracks. Cloven marks, three-toed prints, dragging tails, the delicate stippling of shorebirds, the sliding trails of snails or soft-bodied crawlers. Small herd animals browse the scrub on the drier ground, never forming a dense mass but appearing in groups across the plain: here five at a pool, there a dozen moving between rocky humps. Their calls carry cleanly over the flat land. Predators are harder to see. They lie in shade under ledges, in water colored the same brown as their hides, or motionless among reeds that are too sparse to conceal them completely. The openness has made concealment an art of stillness.

The loudness of the place comes from accumulation. Water drips from a hundred rock faces. Insects saw continuously in the hot hours. Birds argue, call, warn, and answer from every direction. Frogs take over when cloud cover deepens. Wind passes across the reeds with a dry hiss, though everything else is wet. When rain begins, it strikes pools, leaves, mud, and stone with so many different notes that the whole plain seems to become a broad percussion instrument.

Yet nothing in the landscape changes quickly. A puddle may disappear in a day, but the hollow that held it has existed for thousands of seasons. A reed bed moves a few handspans from year to year. A limestone shelf loses a grain at a time. The animals know routes older than their individual lives: crossings between flooded basins, dry-season channels, sheltered eggshell beaches above the highest wet mark. The world seems to have found its settled shape—not fixed, not dead, but patient. Rain continues to lower every rise. Water continues to collect in every yielding place. Life continues to gather where the ground permits it, then scatter again across the wide, shining emptiness.
