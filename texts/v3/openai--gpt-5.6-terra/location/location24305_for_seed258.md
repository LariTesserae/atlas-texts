The plateau has been built, though no one living there would use that word.

From a distance it resembles a valley interrupted by a city, or a city that has learned the broad, patient habits of a valley. Long terraces pass beneath one another in pale green tiers. Walls rise from them—not stone walls, not timber walls, but walls grown of flattened reed, woven root, and the pale inner ribs of enormous leaves. They are too regular to be wild. Their corners have been folded. Their arches have been trained into shape. Every path has a border; every watercourse a lip; every open basin a carefully raised rim of living material. Nothing has been left as bare ground.

There is no ground in the ordinary sense.

Underfoot, the land gives slightly. It is layered: tight moss, rootfelt, corky pads, braided runners, old leaf-sheaths hardened by years of pressure. In places it is springy as a mattress; in others it has been packed into a resilient, wax-dark pavement. The foundations of the plateau are a deep, interlocked mass of stems and roots, continually drinking from the water gathered beneath it. Where a cut opens at the edge of a terrace, one can see the architecture in section: green threads, white fibers, reddish sponge, black water, then more roots descending into mist.

The place is high enough that clouds reach it tired.

They come low over the outer escarpments and catch on the enormous planted structures. Condensation gathers along every rib and seam. Water runs down the inside of leaf-walls in fine shining lines, collects in channels cut by no blade, and pours from the mouths of root-spouts into long communal basins. The rain is frequent but seldom violent. It arrives as a thickening of the air. One moment the far terraces are visible across the valley; the next they have become faded green geometries in a warm silver fog.

There are open spaces, surprisingly many of them. Between the built masses lie wide courts of shallow water, floating meadow, and dark reflective pools. Their surfaces are interrupted by great circular platforms of lily-like vegetation, each one broad enough to shelter a gathering, though no gatherings are apparent. A few platform-fields drift by fractions of an inch over the years, held in place by root-tethers beneath the water. The scale of the place is regional: one can stand on a high gallery and see several districts of it, each marked by a different species of construction. Some are high and vaulted, made of interlocking fronds. Some spread low and wide like woven mats. Others are vertical groves whose trunks have been bent together to form towers, bridges, and latticed lookout chambers.

Yet the distances between these structures remain generous. The plateau breathes around them. A long green causeway may run for a mile through wet open air before reaching another inhabited-looking cluster. Along its sides, water shines in trenches full of curled aquatic leaves. There are few signs that anything travels the causeway. Its surface is unmarked except for the soft inward dents made by falling fruit.

The warmth is not tropical heat. It is the warmth of a high place held above cold country by its own wet respiration: humid, sunlit, and persistent. The daylight is ordinary enough, but it has an odd density here. Sunlight falls through many layers of leaf and fiber, is reflected from water, then rises again as green-gold vapor. The underside of a bridge can be bright. A covered corridor can glow like the interior of a cucumber. At noon, the white flower-funnels that crown certain towers open all at once, and the entire plateau seems to have hung out banners.

The loudness comes from water and plants.

Water falls in sheets from elevated cistern-leaves. Water sloshes beneath floor-mats. Water bubbles in root wells, is expelled in sudden chuffs from pressure bladders, and whispers through packed stems. But the greater sound is vegetal: the prolonged creaking of loaded vines; the repeated wooden knock of seed-pods touching in the wind; the wet tearing sound of a new leaf unrolling; the deep, hollow booming made when the broad basin-plants flex under pooled rain.

At intervals, somewhere beyond sight, an entire wall gives a slow communal sigh.

This is not movement in the usual sense. The landscape does not grow toward a future, does not weather into age, does not collapse and replace itself. It has reached a permanent condition of construction. A root bridge that seems young is not becoming older. A raw-looking cliff of pale unfinished fiber is not a work in progress. It is simply one of the forms this world maintains forever: emergence held in place. The newness of the material is stable. Buds remain half-open without dying. Green scaffolds retain their fresh-cut softness year after year. Water continues to bead on surfaces that never dry, never rot, never quite mature.

Even the geology is unfinished by design.

At the margins of the plateau, where the living construction thins, low mounds of ocher mineral thrust up through the root mass. They look like the beginnings of hills: soft-sided heaps, folded ridges, exposed seams of clay and pumice. But they do not erode. Rain lands on them and runs into carefully grown gutters. Moss presses around their bases without overtaking them. In some places plants have made a frame around a slab of newly surfaced stone, preserving it as if it were an important object. The stone is bare, raw, and strangely tender-looking in the otherwise total vegetal world.

There are animals, but only rarely, and they seem less like inhabitants than punctuation.

A pale, long-legged creature may stand in the shallows with its head hidden inside a flower cup, drinking the pooled rain. Its hide has the granular texture of seed coating. It does not flee. It simply unfolds one folded limb, steps into the water, and vanishes beneath duckweed with no more disturbance than a leaf falling.

High in the woven towers, small dark fliers move from vent to vent. Their wings are nearly silent, overwhelmed by the architecture. They are seen only when they cross a bright patch of sky. No nests are visible. No calls distinguish them from the clatter and murmur of the living buildings.

The principal residents, if there are residents, may be the plants themselves. Their construction has the clear logic of settlement: drainage, shelter, circulation, storage, ornament, outlook. But it has none of the urgency of human work. Doors remain open because there is no threat of entry. Stairways rise to platforms with no final destination. Rows of hollow chambers face the mist, each chamber containing nothing but pooled rain and a rooted fern. Some great halls are grown specifically to amplify sound. Rain striking their roofs becomes a drumroll. Wind moving through their reed-throats becomes a low chord that can be heard across several terraces.

The world’s rules are gentle but exact.

No surface is allowed to be inert for long. If a piece of mineral is exposed, a plant will grow a railing around it. If water gathers, a basin will form beneath it. If a gap opens, vines will span it—not rapidly, not as an event, but as a fact that is already true when next observed. Nothing decays into disorder. Fallen leaves become flooring. Broken stems become handrails. Floodwater becomes an avenue. Even emptiness is constructed: the broad courts are held open by root barriers below the surface, their water kept clear of complete overgrowth so that light may lie in them undisturbed.

At the center of the plateau is a lake that may be a roof.

Its shores are made of rising vegetal embankments, packed and ribbed into grand curved retaining walls. The lake is perfectly calm despite all the falling water around it. Beneath its green-black surface, enormous root columns descend out of sight. Sometimes a slow brightness moves far below, not a creature, perhaps, but sunlight passing through a submerged chamber. The lake reflects the sky with such precision that clouds appear to continue downward into its depth.

Around it stand the tallest constructions: spiraled towers of bound trunks, flower galleries, hanging gardens arranged in vertical bands. Their upper portions disappear into the moist afternoon air. Each tower drips from thousands of points. Each has pale ladders of root extending between it and its neighbors, though these ladders are too narrow and too alive-looking to invite crossing.

Nothing in the landscape asks to be completed. Nothing is waiting for its proper age.

It is a high, wet country of permanent beginnings, entirely made from the patient labor of leaves. Its broad spaces are not wilderness, and its dense growth is not crowding. It is an ordered ecology without tools, a built world without builders, loud with water and wood and breathing green chambers. Beneath the ordinary sun, it remains exactly at the moment when a place first becomes possible.
