The city occupies a high basin where rain never quite arrives.

Above it, the sky is a low, brown-violet ceiling of vapor, luminous only by what it hides: a sun somewhere behind the cloud mass, burning through in dull sheets. Day is not dark, exactly, but perpetually late. The light catches wet surfaces and goes no farther. Everything else—the recesses under walkways, the windows of unoccupied towers, the mouths of storm conduits—remains blue-black.

The basin was once volcanic, or was made to resemble a volcanic place. Its enclosing walls are dark red stone, deeply ribbed by old runoff, rising in terraces to an indistinct rim. They are high enough that the city has no horizon in three directions. In the fourth, a pass opens toward a broader country of salt flats and ruined industrial spires, all of it blurred by heat and wet air.

Water is everywhere, though there is no river.

It appears as sweating seams in the bedrock, silver films on tiled avenues, beads on black pipework, fog pooling beneath elevated rail lines. It gathers in shallow geometric basins that have been built into nearly every surface: plaza floors, rooftops, loading courts, stair landings. The city does not drain water away. It receives it. The old engineers understood that the ground below was hot, that its mineral crust expanded and cracked when allowed to dry, and so they taught the settlement to keep itself damp.

At intervals a slow rain falls from the low cloud. The drops are warm. They make no sharp patter; they strike the city’s mineral surfaces with soft, greasy ticks, then steam gently near the vents. The rain smells faintly metallic, like stone after lightning.

There are no trees, no weeds forcing themselves through pavement, no green stain at the margins of standing water. Life here did not take that direction. The walls and foundations bear pale colonies instead: chalky mats spread in damp shade, crusts of sulfur-yellow filament on the undersides of bridges, dark velvet films around condensers. They are not quite moss, not quite fungus. They grow slowly over iron bolts and ceramic tile alike, eating warmth, moisture, and trace minerals. In certain districts they make the wet pavement subtly resilient underfoot, as if one is walking over a great, cool tongue.

The buildings are extensive but widely spaced, as though planned for a population that never came.

They are large industrial forms: cooling towers with their tops lost in vapor; low refineries of riveted steel and pale concrete; warehouses the size of civic monuments; apartment blocks elevated on narrow pylons above flood courts. Some are joined by enclosed pedestrian tubes, others by service bridges carrying heat pipes, cable bundles, and aqueducts. The infrastructure is dense overhead, braided across the sky, but at ground level there is room enough for empty squares, broad wet boulevards, and rail yards with only one or two cars standing in them.

Every structure shows its age. Concrete corners have rounded under years of condensation. Steel has become orange and black in elaborate vertical streaks. Stone facades, where they exist, are polished by mineral rain into deep, reflective planes. Yet the city is not abandoned. Its doors still cycle open. Its warning lamps still glow weakly through vapor. Pumps still throb beneath the streets. Somewhere within the central works, furnaces are maintained.

The heat comes upward.

It rises from grates in the pavement and from the cracks between old foundation slabs. It breathes out of enormous exhaust stacks in invisible waves that make the dim city bend and double. The basin floor is too warm to touch at noon, though noon cannot be recognized except by a marginal brightening of cloud. Water hisses in the gutters. The metal railings are unpleasantly hot. Inside the housing towers, vents exhale air smelling of wet limestone and hot dust.

Despite the heat, the city is quiet.

There is no ordinary traffic. No crowd noise rolls between the towers. Even the machines operate with a muted, submerged character, as if the vapor has thickened the air enough to damp every sound. A pump starts far away: a heavy, measured knocking. A cable trembles in the wind. Water spills through a roof gutter and disappears into a shaft. Sometimes an automated freight crane moves along a gantry, its wheels giving a long, plaintive squeal before silence closes again.

The few inhabitants have adapted to this quiet. They move in pairs or alone, wrapped in reflective capes against the heat and rain, their faces often hidden behind respirators not because the air is poisonous but because it is dense with mineral mist. Their lamps are small, hooded, pointed downward. From a distance they look less like citizens than like mobile omissions in the wet light. They do not speak outdoors unless necessary. Sound travels oddly in the basin; certain words, given voice near the central retaining wall, return hours later from another district, softened and altered.

There are rules, old ones, posted on enamel signs at junctions and elevator mouths.

**DO NOT FOLLOW A RUNOFF STREAM INTO A SERVICE LEVEL.**

**IF THE GROUND WARMS UNDER YOUR FEET, TURN EAST.**

**KEEP THE CONDENSATE GATES CLEAR.**

**AT THIRD SIREN, LEAVE ALL MIRROR COURTS.**

The mirror courts are plazas paved with black volcanic glass. During rain they become perfectly shallow lakes, reflecting the underside of the cloud ceiling and the skeletal frameworks above. They are beautiful in the dimness, almost like open doors cut into the ground. No one crosses them when the sirens sound. The reason is not fully explained. Beneath them lie heat exchangers, ancient reservoirs, perhaps deeper cavities in the old volcanic body. At times, bubbles rise through the water in enormous, patient clusters. At other times the reflections show lights that are not present overhead.

Fauna is scarce but not absent.

Small white things live in the warm drainage culverts: blind, segmented bodies with delicate translucent limbs, moving in groups through the steam. A broad-winged scavenger sometimes circles near the upper rim of the basin, using thermal currents rather than air in any ordinary sense. It has a dark, mineral-looking hide and a face too smooth to read. It never descends into the city, but the workers watch it when it appears. Its arrival usually precedes a pressure shift, and pressure shifts can bring rain hard enough to fill the lower streets.

At night—the true night, when the hidden sun has entirely withdrawn—the city becomes less empty. Windows awaken one by one in the residential towers. Boiler rooms shine amber behind wired glass. Signal lamps blink along pipe corridors in sequences too slow to feel mechanical. Steam accumulates in the open avenues, lying at knee height, then waist height, carrying the warmth of the ground.

The basin walls disappear. The towers appear to stand alone in a boundless dark, connected by suspended lines and wet avenues that go nowhere visible.

Then the city’s underground waters begin their work.

Gates open beneath plazas. Reservoirs equalize. Hot mineral water moves through buried channels toward the cooling districts, toward the factory cores, toward the far pass and whatever lies beyond it. The flow is almost inaudible, but the whole settlement responds: pipes contract, plates settle, windowpanes tremble in their frames. A person standing still can feel the pulse through the soles of their boots.

It is a functioning place, not a ruin. It has simply learned to function without abundance.

Nothing grows tall here. Nothing multiplies freely. The city does not expand. It persists: wet, hot, high above the surrounding flats, patiently collecting rain from an exhausted sky and passing it through its vast mineral body.
