The lowlands have no ground beneath their growth.

From a distance, the continent appears to be a single green machine laid flat beneath a white sky: an endless plane of leafwork, stalks, folded fronds, pale seed-vaults, and broad vascular membranes stretched taut between distant industrial towers. There is no soil. There is no visible stone. The entire surface, from the horizon to the horizon beyond it, is made of things that have been grown, assembled, welded into continuity, or have learned to perform both acts at once.

The vegetation is not merely planted upon the world. It is the world’s load-bearing material.

Thick trunks emerge in ranks from the low plain, but their bark is ribbed with ceramic insulation and steel-colored cambium. They rise only a few meters before dividing into lattices of branches, conduits, belts, and light-catching leaves. Their roots do not descend; they spread sideways through the constructed substrate, braiding around coolant lines, mineral feeders, signal cables, and the slow pistons that flex the continent. When a root finds a seam in the older structure, it secretes a dry amber resin, hardens it, and makes the seam disappear.

Everything is joined. The fields are roofs. The roofs are leaves. The leaves are solar vanes, each one furred on its underside with fine pale filaments that drink charge from the air. The factories are groves, their smokestacks disguised—or perhaps naturally grown—as hollow flowering stems. Their enormous blossoms open at noon, exposing banks of mirrored pollen-plates and rotating black machinery within. They do not emit smoke. There is nothing wet enough here to make steam, and very little that can burn. Instead, they exhale dry metallic dust in slow, almost graceful plumes. The dust settles on nearby foliage, is scraped away by specialized tendrils, and is fed back into the production organs below.

The sky is startlingly bright.

It has the pale, exhaustive brightness of a lamp held too close to the eye. There is no sun visible in it, only broad sheets of brilliance, as though the whole atmosphere were illuminated from above by another, larger world. Shadows exist, but they are faint green-blue stains under leaves and gantries. The light enters the veins of the vegetation and travels visibly through them in thin milky currents. At dawn—which is difficult to distinguish from the rest of the day—the currents brighten. At some later hour they dim. This is the closest the landscape comes to a day-night cycle.

The air is cool, clean in the way an emptied chamber is clean, and dry enough to chap exposed skin within minutes. There is no rain. No river has ever crossed these lowlands. No droplet clings to the leaves. Water is a legendary industrial substance here, remembered in warning symbols and preserved only in sealed archival cylinders beneath the oldest works.

The plants use other fluids.

Under the skin of the continent runs a circulation of cold oils, powdered salts suspended in electrostatic fields, liquid nitrogenous compounds, and a blue-white sap that behaves more like a current than a liquid. In cuts, the vegetation does not bleed; it produces a pale foaming crystal that grows over the wound in minutes. In deeper breaks, the surrounding tissue contracts, clamps down on damaged pipes, and sends a tremor through the local canopy. Far away, repair flowers unfold. Their petals are articulated sheets of alloy-veined cellulose, and they walk slowly on root bundles toward the injury.

There are roads, though no ordinary road surface remains.

Travel corridors run through the dense plant architecture as long, level clefts. Their floors are made of compressed fiber, black composite, and polished husk material. Along them glide low freight sleds without wheels, drawn by magnetic rails hidden under the surface. They move nearly silently. Each carries harvested leaves, dry seed-capsules, bundles of copper-colored roots, or sections of old machinery being returned to the nurseries for dismantling and reincorporation.

At intervals, vertical elevators rise from the plain: immense stalk-like shafts, each wrapped in spiraling foliage. They descend into the interior, where the actual depth of the continent begins. The surface is only the photosynthetic layer. Beneath it are factories of propagation, sorting caverns, mineral mills, seed foundries, and warm dark galleries where new species are manufactured according to the demands of weather, production, and structural strain.

The lowlands are old enough to have settled into a dense, practical beauty.

Nothing here is new, but nothing is ruined either. The forms have been weathered—not by water or wind, but by centuries of flexion, light exposure, particulate abrasion, root pressure, and continuous repair. The edges of metal plates have softened beneath generations of climbing vines. Old rivets have become seed nodes. Broken chimneys support clusters of silver leaves. A line of abandoned processing halls may be so thoroughly overgrown that it resembles a forest of immense black reeds, until one notices that each “reed” bears a serial number, stamped in letters taller than a person.

The continent makes its own weather, though weather is perhaps the wrong word.

Every few days, enormous sectors of leafwork turn their reflective faces upward in sequence. The resulting shift in brightness moves across the plain like a silent stormfront. Temperatures drop beneath the overturned leaves. Static accumulates in the air. Loose metallic pollen rises from vents and hangs in translucent sheets. Then, miles away, a field of tall filament trees discharges at once: not lightning exactly, but a branching exchange of light between their crowns and the bright ceiling of sky. The charge passes without thunder. For several minutes afterward, every vein in every leaf glows a saturated violet.

At these times, the machinery pauses.

Conveyor roots stop their slow hauling. Blossoms close over their turbines. The freight sleds wait in the road-clefts. Even the repair plants become still. The only movement is the gradual opening and closing of millions of leaves, each responding to a signal transmitted through the common substrate.

Silence dominates, but it is not emptiness.

There is a thin papery rasp where leaf edges touch. There is the occasional soft click of seed-shells opening. In the lower growth, one can hear the quiet tick of cooling metal, the faint elastic creak of trunks under load, and sometimes a distant, almost inaudible chord produced by wind passing through tuned ventilation reeds. The sound seems less heard than felt through the soles of the feet. It can travel for kilometers through the woven continent.

Fauna is scarce, and most of it may not be fauna in any old sense.

Small white things move in the canopy: six-legged maintenance creatures with bodies like folded orchid petals around a copper core. They are seen rarely, usually in pairs, opening clogged pores in the leaves with delicate hooked limbs. They have no obvious faces. When approached, they close themselves into inert buds and remain indistinguishable from the surrounding growth.

Higher up, gliding between the industrial flowers, are occasional sail-creatures made of translucent membrane and rigid black spars. They ride the electrical gradients in the air. Their shadows are more noticeable than their bodies: long geometric shapes passing over the layered foliage. No one has seen one land. It is possible they are autonomous survey devices. It is possible they are animals evolved—or designed—from old signal kites. The landscape offers no distinction between the two.

The greatest life here is collective.

A grove is a production district. A production district is an organ. An organ is part of the continental plant. The continental plant may be a city, and the city may be a single organism extending beneath the low horizon.

In some places, the construction becomes too immense to understand. Vast arches of braided trunk and girded metal rise from the plain, crossing distances that should require mountains. Their undersides are crowded with hanging gardens of machinery: rows of lamps, seed mills, dangling root arrays, and thousands of pale pods that pulse according to no visible rhythm. The arches do not seem to support anything. They may be remnants of a roof that once covered the whole world. They may be ribs. They may be bridges leading toward regions no longer reachable.

Beyond them, the vegetation changes color.

The green becomes gray, then blue-black, then almost white under the hard sky. There are forests of blade-leaves taller than factories, their surfaces stamped with repeating diagrams. There are plains of low red moss that generate electrical heat without flame. There are fields of translucent tubes in which dry spores circulate like weather. There are old zones where the constructed plant matter has become so densely layered that the surface rises and falls very slightly, as though something below is breathing.

The continent does not permit bare places for long.

If a freight sled derails and cuts a trench through the substrate, the wound becomes a nursery. Fibers appear along its edges. Shiny buds form in the exposed machinery. Within a week, the wreck has been screened by leaves. Within a year, its components have been disassembled, translated into botanical form, and made useful again. A broken rail becomes a root guide. A cracked engine block becomes a seed furnace. A human-shaped tool, if dropped, will eventually be enclosed in smooth bark and incorporated into the trunk of something that may live for centuries.

There are rules, although they are not written in any one place.

Nothing may remain separate.

Nothing may remain empty.

Nothing may remain still long enough to become dead.

The place is active at every scale: sap moving through a leaf, cargo drifting beneath a canopy, whole manufacturing forests slowly changing species to accommodate a shift in mineral supply. Yet this activity never becomes noise or haste. It proceeds with the calm density of a body repairing itself in sleep.

Standing on one of the low roads, surrounded by bright foliage and quiet industrial bloom, one has the strange impression that the horizon is close. Every direction is blocked by growth, by scaffolds, by layered trunks and elevated leaf roofs. But the maps—if they can be trusted—show that this living construction extends across a continent, perhaps farther. The same pale light falls on it all. The same dry circulatory systems pulse below it. The same silent factories flower and close.

And somewhere beneath the dense, cool surface, below the roots and engines and seed vaults, the original structure continues to make room for what it has become.
