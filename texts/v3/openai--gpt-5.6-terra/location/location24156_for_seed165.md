At this height the world has thinned without becoming simple.

The plateau lies above the weather that feeds the valleys. Its ground is not quite rock, not quite soil: broad shelves of dark slate, pale seams of frozen grit, and beds of compacted ash-colored dust whose surfaces are ribbed as if water once moved through them for centuries and then stopped in the middle of a thought. The erosion here is old and complete. Every ravine has found its depth. Every escarpment has settled into a profile that seems inevitable. The mountains around the plateau do not look newly broken or dramatic; they look worn into their final expressions, their slopes spread wide beneath mantles of stone.

There is little snow, strangely. The cold is too dry for it most of the time. Frost occurs instead in intimate places: in cracks beneath overhangs, on the north sides of standing stones, inside the mouths of abandoned shelters. It forms hard white fur along the edges of the land. In shallow basins there are black pools, no larger than courtyards, fed by a moisture that seems to collect from the air rather than arrive by rain. A dim drizzle may pass across the plateau only a few times in a year, but condensation is constant. At dawn—or what passes for dawn—the undersides of rock ledges shine with beads of water. Thin wet threads descend cliff faces, vanish into seams, and return elsewhere as slow dripping.

The vegetation has adapted to this restraint. There are no grasses in meadows, no forested slopes. Life occurs in low, deliberate patches: dark cushion plants wedged in stone; blue-gray lichens spreading across flat surfaces; brittle shrubs with silver leaves that lie close to the ground and smell faintly of iron when crushed. In protected depressions, mats of moss hold the moisture and glow an improbable green in the dimness. They are not abundant enough to soften the landscape. They merely prove that the plateau has not entirely refused life.

The open country is interrupted by structures that may be ruins, may be natural formations, or may have become both over time. Small circular walls rise from the ground here and there, each made from tightly stacked local stones. They have no doors. Their interiors are empty except for frost and windblown dust. Some have roofs of overlapping slabs; others are open to the sky. A few stand on isolated promontories where no sensible person would build, their walls facing not outward but inward, as though designed to contain a particular portion of silence.

No tool marks remain visible on them. The stones fit too well to have been casually piled, yet their joints have softened under centuries of weather until they resemble layers in a cliff. Lichen crosses from the ground onto their walls without interruption. Birds do not perch there often.

There are birds, though rarely. High above, a single pale shape may circle for hours without seeming to change position. Along the margins of the black pools, small long-legged insects walk on the surface even in cold weather, making no ripples. Sometimes a lean animal—foxlike, perhaps, but with a coat the color of slate—appears between boulders. It is usually seen at a distance. It pauses, looks directly toward the observer, and then is gone behind a stone that seems much too small to conceal it.

The greatest presence is sound.

The plateau is loud, not with movement but with permanence. Wind presses through the passes in a continuous low bellow, although the air near the ground may be perfectly still. Certain cracks in the escarpments sing in narrow, metallic tones. Water drips somewhere under the rock in a deep cavernous cadence, each drop amplified until it sounds like a hammer blow below the feet. When frost settles, the stone speaks: long, slow reports travel across the plateau, not sharp enough to be breaking, but heavy enough to make the ribs vibrate.

At times, a distant surf can be heard.

There is no sea for many days in any direction. No lake broad enough to make such a voice. Yet the sound arrives from below the western cliffs, rising through the ground in long inhalations and collapsing again. The local stone circles are all turned, in one way or another, toward that direction.

Light remains subdued even when the sky is clear. The sun, if it is the sun, hangs low and pale behind veils of high cloud. Shadows are weak and strangely delayed. A person can walk past a boulder and see their shadow remain on its former side for several breaths before it notices them leaving. In the black pools, the dim sky appears brighter than it does overhead, and occasionally a reflection shows a slope where there is only level ground.

Nothing in the plateau visibly grows. Nothing visibly decays. A patch of lichen at the edge of a path is identical each time it is passed. The same droplets gather beneath a ledge, swell, fall, and are replaced in the same order. A frozen trickle in one ravine has been caught halfway down a stone face for so long that the wet streak beneath it has become darker than the stone around it, but never extends.

This is not death. It is a kind of equilibrium held too perfectly.

The cold does not deepen toward night, because night never completely arrives. The wind does not carry storms in or clear skies away. Erosion has finished its work, yet the rocks still murmur as though grinding against one another far below. The few plants endure without flourishing. The few animals pass through without establishing territory. Even the scattered stone shelters seem less abandoned than paused.

Beyond the plateau, the country falls away in enormous gray steps toward valleys invisible under cloud. One can see far enough to understand that there are further ridges, further shelves, perhaps other dark pools and other roofless circles. But the distances do not behave reliably. A ridge that seems a morning’s walk away may remain on the horizon through three days of travel. A nearby shelter may take an hour to reach, though every step toward it feels ordinary.

The place permits passage, perhaps. It does not permit arrival.
