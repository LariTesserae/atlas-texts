The city is low to the ground and has never learned to rise.

It spreads across a shallow basin where rainwater gathers briefly, shines, and is taken back into the roots. There are no hills beyond it, only a broad, warm flatness wrapped in haze. The horizon is close because of the growth: green walls, green roofs, green towers, green streets. Everything built here has been built by plants, or has become plant before it could remain anything else.

At first the place resembles a ruined town swallowed by gardens. Then it becomes clear that the gardens are the town.

Houses stand in loose clusters, each one shaped from the fused buttresses of enormous trees. Their rooms are hollowed naturally inside living wood, their doors are curtains of hanging roots, their windows are pale membranes that open and close with the weather. Some structures look like old brickwork from a distance, but close up their blocks are packed loam, hard seed shells, and the mineralized husks of old stems. Vines have made arches between the buildings. Lattices of thornwood mark squares and lanes. Broad leaves overlap high overhead, collecting rain and releasing it in heavy drops days after a storm.

There are streets, though few are clear.

Most are narrow corridors pressed between walls of vegetation. The ground is a deep mat of soft green and brown: moss, crushed leaves, fungal fibers, fallen fruit, wet bark, and the delicate white roots of plants that do not mind being stepped on. In some lanes the substrate rises and falls faintly underfoot. It is not unstable. It is breathing, or something near enough to breathing that the distinction stops mattering.

The city is spacious in a strange way. It contains buildings, courts, terraces, bridges, alcoves, wells, and avenues, but no furniture, no carts, no tools, no signs of hands arranging objects. Each chamber is almost empty. A great round hall may contain only one pool of collected rain, black at its center and rimmed in red water-plants. A roofed arcade may stretch for fifty paces with nothing in it but hanging orchids and sleeping birds. Open plazas are broad bowls of grass with a single enormous trunk growing from their middles.

The absence makes every living thing seem deliberate.

Fauna fills the empty rooms.

Small animals pour through the greenery in continuous traffic: bright lizards with leaf-shaped tails, rodents that nest in the folded bases of palms, fat beetles clicking under bark, moths large enough to resemble drifting scraps of cloth. Long-legged birds stalk the water channels and disappear into fern shade. Monkeys—or something with monkey hands and faces too smooth to be mammalian—move along the upper branches, carrying fruit in solemn processions. There are snakes everywhere, but not hidden. They lie looped along warm roots and bridge rails, their patterned bodies as common as rope.

When rain comes, the animals change floors. The ground-dwellers climb. The canopy-dwellers descend. In minutes, the whole city reorganizes itself around water.

Water is never far, but it is rarely open. It lives in gutters made of curled leaves, in depressions between roots, in clay-lined basins under stairways. It threads beneath the living streets in slow brown runs. After a rain, shining channels appear unexpectedly between houses, carrying petals, insect wings, drowned seeds, and the occasional small animal floating upright and unharmed. By noon many of them have vanished. The roots drink them, or the ground closes over them, or the water simply continues downward into a place the city does not show.

The basin is old. Nothing is fresh except growth.

Roots have held its soils in place for generations. The exposed surfaces are smooth with weather, their edges softened by water and the rubbing passage of innumerable bodies. Any wall that begins to crack is quickly stitched with creepers. Any fallen branch becomes a bridge, a nursery, a roof, or a lane. There is no clean division between ruin and construction. The city continuously repairs itself by being eaten, rotted, rooted through, and grown over.

Its rules are botanical, though not peaceful.

A doorway may be open in the morning and sealed by noon because the vine above it has decided to flower. A bridge may lower itself gradually into a pond over the course of a week, not collapsing but bending with the intention of a stem leaning toward light. Whole houses turn, imperceptibly, toward the sun. The city’s courtyards migrate. Wells grow shallow or deep according to the moods of the trees around them. Once in a while a building splits down its middle and releases a cloud of seeds, revealing that it was not a building at all but the fruiting body of something much larger belowground.

No one lives here in the human sense, though the architecture insists that someone should.

There are thresholds at knee height and at shoulder height. There are sleeping niches, communal halls, raised platforms that seem made for gatherings. Some walls bear patterns of pressed leaves in repeating geometries: spirals, grids, branching maps. Whether these are decorations, memories, or simply the tracks of nutrients moving through tissue is impossible to say. In the oldest quarters, enormous roots form plazas under which the earth is hollow. Warm air rises from the cracks. It smells of rain, fruit fermenting, wet stone, and the sweet rot of entire seasons.

The sound is unbearable.

It begins as insects: a screaming electric chorus from every leaf and crevice. Then come frogs calling from hidden drains, birds shrieking from the canopy, wings beating against membrane windows, fruit dropping with dense wet impacts. Roots creak below the soil. Trunks groan as they shift their weight. Water ticks from thousands of surfaces. Somewhere in the city, a vast hollow plant periodically releases its stored air in a bellow that shakes loose dust, pollen, and sleeping creatures from the roofs.

There is no quiet even at night. Darkness changes the instruments but not the volume. The leaves clap in the warm wind. Beetles scrape their wings together like saws. Distant creatures give long, descending cries that seem to move through the walls instead of through the air. The city does not sleep; it only closes its flowers.

During ordinary daylight, the place is bright enough. Light falls in columns through the foliage, rests on wet leaves, and makes green reflections tremble across every interior. Yet the geometry of it is wrong. A sunbeam may enter one house and emerge from the wall of another two lanes away. Shadows occasionally point toward the sun. In some rooms the ceiling drips light rather than water: beads of pale gold gather on leaf tips, swell, fall soundlessly, and disappear before reaching the floor.

At the center of the settlement is a square that is not always there.

On some days it is a small open court, grass-covered and full of grazing animals. On others it is occupied by a tree whose crown cannot possibly fit beneath the surrounding canopy. Its trunk is white, smooth, and warm, with door-shaped seams in its bark. The city seems to orient itself around this tree even when it is absent. Lanes curve toward the space. Roots feed into it. Birds circle above it, though the sky is mostly obscured.

If one stands there long enough, the noise becomes less like noise.

It begins to resolve into a system: warning, invitation, feeding, shedding, mating, rain-sensing, root-contact, the steady internal labor of a living settlement maintaining its own impossible abundance. The city has no machines because it has no need of them. Every surface is active. Every building is alive. Every empty chamber is full of listening growth.

And the great low basin, held together by roots and rain, continues making room for whatever comes next.
