The town occupies a shallow basin no larger than a cathedral close, though it has the gravity of a capital. Its edge is perfectly legible: beyond the last houses, the ground changes at once from pale paving to raw mineral crust, as if the settlement has been pressed into a sheet of salt or ash before it fully cooled.

Nothing here has been eroded enough to seem old. The streets are sharp-sided cuts through a soft-looking white stone. Corners of walls meet in clean, almost painful angles. Stairways climb little rises that have no apparent purpose, stop at blank facades, descend again. The whole place feels newly uncovered—not built yesterday, exactly, but released yesterday from beneath a crust that had held it shut.

There is no water. No gutters, no wells, no cistern mouths. The inhabitants, if there are inhabitants, have no visible provision for rain. The air is dry enough that breath seems to disappear before it can become mist. Fine mineral powder lies in the joints of the paving, though no wind lifts it. It is cool, but not bracing; the cold is stored in the materials themselves. Walls retain it. Doorways exhale it. If one rested a hand against a low parapet, the stone would feel like the inside of a cave.

The buildings are urban in their arrangement, but not in their construction. They stand wall to wall in narrow rows, with little courts, market squares, covered passages, terraces, communal ovens, galleries, towers only two or three stories high. Yet no tool marks show anywhere. Each house appears to have grown into its allotted plot: slabs folded upward from the ground, windows opened as clean voids between layers, roofs overlapping like the plates of a pale fungus. Some walls are translucent in their thin places. In the dimness within, one can see faint seams advancing through the material, slow as the movement of ice.

The town is active.

This is its most unsettling quality.

At intervals, a wall swells outward by the breadth of a finger, then settles. A lane narrows over the course of an afternoon. A flight of steps gains one low additional stair during the night. Whole façades shed their outer crust in delicate, dry sheets, revealing a newer gray surface below. A doorway that led into a storeroom in the morning may, by dusk, open only onto solid mineral. No sound accompanies these changes. There is no groaning, cracking, or grinding. Stone shifts with the discretion of muscle beneath skin.

The street plan accommodates this. Open squares are left between districts like joints in a skeleton. Bridges are short, broad, and built in pairs, so that when one grows shut the other remains passable. Most roofs have shallow white flags planted along their ridges—not cloth, but strips of some fibrous, chalky plant. Their position tells residents which structures are still moving. A flag standing straight indicates a stable roof. A flag slowly curling down means the building beneath it is preparing to rearrange itself.

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. It favors the cracks where two materials meet: along the base of walls, at the edges of plazas, in abandoned thresholds. The plants are gray-green and stiff, each one a cluster of narrow blades coated in waxy dust. They do not seem to draw nourishment from the soil. Rather, they lean toward exposed stone, fastening pale roots directly to it. In certain alleys, mats of dry, black lichen creep up the walls in branching bands. They gather nothing visible from the air, yet thicken steadily on the shaded sides of buildings.

There are small trees in three courtyards, perhaps only six in the whole town. Their trunks are white and jointed, more like stacked bones than wood. Instead of leaves they bear thin, dark fans that open in the brief hours when the sky grows brightest. At all other times the fans are folded tight against their branches. Beneath them, the ground is strangely clean. No litter, no dead leaves, no droppings. The trees create a kind of dry shade so complete it seems materially denser than the surrounding air.

The light never develops into day. A pale lid of cloud, dust, or high mineral haze hangs over the basin. Behind it is a sun that can be guessed at but not seen: a diffuse coin of brightness drifting from one side of the sky to the other. The town’s upper surfaces take on a weak silver color; its lower passages remain blue-gray, cool, and nearly dark. At the end of what passes for evening, the windows in many buildings begin to shine faintly.

There are no lamps.

The glow comes from within the walls: a greenish, granular light running along the mineral seams. It brightens where a structure is about to alter. In a dead-end alley, the walls may glow on both sides, and by morning the alley will be gone, joined into a new chamber whose floor is still warm from its formation. People avoid these places, if people live here. One sees signs of them without seeing them directly: a set of stacked bowls in a recess, a braided screen across a doorway, a low cart with wheels of pale horn, tracks in dust that begin and end abruptly where the paving has changed.

Fauna is rarer still. Small creatures move only at the boundary of sight. There are pale, long-legged things that cling beneath balconies, their bodies flat as scraps of folded paper. They emerge to pick at the black lichen, then vanish into slits in the masonry. Sometimes a bird-shaped shadow crosses the dim sky, though it makes no wingbeat and leaves no call. In the central square, on the rim of a dry circular basin, there are usually three or four insects with mirrored backs. They face the same direction for hours, then turn together when the light shifts.

Silence is not simply the absence of noise here. It is a maintained condition.

No doors bang. No feet ring on the paving. The dry plants do not rustle. Even the occasional movement of the city’s substance occurs without announcement. Sound seems to be absorbed by the walls before it can travel. A spoken word would feel improper, too exposed; it would go nowhere, become small immediately, and fall at the speaker’s feet.

The only recurring disturbance is visual. Every so often a narrow tremor passes through the basin—not a quake felt in the body, but a coordinated adjustment of surfaces. Dust rises in thin vertical threads. The white trees flex their jointed trunks. Lines of greenish light run beneath streets and along roof edges. For several minutes the town looks less like a settlement than the exposed anatomy of some large, sleeping organism.

Then the lights withdraw.

The streets remain, mostly. The courtyards remain. The paths to the three white trees remain. But a threshold has moved, a stair has become a ramp, a familiar wall has opened onto a new square where no square stood before.

In this place, urban life is not the act of building permanence. It is the patient practice of learning what the ground has decided to become.
