The valley is under the world rather than upon it.

No sky is visible. There is a ceiling somewhere above the green—perhaps fifty meters, perhaps five hundred, depending on the hour and the mood of the roots—but it is not stone in any ordinary sense. It is a roof of braided trunks, pale mineral ribs, fungal-looking but dry, and immense leaves pressed flat against one another until they form a living vault. Light comes through it in a dusty gold dilution. The leaves do not drink rain; there is no rain here. Their veins are packed with amber resin and a slow, hot vapor that passes from root to stem to leaf and back again, endlessly circulated through the buried continent of plant flesh.

Every surface is alive.

The ground is not soil, though it yields slightly underfoot. It is a deep mat of root-knuckles, seed husks, crusted pollen, shed bark, chitin, and the fine, hot fibers of crawling green things. In some places the vegetation has grown into low hills; in others it has opened into pits lined with thick red fronds, each frond cupping dry white crystals. The crystals are food, salt, egg-shell, and building material all at once. Animals lick them. Plants extrude them. People, if they are people, quarry them with stone wedges.

There is almost no free water. Nothing gleams wetly. Nothing drips.

Instead, the valley is full of breath.

Warm exhalations travel through hollow stems as wind travels through a canyon. When a rootwood grove inhales, its trunks tighten with a long woody groan and the air rushes inward, carrying dust, spores, and insects toward the pores in its bark. When it exhales, the whole grove whistles. Thousands of these groves breathe out of rhythm, and the valley’s weather is a result of their disagreement: hot gusts, pollen squalls, sudden pockets of thin air, clouds of fragrant oils that blur the mind and make distant colors appear near.

The place is never quiet enough to hear oneself think.

There are grinding calls from animals with stone plates in their throats. There are shrieks of reed-hounds chasing through the undergrowth. There are enormous bellows from the grazing towers: six-legged beasts as tall as houses, with furred lower bodies and crowns of branching jawbone, chewing entire curtains of vine from the cavern walls. The vines protest in a sound like a sawmill. They do not passively endure being eaten. Their severed ends lash, sting, and send low vibrations through the root-mat, warning other vines farther down the valley.

Then the animals answer.

Every creature here seems to possess more limbs, mouths, wings, antennae, or opinions than necessary. Herds of bulb-backed runners flow through the vegetation like poured seed. Their translucent flanks flash with stored light. They crop the tender tips of walking ferns, while striped burrow-lizards ride their backs and eat the parasites nesting in their ear folds. Above, flightless moths with sail-sized wings clamber between trunks, rattling their dry scales. In the dark seams beneath the root-mat, something vast turns over in its sleep; each movement raises the valley floor by a finger’s breadth and sends whole colonies of pale beetles bursting from their tunnels.

The fauna is not merely abundant. It is the valley’s chief weather.

Migrations sweep across it several times a day. A stampede can bend a grove flat. A mating swarm can darken the gold light. Predators travel in wheeling, screaming packs, not always hunting—sometimes herding prey away from dangerous plants, sometimes driving them deliberately into the mouths of those plants, sometimes simply joining the wider panic because stillness is suspicious here.

At the center of the valley, a new ridge is being born.

It has not yet hardened into rock. It is a swollen seam in the floor, a long raw upthrust of mineral foam and pale, unfinished wood. The valley’s plants have recognized it as an opportunity. They crowd its sides in dense bright bands, driving roots into its warm cracks before the mineral can cool. Soft columns rise, inflate, split, and reveal spirals of seed or white stone. The ridge trembles with pressure below. Every few minutes, a crack opens along its length with a report like thunder, and a plume of cinnamon-colored dust erupts into the air.

The animals love it.

Small digging things immediately descend on the fresh material, carving tunnels with hooked beaks. Broad-faced grazers scrape the mineral froth away in sheets. Blind, long-fingered creatures emerge from older cracks and carry hot fragments away to line nests hidden among the roots. By evening, crude scaffolds of branch and bone may appear along the ridge: the work of the valley’s tool-users.

They live scattered through the undergrowth, in shelters that seem at first to be natural knots of vegetation. A closer look reveals a doorway, a woven reed gate, a ring of sharpened stakes, a ladder of tied bones. Their houses are grown as much as made. A young trunk is bent and bound; vines are trained over it; plates of bark are stitched into the gaps with thorn and tendon. Some dwellings stand high among the breathing groves, safe from ground swarms. Others are sunk into the warmer root-mat, entered through narrow tunnels lined with polished shells.

Their tools are simple: chipped black glass, teeth set into wooden handles, stone mauls, needles of thorn, fire-hardened digging spades. Fire itself is rare and treated with caution. Open flame consumes the valley’s oils too eagerly. A cooking pit can become a firestorm; a dropped spark can send a whole wall of dry fern racing upward in orange sheets. Most heat comes from the breathing plants, from mineral vents, from fermentation chambers made inside hollow gourds.

There are rules, though no one has written them.

Do not cut a breathing trunk while it is drawing air; it may take your hand inward with the dust.

Do not sleep under blue-veined leaves; they close at night.

When the floor begins to hum, get off the floor.

Never follow a herd into a newly opened crack, no matter what it is fleeing.

And if all the animals suddenly go silent—if the moths fold their wings, the runners freeze, the reed-hounds press themselves flat, and even the groves seem to hold their immense wooden breath—then find a root thicker than a person and cling to it.

Something beneath the valley is moving.

This happens often enough that the inhabitants have made a kind of ritual of it. At the first silence, they drive their animals into thorn corrals, lash themselves to living supports, cover their ears against the oncoming roar. The roots tighten. The half-born ridge splits wider. Dust rises from every seam. Then the buried organism—or buried machine, or buried ancient forest trying to turn over in the dark—rolls its vast body beneath the land.

The valley shifts.

A hill becomes a trench. A nest opens into the air. A grove leans and roots itself again. One settlement may wake to find its paths overturned and its nearest neighbors separated by a field of newly erupted crystal stalks. By the next day, vines have begun to cross the gap. Animals have made tracks. The tool-users have laid bone bridges and marked the safe passages with dangling strings of teeth.

Nothing erodes much here, because nothing is allowed to remain dead long enough to wear away. Stone is swallowed by roots. Fallen structures become scaffolds for leaves. A carcass is covered in beetles, then sprouts, then new mouths come to feed on what grows from it. The world is young in a continual way: always making raw terrain, always filling it immediately, always too crowded with hunger and growth to become finished.

And still, in the brief intervals between migrations and breath-storms, it can be beautiful.

The gold light settles through the canopy. Warm dust drifts over the root plains. Vast grazers move in silhouette beyond curtains of vine. A cluster of pale shelters clings to a trunk, their little bone ladders swaying. Somewhere far down the valley, a new ridge cracks open with a red-white glow, and every living thing nearby turns toward it—not in fear exactly, but in recognition.

The land is making another place for them to live.
