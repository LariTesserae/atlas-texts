The country lies beneath a country that has not yet learned it is hollow.

Its ceiling is not a ceiling in the ordinary sense. It is a vast, broken underside of stone, lifted high overhead in shelves and domes, with roots of raw mineral hanging from it: white salt teeth, black basalt ropes, translucent curtains of newly formed crystal. Here and there the roof has collapsed in shafts broad enough to admit daylight. The light falls through them in slanting columns, ordinary yellow-white daylight, full of dust and wingbeats. It reaches the cavern floor in separate territories: bright basins, pale terraces, strips of glare along wet stone. Between them are distances of warm shadow.

The land is below the surface, but it is not enclosed. It extends for miles through connected chambers and fault-valleys, underplains and shallow stone seas. One can stand on a ridge of freshly heaved claystone and see, beyond a swarm of moving bodies, another ridge, and beyond that the pale opening of a collapse-bowl where sunlight enters. The horizons are low and irregular, continually interrupted by columns, cliffs, heaps of rubble, and the bulging walls of chambers still in the process of becoming.

Nothing here feels finished.

The ground is young. It swells, wrinkles, settles. Fine seams appear in a floor that was smooth an hour ago; warm vapor leaks out; a ridge rises in granular folds and then, with a muffled internal crack, slumps into a shallow trench. Clay-colored deposits ooze from fissures and harden in lobed forms like poured wax. Mineral crust grows over them almost visibly, delicate at first, then thick enough to break under a hoof or claw. Water does not run in rivers for long. It gathers in sudden slick pools after rain drains down from the world above, or condenses in the high vaults and falls, drop by fat drop, from stone lips. The pools are ringed with salt and churned quickly to mud by the animals.

There are no plants.

Not even moss has taken hold. The damp places gleam bare. The walls are bare. The slopes are bare, except for stains of iron, sulfur, copper-green mineral bloom, and occasional pale films that might be fungus or might merely be the earth curing itself. The absence of greenery makes every living motion conspicuous. Fauna provides the color, the texture, the continual disturbance that roots and leaves might have offered elsewhere.

They are everywhere.

Small pale runners move in dense streams along cracks in the floor, each no longer than a finger, many-legged and soft-bodied, their backs armored with grains of mineral. They flow around stones, divide and rejoin, vanish down openings in the ground. Herds of larger creatures cross the open terraces: blind, warm-furred things with long forelimbs adapted for climbing and digging, their heads shielded by plates of bone. Some bear lantern-like sacs under their throats, not bright enough to illuminate the cavern but sufficient to tint their faces blue or green in darkness. Their calls are deep and percussive, as if someone were striking wet drums far away.

Above them, the air is crowded.

Winged animals circle beneath the skylights and among the pillars: leathery, sparrow-sized forms that catch falling insects; broad membrane-winged gliders that sail from high ledge to high ledge; clouds of tiny silver moths which do not seek light so much as heat. The shafts from the surface fill with them. At noon, their movement makes the daylight seem to ripple. When one shaft darkens under a passing surface cloud, the creatures beneath it change direction all at once, like filings responding to a magnet.

Predators wait at the margins of the bright places. Some resemble nothing known aboveground: low bodies on too many jointed legs, skins faceted like dull gemstones, jaws that open sideways. They do not stalk in silence. Almost nothing in this place is silent. They click, scrape, hiss through mineral-lined throats. The prey animals answer with bellowing alarm calls. The roof drips. Stones shift. Wings beat in sheets. Every chamber carries the noise of the next one through cracks and galleries, so that the land seems inhabited even where no creature can be seen.

The loudest sound is the earth making itself.

There are rumbles from below, brief rolling thunders that travel through soles and ribs. There are pops like musket fire where cooling rock fractures. There are long grating noises from a wall advancing imperceptibly against another wall. Every now and then an entire slope collapses in a white rush of dust, sending animals scattering across the floor. Within minutes the smaller scavengers arrive, not to feed on the fallen stone but to occupy the new crevices before anyone else can claim them.

The creatures obey rules that may be older than the chamber, or may have emerged only because the chamber demands them. Nothing drinks from still water until the first animal has stamped the pool’s surface three times. No herd crosses a fresh mineral seam without waiting for the smallest members to go first. At dusk—if dusk can be said to occur underground, when the shafts overhead turn blue and then gray—the winged animals descend toward the warm fissures, while the digging beasts gather beneath the highest ceilings. They seem to know which parts of the cavern will hold and which will fail.

Human traces occur sparsely, almost shyly, in this abundance.

Near some of the shafts stand little shelters made from stacked slabs and hide stretched over bent bone. Their construction is blunt but careful: low entrances, smoke holes, drainage grooves scratched into the ground with stone tools. The inhabitants—if they remain—have learned not to build into the walls. Instead they make freestanding circles on stable shelves, anchoring them with heavy rocks. There are fire pits lined with fired clay, racks for drying strips of pale meat, bowls carved from soft stone, and heaps of discarded shells from the burrowing animals.

The fires burn poorly in the damp air. Their smoke clings to the roof of each shelter, escaping only slowly. A settlement can be recognized from far away by its brown, persistent haze drifting beneath the cavern vault.

Sometimes a path appears: not a road, but a repeated arrangement of footprints, dragged branches, and hand-stacked cairns. It leads from one daylight shaft to another, or from a habitation shelf down to a water basin. The cairns are important. In this world, the ground changes too quickly for memory alone. A cairn says: this place held yesterday. This rock was solid. This crossing did not open.

But a cairn can also mean: do not step here.

The people use simple tools—stone mauls, sharpened bone wedges, flint scrapers, woven carrying slings—but their knowledge is more sophisticated than their tools reveal. They can hear instability in the walls. They listen with cheeks pressed to the ground. They judge the taste of condensed water for signs of deep mineral heat. They know which animal calls mean a collapse is coming, which mean a rainstorm has begun on the surface, and which mean that something has entered from a newly opened passage.

At times the upper world sends weather down.

Rain comes through the shafts in silver ropes, hard enough to strike the floors and raise a mist. It turns dust into red-brown slurry, fills low places, and sends whole populations of small creatures upward onto stones and shelters. For an hour or two, the cavern resembles an inverted storm valley: waterfalls descending from the sky, channels appearing where none existed, animals packed shoulder to shoulder beneath overhangs. Then the rain weakens. Water drains through new cracks or sinks into hungry sediment. The temporary streams disappear, leaving polished channels that will be erased by the next movement of the earth.

Warmth persists. The air is humid near the fissures, almost breath-like, smelling of wet limestone, animal musk, smoke, and hot metal. In the brighter areas it can feel close and summery; in deeper passages it grows heavy rather than cold. Steam occasionally rises from pools in thin transparent threads. It catches the light and makes the animals passing through it seem briefly insubstantial.

The landscape is not a ruin, though it contains ruins. It is not wilderness, though no plant has claimed it. It is a living excavation, a basin under construction, crowded with creatures that have rushed in before the stone has decided what shape it wants to keep.

Everywhere, the land is opening.

A crack becomes a trench. A trench becomes a steaming corridor. A ceiling falls and admits a new square of sun. Animals gather at its edge by the hundreds, dazzled and crying, while the people below raise their hands against the brightness and begin, immediately, to build a cairn.
