The plateau begins above the last weather.

For three days of ascent the ground rises through belts of dark, wet stone, through cloud levels that cling in torn bands to the slopes, through scattered stands of bent green growth whose leaves are narrow and waxed against the cold. Then the mountain stops behaving like a mountain. Its summit does not narrow into a peak. It opens.

It is a country spread across the roof of the world: a plain so vast that its far side has its own color of sky.

From above, if there were anything alive enough to look, it might resemble an old sea that had hardened in place. Ridges lie beneath the thin soil like the backs of immense sleeping creatures; their surfaces have been planed down by ages of ice, wind, and slow mineral rain. Valleys are broad and shallow, not carved so much as remembered. Everything has been worn toward an ancient balance. Cliffs exist only where the land has recently, and reluctantly, failed to keep its old promises.

The rock is pale grey-green, dense and fine-grained, veined by dark seams that shine after rain. In some places it breaks into stacked, almost architectural slabs. In others it has dissolved into a black powder that gathers in hollows and supports the plateau’s vegetation: low forests of reed-trunks, cushions of vivid moss, fans of silver-green lichen spreading over stone like frost that has learned to feed.

There are no beasts in this place. No birds ride the air. No insects stir in the moss. The plants make the only living motions: stems turning by fractions toward light, pale tendrils withdrawing into their own shadow, broad leaf-fields shivering when a weather front passes overhead. Some of the taller growths open and close their leaf-blades with a slow mechanical patience, gathering condensation from the air and directing it down into their roots. At dawn, whole miles of them may fold at once. It looks like the surface of the world taking a breath.

Water is never abundant, but it is everywhere if one knows the forms it has chosen.

It beads beneath rock overhangs. It streams in hair-thin threads down walls that seem dry from a distance. It gathers in long, shallow basins where the surface freezes clear as glass each night and clouds over by noon. Rain arrives in brief migrations from the lower sky: fine, cold rain that falls almost horizontally and vanishes after an hour, leaving every leaf bright and every paving stone dark. In certain depressions, old channels retain water beneath mats of translucent vegetation. A traveler can hear it moving below the green surface, a careful subterranean sound, as if the plateau has blood but does not wish to display it.

Across this immense upland stand cities.

They do not crowd the land. Each is separated from the next by distances measured in weather. A smoke column seen at the horizon may belong to a settlement ten days away, or to one that has already been abandoned for a century. The cities are low and wide, assembled from the materials nearest at hand: fitted slabs, turf-packed walls, lattices of pale wood, black stone drains, roofs layered with hide, resin cloth, and sheets of hammered soft metal. Their streets follow the natural grain of the plateau rather than oppose it. Where a ridge becomes a road, a road becomes a wall; where an old meltwater channel crosses a district, the channel is roofed over and turned into a market walk.

There are towers, but none are very tall. The wind does not permit vanity here.

Most buildings are partly sunk into the ground, banked against cold and built around shared hearths. Outside the walls are terraces where people cultivate thick medicinal mosses, root bulbs, blue-stemmed greens, and strange pale fungi that grow from arranged stones. There are reservoirs lined with clay and stone, condensation fields made of upright ribs of slate, and miles of narrow catch-ditches designed to persuade rainwater not to leave too quickly.

The people use simple things with extraordinary care. Bone wedges, stone mauls, braided cord, wooden sledges, clay vessels, copper knives worn thin through inheritance. Their cities have no bright machinery, no engines that overpower the air. Yet their construction is precise. Whole neighborhoods are held together by balanced weights and dry-fitted joins. Gates swing on stone sockets polished by generations of hands. A bridge may be built from nothing more than lashed trunks and shaped slabs, but it spans a gorge that seems older than language.

The plateau’s oldest rule is that nothing stays exactly where it is.

This is not obvious at first. The roads appear solid, the cities rooted, the valleys settled into their final forms. But by evening, a distant ridge may have shifted several body-lengths from where it stood at noon. Not violently. Never with the drama of an earthquake. It simply revises itself. Stone folds, accepts a new contour, and falls quiet again.

On some mornings, houses open their doors onto a different street.

The inhabitants account for this in their plans. Their walls are built in sections. Their plazas are marked with movable stones rather than permanent monuments. Every district maintains watchers whose task is to compare the horizon to old measurements: the notch in the western escarpment, the angle of a far white peak, the position of the dark fracture that runs across the southern plain. When the land has altered too much, the city moves with it. Foundations are dismantled, hearthstones lifted, roof frames unlashed, and the settlement walks slowly across the plateau to a more favorable arrangement of ground.

There are older cities that did not move in time.

They lie half-buried in grasses or exposed along slopes where the earth has drifted away. Their streets sometimes continue beneath bare rock, visible through the stone as dim lines and arches, as though the plateau has incorporated them without crushing them. In some ruins, rain falls upward through roofless rooms. In others, shadows arrive before the objects that cast them. A person can stand at the threshold of an abandoned house and hear, faintly, the sounds of its former occupation—not voices, exactly, but the click of tools, the scrape of bowls, the repeated soft tread of someone crossing a floor that no longer exists.

No one travels alone beyond sight of a settlement bell.

The bells are cast from different metals and stones, each with a particular tone. They are not rung often. Sound travels strangely across the high plain, sometimes dying after a few paces, sometimes returning hours later from a sky that has no visible clouds. Most days are nearly silent: wind brushing reed-leaves, water ticking from roof edges, the soft granular settling of old ground. A distant axe stroke can be heard across a valley. So can the sudden crack of a frozen basin opening at night.

When the bells do sound, their notes pass from city to city over astonishing distances. Not always through the air. Sometimes a bell in one town is answered by another beyond the horizon, though there is no interval between the calls. The people say the stone hears first.

The sky is ordinary in its brightness, blue-grey by day, white around the sun, immense at night. But it occasionally loses its depth. Clouds flatten against it like painted shapes. The sun may appear to set in the north and rise again from a fissure in the ground. Once or twice each cold season, the stars descend until they seem caught among the distant ridges, shining from within the moss and the open windows of deserted towers.

These events are not celebrated. Nor are they entirely feared. They are entered into municipal records beside rainfall, road repairs, births, crop yields, and the direction of ground movement.

On the far eastern reaches of the plateau, where the cities grow fewer and the vegetation thins into silver mats, there is a feature called the Still Basin. It is nearly circular, hundreds of miles across, surrounded by ridges that all lean inward as if listening. Nothing grows at its center. No water gathers there, though rain falls over it as elsewhere. The basin floor is smooth black stone, unmarked by dust, frost, or time.

At noon it reflects the sky.

At night it reflects a different country: forests of immense white trunks, rivers suspended in air, distant urban lights arranged in constellations no human map contains. Sometimes people at the basin’s edge glimpse figures moving in that reflected place. Sometimes the figures appear to glimpse them in return.

The cities nearest the basin have built no walls facing it. Their houses turn their blank backs toward the black expanse. Their roads curve away before reaching the shore. Yet each year, in the season of cold rain, delegations walk out onto the stone carrying bundles of seeds, broken tools, fragments of old maps, and the names of those who have died. They set these things down in careful rows. By morning, everything is gone.

In return, occasionally, the basin gives something back: a stone that is warm despite the cold; a length of cord knotted in an unknown fashion; a leaf from a plant that has never grown on the plateau; a child’s wooden carving, wet with rain from a world where rain falls upward.

Still, life persists here—not animal life, not a bright crowded abundance, but the stubborn vegetal and human kind. It gathers in sheltered hollows, along seep-lines, around old walls that have become windbreaks. Green presses from cracks in the most ancient pavement. Condensation collects in patient vessels. Fires burn low in communal halls. People repair their roofs, move their boundary stones, tend the moss gardens, and watch the horizon change.

The plateau is cold, old, and sparsely inhabited. It has been eroding for longer than its cities can remember, and it may be only halfway through becoming whatever it is meant to be.

Yet at the end of a rain, when the clouds split and normal sunlight runs across the wet stone, every ridge flashes pale green, every shallow pool catches the sky, and the distant cities appear for a moment as dark, deliberate marks upon an immeasurable living land.
