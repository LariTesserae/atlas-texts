Below the salt-hard skin of an unnamed basin is a chamber called the Stillworks.

It is not large. A person can walk its length in half an hour, if they know which passages are real and which are merely seams in the rock. The ceiling is low in places and cathedral-high in others, but the whole place has boundaries: a buried factory-room, a mineral hollow, an engine bay enclosed by stone. The walls are fresh-broken and angular, as though the chamber was opened yesterday by a careful explosion. Their exposed faces are pale gypsum, black basalt, amber sulfur, green copper salts. Nothing has had time to soften. There are no drips, no stains of water, no river-polished margins. Every edge keeps its tooth.

The air is warm and dry enough to rasp the throat. It smells of hot metal, salt dust, animal musk, and the dry peppery scent of things growing without soil. Fine mineral powder hangs in it, caught in the weak amber lamps strung along old cable runs. These lamps do not flicker. Neither do the motors beneath them. Nothing in the Stillworks flickers, swells, decays, gathers speed, or slows down. The place has found a balance and been trapped inside it.

At the center lies the machinery: not a factory exactly, though it has the manners of one. Three squat iron compressors sit on plinths of poured slag. Broad belts turn between them with a constant leathery slap. Piston rods vanish into ceramic collars in the floor, working some buried mechanism deeper than the chamber. A flywheel taller than a cart rotates behind a wire guard, black with old grease yet untouched by dust. Heat comes from the machines, but no smoke leaves them. Their exhaust is ducted into cracked stone throats and swallowed by the earth.

The sound is immense for such a confined place.

There is the round, repeated concussion of pistons; the tooth-on-tooth complaint of gears; the dense electrical hum of old transformers; the clatter of loose chain against a casing. Somewhere behind the walls, a pressure valve gives one sharp bark every eleven seconds. It has done so for as long as anyone can remember. Over all of it runs a continuous dry chittering, like innumerable fingernails drawn across fired clay.

That chittering belongs to the animals.

They occupy every practical surface. There are pale, many-legged furnace crickets in the cable trenches, each one as long as a hand and plated in dull translucent scales. They feed on insulation fungus and the mineral crust that forms around warm vents. There are blind burrowing doves—though nothing about them resembles a dove except their soft, throbbing coo—nesting in the hollow machine housings. Their feathers are reduced to gray felt around the head and joints; their bodies are mostly warm skin and folded membrane. They never fly. They climb by pressing suction pads to the stone.

The most numerous are the wheel-hounds.

They are low animals, no larger than foxes, with six limbs and a ring of hard black plates around their middle. When frightened, or when crossing the open floor beneath the compressors, they curl into wheels and roll along the factory’s fixed paths. Their plates strike the ground in rapid percussion: tak-tak-tak-tak-tak. Hundreds move through the Stillworks in orderly loops. One line circles the central flywheel. One passes through a narrow basalt slot near the eastern wall. One enters a pipe large enough to swallow a man and does not emerge until the same animals return from another opening forty minutes later, carrying pale dust in the folds of their bodies.

They are not migrating. Nothing here migrates. Their routes are circuits, as stable as the belts on the machines.

Sparse vegetation persists where warmth, dust, and leaked current meet. Bristle fans sprout from cracks in the floor: stiff, dark-red fronds with coppery ribs. They do not bend. Each has leaves like tiny saw blades, and each leaf is edged with a dry luminous seam. The plants feed on heat and electrochemical seepage. At night—or what would be night, if the Stillworks possessed a sky—their seams glow a little stronger. But the lamps never change, so this is a distinction only the animals seem able to perceive.

Near the old maintenance platforms grow clusters of bell-moss, a gray, fibrous organism that gathers static charge across its hairs. When a wheel-hound brushes it, the moss emits a small blue crack and a sound like a struck glass. The animals deliberately pass through it. Their plates carry the charge away, and for a few moments their rolling bodies spark in the dust.

Scattered through the chamber are signs that people once worked here.

A steel stool stands beside a control cabinet, its seat worn into a shallow dip. A thermos, sealed and empty, has fused to a shelf under a layer of mineral bloom. There are faded yellow hazard lines painted on the floor, though they now pass beneath nests, roots, and wheel-hound tracks. In one alcove, a locker hangs open. Inside is a single glove, stiff with salts, and a paper calendar whose pages no longer turn. The last marked date is not especially old, but the ink has faded beyond reading.

The rules of the Stillworks are learned quickly.

Do not interrupt a wheel-hound route. They will not avoid you; their course was established before you entered and will remain after your bones have dried into the dust. Stand in the gaps between the lines, marked by little piles of shed black plates.

Do not touch the warm copper walls. The veins beneath them carry a low current, enough to lock the muscles of a hand closed until a furnace cricket comes to investigate.

Do not enter the pipe behind Compressor Two. It is not blocked, and it is not dangerous in the usual way. It simply returns every traveler to its mouth after forty minutes, facing the direction from which they came, with more dust on their clothes than they should have acquired.

And never, under any circumstance, silence the machines.

No one is sure whether the machines sustain the animals, the plants, the lamps, or the peculiar stillness of the place. Perhaps they pump some dry gas through the bedrock. Perhaps they grind time into a usable form. Their gauges hold steady: needle positions fixed, pressure numbers unchanged, oil levels perpetually halfway between marks. A dropped bolt lands and remains where it falls. A footprint persists in dust, but somehow never becomes part of a trail; later visitors always find their own prints beside it, equally fresh.

The chamber is full, warm, loud, and almost motionless in its deeper nature. Belts revolve. creatures circle. fans hum. sparks jump. Yet nothing progresses. No nest grows larger. No plant produces a new frond. No animal seems younger or older than another. The machinery does not wear down.

At the far end of the Stillworks, beyond a field of sharp white mineral spires, there is a small steel door set into naked stone. It has no handle on this side. Above it, a dim red indicator remains illuminated.

The label beneath it reads:

**SURFACE ACCESS — TEMPORARILY CLOSED**

The dust before the door is perfectly undisturbed, except for the tracks of wheel-hounds passing across it forever.
