The plateau is warm even in the morning.

It rises above the weather rather than above the world: a broad, old table of stone, its edges broken into deep ravines and pale gullies, its surface so long exposed that nothing sharp remains. Every ridge has been rounded. Every cliff has been combed by wind, water, heat, and centuries of small feet. The land does not look ruined. It looks finished.

From a distance it seems bare: ocher shelves, gray-green flats, seams of dark basalt, and wide fields of powdery red soil. But up close the ground is crowded. Not with plants—there are few of those—but with lives.

The vegetation keeps low and stubborn. Tufts of silver grass grow in cracks where night moisture gathers. There are thornless, leafless shrubs like bundles of green fingers, standing a handspan high and storing water in their translucent skins. In the deeper hollows, broad mats of blue-black lichen cling to stone, softening it only where condensation reliably forms before dawn. A few ancient trees survive beside the rare permanent pools: low-crowned, thick-barked things with roots splayed across the rock as though holding it down.

Water is never quite absent, but it is seldom visible. It arrives in thin ways. A dark line beneath a ledge. A coolness on the underside of a stone. A brief sheen on the mouths of burrows at sunrise. Once every few weeks, clouds gather below the plateau’s lip and climb upward in ragged white folds. Rain then falls in a short, warm veil, not enough to run far, but enough to awaken every depression and old channel at once. The rock darkens. The air smells of iron, dust, and the sharp mineral scent of things opening.

There are basins everywhere, relics of vanished rivers: smooth, shallow bowls in the bedrock, some wide enough to hold a village, some no larger than a footprint. Most are dry. In the wet season they become temporary mirrors, and the plateau gains a scattered sky beneath its sky.

The animals know each basin.

They are the true density of the place. Herds of small, long-legged grazers drift over the flats, nibbling lichens and the tender tips of the finger-shrubs. Their hides are the color of the land, but their ears are oversized and thin, veined with pink; in the heat they hold them open like sails. Behind them come low-bodied scavengers with jointed necks and black, reflective eyes. They move in patient companies, never hurrying, always seeming to have already known where a death will occur.

Along the cliff roads travel great plated walkers, each the size of a small house. Their shells are not hard in the manner of beetle shell, but layered like old shale, furred with moss in the damp months and whitened by dust in the dry. They feed by pressing broad mouths to stone and drawing out the invisible films of mineral-loving life. A procession of them may take three days to pass a single outcrop. Smaller creatures live in the creases of their plates: pale moths, tiny lizards, mites bright as beads of copper. When one of the great walkers pauses, it is difficult to tell whether it has stopped or whether the entire landscape has simply begun moving around it.

The sky holds more animals than clouds. Broad-winged gliders circle on warm air, nearly silent, their wings patterned underneath with false eyes that shimmer when they tilt. Flocks of pebble-sized birds spill from crevices at dusk and settle again before full dark. Their wings make no louder sound than someone turning pages in another room.

There are insects, though they do not buzz. They click their wing cases together at frequencies below hearing, and one feels their gatherings in the soles of the feet: a faint, regular vibration in the ground. At certain hours, the stone itself seems to hum. The locals—if any people once called this place home—would have said the plateau was thinking.

There are signs of habitation, scattered but unmistakable.

On ridges above the dry basins stand little towers of fitted stone, waist-high to a person, with narrow circular openings facing east. No mortar holds them. They have endured because they were built the way nests are built: each stone resting precisely in the patience of the others. Some are occupied by nesting gliders. Some have become dens for foxlike animals with translucent whiskers. One may find inside a tower a ring of polished bones, or a clutch of eggs, or a shallow pool of rainwater perfectly clear beneath a roofless sky.

Elsewhere, thin walls run for a few yards and stop. Steps descend into nothing. Low enclosures have been made from upright slabs, perhaps for shelter, perhaps for ceremony, perhaps simply to catch windblown seeds. Nothing mechanical remains, no blade or vessel or wheel. The structures feel less like artifacts than extensions of the terrain: deliberate arrangements of the same stone that surrounds them.

In the warm afternoons the light is ordinary and exacting. It reveals every old ripple in the rock, every trail braided through dust, every pale scar left by water long gone. Heat rises from the flats, but the high air remains clean. At the edges of ravines, the wind carries smells upward from shaded depths: damp clay, hidden flowers, nests, the sweet rot of fallen fruit from trees that cannot be seen.

The ravines are where the plateau changes character. Their walls are dense with holes, galleries, folded shelves, and ledges. Whole animal communities live there in vertical layers. Above, the gliders perch. In the middle shadows, colonies of round-bodied creatures cling to the walls with soft pads and feed on mineral seepage. At the bottom, where water sometimes lingers beneath gravel, pale amphibians move among roots and stone. The air in these cuts is several degrees cooler. It is also almost completely still.

Sound never gathers into noise. There are calls, but they are infrequent and low. A distant throat-note from an unseen herd. The dry scrape of shell against rock. The single knock of a stone displaced by a burrowing animal. At night, faint wet chirrs rise from the basins that have water, though no one can ever locate the creatures making them.

The strangest rule of the plateau is that its inhabitants do not seem to waste motion.

Predators wait for hours, perhaps days, with their eyes half-lidded in shade. Grazing animals move only from one known patch of nourishment to another. Even the birds circle in slow geometries, widening and narrowing their paths as though following lines drawn in the air. When rain comes, the land becomes briefly hectic—not loud, but intent. Animals emerge from cracks, depressions fill, pale flowers unfold and close within a single day. Then the water sinks, the flowers turn to paper, and the plateau resumes its long composure.

Nothing here appears young except the rain.

At sunset, the old stone gives back the day’s heat. The plateau glows reddish, then violet, then almost blue. Herds gather around the dark mouths of basins. The great walkers settle their weight against wind-carved walls. Above them, the first stars appear with an unusual clarity, as if the high air has been emptied and polished.

In that hour the scattered towers cast long thin shadows across the ground. The shadows touch, separate, and touch again as the light fades. For a moment they resemble a map of roads across an extinct country.

Then night comes gently, full of breathing, hidden feet, slow wings, and the almost inaudible work of water condensing on ancient stone.
