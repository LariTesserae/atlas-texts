There is a hollow beneath the ordinary ground, but it is not a cave in the expected sense. No rock ceiling arches above it. The ceiling is a dense, pale tangle of roots, stems, and translucent leaf-veins, pressed together so completely that they have become a kind of sky. Sunlight passes down through them anyway. It arrives green-gold and steady, not in beams but as a gentle fact of the air. Somewhere above, perhaps only a handspan above and perhaps a mile, the plants have found daylight and retained it.

The hollow is small enough to be missed entirely: a chamber inside a hill, a pocket beneath the root-mat, perhaps no larger than a room. Yet it has the peculiar spaciousness of an empty church. Almost nothing occupies its center. The floor falls away in a shallow bowl of new black soil, loose and glossy, with sharp ribs of freshly exposed clay showing along the walls. It feels recently made, as though the hill has only just opened an eye.

Everything solid is plant.

The walls are layered with thick roots, not buried in earth but woven through it, each as smooth as wet muscle. Some are woody and brown; some are clear as river glass, carrying slow streams of amber sap. At the edges of the hollow, young fronds uncoil from the soil every few minutes. They open, tremble, inspect the air, and either harden into broad green surfaces or melt back into mulch if they dislike what they find. Moss grows in careful, almost architectural panels on the lower roots. Thin white fungi follow the moss, then vanish when the chamber becomes too bright.

There is little water, but no true dryness. The place sweats.

Condensation gathers on the underside of leaves and falls irregularly—one drop here, a brief shower there. The soil drinks it before a puddle can form. In one depression a skin of water persists, dark as a pupil, trembling with every sound. It is fed by a root that has split open and drips clear fluid with the patient rhythm of a living wound. During rainstorms above, the chamber becomes briefly furious: water descends through thousands of root channels, rattling, tapping, spilling in silver threads. Then the roots swell, close, and hold the excess in themselves. The hollow returns to damp warmth and the smell of crushed leaves.

The warmth is not geothermal. It comes from the work of growth. The chamber is alive at a metabolic scale. Roots shift underfoot. Leaf walls inflate and relax. The air has the humid, breath-like softness of a greenhouse, though nothing here was planted or tended by hands.

Sound fills the empty space.

There is a continuous creaking overhead, the slow adjustment of the root ceiling. There are wet clicks from pods opening in the walls. There is the dry applause of innumerable small leaflets turning toward the green light. Sap moves audibly in certain translucent stems, making a deep, intermittent glugging like water in old pipes. When the chamber is still, one can hear the fine tearing noise of roots entering fresh soil.

At intervals, the whole place gives a loud vegetal sigh.

Then the chamber changes.

A root may withdraw from one wall and reappear from another. A hanging vine, seen moments ago, is suddenly lying across the floor, though no movement was visible. The green light can brighten beneath a leaf and dim above it. A drop of water falls upward, reaches the root ceiling, and is absorbed. These reversals are neither constant nor theatrical; they happen with the distracted certainty of natural processes. The plants seem not to recognize a distinction between inside and outside, above and below, growth and recollection.

It may be that the hollow is not actually beneath the earth. It may be beneath the idea of earth: a buried pocket in which a hillside’s roots have become so abundant that they have replaced stone, distance, and direction.

Fauna is rare. A single blind cricket sometimes appears on a root shelf, its body nearly white, its legs dark with soil. It listens to the walls and disappears into a seam. There may be tiny mites in the moss and soft-bodied larvae inside the swollen seedpods, but the chamber belongs almost entirely to vegetation. Even decay is green. A fallen leaf does not rot into absence; it sends up hairlike shoots, turns translucent, and is incorporated into the floor.

There are signs of primitive use, though none of a builder’s hand. Near the water depression, several thorny stems have been stripped clean and laid in a radial pattern. A few flat stones are wedged against a root, as if used to hold it apart. The arrangement is too purposeful to be accidental, too crude to be construction. Perhaps the blind crickets do it. Perhaps a root has learned leverage. Perhaps the chamber itself makes simple tools when it needs to expose a vein of water or keep an opening from sealing.

No artifact remains for long. Anything placed here begins to change allegiance. Wood sends out rootlets. Fiber grows soft and green. A sharpened bone becomes wrapped in a pale sheath of lichen, then begins to flex gently in time with the ceiling. Stone stays stone, but develops a faint pulse of warmth where it touches the soil.

The hollow is young. Its clay edges are still crisp; its root architecture remains improvisational. It is making itself rapidly, testing forms. One day it may close completely, packed solid with leaves and sapwood. Or it may expand through the hill in a series of damp, luminous chambers, each empty at the center, each loud with growth, each holding an impossible daylight beneath the ground.
