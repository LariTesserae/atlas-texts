The city is small enough to miss.

It lies in the hollow between the roots of something that may once have been called a tree, though no one standing within it would use that word. The roots are pale, ribbed columns broader than cottages, rising out of black wet soil and vanishing into a ceiling of woven stems. Their outer skin is not bark but a soft, fibrous felt, damp to the touch, embroidered with silver molds. From fissures in it hang curtains of liverwort and long combs of translucent leaves. Water beads everywhere: on the leaf-edges, along the root ribs, in the hairs of moss, on the backs of moving animals. Nothing ever quite dries.

It is cold in the hollow. Not the hard, clean cold of a winter plain, but a cellar cold, a cold carried in wet wood and saturated earth. Breath appears briefly and is swallowed by the dark. The soil gives underfoot, though paths have been laid over it: split reed matting, bundles of twigs, flat slabs of rootwood cut with stone axes and fitted edge to edge. The people who live here have made a town from what the place offers. They have not cleared the vegetation. Clearing would be impossible, perhaps sacrilegious, perhaps simply temporary; every cut surface greens over within days.

So the settlement has grown by tying, lashing, wedging, and guiding.

Huts are pockets beneath buttress roots, their walls woven from rushes and thick grass blades, their roofs formed of living vines trained over frames of bent branch. Footbridges cross narrow channels of brown water, each bridge softly furred with moss. Fences are not built so much as persuaded: thorny runners are directed around sleeping spaces, animal pens, fungus gardens, and little smoke pits. There is no metal except perhaps a few ancient green-black fragments, treasured and reworked into awls. Most tools are stone, shell, bone, tooth, and sharpened wood. At doorways hang strings of polished vertebrae that click faintly when someone passes.

The city’s streets are dark lanes beneath leaf-ceilings. At noon, there is only a diluted green-black dimness. At night, darkness becomes complete except for the occasional low lamp—a shallow stone cup of fat, a wick braided from marsh fiber—and the cool blue flicker of fungi growing in cracks. The fungi are useful but not trusted. They illuminate nothing steadily; they brighten when disturbed, pulse around footsteps, fade, and appear somewhere else. A child walking through a lane may make a brief constellation of the ground.

There is a great deal of plant life, but not a great deal of open color. Everything is green filtered through shadow, yellowed with cold, blackened by wetness. The leaves are broad where they catch drips, needle-fine where they must shed them. Thick mats of moss climb every upright surface. Pale flowers, almost colorless, open only in the intervals between rain. Their petals curl inward around small reservoirs of warm sap. Ferns unfold from cracks in the root walls like clenched hands slowly remembering themselves. The city has gardens, though it is difficult to distinguish them from wilderness: selected tubers planted among unselected tubers, edible stems marked with knotted reed tags, beds of medicinal mold tended in shaded bowls of bark.

Despite all this growth, the hollow feels spacious in an odd way. There are long, empty stretches of wet path between houses. A single lamp glows far away beneath a root arch. One hears a person before one sees them: the suction of their sandals in mud, the whispered brush of their reed cloak. The dwellings do not crowd one another. Each has a ring of territory—sleeping place, work platform, drying rack, animal shelter—held apart by curtains of leaves and darkness. The city is not densely built because the living structure around it is already architecture. The roots provide walls. The stems provide roof. The ground is floor only by agreement.

And everywhere there are animals.

At first they seem to be part of the vegetation: eyes caught in moss, a tuft of fur among roots, the slow fold of a wing closing where one expected a leaf. Then the scale of their presence becomes apparent. The channels are full of long-bodied, whiskered swimmers that surface without a sound and vanish beneath duckweed. Small antlered grazers pick their way along the root ledges, nibbling fungal shelves. Burrowing things turn the soft ground in every unused corner, leaving glossy black heaps of soil. Hairless pale birds cling upside down beneath leaves, sleeping in clusters, their beaks tucked beneath their breasts.

The city’s domestic animals are not truly domestic. They are neighbors with rituals. Low, six-legged browse-beasts are offered trays of root peelings and in return allow their woolly flank-hair to be combed. Broad-backed marsh hounds sleep under bridges and wake only when an unfamiliar scent enters the hollow. Little black foragers, somewhere between rats and crabs, collect dropped seeds from the paths; residents leave them specific scraps and expect them to keep the mold-eaters down. A person might call this husbandry, but the animals would not.

There are predators too, though they are rarely seen. Their signs appear on the wet surfaces: three parallel grooves in the mud, a clean-broken stem, a sudden silence among the upside-down birds. The people do not speak much about them. Children learn which root corridors are forbidden when the dripping becomes irregular. Adults hang bundles of bitter leaves at lane entrances. These are not believed to stop a predator outright. They are believed to make it choose another way.

The place is mostly quiet. Its quiet is made of innumerable small sounds kept beneath a threshold: water ticking from leaf to leaf, the soft continuous digestion of mud, insects tapping in hollow stalks, distant chewing, wings adjusting in sleep. Rain is frequent but seldom dramatic. It comes as a thickened darkness, a minute change in the smell of the air, then a patient pouring through the canopy. The roots drink loudly during heavy rain. Water runs in pale threads down their ribs and gathers in shallow basins around the houses. People lift their woven thresholds, secure their tools to rafters, and go on working.

Beyond the hollow, no one is certain what exists.

A few paths climb outward along young ridges of exposed peat and freshly thrust root. The landscape seems to be forming even now. New channels appear after each hard rain. Root walls split open to reveal red soil and white grubs. Whole shelves of earth slide down and are immediately netted by eager tendrils. It is a young place, unstable beneath its ancient growth: a low wet country still deciding where its ground will be. The town survives by moving slightly. A house that sinks is not rebuilt exactly where it stood. Its occupants carry the hearth stones, the sleeping mats, the bone hooks, and establish themselves beneath a neighboring root. In a generation, a lane may become a pond, a pond a garden, a garden a wall of fern.

There are rules suited to such a city.

No fire may be set directly on the ground, because the ground is alive and remembers heat.

No root may be cut across its grain, because it will bleed a clear fluid that attracts too many mouths.

No one walks alone after the blue fungi begin to pulse.

If water gathers inside a home, it is not immediately driven out. First it is watched. If it ripples without rain, the house is abandoned until morning.

The oldest rule is simpler: do not name all the animals you see. To name them all would be to claim knowledge of the hollow, and the hollow has a way of correcting that claim.

In the deepest part of the city stands a communal chamber inside a root that has opened naturally like a split seed. Its interior is lined with woven reed panels and shelves of jars. Here are stored dried spores, bitter bulbs, preserved fish, feathers, shed skins, small tools, and maps made from flattened bark. The maps are not maps of fixed streets. They show wetness: where water rose in a particular year, where the predators passed, where a useful fungus appeared, where a child disappeared and was found again three days later asleep among warm roots.

The people gather there during the longest rains. They speak softly, if at all. Their lamps make amber islands in the enormous dark. Outside, the living city shifts, drips, feeds, flowers, hunts, and closes over its own wounds.

By morning, there may be a new path.

Or a new pool.

Or a fresh green wall standing where the night before there had been open air.
