The city hangs below the snowline, though no one agrees what it hangs from.

Its oldest districts are bolted directly into the black flank of a mountain that is not yet finished becoming a mountain. The rock is raw, granular, still full of heatless motion: slopes gather themselves by inches, plates of slate emerge from the soil like wet teeth, and each decade a new ridge is found where surveyors had once marked open ground. There are no clean horizons. The high country is always assembling itself behind clouds.

The settlement occupies a long basin at an impossible elevation, a trough between rising shoulders of dark stone. Its streets do not lie level. They climb, plunge, double back through galleries, cross on iron bridges over vacant air, and disappear into broad elevator shafts drilled through unstable rock. Freight trams travel at a patient, groaning pace between terraces. Their cables vanish upward into grayness, carrying ore carts, milk canisters, lumber from the lower wet forests, cages of barking animals, and occasionally entire prefabricated rooms suspended beneath them like toys.

Everything is damp, though little water is visible at once.

Rain comes in narrow vertical strips, drifting across one district while another remains dry under a lid of low cloud. Condensation gathers continually on pipes and railings. It slicks the city’s old masonry, falls from the undersides of overpasses, and pools in shallow roof gardens where grasses have taken root. Beneath the pavement are innumerable channels: storm tunnels, cooling culverts, abandoned aqueducts, supply lines, and creeks that the city buried only to find them returned elsewhere.

The water has a mineral taste. It runs pale green from certain taps, black from others. In the mornings, steam rises from grates in the central market, not warm enough to comfort anyone, only warm enough to make the air smell of iron, wet wool, oil, and crushed moss.

The vegetation is persistent rather than grand. It fills every pause in construction.

Birches bend from cracks in retaining walls. Thick moss sheets the north-facing factories. A dark, shiny groundcover climbs electrical poles and blooms with tiny white mouths after rain. On the outskirts, where the city loosens into quarry fields and rail yards, wet meadows occupy the spaces between spoil heaps. They are bright with reed grass, low willow, and flowering plants whose roots appear to prefer contaminated soil. Ferns grow inside disused ventilation towers. Vines enter apartments through windows left open for years and make green partitions between rooms.

At the basin’s upper edge there are forests, but only in broken strips: stunted conifers, alder thickets, dense brush blackened by mist. Beyond them is bare geology, newly exposed and unclaimed. The mountainside lifts itself in rough shelves toward cloud, sparsely painted with snow that does not melt so much as migrate. Even in summer, wind comes down from the unseen heights cold enough to turn rain into needles.

The city is industrial, but its industries have adapted to uncertainty.

There are foundries in the lower terraces, their furnaces fed by ore from the mountain’s inner seams. There are workshops that make bearings, cable housings, pressure doors, lamp casings, and the specialized rivets required when a building shifts a few centimeters every winter. A vast waterworks stands at the eastern end of town, pumping from a reservoir no map successfully contains. The reservoir is said to move at night. Its surface slips from valley to valley beneath the ground, choosing different exits depending on the weather.

No building is expected to last unchanged. Foundations are designed with rollers, springs, and sacrificial walls. Apartment blocks wear numbered braces on their exteriors, added one generation at a time. At street corners, brass plaques announce the current angle of certain towers. Children use these angles as local directions: meet me past the seven-degree house; take the stair behind the leaning pharmacy.

Construction never ends, though it is rarely hurried. Cranes turn slowly above the rooftops, their long arms vanishing into fog. Men and women in reflective coats inspect cracks with instruments that listen to stone. Tunnel crews advance only a few meters a month, shoring each fresh passage before the mountain decides whether it will tolerate it. There is a sense that the whole settlement is not being built so much as negotiated.

The sound of the place is broad and intermittent.

A tram bell rings from somewhere below, muted by mist. A mill begins its shift with a deep, circular hum that travels through the ground and makes cups tremble in cupboards. Conveyor belts clatter under corrugated awnings. Fans roar in the mouths of tunnels. Dogs bark from balconies. Flocks of small dark birds lift from rooftops in synchronized bursts, their wings making a sound like rain striking paper.

And beneath all of it is a slow pressure-noise: the internal settling of the range. It is too low to hear most days. One feels it through the soles of boots, through a bedframe, through the metal handle of a bus door. At night, when the machinery shuts down in parts of the city, the noise becomes more distinct. A dull, thoughtful grinding. A mountain considering another shape.

Animals are everywhere.

Goats occupy the high retaining walls and impossible ledges of the old quarter. They belong to no one officially, though each wears a numbered copper tag issued by the municipal department of slope management. They graze on rooftop gardens, eat laundry, and stand calmly beside warning signs during minor collapses. Long-bodied foxes hunt among the rail depots. Their fur is silvered by industrial runoff, and their eyes reflect blue under street lamps.

In the tunnels, pale moths cluster around maintenance lights by the thousands. There are salamanders in the wet power conduits, eel-like things in the reservoir pipes, and birds that nest inside the heat exchangers because the warmth confuses their sense of season. Above the city, condors or something closely resembling condors circle in slow silence. They seem enormous until they descend and prove themselves larger.

The people have learned not to ask where certain animals came from.

Sometimes a herd of antlered creatures appears on the upper tramline after a storm, standing between the rails and facing uphill. They have no tracks behind them. The trains wait until they leave, which can take hours. Sometimes fish are found swimming in rainwater pooled on flat roofs, gasping gently when the puddles shrink. In the oldest neighborhoods, residents leave bowls of water outside their doors—not for strays, but for whatever is thirsty in the walls.

Light is rationed by weather rather than law.

For much of the year the basin receives a gray, diluted brightness that makes noon feel like late afternoon. The sun may be visible only as a pale coin behind layers of cloud. Electric lamps burn all day in workshops, stairwells, and public passageways. Their yellow light shines on wet pavement and is broken into trembling strips by every puddle.

When darkness comes, it does not fall cleanly. Fog enters first, flowing along streets and through open factory gates. Then the upper city disappears, then the cranes, then the bridge lights, until each neighborhood becomes an island of lamps and muffled sound. Red aviation beacons blink far overhead, marking structures that may be towers, cable pylons, or merely tall things moving in the clouds.

There are rules, posted in black lettering on white boards at entrances to transit stations, mines, schools, public baths, and residential halls.

Do not follow running water uphill.

If you hear your name from an unlit service tunnel, report it before sleeping.

All livestock must be accounted for during periods of atmospheric inversion.

In the event of a second moon, remain indoors until municipal clearance.

Never anchor a structure to blue stone.

The rules are practical. Everyone says so. They were written after accidents, inspections, committees, and years of hard-won civic knowledge. Yet no one remembers the accident that produced the rule about uphill water. No one can identify blue stone until it has already caused a problem.

At the center of town is a square paved in enormous slabs of unfinished rock. The slabs are always wet. Beneath them, something presses slowly upward. The square rises by a fraction each year, carrying lamp posts, benches, market stalls, and the statue of an engineer whose face has been worn smooth by rain. The city has built flexible joints around the square, and the roads descend away from it in gentle, newly formed grades.

On market days, vendors arrange their goods around the growing ground: mushrooms cultivated in ventilation shafts; wool from the municipal goats; lamp oil; machine parts; dried mountain herbs; trout from the reservoir; jars of luminous lichen; maps printed that morning and already obsolete by evening.

Above the stalls, cable cars arrive and depart through the dimness. Below them, the mountain continues its slow work. It lifts the city, shifts its foundations, changes the course of water, opens a seam, closes a road. The people respond with braces, bridges, pumps, measurements, and patient adjustments.

Nothing here is stable, but almost nothing is sudden.

The city persists because it has accepted that persistence is not the same as stillness.
