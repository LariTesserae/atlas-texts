The plateau lies above the weather but not above its consequences.

It is a broad country of pale stone, lifted into a blue, almost metallic sky. The ground is not flat exactly; it has been worn into shelves, dry basins, ribbed slopes, and long shallow valleys that seem at first to be empty. From a distance the land has the color of old bone, ocher clay, and white mineral dust. Up close it is complicated: volcanic glass embedded in chalky sediment, dark seams of ironstone, crusts of salt that glitter under the sun, lichen-black fissures where cool moisture has gathered for centuries.

The elevation makes the light hard and clean. Shadows are sharply cut under rocks and walls, but the air itself glows. At noon, the far ridges look less like mountains than like pieces of another terrain seen through clear water. Clouds pass low enough to graze the upper escarpments. They do not often become rain. More commonly, they arrive in a slow procession of gray undersides, lay a brief coolness over the stone, and leave beads of condensation along every exposed edge.

That is how water lives here.

It condenses overnight on the undersides of boulders, on the frames of abandoned towers, on the waxy leaves of low shrubs. It gathers in hairline channels cut into the rock and runs, for a few minutes after dawn, toward small hidden cisterns. There are pools in the deeper folds of the valley—dark, mineral-tasting, rimmed with white deposits—but they are rare enough to become landmarks. Each has an ecology arranged around it with great care: reedlike grass, mats of copper-green algae, insect swarms that rise only after sunset, and small pale animals that emerge from cracks to drink without stirring the surface.

Vegetation does not cover the plateau. It persists in deliberate-looking intervals.

There are thorn trees in certain drainage lines, their crowns flattened by wind, their roots forced deep through fractured stone. There are clusters of blue-gray succulents whose leaves open at night and close before the sun becomes severe. Some hillsides carry a skin of brush, low and aromatic, leaving much of the rock exposed between plants. In shaded clefts grow fernlike things with translucent fronds, each strand holding a fine internal lattice of water. When the morning light crosses them, they shine as if wired with silver.

The animals are more numerous than the plants would seem to permit.

Small, quick-bodied grazers move across the open ground in scattered groups: six-legged antelope-shaped creatures with narrow heads and reflective dark eyes, feeding on nearly invisible films of growth among the stones. They are always a little farther away than expected, not because they flee, but because their coats take on the colors and grain of the land. Their young shelter beneath overhanging rocks, where the stone remains cool.

Higher in the air, broad-winged scavengers circle on thermal currents. Their wings are veined and nearly transparent, so that the sky shows through them. At dawn they perch atop the old installations, folding themselves into shapes like weathered machinery. There are burrowing animals, too: glossy black, warm-bodied things that tunnel through salt crusts and leave branching marks across the basins. At night, they come out in hundreds. The ground then appears to crawl with dropped stars, because each animal carries along its flanks a dim line of blue bioluminescence.

Predators move with less visibility. Occasionally one sees the long silhouette of a ridge-cat crossing a distant shelf, its back rising and falling between stones. It has too many joints in its legs, or perhaps the eye cannot correctly understand the way it moves. It leaves no tracks. The grazing animals do not call when it is near. They simply redistribute themselves over the landscape, each turning a different way until the whole herd has become a dispersed pattern.

Despite this life, the country is quiet.

There is wind, but often it passes far overhead, combing the upper grasses or whispering along a cliff face without reaching the valley floor. There is the minute ticking of heated stone expanding in sunlight. There are dry insect wings, a distant scrape of hooves, the nearly inaudible pour of condensation down a channel. Sound does not travel naturally here. A shout thrown across one of the basins seems to fall apart after a few meters, as if the air has been arranged into soft layers that absorb it.

The scattered structures contribute to this impression.

They are not ruins, though they have stood long enough to acquire the appearance of ruins. A slender white tower rises from one ridge, featureless except for a dark slit extending from its base nearly to its top. In another valley, a sequence of low black arches crosses a dry wash without touching it. There are circular platforms on remote shoulders of stone, each perhaps thirty meters wide, their surfaces mirror-smooth despite windblown grit and centuries of weather. No roads lead to them. No cables join them. No smoke, light, or obvious human activity surrounds them.

Yet they are active.

At certain hours, the air above a platform trembles. Dust lifts from the ground in slow vertical ribbons and hangs, rearranging itself into loops, branching diagrams, or flocks of geometric motes before settling again. The black arches sometimes contain an inward darkness deeper than shade. Animals avoid them, though not in panic. Birds will perch on top of an arch, preen, and depart, but none fly through.

The tower on the ridge produces water. Not much—only a sustained, patient seepage from its lower wall, enough to feed a narrow channel and a grove of thorn trees that should not be able to live there. The water is warm at first, cooling as it travels downhill. Its channel has been colonized by grass, insects, frogs with translucent bellies, and a species of tall white wading bird that appears to be made mostly of folded legs.

Farther out, beyond the central valley, the land bears larger signs of a system whose scale cannot easily be estimated. Lines of polished stone run over hills and disappear into the ground. Some are ancient aqueducts, perhaps; others change position slightly between visits. A field of upright slabs stands on the western plain. From noon to late afternoon each slab casts a shadow in the wrong direction, and in those shadows grow mosses not seen elsewhere on the plateau.

The people who built these things are not visible. If they are present, they have adapted to a way of habitation too subtle for casual observation: inside the stone, beneath the salt basins, distributed through the weather instruments of the land. There are small shelters near some water pockets—single-room structures with thick walls, shaded courtyards, and doors that open only after the occupant has stood quietly before them for several minutes. Their interiors are cool. They contain no furniture except a low bench, a basin, and a wall that shows the surrounding terrain in different seasons.

The world has rules, though they reveal themselves slowly.

Water is allowed to collect, but never to flood. The old devices guide it invisibly away from catastrophe and toward persistence. Erosion has been moderated rather than stopped: cliffs crumble in measured increments; channels deepen but do not cut too far; loose slopes settle after every rare storm into forms that remain stable for generations. The landscape is neither wild in the old sense nor controlled in the agricultural one. It has been given boundaries, and within those boundaries it continues to make itself.

Nothing here is entirely abandoned. The structures have become parts of the ecosystem, as dependable as cliffs or springs. Animals use them for shade. Plants root in their seams. Condensation gathers on their surfaces. Their impossible workings occur without ceremony, not demanding attention, not announcing a purpose.

By late afternoon, the brightness softens. The pale rocks become gold, then rose, then violet in the basins. Warm air drains slowly from the high shelves. The thorn trees cast long, ragged shadows across the mineral ground. A herd moves toward one of the hidden pools, each body lit along the spine by the low sun. Above them, a transparent-winged scavenger turns once, silently, and drops behind a ridge.

Then the first lights appear: blue along the burrows, faint green in the wet ferns, a narrow white line rising up the impossible tower. Across the dry wash, the black arches become almost indistinguishable from the darkening stone.

The plateau does not become loud at night. It becomes attentive.
