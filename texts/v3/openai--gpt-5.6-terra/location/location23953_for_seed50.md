High above the ordinary weather, where the mountains have thinned into black shelves and pale stone ribs, there is a water-filled chamber hidden inside the crown of a peak.

It is not a lake in the usual sense. No open shore gives onto it. One reaches it through a seam in the mountain, a crack scarcely wider than a body, where cold rain blows upward instead of down. Beyond that crack is a hollow no larger than a village square, though it seems at first immeasurable because the water has no clear boundary. The whole interior is submerged: floor, walls, air—or what would be air. The chamber is full to its highest fissures with lucid, moving water.

Yet daylight enters.

It filters through high blue ice in the roof, through mineral-thin curtains of water pouring in from snowfields far above, through openings that do not seem large enough to admit the amount of light they provide. By noon the place is bright, almost ordinary in illumination. Every suspended grain of rock dust is visible. Every bubble rising from the floor shines white and silver. The water is cold enough to make the bones feel separate, but not deadly cold. It carries a mineral taste: slate, iron, a faint bitterness like old rain caught in a wooden bowl.

The deafening sound comes from everywhere.

Water falls into the chamber through seams in the ceiling with the force of weather. It enters in white ropes and flattened sheets, colliding with the submerged volume so violently that the entire pool shudders. The sound should be muffled underwater, but it is not. It travels through stone, teeth, chest, the little nails of the hand. It is a continual deep roaring joined by shrill impacts: pebbles chattering in currents, bubbles collapsing, ice cracking somewhere within the mountain. The chamber has no silence. Even in the lee of a buttress, even with one’s head pressed to the pale clay of the floor, the noise remains, a huge sustained vibration.

The mountain is very young here.

Its interior is still deciding what shape it wants. Fresh gray strata push from the walls in soft, unfinished folds. Knobs of volcanic glass emerge from gravel beds and vanish again beneath deposits of silt. Limestone blooms in thin, chalky plates along the floor. In some places a handprint pressed into the mineral mud will be gone by the time the hand is withdrawn; in others, water lays down a skin of translucent stone around a footprint, preserving it for an hour, a day, perhaps a season.

Nothing is ancient enough to be settled. The stones are raw and sharp. The channels are provisional. A wall may become a floor during a single storm. Every incoming torrent brings a new cargo—sand from one valley, red clay from another, needles of frozen grass, a drowned beetle, a scrap of bark—and the currents arrange these materials into temporary bars and terraces. Then the next surge erases them.

Near one side of the chamber, where the water turns slowly around a column of darker rock, a sparse garden holds on.

It consists of long brown-green threads rooted in cracks, mountain moss growing underwater in thick dark cushions, and several pale plants with leaves like transparent fingers. Their stems bend flat in the current. Once in a while they lift, trembling, when the flow slackens. On the highest ledges, just beneath the ice-light, small tufts of grass occupy pockets of sediment. Their blades reach into water as if they remember wind but cannot find it.

There are almost no animals.

A few blind fish move through the brighter water, silver only when they cross a shaft of light. They are narrow as sewing needles, with large soft mouths designed to catch whatever falls. There are stone-colored crabs beneath the sediment shelves, their shells packed with grit. Occasionally, a highland bird is swept through one of the ceiling fissures and appears briefly in the chamber, wings spread and useless, spinning among the falling streams before some hidden current lifts it away or draws it downward. Most visitors see no creatures at all. The place feels too violent for life, though small lives persist in the cracks.

There are signs that people have entered.

Not many signs. Not a settlement. On a shelf halfway up the eastern wall stands a platform of lashed branches weighted with stones. It should have been torn away long ago, but it remains, rebuilt so often that its wood ranges from fresh white splinters to black water-polished limbs. Nearby, a ladder of knotted rope descends into the water and disappears beneath a shelf. The rope is made from grass, hide, and twisted roots; it is swollen and slick, but carefully maintained.

On the floor are a few cairns, each no higher than a knee. Their stones are selected for color: one stack of white quartz, one of red shale, one of greenish river rock. They are not tombs. They are markers. The people who come here use them to tell the currents apart.

A white cairn means: the water from the west wall is rising.

A red cairn means: do not enter the lower passage; the mountain is grinding new stone there.

A green cairn means: there is breathable space ahead.

This is the most important knowledge of the hidden chamber: it is not entirely without air.

At unpredictable intervals, the turbulence gathers itself. A vast spiral forms in the center of the pool, drawing loose material, fish, plant fragments, and bubbles into its turning core. The water level falls by inches. Somewhere below, passages open. In certain hollows under the shelves, trapped air swells out through fissures, creating rooms of breath within the mountain. These pockets last only a little while. A traveler can surface in them, strike a flint against a dry stone, listen to the roaring water outside, and see the ceiling close above in blackness.

The people of the upper valleys come here rarely, and never alone. They bring bone hooks, stone hammers, leather bags of oil, and narrow clay lamps that cannot stay lit underwater but are useful in the breathing pockets. They come for a substance that grows in the chamber’s newest cracks: a pale mineral foam, soft as wet chalk at first, hardening when exposed to the outer air. Mixed with ash and fat, it seals boats, roofs, wounds, and the seams of winter jars. They call it mountain milk.

There are rules, though no one has written them.

Do not take more mountain milk than will fit in one hand.

Do not build a cairn from stones already placed.

Do not follow a fish downward.

When the water suddenly clears, leave.

When it suddenly fills with bubbles, hold still.

If you hear a sound beneath the roar—a low knocking, slow and regular, as if someone is striking a wooden door from inside the rock—put your palm against the wall and wait. The mountain may be making a new room. Or closing an old one.

At the uppermost edge of the chamber, beneath blue ice and the muffled white pressure of the snowfields, there is a ledge just large enough for two kneeling people. It is the only place where a face can rise fully from the water. From there, through a fracture in the mountain, one can see the outside world: a thin slice of bright cloud, distant slopes, perhaps the black wing of a bird crossing the daylight.

Then another surge arrives, and the view is gone.

The water rises over the ledge. The chamber returns to itself: bright, cold, nearly empty, full of motion. A small secret ocean held inside a high unfinished mountain, continually being made by the forces trying to destroy it.
