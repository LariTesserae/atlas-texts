The settlement is built in the throat of a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

From the outer range it cannot be seen. The peaks around it are pale, steep, and newly broken, their faces all raw planes of blue-black stone and iron-colored dust. There are no snowfields despite the altitude. No streams cut the slopes. Nothing green has found a hold in the cracks. The whole massif is dry enough that a dropped glove becomes a mineral object within a season, stiffened with frost-salts and metallic grit.

But one cleft, scarcely wider than a street at its mouth, descends almost vertically through the rock. Inside is the city.

It is called a city only because there are addresses, shifts, taxes, a signal authority, and a public killing floor for faulty machinery. Physically, it is more like a dense mechanism that has accumulated inhabitants. Galleries are bolted into the shaft wall, one above another, connected by ladders, hoists, narrow bridges, and freight rails that disappear around corners. Workshops occupy old pressure chambers. Homes are tucked behind turbine housings and beneath the curved backs of ore hoppers. Pipes cross every open space in bundles: compressed air, kiln gas, lubricant, heat-transfer brine—though there is no true water in the place, and nobody uses the old word except as a joke.

The mountain makes its own weather.

Cold gas rises from far below, driven through ancient volcanic conduits and newer industrial boreholes. It comes up in gusts that can overturn carts, lift sheets of discarded insulation, and make the hanging lamps swing hard enough to strike the walls. Lower down, furnaces and generator stacks exhale warmth. The two currents meet throughout the shaft in sudden rolling turbulences: dry fogs of powdered mica, hot veils of chemical vapor, spinning columns of ash-gray dust. A person can step from a place where their eyelashes are frosting to a metal stair warm enough to burn through a boot sole.

Nothing is still for long. The rail cages shudder past. Exhaust shutters slam open and shut. Conveyor belts carry crushed mineral upward in a perpetual black stream. Somewhere in the walls, excavation charges go off with muffled, geological thumps. Yet the dominant sound is not loudness. The city is mostly quiet in the way a factory heard from inside its own body is quiet: a low hum in the bones, intermittent chain-rattle, the long sigh of pressure releasing. People speak close to one another. Animals sleep through almost everything.

The animals are what first surprise visitors.

They are everywhere: dense, practical, largely unremarkable creatures adapted to the vertical dark. Narrow-backed pack goats with blunt hooves climb routes no human could use, carrying canisters of welding gas in padded frames. Pale cliff hens nest in the warm cavities behind electrical cabinets, their eggs prized because they can be cooked on a radiator. Long-bodied dust ferrets live in the cableways and hunt the moths that breed in the lamp housings. There are heavy, hornless oxen at the lower loading decks, bred compact for turning capstans in thin air. Their breath is white, their hides dark with oil, and their bells have been removed because the sound unsettles the lift engines.

Smaller life thickens the city’s hidden surfaces. Lice in felt coats. Gray gnats around heat vents. Blind beetles feeding on grease and flakes of skin. Tough little mites that gather in the seams of respirator masks and are carefully brushed out at the end of every shift. No one calls this abundance wild. Every animal has a use, a nuisance category, a licensed keeper, or a designated poison.

There is no vegetation, not even the stubborn velvet of moss. The only thing remotely resembling it is a pale crust growing over the warmest exhaust baffles: a mineral-eating film, greenless and dry, which spreads in branching patterns and makes the metal underneath brittle. Maintenance crews scrape it away weekly. Children collect the flakes because they hold a charge in the dark and flash faintly when crushed between gloved fingers.

Light arrives by infrastructure rather than sky. At the cleft’s narrow upper mouth, daylight leaks in for a few hours as a cold slate glow, falling through cables and bridgework in thin tilted columns. Most of the city is lit by caged bulbs, inspection lamps, furnace windows, and the blue-white arcing of welders. Faces appear then disappear. Entire neighborhoods are known by their light: the amber kilns, the green emergency corridor, the violet lamp quarter where old-spectrum bulbs are repaired because their strange color makes surface cracks in ore easier to see.

At this elevation the air is poor, and the city has rules about breath.

Nobody runs uphill unless an alarm is sounding. Open flames are forbidden above the Forty-Second Gallery, where the gas mixture changes. Newborn animals are kept in pressure rooms until their lungs harden. Each household maintains an air ledger: the filters cleaned, the masks patched, the oxygen tablets issued for guests. Windows do not exist. Doorways are fitted with thick canvas curtains, chain screens, or double hatches, not to keep out rain but to manage drafts. A draft can feed a furnace wrongly, kill a lamp bank, strip heat from a nursery, or send a cloud of conductive dust across a switchyard.

The city is young, as the mountain is young. Its streets do not have settled names. Every month a platform shifts, a bore opens, a retaining wall is added, an old gallery is declared unsafe and abandoned to the ferrets and beetles. The central freight elevator was installed only twelve years ago and already has three unofficial replacement routes braided around it: goat stairs, a dangerous cable seat, and a chimney crawl lined with handholds polished by thousands of gloves.

Beyond the settlement’s working lights, the shaft continues down.

No one agrees how far. Survey instruments fail in the deeper turbulence. Ropes come back coated in a fine silver dust that corrodes steel but leaves skin untouched. Sometimes a warm wind rises from below carrying a smell like struck stone and old coins. It makes the animals restless. The cliff hens go silent; the goats crowd their pens; the oxen turn their heads toward the floor.

Then the city pauses—not dramatically, not all at once. A machinist takes a hand off a lever. A child stops halfway up a ladder. A hundred small creatures settle into the narrow warm spaces between pipes.

And for a moment, among all the engines and animals and stacked human rooms, the mountain can be heard building itself around them.
