The city stands on a plain that is not yet entirely land.

Its streets are laid across broad, low shelves of pale clay, each shelf only a little higher than the next, so that water lies everywhere just beneath the surface. There are no rivers in the ordinary sense. Instead, the ground sweats. Warm water gathers in footprints, fills shallow cuts between buildings, beads from retaining walls, and darkens the bases of every pillar. The air has the close, mineral smell of wet pottery. It is warm enough that bare skin shines constantly with moisture, yet the sky never clears enough to offer heat directly. Light arrives through a thick, unbroken ceiling of gray cloud: a low, dim illumination without sunrise or shadow.

Nothing green grows here.

There are no weeds in cracks, no grass along the embankments, no moss on the wet stone. The plain is too young, the soil too raw. In places it still has the soft, blunt geometry of sediment that has recently been deposited and has not decided what it will become. Whole districts are built upon packed mud, crushed shell, and layers of fired brick. The citizens drive long rows of stakes down through the yielding earth, lash them together with fiber, and raise platforms above the seepage. Their city is a mass of low constructions: mud-brick rooms, reed-mat roofs, kilns, covered walkways, communal courtyards, embankments faced with stone. Nothing rises very high. The land will not bear towers, and the dim sky presses low above it anyway.

Yet it is dense almost beyond comfort.

The houses meet wall to wall. Their roofs overlap. Lanes narrow into shoulder-wide passages where clay jars, drying nets, stacks of fuel cakes, fish baskets, and sleeping animals occupy every spare space. Elevated footpaths cross over flooded alleys. Bridges join second-story doors. Beneath the boards, dark water nudges quietly at posts. A visitor could travel for miles across the settlement and rarely touch open ground.

The city extends outward in rings and fingers, following firmer ridges in the wet plain. At its edge are unfinished quarters: neat grids of posts standing in water, fresh walls the color of wet bread, new canals no more than straight scratches in the mud. The terrain is still being made, and the city makes itself with it. Kiln ash is dumped into shallows. Broken pots and shell are packed beneath roads. Whole neighborhoods rest on deliberate layers of debris. The people do not conquer the marshy ground so much as continually persuade it to hold them.

Everywhere are animals.

Long-legged white birds stalk the shallow basins between outer houses, stabbing at the water for eels and grubs. Dark waterfowl crowd the drainage pools. Goats balance on roof edges and plank rails, their hooves clicking on fired brick. Pigs root in communal refuse yards where the ground is driest. Dogs sleep under carts, then bolt awake at any disturbance. Oxen stand chest-deep in the broader muddy channels, fitted with yokes that have gone black with damp. In the market districts, cages of small birds hang from eaves, and woven pens hold turtles, frogs, and broad-backed river fish.

There are insects in tremendous number: flies in the food lanes, mosquitoes in the shaded wet passages, dragonflies over the open pools, beetles in the ash heaps. Their presence is not seen as an invasion. They are part of the city’s fixed population, as ordinary as gutters and pots.

The sound is enormous.

It begins with the animals: the harsh crying of marsh birds, the mutter of pigeons under rafters, pigs squealing, dogs barking, goats calling from roofs. Beneath that is the human city—many thousands of voices in close spaces, arguments passing through windows, market cries, children shouting on the plankways, boatmen calling at canal crossings, potters striking clay from molds, stone pounders working at the quays. Kilns roar through their vent holes. Waterwheels groan in shallow channels. Wooden mallets beat stakes into mud with a slow, repeated thunder.

And always, from every quarter, there is the ringing.

The city has no metal bells. Its bells are fired-clay vessels, stone slabs, hollow wooden frames, and suspended shells. At crossings and gates, people strike them to signal passage, work shifts, warnings, trade, worship, births, deaths, and the movement of animals through narrow lanes. Their tones are dull, booming, cracked, and multitudinous. The air itself seems to vibrate with pottery.

No one expects quiet. Quiet would mean the channels had stopped flowing, the kilns had gone cold, the animals had vanished, the city had emptied. Noise is proof that the precarious arrangement still holds.

The strange thing is that, despite all this apparent bustle, nothing truly changes.

The wetness does not deepen or recede. The cloud cover never breaks. The same warm dimness occupies every hour. Water stands at the same marks on the walls. The unfinished districts remain unfinished, though workers labor in them every day. New bricks are made, old bricks break, and the city’s materials circulate endlessly—wall into rubble, rubble into roadbed, roadbed into platform, platform into wall—but its shape remains. A person could leave for a generation and return to find the same bird perched on the same piling, the same goat path across the roofs, the same three clay towers leaning toward one another above the eastern basin.

Even erosion has not begun its long work. Edges stay raw. Fresh-cut banks retain the sharpness of a tool mark. Clay mounds do not slump. The plain appears newly arrived in the world and permanently held at that first moment of arrival.

The people accept this without ceremony. Their lives are organized around maintenance rather than progress. They patch roofs, re-lash posts, re-fire bricks, dredge channels that never fill, and replace gates that never rot through. Their tools are simple—stone adzes, bone needles, wooden shovels, clay lamps, woven baskets, copper awls scarce enough to be inherited—but they are used with intimate competence. Every household knows the texture of safe mud, the sound of a failing piling, the proper angle for a roof gutter, the kinds of animal cries that mean hunger, disease, courtship, or flood, though flood never comes.

At the city’s farthest edge, beyond the last platforms, the open plain continues under the dim sky. It is not empty. It is crowded with shallow pools, animal trails, upright stakes waiting to become buildings, piles of brick awaiting walls, and low hummocks of pale sediment. Far away, indistinct in the grayness, other dense settlements rise from the wet ground in the same manner: low, broad, noisy, and seemingly eternal.

Between them are no fields.

There is nothing to cultivate.

Instead there are ponds, fish traps, bird rookeries, salt pans, shell beds, clay pits, and carefully tended animal enclosures. The land gives water, mud, fish, eggs, reeds, and passage. The city gives it weight, heat, noise, and a permanent pattern of human hands.

It is a warm world at the edge of becoming, packed tight with life that has nowhere else to go.
