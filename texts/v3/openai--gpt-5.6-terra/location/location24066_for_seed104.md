The high water has no shore.

It begins somewhere below, in a darkness that may be the original ground, and rises through a continent of drowned air. Warm currents move upward in slow transparent columns, carrying leaves, seeds, pale pollen, and the occasional loosened piece of a world that has been giving way for longer than memory. Above, what might once have been sky is another depth: a green-black vault of water clouded with suspended light. Rain falls there, but it does not fall downward in any simple sense. It gathers in sheets and ropes, crosses the submerged heights, and becomes part of the larger water without a sound.

The landscape is a range of very old mountains, nearly erased but still immense. Their slopes are not sharp. They have been softened into long backs and folded shoulders, their stone worn into ribs, basins, terraces, and hollows wide enough to contain their own weather. Whole valleys lie under warm blue-green water. Other valleys are filled with dense fog, so saturated that swimmers—or things that swim—can pass from liquid into vapor without immediately noticing the change.

At the higher elevations, the water thins. Not into air exactly, but into a drifting medium full of rain and floating droplets. Great plateaus emerge there, black and red-brown stone gleaming with wetness. They are never dry. Water runs over every surface in a lacework of shallow streams, gathers in clear bowls carved by ages of erosion, spills across the edges, and descends for miles in slow, veiled cataracts. The falls make almost no audible roar. Their force is too constant, their scale too enormous; sound is swallowed by water, moss, distance, and the heavy pressure of the place.

Vegetation has entered every crack that time made.

Forests grow underwater along the mountain flanks: groves of flexible, dark trunks rooted in silt and stone, their crowns spreading into broad fans of translucent green fronds. The leaves are not leaves in the familiar sense. They are layered membranes, thin as skin, which open and close with the currents. When a warm current rises from the depths, an entire forest turns at once, thousands of crowns orienting toward it. In dimness they produce their own vague, pearled illumination—not bright enough to banish the dark, only enough to reveal the shape of the water between branches.

Higher up, in rain-country, the vegetation grows heavier. Moss lies in cushions over the basalt. Vines span clefts that have no visible bottom. Tall reeds take root along the rims of suspended lakes, their stems thick and jointed, crowded so closely that their upper reaches make a ceiling. Some bear deep purple bladders filled with water; others hold luminous insects or spores within them, though the insects are rarely seen moving. Ferns grow broad as sails, pressing flat against the wet cliffs. Their undersides are silver, and in a windless moment they tremble as if something large has passed beneath the mountain.

The place is dense not because it is crowded with animals, but because nothing is empty. Every volume of water is occupied by drifting life, mineral dust, roots, shed skins, strands of algae, slow bubbles from buried faults, and the dark, soft sediment of centuries. The rock itself is full of tunnels. Water circulates through it. Warmth circulates through it. In some places the mountain seems to breathe: a cliff face exhales a cloud of mineral-rich water, and the nearby vines lift and tighten in response.

Fauna is scarce enough that any sighting becomes an event.

There are long white creatures in the deep channels, seen only as bends in the darkness or as pale markings moving beyond a curtain of roots. They are too large to be fish, too quiet to be anything that hunts in the ordinary way. Sometimes one passes under a drowned forest and all the fronds incline toward its wake. Sometimes, far above, a single black-winged animal glides through the rain, using the dense atmosphere as though it were water. It circles the old peaks for days without flapping, then disappears into a cloudbank.

Smaller lives exist, but cautiously: transparent snails grazing the stone, brief sparks of crustaceans beneath algae, thumb-sized silver things that retreat into crevices when watched. There are no herds, no cries, no constant chatter of a living wilderness. The silence has not been made by absence. It has been made by adaptation. Here, creatures that make too much disturbance are carried away by the currents, or noticed by whatever keeps the depths so still.

There are traces of building, scattered and half-forgiven by the landscape.

On a ridge just below the rainline, a wall of fitted stone rises from a field of moss. It is only one wall, perhaps thirty paces long, its openings shaped like narrow windows or perhaps gills. Beyond it there is no floor, no roof, no companion structure—only a drop into clouded water. Farther down, among the roots of a drowned grove, upright pillars stand in a perfect circle. Their carvings have been erased by algae, but their arrangement remains too deliberate to be natural. In the deepest basins are stairways descending into silt. They do not lead down so much as they continue the idea of descent, step after softened step, until stone becomes mud and mud becomes black water.

No tools are found. No metal survives. Nothing suggests a machine. The ruins feel less like the remains of a civilization than temporary agreements made between stone and living things. Perhaps they were never houses. Perhaps the mountains once grew these structures as reefs grow their chambers. Perhaps the walls are scars.

The laws of direction are unreliable here.

Down is usually where the warmer water comes from, but not always. A person climbing a wet plateau may find that the rain begins striking from below. Pools can hang beneath overhangs, their surfaces level with an unseen gravity. In certain valleys, loose leaves drift upward into a ceiling of water and remain there, forming a dark floating soil from which roots descend. The old mountains endure this without surprise. Their erosion has been so complete that they no longer resist transformation. They receive water, fog, root, collapse, and reversal as parts of the same long weather.

At intervals, the whole region changes state.

The deep currents intensify. Water rises through fissures with a patient violence. Forests bend. Entire shelves of moss detach from cliffs and float away intact, carrying their miniature gardens with them. Rain thickens in the upper world until distances vanish. Then the high plateaus become islands in an ocean falling from every direction. The drowned valleys clear briefly, and from far off one can see the vast architecture of the land: ranges upon ranges, rounded by time, submerged in a single breathing system.

When the surge passes, new channels have formed. A cliff has lost a face. An ancient stair is buried. A grove has migrated a mile upward, held together by roots and buoyant peat. Nothing has been destroyed in the immediate sense. It has simply been redistributed.

The light remains dim, filtered through too much water and too many layers of green. It comes in diluted bands: yellow-grey near the rainward heights, emerald beneath the forests, deep cobalt in the open basins, and finally a warm absolute black below. At certain hours—which are not necessarily hours—a low illumination appears inside the water itself. It outlines the ancient slopes, the hanging gardens, the ruins, the slow columnar currents rising from the world beneath.

Then the landscape can be seen as a whole: not a flooded mountain range, not an ocean, not a forest, but a vast old body held in suspension. Its bones are stone. Its blood is warm water. Its skin is moss and frond and cloud. It has been wearing away for ages beyond counting, becoming softer, denser, more inwardly alive.

And it is still changing, quietly, all the time.
