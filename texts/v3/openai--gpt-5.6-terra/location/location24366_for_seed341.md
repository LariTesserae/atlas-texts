The town occupies a shallow basin where the ground has never quite decided whether it is land.

It is low country, nearly level, but not flat: the streets rise and fall by inches, following old ridges of packed shell, poured slag, and whatever firmer matter people found beneath the black soil. Between them lie long wet seams. Water stands in gutters even after weeks without rain. It comes up through cracks in the pavement; it pearls on the undersides of bridges; it sweats from retaining walls built around things nobody remembers constructing. The whole place seems to have a water table at knee height, pressing patiently upward.

There are only a few streets, perhaps twelve that properly deserve the name, arranged around a flooded freight square. The square is not a plaza so much as a broad sheet of dark, warm water with rails disappearing into it. At its middle stands a derelict loading crane, orange with age, its hook held a few feet above the surface. The crane still turns at certain hours. No one is seen operating it. Its gears complain, then catch, and the great arm swings slowly over the water as if searching for a barge that sank years ago. When it stops, it drips.

The buildings are built close enough to feel municipal but far enough apart that empty lots persist between them: wet gardens, heaps of brick, strips of reeds, concrete foundations flooded into rectangular pools. Every structure has learned a different accommodation with the damp. Some stand on tall pilings, their lower stories left open and full of shadow. Some have been raised in successive layers—one brick floor upon another, stairs beginning halfway up a wall, old doorways opening onto water. Others sit stubbornly at ground level and wear a green-black tide mark around their facades.

There are factories at the basin’s edge, though none is large. A boiler works, a salt-processing shed, a machine shop where the windows glow dirty amber. Their roofs are corrugated metal patched with tar, then patched again with sheet rubber, flattened cans, and mats of vegetation. Steam spills continuously from their vents. It does not rise very far. The air is too wet and heavy. It spreads under the roofs and among the narrow streets, becoming a low ceiling that catches every light and turns it into a dull halo.

The heat does not blaze; it presses.

Even in dimness, the temperature is unmistakable. The iron handrails are warm. Rainwater in the drains is warm. The walls release stored heat through the night, so that the town feels less like it is cooling than sweating. When rain comes—and it comes often—it arrives hard and brief, hammering roofs, filling every depression, drumming on the broad leaves that grow wherever masonry has loosened. Then the rain ceases, and vapor rises at once from asphalt, brick, timber, mud, and machinery. The streets become mirrors for a few minutes. Then the mirrors break under ripples from pumps, runoff, and the slow drip of every eave.

Vegetation is lush but scattered according to the town’s own logic. There are no sweeping forests. Instead there are enormous leaves in alleys, cane-like clumps behind warehouses, fig roots worrying the joints of old walls, flowering vines thrown over power lines. Ferns grow from the mouths of drains. Moss gathers in the lettering of municipal signs. In one vacant lot, a banyan has enclosed a rusting transformer cabinet in its roots; the cabinet still hums inside the tree, and at night a thin blue light pulses between the roots.

Nothing eats the vegetation. No birds worry the fruit. No insects circle the lamps. No frogs call from the drainage ditches, though the ditches are perfectly made for frogs. The absence is not silent, because the town is loud, but it is noticeable in the shape of the soundscape: no chirr, no wingbeat, no furtive movement in leaves. Fruit ripens untouched, falls into warm water, swells pale, and dissolves. Vines advance without being chewed back. Fungal shelves widen on timber posts and remain pristine.

The loudness comes from systems.

Pumps throb beneath the streets, working continuously to keep lower basements and service tunnels from filling. Their rhythm is slow and immense, felt in the soles of the feet as much as heard. Somewhere near the freight square, a belt-driven generator clatters behind corrugated walls. The electrical grid has a deep wet buzz. Relay boxes click in sudden sequences. Overhead, old cable bundles sag between poles, and in rain they hiss like frying oil.

There are sirens too, but they do not signal emergencies exactly. They sound at dawn, though dawn is often only a gradual thinning of gray. They sound again when the basin’s main pumps change over. A third siren, lower and longer, announces the opening of the floodgates at the town’s southern edge. Then the water in certain gutters begins to move with purpose, carrying leaves, ash, floating scraps of wood, and the soft skins of fallen fruit toward the canal.

The canal is the town’s boundary.

It passes under a concrete arch on one side and vanishes into reed-dark marsh on the other. The marsh is not wild in the usual sense. It is threaded with pipes, narrow levees, inspection platforms, and half-submerged utility pylons. Some of these installations are still maintained. Others have become part of the wetland, wrapped in vines and blackened by years of rain. Beyond the canal, the horizon is close: a flat band of reeds, steam, and low cloud. There is nowhere grand to look toward. The place contains its own weather and its own distances.

People live here, though rarely in crowds. A figure may cross a footbridge with an umbrella made of clear plastic, disappear behind a pumping house, and not be seen again for an hour. Doors open briefly, releasing light and the smell of hot oil, wet fabric, fermented grain, machine grease. Windows are often covered with slatted shutters to keep rain out while allowing air through. In the evenings, small rooms glow behind them: kitchens, repair benches, offices of flood management, a bar with its floor raised above the street by a full meter.

The town has rules, posted on enamel signs in several places.

**DO NOT WALK IN STILL WATER AFTER DARK.**

**IF THE GROUND VIBRATES, STAND ON METAL OR STONE.**

**KEEP ALL DRAINS UNCOVERED.**

**NO OPEN FLAME DURING LOW FOG.**

The signs are old enough that their instructions have become part of the architecture. A person follows them the way one ducks under a beam or avoids a broken step. Still water is avoided not because it is visibly dangerous, but because it sometimes changes. A quiet pool in an empty lot may acquire a circular current after midnight. Water in a stairwell may stand three steps higher than it did an hour before, without rain. On rare nights, it gives off a faint interior light: not bright enough to illuminate anything, only enough to reveal its own depth.

The town does not panic over this. It has been here too long.

Its erosion is patient and accomplished. Brick corners have softened. Steps sag slightly toward drains. Concrete has opened into rounded cracks filled with root and moss. Old industrial paint flakes in long curled strips, revealing earlier colors beneath: yellow, blue, a municipal green that no longer appears anywhere else. The buildings have not been ruined so much as edited by heat, water, and use. Every surface bears evidence of a compromise that worked, at least for a while.

Toward evening, when the rain has passed and the dim sky goes from white-gray to violet-gray, steam gathers over the flooded square. The crane begins its daily turn. Pumps deepen their rhythm. The canal gates open with a noise like a large animal breathing, though there are no animals anywhere in the basin, no eyes in the reeds, no life watching from under the water.

Only the town watches itself.

Its lamps blink on one by one along the raised walks. In their reflections, the streets appear to continue below the surface: another wet, upside-down town, full of inverted roofs and trembling wires. For a moment, with the machinery loud around it and hot rain beginning again on the metal, it seems possible that the reflected town is the original one, and this dim settlement above it is only what has managed to remain afloat.
