The country has not finished hardening.

It lies at an extreme height, though there is no clean horizon from which to measure it. The ground rises in unfinished shelves and black terraces, climbing through a darkness the color of cooled iron. Below, or perhaps merely farther away, other levels open: broad mineral basins, high ridges still steaming from their seams, valleys roofed by slow-moving dust. The air is warm enough to feel thick against bare skin, but it contains no rain and no promise of rain. It tastes of hot stone, salt, sulfur, and something metallic without being metal.

There is almost no water here. Nothing flows. Nothing beads or drips. The rare hollows in the land are not ponds but shallow pans of fine pale powder, smooth as dried plaster until the ground shudders and breaks their surfaces into scales. Even the vapor rising from cracks is dry—mineral breath, carrying heat but no moisture. It gathers in dark veils over the cliffs, then thins and disappears without ever becoming cloud.

The terrain is young in the way a wound is young.

Plates of raw rock press up through beds of ash. Their edges are sharp, unweathered, and frequently glowing from within with a dull orange-red that gives no real illumination. In some places the crust has buckled into long, high folds like the backs of buried animals; in others it is opening along straight fresh faults, revealing layers of white salt, green-black glass, and granular red stone. Whole slopes slump at once. A ridge may stand for an hour, then soften, split, and pour downhill in a slow avalanche of hot fragments.

Erosion has not yet learned this place. There are no softened ravines, no river-cut canyons, no rounded boulders. Everything is new-faced: broken planes, splintered ledges, brittle columns thrusting up from the earth before they have decided whether to become towers or collapse. The ground looks less formed than assembled in a hurry.

Despite the violence, the land is sparse. There are long expanses where nothing interrupts the blackness except a scattering of pale stone teeth and the dim ember-light within distant cracks. Space dominates. A person could cross an entire basin and see almost no sign of another presence, only their own path marked briefly in dust before the next tremor erased it.

Here and there, on stable patches of basalt, stand small structures.

They are not machines, and do not feel made by a technological people. Some are circles of upright slabs, fitted without mortar, their surfaces scored with heat fractures. Some are squat shelters half-buried in ash, constructed from flat mineral plates laid over one another in careful spirals. A few are tall, narrow cairns built from white stones that do not occur naturally nearby. They look old only because the country around them is so new. Their interiors are empty. There are no tools, no fabrics, no bones, no stored water. Perhaps they are markers. Perhaps they are places where something once waited for the ground to settle.

The darkness is not absolute. The sky holds a low bruised glow, as though a sun exists behind several layers of smoke and stone. It is enough to distinguish distances poorly: a nearby cliff is black; a farther one is charcoal; the farthest ridges are faint violet silhouettes suspended in dust. At intervals, broad sheets of static light ripple high overhead, colorless and silent. For a moment the entire landscape becomes visible—the broken plateaus, the pale dust pans, the scattered stone shelters—and then it returns to obscurity.

There is no plant life. No grass grips the slopes. No lichen softens the rocks. No thorn, root, leaf, or seed has found a foothold in the fresh and shifting mineral skin. Yet the land is not entirely sterile. Along the warmest fissures, thin films form: matte gray blooms that spread across the rock in branching fans, neither moss nor fungus exactly. They shrink when the cracks cool and return when the heat rises. In the deepest vent mouths, translucent crusts cling to the walls, slowly inflating and collapsing as the earth exhales. They may be alive, or may simply be another way minerals arrange themselves under pressure.

No animals move among them. There are no wings crossing the dim sky, no feet in the dust, no calling from unseen gullies. The absence becomes one of the landscape’s largest features. Sound has nowhere to go but across empty distances.

Mostly, there is quiet.

Then the world rearranges itself.

A deep crack travels beneath the ground, too low to be called a sound at first. It arrives through the soles, through the teeth, through the ribs. Distant shelves answer with a volley of dry breaks. Dust slides from cliffs in soft whispering curtains. Sometimes a vent opens with a blunt report and throws up a fountain of incandescent grit, not flame exactly but powdered stone heated beyond its patience. It arcs into the darkness and lands miles away, ticking against the ground as it cools.

The storms here are storms of matter. Black winds race through the high basins, lifting sheets of ash, salt, and razor-fine glass. They do not bring weather from elsewhere; they are made by the land’s own heat and motion. They carve no lasting shapes, because the rock is too young and continually remade. A storm may expose a white ridge one day and bury it the next. The little slab shelters survive only in the lee of larger faults, and even those have been partially swallowed, their entrances reduced to low dark slots.

At the highest elevations, the earth seems nearly suspended. Great rafts of stone hover in the haze—not floating, perhaps, but held up by columns of heat and dust so dense that their bases cannot be seen. Their tops are broad and flat, carrying fields of loose black pebbles that tremble constantly. Every so often one of these high platforms sheds an edge. The fallen mass descends slowly through the murk, breaking apart in silence until the delayed impact rolls through the country like distant thunder.

There are rules here, though they are not stable rules.

Nothing keeps its shape merely because it has acquired one. Heat rises from below and insists on change. Weight insists on collapse. The dry air preserves every exposed surface perfectly until the next fracture destroys it. Darkness hides the making of the world, but not its consequences. A path becomes a fault. A shelter becomes a cairn. A cairn becomes a scatter of stones, and the scatter is swallowed by a new shelf of ash.

Still, among the upheaval, there are pauses.

On certain broad terraces, between one tremor and the next, the dust lies entirely still. The warmth of the ground comes upward through it. The sky glows faintly. Far off, a ridge lifts itself grain by grain from a basin that did not exist yesterday. In such moments the place feels less like a ruin or a disaster than a beginning: a high, dry world practicing the difficult act of becoming solid.
