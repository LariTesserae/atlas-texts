The high country has been worn almost past the idea of mountains.

What remains are enormous old backs of stone, tilted into the weather: a country of rounded ridges, broad shelves, and ravines so ancient that their cliffs have slumped into green slopes. Nothing is sharp except the occasional exposed seam of dark rock, and even these are furred with moss, root-mats, lichens, and small flowering plants. The land seems to have been softened over immeasurable time by rain, frost, hooves, and the patient pressure of things growing.

It stands very high above the lower world. On clear mornings, a traveler can look from one ridge and see ranges of other green-backed uplands fading blue in every direction, with clouds moving below some of the passes. The air is thin enough to make climbing a matter of breath and patience, yet it is warm where the sun settles on the slopes. The warmth has a wet edge to it. Rain does not often fall in grand storms here; it comes in passing veils, in brief silver showers, in fog that condenses on every leaf. At dawn, the grasslands sweat. Water shines in cups of stone, in folded leaves large enough to hold a hand, in black peat pools hidden beneath the vegetation.

There is very little bare ground.

The plants are not merely upon the landscape; they are the landscape’s upper skin. Great grasslands sweep over the rounded hills, but grass is only the first layer. Beneath it are dense cushions of herbs and old root-webs. Bracken fills shallow gullies. Thorn trees grow in the shelter of rock outcrops, their roots prying apart stone that has already been split by a thousand winters. In some valleys, huge woody plants have bent and interwoven into low, vaulted thickets. Their trunks never grow tall—wind and altitude deny them that—but they spread outward, braided together, making living walls and roofs.

The empty spaces are correspondingly immense.

A person may walk all day across an open saddle without meeting another person or seeing any building more substantial than a smoke stain on a distant slope. The vegetation is abundant, nearly smothering, but it does not crowd the eye in the way of a forest. The country remains wide. There are broad, exposed distances between groves, between standing stones, between the few inhabited hollows. The sky is always a major part of the land: pale and wide at noon, close and violet when weather gathers, full of immense moving shadows.

And everywhere, animals.

At first the eye notices the large ones: long-legged browsing beasts with dark, damp noses, moving in loose herds through the grass; heavy woolly grazers along the high slopes; bright-plumed ground birds erupting from the fern beds; packs of lean ridge-hounds following the scent of something unseen. But the longer one looks, the more the whole place resolves into movement. Insects shimmer over wet ground. Beetles travel on every fallen stem. Small mammals rustle beneath the root-mats. Birds occupy every layer of the air, from tiny hovering nectar-feeders around the flowers to broad-winged scavengers circling the thermal currents.

The animal life is so thick that trails are made and remade daily by passing bodies. The earth near waterholes is cut into a thousand overlapping hoofprints. On the open uplands, paths run like braided cords through the vegetation—not roads, but the accumulated decisions of generations of grazing creatures.

The sound is almost unbearable in certain hours.

At sunrise, the entire plateau seems to wake at once. Bird calls strike from every slope and return from every hollow. There are whistles, clacks, deep booming throat-notes, shrieks from the thorn groves, and the constant high thread of insects. Herd animals call to their young. Predators answer one another from ridge to ridge. Wind pushes through the dry stalks with the sound of surf, while hidden water slips and gurgles under moss and roots.

Even at night, silence never properly arrives. It only changes instruments. The day birds settle, and then the frogs begin in the damp hollows. Something large coughs in the distance. Wings beat heavily through the dark. The vegetation itself ticks and sighs as moisture gathers and drips from leaf to leaf.

The people who live here do not try to tame much of it.

Their structures are scattered and small: stone windbreaks roofed with turf; low circular shelters woven from branches and sealed with clay; raised drying racks on ridges where meat, seedpods, and medicinal leaves can catch the wind. Some are no more than a platform tucked beneath a bent tree, with a hearth ring beside it. Others are seasonal enclosures made from stacked slate, used when herds pass through. The oldest shelters have become almost indistinguishable from the hills. Grass grows over their roofs. Roots thread their walls. A doorway may be marked by a hanging hide, a line of bleached bones, or an upright stone rubbed smooth by generations of hands.

Their tools are simple and durable: flaked blades of black stone, bone needles, stone-headed digging sticks, woven traps, wooden flutes whose tones carry over long distances. Metal is rare enough to be inherited. A small bronze hook, a tarnished knife, or a circle of worked copper may remain with a family for centuries, repaired rather than replaced.

The people travel lightly because the land is too alive to permit fixed borders. A valley that is safe in one season becomes the feeding ground of dangerous beasts in another. A pass may close under fog and rain. A spring may disappear beneath a root-bloom, then return fifty paces away. The rules of the place are practical and old:

Never camp in the lowest hollow, because the damp gathers there and so do the night-feeders.

Never follow an animal trail into a thicket after dusk.

Do not cut the braided trees unless you are prepared to lose the shelter of their descendants.

When the ridge birds go quiet, listen for the large thing moving beneath the grass.

The landscape has one slight strangeness, subtle enough that newcomers mistake it for weather. In the oldest parts of the plateau, certain plants respond to passage. Not quickly, not dramatically; a hillside does not turn its face like an animal. But the high flowering grasses lean after herds have passed, and the leaf-vaults over old paths sometimes close more tightly in the hours after human footsteps. The people say the ground remembers weight. They do not find this remarkable. A place covered so completely in living tissue ought, they say, to notice who walks upon it.

By afternoon, rain may drift through one valley while another lies bright and dry a mile away. Steam rises from warm stone. Animals continue their migrations across the open slopes. The high land breathes, feeds, calls, grows, decays, and slowly covers every abandoned thing.

Nothing here feels newly made.

The ravines, the roots, the worn stones, the animal paths, the small human shelters—all belong to a world that has had a very long time to become itself.
