Below the oldest district of an unnamed city there is a place called the Cistern Quarter, though it is not a quarter in any ordinary sense. It occupies a single buried chamber, no larger than a market square, pressed between foundation stone and bedrock. The people who built it never expected it to grow. There was nowhere for it to grow into.

Its ceiling is low enough that the highest roofs nearly touch it.

Everything in the chamber has been fitted into everything else: houses wedged into niches, workshops built over sluices, walkways nailed between stone piers, sleeping shelves above doors, doors beneath stairs, stairs descending into other people's kitchens. The whole settlement seems less constructed than packed—like debris gathered in the bend of a river, except every beam, pot, ladder, and lintel has been deliberately placed. It is dense with use. One can stand in the central passage and hear a spoon against a bowl, a knife sharpening, an argument behind a plaster wall, a wooden valve being turned somewhere overhead.

There are perhaps forty dwellings, though no one agrees on the number. Some are only curtained alcoves. Some extend into cracks in the rock, their rooms thin as coffins. The walls are made from salvaged brick, fired clay tile, old millstone fragments, dark local stone, and timber gone black with damp. Iron is too valuable to waste: a single hinge may have changed hands for generations. Most mechanisms are wood, leather, stone, rope, and carefully balanced weights. Waterwheels no larger than serving trays turn slowly in channels under the floors. Their movement works bellows, lifts buckets, drives tiny grinding stones, and rings warning clappers when a conduit clogs.

Water is everywhere, but never dramatically.

It sweats from the walls in thousands of clear beads. It collects in the seams between stones, drips from ceiling ribs, and moves blackly through narrow gutters cut along every footpath. The air feels rinsed and cold against the face. It smells of wet slate, clay, old smoke, lamp grease, and the mineral tang of deep water. At ankle height there is always a faint chill, as if the chamber is standing in a shallow invisible pool.

The settlement was built around a spring that rises from a circular shaft at its center. The shaft has been capped with a lattice of heavy stone bars. Through the gaps, one can see water moving far below: not rushing, but turning upon itself in a slow, tireless dark. The inhabitants draw from it by means of hand-wound reels. They do not throw offerings into it. They do not lean over it for long. A rule, repeated to every child, says that the spring remembers the shape of anything reflected in it.

No plants grow here. Not moss, not algae, not the pale rootlets that sometimes reach down from streets above. The wet walls remain bare. The damp never greens. Even scraps of food seem reluctant to sprout or rot; they dry, soften, sour, or are eaten, but do not put forth life. This absence is conspicuous. Clay pots hold no flowers. Window ledges—which look upward into nothing but stone—are empty except for tools, cups, and occasional little cairns of chalk-white pebbles.

There are animals, but few. Small blind crabs sometimes emerge from drainage channels, translucent and slow, their shells nearly colorless. They are gathered carefully and returned to the water, never cooked. Once in a while a surface rat finds a crack and arrives half-mad with panic; its presence throws the entire quarter into silence until it is caught or disappears. The most familiar creature is a long, white moth that appears only near the lamps, one at a time, perhaps three or four in a year. Nobody knows what it eats. Nobody has ever seen a caterpillar.

The loudness of the Cistern Quarter comes from containment. Sound has no place to go.

Drips strike basins with bell-like precision. Wheels knock. Water slaps beneath floorboards. The stone carries every hammer blow and sends it through the chamber as a softened but persistent pulse. Voices are commonly raised, not in anger but necessity. Conversations travel along pipes. A person on the east walk can hear a lullaby from a west-side sleeping niche, though the singer may have no idea they are being heard.

There is also the continual sound of the upper city: a distant weight, filtered through soil and masonry. Cart wheels pass overhead as a muted thunder. Footsteps become granular rain. At certain hours, when traffic gathers in the square above, the ceiling gives a low collective hum. The people below call this the Sky Speaking. They do not interpret it as words. It is simply proof that the dry world continues.

Light comes from squat lamps made of clay, with thick wicks burning rendered fat or oily sediments skimmed from old channels. Their flames are small and guarded by cloudy glass. Every surface has been darkened by soot except where many hands have worn it smooth: railings, doorframes, valve handles, the rims of water jars. Light pools around these places and fades quickly. A few mineral seams in the ceiling hold a dull bluish glimmer, insufficient to see by but enough to make the darkness seem inhabited.

The chamber has not changed in anyone’s memory.

A leak that has fallen in the same spot for two hundred years is not called a leak but a clock. A stone step hollowed by feet is left untouched, even though it collects water; it is a known hazard, and known hazards are preferable to repaired ones. Families inherit sections of pipe, stretches of gutter, particular roof patches, particular noises. To live here is to know which groan belongs to which beam, which drip means ordinary seepage, which means the spring is rising by a finger’s width.

Change is treated not as possibility but as damage.

Nothing is moved without leaving a marker: three chalk lines on a wall, a knot in a cord, a spoken notice passed from doorway to doorway. New construction is built in the old manner, using pieces taken from things that have already failed. The settlement’s geometry is consequently ancient and self-referential. A bridge may be made from the door of a demolished room; the room’s threshold may become a shelf; the shelf, years later, might be cut down into wedges for a waterwheel. Matter circulates, but the arrangement remains almost exactly itself.

The residents rarely go to the surface, and surface people rarely come below. Those who do descend are struck first by the noise, then by the closeness, then by the fact that the quarter does not feel abandoned despite its dimness. It is intensely occupied. Each small pocket of space has a purpose. Each wall bears the polish of shoulders. Each channel is watched. The city above may have avenues, weather, trees, windows opening onto distance.

Here there is only stone, water, work, and the crowded persistence of people who have made a world in the thickness of a wall.
