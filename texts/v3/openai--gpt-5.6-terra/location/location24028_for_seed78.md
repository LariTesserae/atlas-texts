At this altitude the world has not yet decided whether it is mountain, forest, or weather.

The ground rises in long black folds beneath a roof of living growth. There is almost no open earth. Every ledge, talus slope, ravine wall, and outcropping is covered by plants—not merely draped in moss or rooted with trees, but made habitable by a single immense vegetal occupation. Pale root-mats knit the young stone together. Vines as thick as ship ropes cross empty space from crag to crag. Great fronds, dark green nearly to black, unfurl from clefts in the rock and hold the cold sky away.

Yet the country is spacious. It is not a crowded jungle. The plants make vast architecture with long intervals beneath it: halls of air, ravines open for miles, hanging canopies suspended over unlit valleys. A person could travel for days under a single leaf-vein, with nothing beside them but the slow dripping of condensation and the distant motion of branches far too large to understand at once.

The land is young. Peaks have recently shoved themselves upward, their faces sharp and raw where the roots have not yet conquered them. Black basalt shards glitter with ice. New ridges stand like broken teeth above the plant cover. In places, warm vapor leaks from narrow cracks, immediately cooling into white breath; in others, rainwater gathers in temporary bowls among the roots, dark and shining, then vanishes through porous stone. Water is never abundant enough to make a river of the place, but it is everywhere in small ways: beading on the undersides of leaves, running in brief silver threads down stalks, frosting the furred bark of trunk-vines before dawn.

There is little daylight. The upper atmosphere is usually masked by an overcast of low, bruised cloud, and what light comes through is blue-gray and diluted, the color of light seen through old ice. Much of the country lives in permanent twilight. Beneath the high canopy it is darker still. The plants have adapted: some hold a faint milky luminosity in their ribs; some develop powdery reflective skins, so that a traveler’s torch or lantern returns from the walls in soft dull halos. At night, when the clouds open, stars appear in thin hard ranks between the leaves.

The cold is patient rather than murderous. It lies in the stone and in the long breaths of wind that move through the high passes. Ice forms at the edges of every pool. Leaves stiffen and chime together. But the vegetation is warm at its core. Close to the great root systems, there is a vegetal heat, a damp fermenting breath. The forest-mountain smells of crushed evergreen, wet soil, bitter sap, and the faint mineral odor of fresh-broken rock.

Sound is unavoidable.

The wind moves through the canopy like surf breaking in an underground chamber. Huge leaves slap and fold. Hollow stems boom when gusts enter them. Water falls unpredictably—one drop at a time, then suddenly in a heavy rush as a tilted leaf empties its collected rain onto the stones below. In the distance there are collapses: young cliff faces shedding rock, root-bridges tearing loose and falling for seconds before they strike anything.

And among all of it are animals.

They are numerous, though seldom close. Most travel by the upper growth, where there is more food and less cold. Their calls descend through the darkness: trilling flocks that migrate along vine highways; barking, antlered climbers with broad padded feet; long-winged gliders that cry like torn fabric as they pass from ridge to ridge. In the lower ravines, pale burrowing things move beneath the root-mats, their collective shifting audible as a dry, granular murmur. Predators are heard more often than seen. Their calls do not echo properly; the plants drink the sound, pass it along their stems, and release it somewhere else.

Some nights the entire forest seems to be shouting to itself.

The people who come here do not build much. There are too few level places, too much growth, too much weather. Their structures are scattered and temporary: little platforms lashed into the crooks of colossal roots; low stone shelters built against warm vents; ladder towers leaning up through the canopy to harvest seed pods, resin, and nesting eggs. The tools are simple—flint wedges, bone needles, stone adzes, blades of sharpened obsidian, cords braided from the long white fibers stripped out of vine bark.

They mark routes with bundles of dried luminous moss tied beneath leaves. A trail may consist only of these lights, each one far from the next: a small greenish ember in a world otherwise entirely swallowed by vegetation and dark.

There are rules, learned before they become superstition.

Do not sleep beneath a leaf whose edges curl upward; it is collecting rain.

Do not cut a root that crosses bare stone; it may be holding the slope together.

When the canopy suddenly goes silent, climb or hide. Something large is moving above.

Do not follow a voice in the fog unless you can hear its feet.

And if the ground begins to hum, leave the ravine. The mountain is still growing.
