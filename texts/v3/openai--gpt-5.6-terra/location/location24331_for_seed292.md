At this height the water has no business being there, yet it has been there long enough to acquire jurisdiction.

The valley lies above the cloud line, a drowned basin in an ancient mountain range whose peaks have been worn down almost to their roots. The mountains are not sharp. They have spent too many ages under weather. Their slopes are broad-backed and fluted, their cliffs softened into tiers of dark stone, and every ledge is filled with black water. From a distance the whole country appears to be a chain of inland seas poured into the hollows of a ruined highland.

Rain comes almost continually, but not as a fine mist. It arrives in slanting gray walls, sudden and muscular, racing across the lakes under low skies. The water level rises and falls by inches in an afternoon. Streams become rivers, rivers spread into marshes, marshes disappear beneath wind-ruffled sheets of cold water. Then, for an hour, the rain thins and the landscape emerges: drowned tree trunks, islands of peat, the black backs of rock ridges, strips of gravel road, a derelict loading crane standing knee-deep in the lake.

The basin is cool even in its brief summer. Snow stays in creases high on the surrounding slopes, shaded beneath old escarpments, while below it, in the wet valleys, green life crowds itself into every possible space. Alder and willow grow from flooded banks, roots exposed and tangled like woven baskets. Moss puts a thick, brilliant skin over stone walls and engine housings. Ferns fill the cuttings beside old rail beds. Dense mats of reed, sedge, and watergrass bow under rainfall and rise again after it passes. On floating islands of root and peat, small birches lean together, their pale trunks reflected in water the color of iron.

There are animals everywhere, though they are often heard before they are seen. Geese and gulls call from the lake. Cormorants sit on the rusting tops of pylons with their wings spread to dry whenever the weather gives them a chance. Trout run up the colder channels in flashing groups. Eels inhabit the deep, slow ditches that were once drainage canals. Otters leave slides in the mud around the abandoned foundations. Deer come down from the slopes at dusk and stand chest-deep in the shallows, feeding on submerged grasses. In the reed beds there is constant small movement: insects, frogs, water voles, nesting birds. The country is not pristine—nothing so clean or solitary—but it is full.

Its sounds are broad and relentless. Wind comes over the surrounding summits and falls into the basin with force enough to flatten the reeds. Water strikes broken concrete, rattles through culverts, pours from one terrace to the next. Rain hammers roofs, lake surfaces, metal tanks, and leaves. The calls of birds carry an astonishing distance across open water. Every now and then, beneath the natural roar, a machinery sound begins: the low thump of a diesel pump, a cable whining on a winch, a generator catching unevenly and then settling into a steady industrial growl.

People have not abandoned the basin, exactly. They have adapted to it in a piecemeal way.

A few structures persist on raised ground: a weather station painted white and red, a pump house of corrugated metal, a stone cottage with a newer roof, a fish-processing shed near the old rail siding. Their lights appear early in the afternoon when the rain darkens the sky. There are narrow boats with covered engines tied to posts along flooded lanes. Some are used to check traps and nets; some carry repair crews to substations and intake gates; some simply take children across water that used to be pasture.

The old industrial works are scattered rather than concentrated. There is no active town, no factory district—only remnants of extraction and water control embedded in the terrain. A narrow-gauge railway appears from under a screen of willow, runs across a causeway, and ends in the lake where its final section collapsed decades ago. A rusted bucket-wheel excavator stands in a half-flooded quarry, its enormous circular teeth softened by moss. Concrete spillways, built to impose a simpler order on the mountain water, have become waterfalls in their own right. The newer installations are smaller and more stubborn: hydroelectric turbines housed in low sheds, transmission lines strung from ridge to ridge, pumps protecting a few usable fields, weather instruments recording rain no one can stop.

The people who remain know the water’s habits. They mark safe routes with tall poles because landmarks vanish when the lake rises. They build doors high, keep fuel dry, own more rope than furniture, and speak about currents in the flooded roads as people elsewhere speak about traffic. Children are taught to swim before they are trusted near the banks. The rule of the place is simple: nothing stays dry merely because it was dry yesterday.

Another rule follows from the first: everything must be tied down, raised up, or allowed to drift.

The older rock accepts this without drama. It is a landscape at the end of a very long argument with weather. The cliffs no longer resist erosion; they guide it. Water finds the same channels it found before any road or mine existed. It follows fractures through slate and granite, gathers in bowls scoured by ice, drops over ledges rounded by ten thousand winters. Human structures, too, are slowly being sorted into what can endure and what cannot. Steel loses its paint. Wood darkens. Concrete cracks and grows grass. A pump house remains useful if someone maintains it; otherwise it becomes one more island for birds.

In breaks between storms, ordinary daylight spreads over the basin. The clouds lift enough to show the upper walls of the mountains, blue-gray and immense. Water reflects them in broken pieces. The lushness seems almost excessive against so much stone: green hillsides, wet leaves, reeds trembling in the wind, moss over every discarded beam. Smoke from a generator or stove rises briefly and is pulled apart.

Then the next rainfront arrives along the high ridges, whitening the distance. The lake roughens. The drowned roads disappear once more. Engines start in their sheds, birds lift from the reeds, and the whole high valley returns to its prevailing state: cold, loud, living, flooded, and in motion.
