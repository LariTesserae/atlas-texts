High above the city’s usable levels, behind a service door with no sign on it, there is a constructed ravine.

It occupies the seam between two process towers: a narrow vertical country of steel retaining walls, poured channels, cable ladders, inspection bridges, and banks of machinery standing apart from one another in the damp. It is not large. From one end to the other, perhaps a person could walk it in fifteen minutes if the alarms were quiet and the surfaces were dry. But it has weather, elevation, runoff, seasons of a kind. It feels removed from the building around it, as if the building has accidentally manufactured a piece of high terrain inside its own ribs.

The floor is made of sloped concrete panels, all young and pale beneath their stains. Their edges are crisp. Expansion joints run through them like dark clefts, spilling thin water into lower trays. There is no soil anywhere. No rock. Every apparent ledge is a bracketed slab; every cliff is a wall of riveted plate; every streambed is a fabricated gutter armored in grating. The place has the sharpness of recent construction, though its wetness has already begun to alter it. Mineral ghosts spread from bolts. Rust-colored threads flow out of seams and stop in fan-shaped deposits. Lime blooms rise where cold water persists at the base of a wall.

Water is always arriving.

It condenses on the chilled pipes that cross overhead in bundles, gathers under insulation jackets, slides down the tower faces, and falls into the ravine in irregular bright cords. Some comes down deliberately through square-mouthed outlets: warm gray process water, clean roof runoff, coolant overflow, collected fog. It falls from heights of ten or twelve stories and strikes perforated decks below with such force that the decks tremble. The spray hangs in the air, thick enough to make distant lamps appear haloed and close objects appear farther away than they are.

At the lowest point, a sump works continuously. Its black water rises and descends behind a grated opening. Pumps take turns without human ceremony. One starts with a heavy electric cough; another gives a deeper answer from somewhere beyond the wall. Valves snap. Pressure changes pass through pipes in shuddering waves. A broad discharge tube periodically sends a surge down the central channel, and for several minutes the constructed ravine becomes a torrent: water racing over ribbed concrete, catching in steps, throwing itself white against steel baffles before being swallowed into the drainworks.

The sound is too large for the space.

It is not one sound but layers pressed together until they become physical: fans roaring in shafts; pump motors turning with a wet, strained hum; rain or condensate hammering on sheet metal; steam hissing from relief lines; relays clacking; warning bells making brief, hopeless appearances above the rest. Conversation would be impossible here. Even thought takes on the rhythm of machinery. The noise fills the chest and makes loose tools vibrate on handrails. In the narrowest portions of the ravine, reflections multiply it. The walls seem to shout back.

Yet there are small intervals of quiet—not silence, never that—where a pump completes its cycle and the heavy flow has not yet resumed. In those intervals, individual drops can be heard striking particular surfaces. A slow drip on a hollow guardrail. Water ticking across a cable tray. A thin stream finding the lip of a joint and falling into darkness.

Vegetation has not been planted, exactly. A few persistent green things have found the margins where dust, leaked nutrient solution, and mineral sediment accumulate: a fist-sized patch of moss beneath a weeping flange; pale algae in a trough that receives light from a maintenance window; two or three wiry blades growing out of a filter box whose mesh has caught years of airborne debris. They are sparse enough to seem almost accidental, but they make the place feel inhabited by a slower process than production.

There are no animals. Nothing wings through the mist. Nothing skitters under the platforms. The absence is conspicuous, especially in a wet place. No insects take advantage of the lamps; no birds nest in the warm ducts. The ravine’s ecology is composed of water chemistry, oxidation, bacterial films, electrical heat, and the slow migration of deposits across built surfaces. Its life is in the green-black slick on a constantly wet wall, in the trembling membranes of foam gathered at a drain, in the invisible work of corrosion beneath paint.

Light enters only where it has been permitted. High above, frosted industrial panes admit a colorless diluted day. Most of the illumination comes from sodium fixtures and service lamps mounted beneath grilles: orange pools, pale blue inspection strips, a red beacon revolving behind a sheet of rain. The wet surfaces carry this light everywhere in broken fragments. A pipe shines as though it has been lacquered. The channel floor becomes a long black mirror disturbed by runoff. In a puddle, an entire tower may appear upside down, complete with its blinking warning light.

The air is cool despite the machinery. It smells of wet cement, hot insulation, metal filings, ozone from old electrical cabinets, and the faint medicinal scent of treated water. Mist settles on clothing quickly. Handrails are cold and slick. Every surface seems either recently washed or perpetually about to be washed.

There are rules here, posted in fading black letters on white enamel signs:

**KEEP TO MARKED GRATING.**  
**DO NOT ENTER CHANNEL DURING AUTOMATIC FLUSH.**  
**HEARING PROTECTION REQUIRED.**  
**PUMPS MAY START WITHOUT WARNING.**

The final rule explains the landscape better than any map could.

Nothing waits for a visitor. The ravine does not become picturesque in the presence of a person. It continues its cycles: gathering water from the elevated structure, moving it downward by designed gravity, cooling itself, cleaning itself, carrying away what it produces. Its slopes are young because they are maintained; its cliffs are sharp because they were cut by machines; its streams have been engineered to forget their own paths.

And still, in the dim space between the towers, with water descending from manufactured heights and green beginning at the edges of drain grates, it resembles a mountain place.

A hidden, deafening valley.

A weather system built entirely by human hands.
