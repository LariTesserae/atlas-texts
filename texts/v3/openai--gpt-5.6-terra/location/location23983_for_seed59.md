The settlement occupies a wet shoulder of mountain where the slope briefly relents before falling away into cloud. It is not large: a few hundred paces from its upper gate to the last reed houses at the brink, perhaps. But it is packed so tightly with roots, roofs, animals, people, platforms, pools, poles, and dripping leaves that it feels less like a village than a knot tied in the mountainside.

Water is everywhere, though there is no single river through the place. It comes out of the ground in clear threads. It beads on dark stones. It falls from roof-thatch, runs through channels cut with stone adzes, gathers in foot-deep hollows, then disappears beneath mats of roots. The mountain is made of layered slate and pale porous rock, worn smooth where hands and bare feet have passed over it for generations. In the oldest parts of the settlement, the paths are not paths so much as shallow watercourses with stepping stones laid through them. Children know where the moss is slick. Goats know which planks flex. Dogs drink from every gutter.

The people have built with what the slope provides: split cedar, woven willow, bark sheets, stone gathered from old slides, and living trunks trained into walls. Many houses begin as a circle of saplings bent inward and tied together; over years, they thicken and fuse, becoming solid green ribs beneath layers of reed thatch. A house may have roots growing through its floor and branches holding up its smoke hole. The larger communal buildings are made from whole groves persuaded into shape—arched halls whose walls are leaf, bark, and woven vine, their interiors smelling of damp wood, hearth ash, wool, and fermented grain.

Nothing is separate from vegetation for long. The retaining walls are thick with ferns. Moss fills every joint in the stone. Broad-leaved plants rise from roof edges. Squash and beans climb ladders intended for people. A low fence becomes a hedge; a hedge becomes a nesting place; a nesting place becomes an argument between households when the birds begin eating the seed stores. The mountain seems to be slowly reclaiming the town, but the town has long ago accepted this and made a craft of directing the reclamation.

At the upper edge, water is caught in a chain of shallow ponds, each bordered by flat stones and dense rushes. These are not ornamental. They hold fish, frogs, water beetles, and the small pale ducks that the settlement keeps in noisy, quarrelsome flocks. Overflow from the ponds runs downhill through carved troughs, under footbridges, beside workshops, and into garden beds terraced along the slope. Every usable patch of earth is cultivated. Cabbages sit in raised rings of stone. Green onions grow between the doorsteps. Medicinal herbs occupy damp cracks in the walls. Under the eaves, strings of mushrooms fruit from rotting logs, their caps silver-gray in the cool daylight.

The air is cool enough that breath sometimes shows at dawn, especially when cloud settles among the buildings. By midday the light is ordinary and clear in breaks between weather: pale sun on wet leaves, shadows under platforms, a white glare on standing water. Yet the mountain rarely feels dry. Rain may pass in ten minutes—hard, rattling rain that turns every roof into a drum—or remain all day as a fine mist that softens the far cliffs and makes the settlement seem gathered inside its own breath.

It is a loud place.

Water is always speaking: drip, rill, overflow, sluice, splash beneath walkways. Wind pushes through the high branches with a wet rushing sound. People call from one level to another because walls and vegetation obscure sight. Stone pounders strike grain in mortars. A leatherworker scrapes hides beneath an awning. Axes bite green wood. Someone is always splitting kindling, mending a basket, beating mud from a woven mat, urging an animal away from a garden.

And the animals are never quiet.

There are goats on every slope, narrow-footed and clever, standing on terraces where they have no right to stand. There are pigs rooting in designated muddy pens and escaping them daily. Chickens fill the paths, underfoot and indignant. Ducks make the ponds tremble with their complaints. Small mountain cattle, shaggy and dark, are tethered under broad eaves where their warmth steams the air. Dogs patrol the walkways in pairs, barking at strangers, hawks, clouds, and one another. Cats live in the grain sheds and on the roof beams. Swallows nest beneath the bridges. Ravens gather on the high poles at the edge of the drop, watching for scraps.

Beyond the last houses, the vegetation becomes wilder but no less dense. Alder and mountain ash stand among dripping boulders. Ferns rise waist-high. Blue-black berries crowd the shaded ground. There are deer trails, boar wallows, fox dens under fallen roots. In the evening, the forest begins to answer the settlement: frogs from the pools, night birds from the ravines, the coughing call of something larger moving through wet brush. The people keep fires burning at the outer platforms, not because the wilderness is magical or unknowable, but because animals are hungry, weather turns quickly, and a child can vanish from sight after stepping three paces into the green.

The village’s rules are practical and old. No one blocks a water channel. No one cuts a living wall without telling the household whose roof it holds. Goats are tethered after the second evening bell. The upper ponds are for drinking before washing. Waste goes downhill, always downhill. A bridge that creaks is repaired before sleep, because rain can come at night and turn a trickle into a brown, branch-filled surge. Every family owes labor to the retaining walls before the wet season. Every child learns the names of the safe stones.

The mountain has been weathering for ages, and the settlement has learned its moods. Its terraces are not new cuts but softened old ones, their edges rounded by moss and repeated repair. The great stone stair at the center has sagged slightly with the slope; the handrail beside it is a polished living trunk bent long ago by hands now forgotten. There are old landslide scars beyond the gardens, healed over with brush and young trees. There are boulders in the lower quarter that everyone builds around because moving them would be harder than accepting them as part of the town. One supports a smokehouse. Another forms the back wall of a family’s sleeping room. A third is used as the public notice stone, where messages are scratched in charcoal or tied on strips of bark.

Life here is turbulent not because the place is falling apart, but because everything is always in motion. Rain changes the paths. Animals change the traffic. A branch comes down and three households reorganize their roofs. A pond overflows and everyone runs with baskets, shovels, and bare hands. A sow gives birth beneath a stairwell. A trading party arrives from the lower valleys with salt, clay pots, and bronze needles, and the narrow lanes become a market for an afternoon. Then fog rolls in, swallowing the outer terraces, and the whole settlement draws inward around its hearths and animal sheds.

From below, seen through breaks in the trees, it might look like a dark green mound clinging to the mountain: smoke lifting from leaf-covered roofs, pale strips of water shining between them, birds turning above it in dense sudden clouds. Up close, it is a crowded, wet, warm-bodied place, full of work and noise. A place where the ground is never entirely still, where roots hold the houses together, and where every wall, path, and roof has been made in negotiation with water.
