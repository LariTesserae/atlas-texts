The valley is low enough that it has never learned the difference between ground and water.

At night—though night may be the only condition here—the land lies broad and shallow beneath a ceiling of black vapor. The horizon is present mostly as an idea: a darker belt of reeds, derricks, embankments, and half-sunken machinery arranged across the wet plain. There is no moon. There are stars occasionally, but they shine from beneath the water rather than above it, trembling in flooded wheel ruts and in the broad, tannin-dark canals that divide the ground.

The air is warm and close. It has the weight of a boiler room after rain, smelling of iron, wet clay, sour green growth, hot oil, and the sweet rot of things that have had time to soften without quite decaying. Moisture gathers everywhere. It beads along cables and handrails, swells on the edges of corrugated roofs, gathers in the sockets of old bolts. The rocks, where rocks rise from the mud, seem to sweat. Their faces are raw and newly exposed, split into bright angular planes as though the valley had only recently cracked open and had not yet had time to become rounded.

The terrain is made of young things: fresh-cut channels, unstable spoil banks, sharp heaps of red-black slag, pale sandbars thrown up by water that is not entirely trustworthy. In places the soil opens in long seams, revealing layers of clay, coal-colored sediment, shells, and buried metal. Some of these seams breathe warm vapor. Some fill slowly with water. Some lead down farther than their shape should permit, descending in narrow black stairs that continue beneath the waterline.

Vegetation has not taken much hold. There are salt grasses, clotted sedges, and ranks of reed stems with blades sharp enough to slice bare skin. Their roots make dense floating mats over the ditches. A few trees stand apart from one another: low mangrove-like things with swollen roots, or slender palms whose crowns are sparse and gray with ash. They do not form forests. They mark boundaries. One tree may stand beside a pumping station, another beside an abandoned concrete shrine, another alone in five acres of mud, each with its roots exposed and shining in the dark.

Yet the lack of vegetation does not mean emptiness.

The valley is crowded with life.

Every flooded depression stirs. Thin fish roll near the surface, their sides giving off a slow bluish gleam. Mudskippers drag themselves along the banks in numbers too great to count, leaving lacework trails that crisscross and vanish under new seepage. Insects float in dense low clouds without making a recognizable hum. Their wings catch no light, but their bodies wink on and off like small machine indicators.

There are birds, though few are ever seen whole. Long-legged waders move through the reeds with the care of surveyors. Their feet touch water without breaking its surface. Somewhere beyond the visible works, a flock lifts in silence, and for a moment the darkness itself appears to flex. Frogs call only at long intervals: one hard note, then another several minutes later from impossibly far away. On the spoil banks, pale dogs or dog-shaped animals stand with their heads lowered. Their eyes do not reflect the lamps. They are often found facing the same direction, as though listening to a distant engine.

The lamps are scattered and industrial: sodium globes over a dock, a humming blue tube in the window of a tool shed, red warning lights along the arms of a dredge. But their illumination is weak, swallowed close to the source. A lit shack seems to float by itself in an ocean of dark. A locomotive can pass three hundred yards away and be known only by the dull red movement of its cab window and the slow rearrangement of mist around it.

The machinery is old but not dead.

Across the marsh, pumps work at an almost unnoticeable pace. Their pistons rise and fall once every few minutes. Conveyor belts sag between pylons, carrying black stone or wet ore toward buildings with no apparent destination. The belts move slowly enough that birds nest beneath them. Rusted cranes pivot by degrees. A dredger sits in a borrow pit with its toothed bucket half submerged; each dawnless hour it draws one mouthful of mud from the water and places it on the bank, where the mound has become a small hill over the years.

No one seems to be in charge of these processes. The scattered buildings are occupied intermittently. A watch house with one yellow window. A canteen on stilts, its door tied shut with wire. A fuel depot where the tanks are wrapped in vines and condensation. Long ago, perhaps, this was an extraction district: peat, salt, iron-rich clay, something pumped from below. The industry remains because stopping it would be more difficult than allowing it to continue.

There are rules in the valley, although no signs list them clearly.

Water rises toward noise. A shouted word can draw a shallow sheet across dry ground, creeping toward the speaker with the patience of spilled oil. For this reason people communicate with lamps, taps on pipes, and careful gestures. Engines are kept muffled. Doors are padded. Even the birds have adapted; their wings are broad and soft, and their calls are separated by long silences.

Another rule is that reflections arrive late.

If someone walks past a canal, their reflection may remain standing in the water after they have gone. It does not move much. It may turn its head. The workers avoid looking down after crossing a bridge, particularly if they are tired or feverish. There are stories of a man who saw his reflection climb out and take the lantern from his hand. By morning the man was still there, but he had become the reflection: wet, silent, and unable to cast a shadow.

The ground also remembers weight. Footprints persist in mud that should have flooded hours ago. Tire tracks remain sharp for years, even when the vehicles that made them have rusted into the reeds. Old paths sometimes rise slightly above the plain, built not from soil but from compressed repetitions of passage. You can walk on a track made by generations of workers, animals, and machines, and feel beneath your feet the faint layered pressure of all their movement.

Far out in the open dark are structures that seem too large for the valley’s purpose. A cooling tower, perhaps, though no plant connects to it. A black lattice mast whose upper half cannot be seen even on clear nights. A circular reservoir ringed with concrete steps, full to the brim with perfectly still water. From time to time, the reservoir reflects a dawn: pink cloud, pale sun, bright flying birds. The valley around it remains in complete darkness.

Nothing changes quickly here. Rain comes often, but gently, arriving as a fine warm settling rather than a storm. Channels widen a finger’s width each season. Pump stations sink lower into their foundations. The reeds inch outward over the spoil. Metal slowly becomes mineral-colored, then furred with algae, then hidden. Whole sheds subside until only their roofs remain above water, and even then their electric lights may continue to burn under the surface.

The people who live along the embankments—if they are people—build lightly. Their houses stand on pilings or old shipping containers, joined by narrow walkways. They keep caged lamps, sealed food tins, boots with high rubber tops, and ropes for finding the way home through mist. They fish with nets that catch both fish and fragments of machinery: gears, teeth, coins, small polished bones, screws stamped with dates from the future. They do not travel far after the pumps begin their midnight cycle.

In the deepest part of the marsh, where the drainage canal meets the old excavation, there is said to be an opening beneath the water. It is not a hole so much as a region where the water becomes vertical. Reeds bend toward it. Fish circle it endlessly, growing pale and blind with age. The dredger has been pointed away from it for decades.

Sometimes, on the quietest nights, the opening emits a low pressure that can be felt in the ribs but barely heard. Every animal in the valley becomes still. The pumps hesitate. The black water smooths itself. Then, far below the mud, something vast turns over in its sleep, and the lights in every scattered building dim together.
