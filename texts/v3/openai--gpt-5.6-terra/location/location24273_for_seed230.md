The town occupies a shelf of stone high above a valley that is mostly weather and distance. It is not large enough to have districts, exactly, but it has accumulated the habits of a city: lanes with names, a market square, retaining walls repaired so many times that their repairs have become their principal material, and a public cistern whose water is accounted for with more care than grain.

From below, it appears as a low, pale arrangement against the mountain: flat roofs, smoke-darkened parapets, narrow towers built less for grandeur than for watching the paths. The buildings are made from the mountain itself, quarried from a band of gray-green stone just above the settlement. Their walls are thick, their doors small, their windows shuttered against the cool wind. Roofs are laid with slate, weighted by rounded rocks. Here and there, old timber balconies extend over the lanes, their rails polished smooth by hands and sleeves.

The place is bounded. A cliff forms one edge; a dry ravine, deeply cut and old, forms another. On the remaining sides stand walls no taller than a person and a half, built in patient courses of stone. They are not meant to stop an army. They hold back goats, mark the town’s limits, and remind travelers that beyond them there is only slope, scree, and the long ascent toward colder ground.

Water arrives irregularly and is never wasted. Rain falls perhaps once a week, perhaps only as a brief silver drifting through the upper streets. Fog sometimes pushes over the ridge at dawn and clings to the roofs. The people have placed cloth screens and combs of reed along the walltops to catch its beads. By morning, condensation gathers in channels carved into stone and runs, drop by drop, into covered jars.

There is one spring, but it is not dramatic. It emerges beneath a shrine built into the rock, filling a shallow basin darkened with mineral stains. The water is clear, cold enough to ache in the teeth. Around it, the paving is always damp. Moss grows in the cracks there, green-black and soft, one of the few lush things in the settlement. Children are told not to scrape it away, because the moss is believed to keep the spring from forgetting its way.

Vegetation survives in deliberate pockets. A few wind-bent junipers stand outside the wall, twisted low and silver at their tips. In courtyards, people keep pots of onion, bitter greens, and blue-flowering herbs. A terrace below the town supports a thin orchard of pear trees, each trunk protected by a circle of stones. The trees do not thrive so much as persist. Their fruit is small, hard, and valued. Near the ravine, dry grasses collect in tawny clumps, and little white flowers appear after rain, so briefly that their presence is spoken of more often than it is seen.

There are few animals. Goats move among the stones beyond the wall, attended by a single old dog whose bark is rare and economical. Ravens cross overhead, usually alone. Mice live in the grain rooms. At night, if the air is very still, a fox may pass the refuse pit below the southern stair, leaving narrow prints in the dust. No one considers this remarkable. The fox has always been there, or has always been replaced by another fox much like it.

The town’s streets are steep and carefully worn. Steps are cut directly into bedrock where possible. Elsewhere, stone blocks have settled under generations of feet until each has a shallow central hollow. Water channels run alongside them, dry most days, clean after rain. The main lane rises from the gate to the square in a slow turn, allowing pack animals to climb without breaking their legs. Its paving is patched with slate, river stone carried from far below, and fragments of old millstones.

At the center stands the market hall: an open-sided structure with heavy wooden posts and a roof of overlapping stone slabs. On market mornings, which come at regular intervals and are the nearest thing the town has to a festival, blankets are spread on the ground with salt, wool, dried roots, crude iron nails, tallow candles, pottery, and the occasional piece of colored glass brought up from the lowlands. Trade is quiet. Prices are remembered rather than negotiated loudly. A person may stand before a basket of turnips for several minutes before speaking.

The technology of the town is old, hand-kept, and sufficient. There is a smithy with a squat charcoal furnace, bellows made from stitched hide, and a blackened anvil set into a stump. Iron is precious but known: used for hinges, knife edges, picks, nails, and the thin bands around water barrels. Most tools are wood, stone, fired clay, bone, rope, or leather. The potter’s wheel turns by foot. Grain is ground with paired stones. Messages travel by walker, tied into a cord or pressed onto a small clay tablet, because paper does poorly in the damp seasons and is too costly to waste.

Nothing in the town is new enough to stand out. The houses have been altered, extended, narrowed, joined, and divided over so long a time that each one contains the shapes of previous uses. A stable may have once been a kitchen. A family room may preserve the stone lip of an older well. In one alley, a bricked-up doorway is still painted with a faded red hand, an old ward against illness. No one can say when it was painted. No one tries to remove it.

The air is cool even at midday. Sunlight reaches the shelf cleanly, with little haze, and brings a modest warmth to the south-facing walls. In shadow, breath can be seen for much of the year. Smoke rises from chimneys in thin, straight threads unless the wind comes down from the pass. Then it catches in corners, smelling of peat, juniper, wool grease, and the faint mineral tang of wet stone.

Sound does not travel far here. The ravine takes it. Most of the day consists of small noises: the scrape of a broom across paving, a mallet closing a barrel hoop, a kettle lid trembling over a fire, a goat’s hoof striking stone, the brief clatter of shutters being opened. From the smithy comes an occasional measured ringing. From the spring, a nearly inaudible dripping. Voices are low, partly from custom and partly because loud speech feels exposed beneath the open sky.

At evening, lamps appear behind oiled parchment and horn panes. Their light is weak and yellow, enough to make doorways warm against the blue stone around them. People sit close to walls or within courtyards, mending harness, sorting beans, twisting cord, shaving pegs for some repair that cannot be postponed forever. The square empties. The gate is pulled shut, not barred heavily, but secured with a beam polished by the same hands each night.

Above the town, the mountain remains much as it has always been: ledges, seams, dark streaks where moisture seeps from hidden fractures. Below, the valley opens in muted colors—brown grass, ash-gray rock, a distant line that might be a river when the light catches it. The settlement does not grow toward either view. It has found the portion of ground it can keep, and has settled into it with the permanence of lichen in a crack.

Even the weather seems to respect this arrangement. Rain comes, then passes. Frost forms and retreats from the shaded stones. A little moss thickens around the spring; a little dust gathers on the upper stairs. Walls lean but do not fall. The people age, trade, repair, draw water, and bury their dead in the stony field beyond the north wall, where each grave is marked by one upright slab.

Nothing much changes. That is not a failure of imagination in the town, but its achievement. High in the cool air, among sparse trees and patient stone, it has made a life that does not need to become something else.
