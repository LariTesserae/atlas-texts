The plain has no horizon in the ordinary sense. It is too broad, too low, and too unfinished for one. Distance does not resolve into sky; it dissolves into a brown-violet vapor that hangs close over the ground, reflecting a dull furnace-light from somewhere above the clouds. The air is hot enough to soften resins and bring sweat immediately to the skin, but it is never dry. Moisture beads on every surface: on walls, on tools abandoned beside ditches, on the enormous slabs of manufactured ground. It gathers, swells, falls, and gathers again. Nothing here has the chance to become dusty.

The land is made.

Not built upon, not altered, but made down to whatever lies beneath one’s feet. The ground is a continental floor of interlocked plates: black baked clay, pale fired brick, porous ceramic blocks, fused salt, lengths of hammered copper greened by condensate. Some of the plates are as wide as villages. Others are scarcely larger than a hand and are fitted together with an elaborate patience that seems at odds with the immense scale of the work. Their edges have not yet worn smooth. They rise, settle, lift again. New seams open along straight lines and release hot vapor.

There are no mountains, though there are elevations: long artificial swells, ridges formed from the overlap of thousands upon thousands of laid courses. They roll away across the wet plain like the backs of buried machines. At their crests stand sparse constructions—tall tripod towers of timber darkened by heat, scaffolds wrapped in damp fiber, round kilns whose chimneys reach into the low cloud. The towers are not high enough to command the landscape. Nothing is. At this scale, a tower is only a thorn.

Everywhere, the world seems under construction by workers who are absent.

Stone mallets lie in shallow pools. Bone-handled awls, clay buckets, wooden sledges, baskets woven from an unknown mineral thread: all are placed neatly or dropped mid-task, and none are old enough to have decayed. In the dimness, some tools appear to shift position between glances. A row of wedge-shaped stones may be pointing east when first seen and west when looked at again. The change is never accompanied by a sound.

There are no plants. No grass in the cracks, no algae along the hot water, no reeds in the marshy basins. Even mold has failed to take hold. Yet the landscape has growth.

At the margins of the shallow water, pale mineral forms bud from the paving. They begin as blisters in the ceramic, then rise into curled lips, hollow tubes, cupped sheets, lattices like the internal structure of shells. They are not alive in any recognizable way, but they behave with the persistence of life. By morning they may have assembled themselves into low thickets of chalky ribs; by evening, a sheet of rain or a pressure wave from below will collapse them into slurry. The slurry hardens overnight into fresh paving, stamped with tiny repeating marks.

In some districts, the ground manufactures walls.

A line of bricks pushes upward one by one from a flat surface, each wet and red and visibly warm. They stack themselves with slow, muscular certainty. Mortar extrudes from the joints. By the time the wall is chest-high it has curved, perhaps into a corridor, perhaps into the beginning of a room. Then the ground beneath it rearranges. The wall leans, splits, folds down, and is quietly reabsorbed into the floor. A person might walk for days through the remains of such aborted architecture: doors opening onto nothing, stairs descending three steps into solid material, rooflines half formed at ankle height.

The water is frequent but shallow. It lies in channels that have been carved not by erosion but by decision. They are too straight at first, then abruptly meander where the land has changed its mind. The liquid is hot, cloudy, and faintly metallic. It runs over brick and bronze with a soft, continuous whisper. There are no fish, no insects skating its skin, no roots drinking from it. Steam rises from it in strands, adding to the damp fog.

At intervals the channels swell without rain. Water climbs their banks, flows across the plain in a thin mirror, and gathers in every depression. In the reflected dimness, the world doubles: kilns become inverted wells, scaffold towers hang downward forever, and the low clouds appear to be another tiled floor overhead. Then a distant tremor passes through the constructed earth. The water drains all at once through millions of hairline gaps, leaving the surfaces washed and gleaming.

The storms do not travel across this place. They happen everywhere, selectively.

One section of plain may be calm beneath humid darkness while, a mile away, a column of boiling mist turns slowly over an unfinished reservoir. Beyond that, a rainstorm falls upward from the paving into a black pocket in the clouds. Lightning is uncommon, but when it comes it is low and horizontal, crawling across walls and puddles in branching blue veins. It never seems to strike. It searches.

The principal sound is water touching things: drips from a copper lintel; runoff ticking through a screen of pottery; hot condensation striking a kiln hood and vanishing. Under that is a deeper sound, nearly below hearing—a long grind, the pressure of slabs moving into alignment. Sometimes the ground emits a single heavy knock from far beneath, as if a giant mallet has struck the underside of the world. Then silence returns, expansive and thick.

The heat has its own presence. It comes from below through the constructed strata, rising through vents, cracks, and the hollow cores of unfinished buildings. The air shimmers close to the ground, though the light is too dim for a clean shimmer. Things blur at their edges. A distant ridge may seem to breathe, expanding in the haze; a line of walls may turn into a procession of upright figures, until closer approach reveals only masonry.

There is no sun visible. Perhaps there is one above the vapor, but the light that reaches the plain is red-gold and exhausted, as if it has passed through many roofs. At certain hours, broad regions brighten from underneath. Thin seams between ground plates begin to glow, first orange, then white. The illumination travels in deliberate grids across hundreds of miles, revealing the wet land as a vast diagram. Pools flash silver. Towers cast long, weak shadows. Newly made structures stand in brief clarity before the seams dim and the fog closes in again.

The place has rules, though they are not stable enough to become laws.

A constructed thing left alone will eventually attempt to join another constructed thing. Two loose bricks may drag themselves through mud until their faces meet. A broken handle may seek the bucket it once belonged to. A fallen wall will slowly orient its fragments toward its foundation, though it may rebuild itself incorrectly: windows underground, threshold facing the ceiling, bricks arranged in a language of weight rather than purpose.

Nothing can remain a ruin for long. It either becomes material again or becomes architecture. The world dislikes the intermediate state.

Likewise, no surface remains truly level. Flatness is only the earliest stage of a future shape. The immense plain is always buckling into forms—ramps, basins, terraces, circular foundations, walls that begin as ridges. Yet because the process is turbulent and blind, every formation is interrupted by another. The place is perpetually on the first day of its own construction.

There may once have been makers here. The primitive apparatus suggests hands: rope, wedge, pulley, lever, kiln, chisel. But the amount of material exceeds any imaginable workforce. The plain is a worksite expanded beyond the scale of labor, an artisan’s grammar spread across a continent and left to continue without artisans. Perhaps the tools are not abandoned. Perhaps they are the makers, resting between gestures too slow or too vast to witness.

At the farthest visible edge, where dim cloud meets wet masonry, enormous frames emerge from the vapor. They resemble cranes made of dark timber and fired clay, but their beams vanish upward and their foundations vanish downward. Cables hang from them into the haze, taut and trembling. Now and then one cable rises, hauling nothing visible. The ground beneath it wrinkles. A fresh district of paving appears where there had been water.

Then the vapor closes.

The heat presses on. Water collects in the joints. Somewhere, a new brick lifts itself from the floor.
