The city lies low in a broad green basin, where the land has not yet decided what shape it wants to keep.

Its ground is new. Dark volcanic shelves push up through the soil in long, clean fractures, their edges still sharp enough to split a boot sole or shear the roots of an incautious tree. Between them, softer earth has gathered: black loam, powdered glass, pale mineral sand. The whole valley feels recently uncovered, as though an older world has been peeled away and this one is still cooling beneath it. There are no mountains close by, only a ring of low, unfinished ridges in the distance, hazed over by foliage and dim weather.

Vegetation has taken the opportunity with a kind of violence.

Huge-leafed creepers spill from every cut in the stone. Ferns as tall as small houses occupy the drainage hollows. Trees rise from cracks that seem much too narrow to hold their roots, their trunks smooth and whitish, their crowns knitting across the gray sky in patches. Some grow in disciplined groves, planted centuries ago along roads that no longer entirely exist; others swarm over retaining walls, transit pylons, abandoned construction frames, and the old embankments at the basin’s edge.

It is not wet, exactly. The valley has no great river and rarely enough rain to make streams. But moisture is always arriving from somewhere else. It beads on metal railings at noon. It gathers under leaves in heavy clear droplets. Every few days, warm rain passes over in a brief slanting sheet, then vanishes, leaving the streets steaming. At night, condensation pours from the undersides of the city’s elevated structures in steady silver threads. There are pools where the ground has sagged around hot mineral vents, each one edged in waxy reeds and bright orange moss.

The city itself has been built sparsely across this open terrain, as though its inhabitants valued distance more than walls.

A tower may stand alone in a field of reeds, joined to the next tower by a bridge so thin it is almost invisible against the clouds. Whole districts consist of platforms on narrow stems, raised above the growing things. Other neighborhoods are low, compact clusters tucked into stone folds, their roofs stepped and gardened, their outer surfaces coated with ceramic scales that open and close in response to heat. Long transit lines cross the basin on elegant arcs, but they do not appear to rest on supports. Cars move across them without wheels, floating with a restrained and constant hum.

There are streets, though few are meant for ordinary vehicles. Most are broad mineral paths, pale in the dimness, designed to drain the intermittent rain and to give migrating animals room to pass. At certain hours, the city’s moving systems yield to herds.

The animals are everywhere.

Long-legged grazing things with translucent ears come out of the fernlands at dusk and drift beneath the elevated stations. Small six-winged insects cloud around the warm lamps, their bodies flashing green-blue like sparks trapped in resin. Great black waterbirds perch on the silent rails, waiting for insects to rise from the condensation channels. In the hollows, something broad-backed and amphibious turns over stones with a sound like wet masonry. Predators keep to the ridges and the engineered woodland corridors, though occasionally one is seen crossing an open plaza: tawny, low-bodied, ribbed with luminous organs beneath its hide, unhurried in the manner of an animal that has never needed to fear a fence.

The people do not fight the fauna so much as negotiate around it. Buildings project fields that animals can sense but cannot see. Garden terraces close their membranes when burrowers approach. Public signs bloom along paths in colors visible to both human eyes and the patterned eyes of the basin’s larger creatures. The city’s rules are embedded less in law than in response: do not block the old migration grades; do not drain a condensation basin; keep sound barriers active during nesting cycles; never cut a root bridge unless the city has already begun growing another.

None of this makes the place quiet.

It is deafening.

The valley is full of sound at every hour, a vast mechanical-biological weather system. The transit arcs sing in pitches too low to locate. Vent stacks release pressure with immense hollow roars. Foliage rattles and slaps under the passing wind. Thousands of insect wings make a high electrical hiss over the wetlands. The larger animals call to one another across open ground, their voices carrying like foghorns through the dim green distance. From beneath the city comes the repetitive thunder of invisible systems adjusting the soil, shifting weight, deepening foundations, redirecting roots and water.

Sometimes the sound rises until conversation becomes impossible. Then people gather under the acoustic canopies along the paths, where the air is suddenly soft and still. The canopies are not roofs in any familiar sense. They are woven lattices of engineered fiber, grown rather than assembled, that catch certain frequencies and pass others through. Under one, rain can be heard as separate drops. Outside it, rain is swallowed by the city’s continuous roar.

The technology is everywhere, but it has become difficult to distinguish from ecology. Vines wrap around maintenance spines and are fed trace minerals through them. Pavement heals cracks by lifting pale threads from beneath itself. In the evenings, a faint blue mist moves between buildings, carrying microscopic machines that settle onto surfaces and eat corrosion, pollen, and exhaust. The mist smells vaguely of stone after lightning. It avoids skin, animal fur, fruit, and open water with a precision no one bothers to remark upon anymore.

The light is poor even at midday. Heavy cloud covers the basin for long stretches, not storm-dark but pearl-dark, the color of old aluminum. The tree crowns deepen it. Towers catch what little brightness there is and distribute it downward through internal shafts, so that some plazas glow softly from their own paving stones while the surrounding fields remain shadowed. At night, the city refuses brilliance. Light is kept low and amber near the ground, violet higher up, and entirely absent across the migration corridors. The ridges become black silhouettes. The distant infrastructure hangs in the air as lines of faintly lit bone.

Beyond the developed districts, the basin continues for kilometers: stands of wet forest, raw stone uplifts, empty engineered farms gone feral, broad open lawns maintained by grazing animals and silent autonomous cutters. There is room here—far more room than the population requires. The emptiness is not abandonment. It is deliberate, perhaps even sacred. Space has been left for roots, for floodless water, for animal passage, for the movement of air and noise across the low land.

And yet the valley never feels vacant.

Something is always emerging: a new green slope from a construction scar, a line of white towers from the trees, a herd crossing beneath a train, a sudden warm rain. The world is young enough that its seams still show. The city is old enough to have learned that it cannot cover them.
