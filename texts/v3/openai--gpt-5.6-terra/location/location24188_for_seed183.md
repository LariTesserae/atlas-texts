The plateau begins above the weather.

It is not a single flat height but a country of heights: shelves, basins, tilted pavements, narrow ridges that rise and fall behind curtains of vapor. The ground is pale stone—chalk-white in some places, blue-gray in others—and it has been worn into old, dependable forms. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even the sharpest cliffs have a settled quality, as if they have been cutting the sky for a very long time and have learned exactly how much wind they can bear.

Water is everywhere, though there is no river broad enough to name. It runs in seams beneath translucent mineral skins. It gathers in black hollows. It drips from overhangs in steady strands. It travels down walls in sheets so thin they look at first like reflected light. On the high flats, rainwater does not always lie in pools; it enters the stone and illuminates its grain. Whole pavements shine from within, their cracks full of moving silver.

The air is cool and painfully clear between storms. Brightness descends without softness. The sun—or whatever performs the office of the sun here—makes every wet surface flare. Distant cliffs appear close enough to touch, until a shift in the haze reveals that they are many miles away. Sometimes the horizon bends upward. Sometimes it has folds, like a length of damp cloth being lifted from beneath. The landscape accepts this without disturbance. Water continues dripping. The stone continues weathering.

There are no trees, no grass, no moss at the edges of the water. Nothing roots in the cracks. Nothing flies across the open spaces. The lack of animal sound is so complete that the other noises become enormous.

Rain arrives audibly before it arrives visibly: a far-off rasp, then a gathering rattle, then a descending roar across the rock fields. Water strikes the plateau in hard bright needles. It ricochets from slabs, fills channels, rushes through natural staircases, and plunges over cliff lips in white noise. When the rain passes, the land does not become quiet. Steam hisses from dark fissures. Stone shifts somewhere beneath the surface with a long, grinding throat-noise. Crystals growing in the wet seams make small, constant ticking sounds, like innumerable tools at work behind a wall.

The ground is dense with mineral presences. There are columns packed shoulder to shoulder in certain valleys: some round and fluted, some squared by strange natural fracture, some twisted as though soft when they cooled. There are fields of leaning stone blades, their edges dulled by centuries of rain. There are black glass slopes that reflect the sky with unnerving fidelity, except that the reflection is often delayed. A cloud may pass overhead and only later cross the surface below. On particularly bright days, pools show a sky belonging to another hour: dusk in the middle of noon, stars trembling beneath rain.

Along the ridges stand scattered shelters.

They are primitive constructions, but they are not careless. Low walls have been made from fitted slabs, with no mortar visible between them. Some shelters are circular, their roofs formed from overlapping stone plates. Others are mere windbreaks built against boulders, with dark entrances and narrow drainage ditches scratched into the ground beside them. Here and there a cairn marks a crossing, or a line of upright rocks directs a traveler around an invisible danger.

The tools left in and around these places are simple: hammerstones, chipped wedges, shallow bowls, spatters of fired clay, lengths of braided mineral fiber that may once have been rope. At one shelter, rain has collected in a row of hand-carved basins. At another, flat stones have been set upright in a circle, each one carved with a different arrangement of grooves. When water runs through the grooves, the stones sing.

Not metaphorically. They sing.

The notes are low at first, almost below hearing: a pressure in the teeth, a vibration in the ribs. Then, as the basins fill and spill in sequence, the circle begins to voice a chord. The sound carries across the wet plateau. It is joined by other circles farther away, sometimes answering, sometimes arriving half a beat late. In fog, the country seems inhabited by immense buried instruments.

No one is seen maintaining them.

Perhaps no one needs to. The land has become practiced at its own maintenance. Water follows channels cut by hands long vanished; frost opens the joints precisely where a fresh stone is needed; small collapses furnish building material beside the old walls. The shelters endure not because they are untouched, but because every ruin here is slowly made useful again by pressure, wetness, and time.

The cliffs are especially strange.

Their faces contain strata that do not agree about gravity. One band sheds pebbles downward. The next sends them drifting sideways, slow as falling ash. Higher still, rain can be seen running upward in thin silver threads until it disappears into a cloudless brightness. There are caves whose entrances are on vertical walls, yet their floors slope toward the open air as if the inside of the cliff has been turned ninety degrees. A person standing near one of these mouths might hear water splashing deep within, then see a droplet emerge and climb away across the stone.

The plateau is full of rules like this, local and firm. They do not feel random. They feel old.

In one basin, every sound is repeated from the nearest ridge rather than echoed back from a distance. A footstep there seems to occur twice: once under the foot, once overhead. In another, shadows remain fixed while their objects move through the day. There are patches of ground where wet footprints appear a few paces before anyone steps. A long road of flat stones crosses a saddle between two valleys; walking it in one direction takes an afternoon, but walking back by the same route can bring a traveler home before the clouds have changed.

The road is marked by little stacked shrines, each built with three stones: a base, a lintel, and a narrow upright piece in between. Water beads constantly on their surfaces, even under a fierce dry glare. Some have offerings in their sheltered spaces—smooth stones, broken bowls, a worked bone-colored object that is not bone, copper-green pebbles threaded on cord. These objects are often warm despite the cool air.

At the highest elevations, the plateau breaks apart into islands of rock suspended in a bright void. They are not far above the land; from below, their undersides can be seen through gaps in the cloud. Water pours from them in vertical falls, though the falls sometimes stop halfway down and become hanging columns of rain. The suspended islands are heavily weathered, carrying their own basins, their own shelter walls, their own clusters of singing stones. They move so slowly that their motion is only apparent when a familiar ridge is compared with a remembered one.

Nothing grows upon them.

Yet the world does not feel dead.

The mineral surfaces react to weather with something like attention. Pale crusts close over fresh cracks. Dark lichenoid-looking stains spread across stone after rain, though they are not alive and leave no spores, no roots, no trace when scraped away except a faint warmth beneath the surface. In flooded depressions, needle crystals rise overnight in ranks, then dissolve at dawn with a faint glassy clatter. Some walls exhale fog from pores too small to see. Others pulse once, very gently, when thunder passes overhead.

The weather moves constantly through this country. Brightness, cloud, rain, vapor, hard blue openings, and sudden sheets of white hail trade places over the course of an hour. Storms roll below the upper ridges, their cloud tops spilling into valleys like boiling water. Lightning rarely strikes from sky to ground. More often it moves horizontally across the land, leaping from wet pillar to wet pillar in branching white lines. The sound comes afterward as a huge, close concussion, followed by the long ringing of the stone.

After such a strike, the ground may be changed. A new crack opens, filled with clear water. A low hill is found to have shifted several yards, leaving a trench of exposed darkness behind it. A shelter wall stands intact but faces the wrong direction. Once in a while, a whole section of the distant horizon is simply absent for several minutes, replaced by brilliant blankness through which rain can be seen falling.

Then the familiar mountains return, wet and patient, as though nothing has happened.

At evening—if evening is the right word for the slow cooling of the light—the plateau becomes blue. Water gathers its brightness into the channels. The high suspended stones darken overhead. The singing circles quiet as their basins drain, leaving only individual notes: a drip, a tone, a drip, another tone from beyond the ridge.

The scattered shelters become almost invisible then, part of the rock except for their small dark openings. There is nowhere for animal life to hide, nowhere for leaves to stir. The land is open, exposed, and crowded with stone, water, weather, old craft, and impossible continuities.

It does not wait for a visitor.

It goes on making its sounds.
