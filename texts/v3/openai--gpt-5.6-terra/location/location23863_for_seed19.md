At the roof of the world there is a country of old stone, too high for weather to properly reach.

The land is not mountainous in the young, violent sense. Nothing here is climbing or breaking. The peaks have already spent their sharpness. They rise in vast pale backs and black ridges, rubbed smooth by epochs of wind that no longer blows. Their slopes are folded with terraces, shelves, and long fields of splintered mineral. Every exposed surface has the faintly polished look of a bone kept in a grave for a thousand years.

There is no water.

Not even a hidden thread beneath the stones, not even frost in the shadows. The cold is dry and exact. It inhabits cracks without becoming ice; it makes metal brittle, hides breath almost as soon as it leaves a mouth, and gives the sky an enormous clarity. The air is thin enough that distance seems close. A ridge fifty miles away is visible as clearly as the stones at one’s feet, its layers of ocher, violet, chalk-white, and dark green mineral drawn with unnatural precision.

The valleys between the high mesas are crowded.

Not with forests, or buildings, or people exactly, but with animals: innumerable animals, pressed across the dry basins and hanging from the slopes in configurations that appear at first to be herds. A closer look makes the eye uncertain. There are woolly, six-legged grazers with tall spiraling antlers; long-backed things like pale lizards wearing coats of matted silver hair; flocks of membraned gliders resting upright on cliff faces, folded so tightly they resemble vein-patterns in the rock. Beetles the size of plates drift in slow columns through the air, their translucent wing-cases catching the ordinary afternoon light.

Nothing hunts.

Nothing flees.

A narrow-horned beast may stand with its muzzle against the flank of a creature that ought to be its prey. A catlike animal, all black joints and white teeth, sleeps beneath the chest of a towering grazer. Thousands of small, blind bodies work through the gravel in dense, patient knots, yet leave no trails behind them. There are too many lives here for the barrenness of the place, and too little movement for life to feel alive. The ground seems to supply them with something more basic than food: a mineral instruction, perhaps, absorbed through hoof and claw and scaled belly.

The sparse vegetation is likewise not truly green. It grows from seams in the stone as stiff gray fans, rust-colored cushions, and branching thickets of transparent tubes. In sheltered hollows stand little groves of ironwood: leafless trunks no thicker than wrists, each rising only a few feet before dividing into intricate antler forms. They make no seeds. Around their roots lie beads of cloudy quartz, and from these, after many years, another small tree seems simply to have appeared.

The few human structures are difficult to identify as human.

A watch hut made of stacked slate sits on a pass, half merged into the slope. Its doorway is open, but the interior contains only a stone bench, a clay lamp with no oil, and a row of finger-length tools made from black glass. Farther down the valley, a bridge crosses a crevasse that has no bottom visible from above. It is a modest bridge, only six paces long, constructed from lashed animal bones and smooth red planks. No road approaches it. At either end, hoofprints, pawprints, and bare human footprints lie impressed in the dust—perfectly preserved, perfectly overlapping, perhaps made on the same day, perhaps made before the mountains became old.

Here and there are cairns. Some are carefully built from flat stones. Some are made from skulls. Some are indistinguishable from the animals gathered around them until, after a while, one notices that the animals have not blinked.

Sound is scarce.

There is the dry click of a hoof settling on shale, the papery rasp of a glider adjusting its wings, a distant chime where mineral spires touch one another. Even these sounds seem not to travel through the air so much as arrive directly inside the listener’s head. Voices do not echo. A shout goes only as far as its speaker’s outstretched hand and then hangs there, visible for a moment as a faint distortion in the cold light.

The sky is normal in color most of the time: a hard, lucid blue, darkening toward near-black above the highest ridgelines. The sun shines cleanly. Shadows fall in the expected direction.

But occasionally a shadow remains after its owner has moved.

An animal will walk away from a boulder, and its silhouette will still be standing against the boulder’s face, breathing slowly in black. A flock will rise from a cliff, yet the pattern of their folded bodies stays embedded in the stone. At noon, a person can sometimes see their own earlier footsteps on a slope they have not yet descended.

No one in the high country treats this as a marvel. The scattered shelters have rules scratched into their doorframes in a common, blunt pictographic language:

Do not drink what is not water.  
Do not count the animals.  
If you find yourself ahead of yourself, wait.  
Never move a stone that has chosen a shape.

The last rule is the oldest. It may be the only one that matters.

Because this landscape has settled. It has reached an equilibrium beyond seasons. Its dust does not accumulate, its cliffs do not fall, its creatures do not noticeably age. A carcass may lie on a ridge for centuries without rotting, while around it its descendants—or repetitions—stand in a quiet circle. The sun crosses the sky, but somehow its passage does not alter the day. Night comes, cool and star-thick, and the same herds stand in the same places beneath the same cliffs.

In darkness, the mineral plants show a low internal glow. The valleys become dense with blue-gray points: eyes, quartz beads, lamp-stones, and things beneath the gravel whose names were lost with the first people to come here.

From a distance, the entire region resembles a map of a vanished sea: ridges like frozen waves, basins like drained harbors, animal multitudes gathered along invisible shores.

It is a place without water, but not without depth.
