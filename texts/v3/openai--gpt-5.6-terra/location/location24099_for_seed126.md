The world is low and warm and almost level, but it is not a plain in any natural sense. It is a constructed surface extending farther than weather can explain: a continent of fitted mineral plates, pale ceramic ridges, black heat-sinks, lattices of metal grown into stone-like forms. Nothing beneath it is soil. Nothing has ever been permitted to become soil.

At first glance it resembles a desert built by someone with infinite patience. White expanses lie between fields of machinery, dazzling under a hard, nearly vertical sun. The white is not salt, though it has salt’s dry brilliance. It is a crust of exhausted manufacturing compounds, minerals separated and redeposited over ages by processes too slow to watch. In places it is quilted with hexagonal seams a hundred paces across; in others it has cracked into long, shallow polygons, each edge darkened by dust that has nowhere to go except along prescribed channels.

The land is dense with structures, yet it does not feel crowded in the way a city feels crowded. Its density is vertical and internal. Every horizon is occupied by forms: low vaults, towers with no windows, repeating banks of slanted collectors, immense ribs rising from the ground and returning to it miles away. The great works sit so close together that the distance between them often seems accidental, left over rather than designed. There are corridors narrower than streets and wider than valleys. Their walls are not buildings exactly; they are the exposed sides of deeper systems.

Much of the landscape appears ancient because it has reached a condition beyond ruin. Its makers did not abandon it. They perfected its maintenance until maintenance became indistinguishable from age.

The tall things have softened profiles. Their corners have been rounded not by rain or wind, but by the steady passage of abrasive dust, by thermal expansion repeated across epochs, by swarms of repair matter polishing stress fractures before they can become breaks. A tower may lean a degree toward the east, held in that position by internal counterforces that have been calculated for ten thousand years. Plates buckle in graceful, frozen waves. Conduits sag between pylons with the heavy composure of old ropes, though no force known to ordinary engineering could make their materials behave so gently.

There is no open water. There are no rivers, no ponds, no damp shadows with moss around them. The place has systems for cooling, cleansing, transport, and exchange, but they do not use water. Heat travels through shimmering columns of ionized gas. Waste is eaten by lattices of catalytic crystal. Matter is moved as dust, as charge, as tightly guided packets of light running within the ground. Where another world would have drainage ditches, this one has narrow trenches filled with a slow, dark vapor that creeps downhill despite the near-flatness of the land.

Those vapors are one of the few signs that the world is active.

They appear in morningless cycles, rising from vents in the lower basins and flowing in sheets between the structures. They do not obscure anything. Rather, they sharpen edges, bending the bright light around corners and outlining invisible fields. A traveler standing beside one would feel warmth at the ankles and a faint pressure in the teeth. After several hours the vapor thins, crystallizes briefly on exposed surfaces as black frost, and is drawn back into the buried works.

Above, the sky is enormous and mostly empty. It is a pale gold close to the horizon, fading into an almost white blue overhead. There may be an atmosphere; there may only be a managed envelope of gases deep enough to carry heat and dust. It makes weather, of a sort. Vast thermal fronts move across the fabricated plain. In their advance, the sun’s glare becomes granular. Distant towers vanish behind curtains of airborne mineral powder. The powder does not howl. It slides and hisses too softly to be heard at any human distance.

Sound is scarce here.

The ground sometimes emits a low tone, more felt through the feet than heard: a long structural note as one buried region adjusts to the expansion of another. A cluster of shutters may open on a tower face with a sequence of dry clicks. Dust moving across a ceramic field makes the faintest brush of sound. The greater engines are silent, either because they have learned silence or because their operations occur in scales of frequency that living ears cannot keep.

Even activity has become quiet through age.

Along the corridors and ledges grow sparse arrangements of vegetation—if vegetation is the right word. They are not green. Some are fans of copper-colored filaments that unfold during the hottest part of the cycle, presenting themselves to the light. Some are black cushions, hard as fired clay, embedded in seams between plates. Others resemble transparent reeds made of jointed glass, each one holding a vertical line of luminous dust. They do not drink. They intercept stray energy, trace elements, static charges. Their roots, if they have roots, are threads no thicker than hair extending into manufactured stone to negotiate with its circuitry.

They occur in careful, lonely colonies: a narrow band along the lee of a heat wall, a thousand dark discs at the base of a conduit, a garden of glass stems occupying the roof of an inactive vault. Their presence is not wild in the ordinary sense. Yet they reproduce in imperfections, colonize places the builders did not explicitly reserve for them, and slowly alter the surface chemistry around their anchors. The world tolerates them. Perhaps it needs them.

There are few animals.

Occasionally a shadow crosses the bright ground: a six-limbed glider with membrane wings of matte silver, flying low enough that it seems to skim the surface without disturbing the dust. It may be a machine, or an animal descended from one, or a maintenance device that has adopted nesting habits. It lands beneath collector arrays where the air is warm, folds itself into a shape like a discarded piece of metal, and remains still for days.

Small things move more often, but are difficult to see. Minute jointed forms emerge from cracks at the edge of the dark vapor channels. They travel in lines across the white plates, harvesting deposits too slight for larger systems to notice. In bright light they look like loose filings arranging themselves. At night—if night ever falls fully—they give off an intermittent blue signal, each one answering another across the ground. The signals are not loud. They are simply present, like thoughts passing through a sleeping machine.

The landscape’s true inhabitants may be the systems themselves.

There are regions where the built surface behaves almost like an organism. A field of black tiles turns to face the sun in a slow, coordinated motion, each pivot silent, each angle minutely different. A ridge vents a cloud of metallic spores that drift for hundreds of miles before settling into worn seams and becoming new conductive material. Entire plazas of pale blocks lift a finger-width, rotate, and reseat themselves to redistribute the stresses of the crust below. On the scale of a day these changes are subtle. On the scale of a century, mountains are moved.

Not raised, perhaps. This world has little appetite for height.

Its oldest intelligences seem to prefer low forms: vast disks, buried chambers, horizontal engines, surfaces that spread rather than climb. The tallest towers are exceptions, relics of an earlier architectural impulse. Most of the important work occurs beneath the plain, where layers of constructed geology descend farther than any excavation should be able to descend. There are foundations under foundations. Cities under service levels under heat reservoirs under antique command halls whose doors have not opened in an age. The entire planet may be a single machine made from the remains of previous machines, each generation paved over but never entirely erased.

Sometimes the ground opens.

Not violently. A seam widens with ceremonial precision, and a long section of surface folds back. From below comes light stronger than sunlight: clean, colorless, depthless illumination pouring upward from mechanisms arranged too far down to resolve. Suspended shapes rise through it—cargo, perhaps; components; dormant bodies; sections of a structure being transferred from one century to another. Then the seam closes. The dust settles across it. Within minutes the opening is difficult to find.

There are rules here, although no sign displays them.

Nothing is allowed to decay into randomness. Fracture is collected. Heat is redistributed. Every particle that leaves a surface is expected somewhere else. There are zones where the walking ground is firm and zones where it will gently lower a traveler into its internal circulation, not as a trap but as a correction. There are old avenues marked by embedded bands of amber light; following them is safe, though no one remains to say why. Beyond them, the material of the world may be working on a timescale too slow to perceive and too strong to interrupt.

The brightness makes everything seem exposed, but the place is not simple. Under the glare lie innumerable hidden negotiations: dust against charge, temperature against shape, ancient directive against emerging habit. The world is crowded with made things and nearly empty of witnesses.

At the far horizon, where the pale crust meets the white sky, structures merge into a single dark line. The line appears natural from a distance, like a mountain range. Coming closer reveals it to be a wall of closely packed vertical mechanisms, each miles long, all facing the same direction. They have stood there for an age, receiving the sunlight, shedding heat, and waiting—or remembering how to wait.

In the quiet between their shadows, the sparse glass reeds turn toward the warmth.
